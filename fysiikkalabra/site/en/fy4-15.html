Hydro and wind power as energy converters

Why does the same water of the Vuoksi river give three times as much power at Imatra as at Tainionkoski? And why does the power of a wind turbine grow so much more than the wind speed?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how hydro and wind power plants convert the potential energy of water and the kinetic energy of wind into electricity.
  • calculate the power, efficiency and produced energy of hydro and wind power plants.
  • justify why the power of a wind turbine grows with the third power of the wind speed.

Prior knowledge

  • Potential energy is $E_\text{p} = mgh$ and kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy
  • When only gravity does work, potential energy turns into kinetic energy and mechanical energy is conservedchapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle
  • Power tells how fast energy is converted: $P = \tfrac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}$chapter Power

1Think about it

The Vuoksi river, flowing out of Lake Saimaa, runs first through the Tainionkoski power plant and a few kilometres downstream through the Imatra power plant. The same water turns the turbines of both, but the power of Imatra is $192\,\text{MW}$ and that of Tainionkoski $62\,\text{MW}$. The power of a wind turbine, on the other hand, varies with the wind: in a weak wind it is small, but when the wind strengthens only a little, the power can grow many times over.

What do the powers of a hydro and a wind power plant depend on, and why does the wind speed affect the power so strongly?

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    A power plant converts energy

    Hydro and wind power plants do not produce energy out of nothing. They convert the mechanical energy of water or air into electricity. Water behind a dam has potential energy $E_\text{p} = mgh$ relative to the water below. When the water flows down a pipe, the potential energy turns into kinetic energy, as in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle. The fast stream of water pushes on the blades of the turbine and makes it rotate (in many turbines the pressure of the water also does work, but the energy chain is the same), and the shaft of the turbine turns a generator, which converts the energy of rotation into electricity. After the turbine, the water flows slowly on to the tailwater. The difference in height between the upper and the lower water surface is called the head $h$.

    Power tells how much energy per second

    A power plant is described by its power: how much energy it converts in one second. When one cubic metre of water, that is $1000\,\text{kg}$, falls the $24\,\text{m}$ head of Imatra, it releases potential energy $mgh \approx 235\,\text{kJ}$. If hundreds of cubic metres flow through the turbines every second, the power is hundreds of megawatts. So power grows for two reasons: when more water falls each second, and when it falls from higher up. The water of the Vuoksi flows first through the Tainionkoski power plant and then through the Imatra power plant, so the flow rate is the same at both. The head at Imatra is about three times as large, and so is its power.

    Efficiency

    Not all the energy the water loses becomes electricity. The water rubs against the walls of the pipe, the turbine and the generator warm up, and the water leaving into the tailwater still has a little kinetic energy. The efficiency of a power plant is the ratio of the electric power it delivers $P_\text{s}$ to the power taken in $P_\text{o}$:

    $$\eta = \frac{P_\text{s}}{P_\text{o}}$$

    where $\eta$ (eta) is the efficiency, given as a decimal number or as a percentage. Efficiency is at most $100\,\%$. In real devices it is usually below $100\,\%$, because part of the energy taken in does not end up in the intended useful form, here electricity. The efficiency of a large hydropower plant is about $90\,\%$.

    The kinetic energy of the wind

    In a wind turbine the source of energy is the kinetic energy of moving air, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. The wind pushes on the rotor blades and makes the rotor turn, and the air slows down as it passes the rotor. The rotor cannot stop the air completely, because the air must be able to flow away behind the rotor. So even an ideal rotor can capture only about $59\,\%$ of the power of the wind flowing through it, and a real wind turbine captures less than half. Air is light: a cubic metre has a mass of only about $1.25\,\text{kg}$. This is why the rotor of a wind turbine has to be large, so that a lot of air flows through it every second.

    Hydro and wind power

    Hydropower

    The flow rate $Q$ tells how many cubic metres of water flow through the turbines each second. In time $t$ a volume $V = Qt$ flows through the turbines, and its mass is $m = \rho V = \rho Q t$. Its potential energy over the head $h$ is $mgh = \rho Q t g h$, so the power the water brings is $P_\text{o} = \rho Q g h$. By the definition of efficiency, the electric power is $\eta$ times this:

    $$P = \eta \rho Q g h$$

    where $P$ is the electric power of the plant ($\text{W}$), $\eta$ the efficiency, $\rho$ the density of water ($1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$), $Q$ the flow rate ($\text{m}^3\text{/s}$), $g$ the acceleration due to gravity ($9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$) and $h$ the head ($\text{m}$). The formula assumes that the kinetic energy of the water in the upper and lower water is small, so the energy that is converted is the change in potential energy.

    Wind power

    In time $t$ the air flowing through the rotor fills a cylinder whose cross-sectional area is the area swept by the rotor, $A = \pi r^2$, and whose length is $vt$. The mass of this air is $m = \rho A v t$, and its kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3 t$. So the power of the wind flowing through the rotor is $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3$.

    The turbine converts the fraction $\eta$ of this into electricity:

    $$P = \frac{1}{2}\eta \rho A v^3$$

    where $P$ is the electric power ($\text{W}$), $\eta$ the fraction of the wind's power that becomes electricity, that is the efficiency of the turbine, $\rho$ the density of air (about $1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$), $A$ the area swept by the rotor ($\text{m}^2$) and $v$ the wind speed ($\text{m/s}$).

    Why is the speed raised to the third power?

    When the wind speed doubles, twice as much air flows through the rotor each second, and each kilogram of air has four times as much kinetic energy. So the power grows by a factor of $2 \cdot 4 = 2^3 = 8$. In the power curve of a real turbine, the power grows about $8.9$ times when the wind speed goes from $5\,\text{m/s}$ to $10\,\text{m/s}$, because the efficiency is slightly better at the higher speed. From a wind of about $13\,\text{m/s}$ on, the power stays at $1650\,\text{kW}$. This is the rated power of the turbine, which the generator cannot exceed, so the blades are turned to let part of the wind pass by. Then the formula with a constant efficiency no longer describes the turbine.

    Common misconception

    People often think that a power plant produces energy. A power plant converts energy: the electricity of a hydropower plant comes from the potential energy of water and the electricity of a wind turbine from the kinetic energy of the wind, and some of the energy always turns into something other than electricity. A second mistake is to assume that double the wind gives double the power. The power grows with the third power of the speed, until the turbine reaches its rated power. A third mistake is to mix up power and energy: power is given in watts, and energy is obtained by multiplying power by time. A power plant with a power of $3\,\text{MW}$ produces $3\,\text{MWh}$ of energy in an hour.

    Summary

    • Hydro and wind power plants convert mechanical energy into electricity: a hydropower plant the potential energy of water, a wind turbine the kinetic energy of air.
    • The efficiency $\eta = \tfrac{P_\text{s}}{P_\text{o}}$ is at most $100\,\%$; for a large hydropower plant it is about $90\,\%$ and for a wind turbine below $50\,\%$.
    • The power of a hydropower plant is $P = \eta\rho Q g h$: it is proportional to the flow rate and the head.
    • The power of a wind turbine is $P = \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v^3$: double the wind speed gives about eight times the power, until the turbine reaches its rated power.
    • Energy is power times time, $E = Pt$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      The rotor of a wind turbine has a diameter of $120\,\text{m}$. The wind speed is $9.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the density of air is $1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$. The turbine converts $45\,\%$ of the power of the wind flowing through the rotor into electricity. What is the electric power of the turbine?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The rotor diameter is $d = 120\,\text{m}$, the wind speed $v = 9.0\,\text{m/s}$, the density of air $\rho = 1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$ and the efficiency $\eta = 45\,\%$. We are asked for the electric power $P$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Air flows through the rotor every second, and its kinetic energy per second is the power of the wind, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3$. The turbine converts the fraction $\eta$ of it into electricity, so the electric power comes from the formula for the power of a wind turbine. The area $A$ is the circle swept by the rotor.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The formula gives the asked power directly, once the area is calculated from the radius $r = \tfrac{d}{2}$:

      $$\begin{aligned} P &= \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v^3 \\ A &= \pi r^2 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The radius is $r = 120\,\text{m} : 2 = 60\,\text{m}$, and the efficiency as a decimal number is $\eta = 0.45$. The other quantities are in SI units, and

      $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}^3}$$= \tfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^3}$
      $= \text{W}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate the area and then the power:

      $$\begin{aligned} A &= \pi r^2 \\ &= \pi\cdot(60\,\text{m})^2 \\ &= 11\,309.7\,\text{m}^2 \\ P &= \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v^3 \\ &= 0.5\cdot 0.45\cdot 1.25\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \\ &\quad\cdot 11\,309.7\,\text{m}^2\cdot\left(9.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^3 \\ &= 2\,318\,849\,\text{W} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The electric power is about $2.3\,\text{MW}$, to two significant figures like the wind speed $9.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the efficiency $0.45$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The turbine of the power curve, with a rotor diameter of $82\,\text{m}$, gives a power of $1017\,\text{kW}$ at a wind speed of $9\,\text{m/s}$. The area of this rotor is $(120 : 82)^2 \approx 2.1$ times as large, so the power should be about $2.1 \cdot 1.0\,\text{MW} \approx 2.2\,\text{MW}$. The result $2.3\,\text{MW}$ is of the same order of magnitude. The power of the wind is $5.2\,\text{MW}$, and less than half of it becomes electricity, as it should.

      • Power tells energy per unit of time. Energy is obtained by multiplying power by time, and a day is $24\,\text{h}$: $E \approx 2400\,\text{kWh}$.

      • Density is needed because potential energy is calculated from mass: $\rho Q = 2000\,\text{kg}$ of water falls every second. The error is in the energy: a day is $24\,\text{h}$, not one hour.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      Why can a wind turbine not convert all the kinetic energy of the wind flowing through the rotor into electricity, even if there were no losses at all?

      • Air is light, but tonnes of air flow through a large rotor every second, and its kinetic energy is transferred to the blades. Not all of it is captured, because the air must be able to flow away behind the rotor.

      • In a wind turbine the air does not rise upward, so the potential energy does not change. The air slows down at the rotor, but it cannot stop completely, because it must get away behind the rotor.

      • Correct. If the rotor stopped the air, no new air could flow through. The air continues on more slowly, and the kinetic energy it still has is left unused. The theoretical upper limit is about $59\,\%$.

      • The direction of rotation does not split the wind in two: all the air flowing through the rotor pushes on the blades. Not all the kinetic energy is captured, because the air must be able to flow away behind the rotor.

      3. Explain

      Two similar wind turbines are in different places. At the first place the wind blows steadily at $6\,\text{m/s}$, and at the second at $8\,\text{m/s}$.       Model answer

      The power of a wind turbine is a fraction of the kinetic energy of the air flowing through the rotor each second. When the wind is $8\,\text{m/s}$ instead of $6\,\text{m/s}$, $8 : 6 \approx 1.33$ times as much air flows through the rotor each second. In addition, each kilogram of air has kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ that is $(8 : 6)^2 \approx 1.78$ times as large. So the power grows by a factor of $(8 : 6)^3 \approx 2.4$. This holds when the turbines are similar, that is the area of the rotor and the efficiency are the same, and the wind is lower than the speed at which the turbine reaches its rated power.

        Finally, three quick questions. Answer first on your own and open the answer only after that.

        1. The electric power of a hydropower plant is $50\,\text{MW}$ and its efficiency $90\,\%$. How much power is taken from the water, and where does the difference end up?
          Answer

          The power taken is

          $P_\text{o}$$= \tfrac{P_\text{s}}{\eta}$
          $= \tfrac{50\,\text{MW}}{0.90}$
          $\approx 56\,\text{MW}$

          The difference, about $6\,\text{MW}$, does not end up as electricity: it turns into internal energy through friction in the pipes, the turbine and the generator, and part of it remains as the kinetic energy of the water discharged to the tailwater.

        2. Two hydropower plants give the same electric power with the same efficiency. The head of one is twice the head of the other. How do their flow rates differ?
          Answer

          The flow rate of the higher plant is half that of the other. The power is proportional to the product $Qh$, so when $h$ doubles and the power stays the same, $Q$ halves: every cubic metre gives twice as much energy.

        3. Why does the power of a wind turbine stop growing when the wind becomes stronger than the speed at which the turbine reaches the top of its power curve?
          Answer

          The turbine has reached its rated power, which the generator cannot exceed. The blades are turned so that part of the wind passes by, so the fraction of the wind's power that becomes electricity decreases as the wind strengthens, and the power stays constant.

        1. Core

          The head of a hydropower plant is $15.0\,\text{m}$, and $120\,\text{m}^3$ of water flows through the turbines every second. The efficiency of the plant is $0.900$.

          What is the electric power of the plant?

          MW
          Answer

          15.8922 MW

          Show the solution

          Given

          head $h = 15.0\,\text{m}$, flow rate $Q = 120\,\text{m}^3\text{/s}$, efficiency $\eta = 0.900$; density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the electric power $P$ in $\text{MW}$

          Principle

          The water falling through the turbines loses potential energy $mgh$, and the mass $\rho Q$ falls every second. The plant converts the fraction $\eta$ of this power into electricity, so we use the formula for the power of a hydropower plant.

          Formula

          $P = \eta\rho Q g h$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so the power comes out in watts; finally $1\,\text{MW} = 10^{6}\,\text{W}$.

          Substitution

          $P$$= 0.900 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 120\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 15.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 15\,892\,200\,\text{W}$
          $\approx 15.9\,\text{MW}$

          Answer

          $15.9\,\text{MW}$

          Three significant figures like the head $15.0\,\text{m}$, the flow rate $120\,\text{m}^3\text{/s}$ and the efficiency $0.900$.

          Reasonableness

          A cubic metre of water gives about $1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 15\,\text{m} = 150\,\text{kJ}$ in a $15\,\text{m}$ drop, so $120$ cubic metres per second give about $18\,\text{MW}$, and $90\,\%$ of that is about $16\,\text{MW}$. The power is smaller than the $192\,\text{MW}$ of Imatra, because both the flow rate and the head are smaller.

        2. Core

          One cubic metre of water, with a mass of $1000\,\text{kg}$, falls through the turbine of a power plant from the upper reservoir to the tailwater, $24.0\,\text{m}$ lower.

          By how much does the potential energy of the water decrease?

          kJ
          Answer

          235.44 kJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of water $m = 1000\,\text{kg}$, height difference $h = 24.0\,\text{m}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the decrease in potential energy $E_\text{p}$ in $\text{kJ}$

          Principle

          Gravitational potential energy is proportional to the height above the chosen zero level. When the zero level is the surface of the tailwater, the potential energy of the water in the upper reservoir is $mgh$, and that is exactly how much it decreases when it falls to the tailwater.

          Formula

          $E_\text{p} = mgh$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so the energy comes out in joules; finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{p}$$= 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 24.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 235\,440\,\text{J}$
          $\approx 235\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $235\,\text{kJ}$

          Three significant figures like the height difference $24.0\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The estimate $1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 24\,\text{m} = 240\,\text{kJ}$ is close to the result. If hundreds of cubic metres fall every second, the power is hundreds of times $235\,\text{kW}$, that is tens or hundreds of megawatts, as in large hydropower plants.

        3. The power of the water flowing into the turbine of a hydropower plant is $50.0\,\text{MW}$, and the generator gives an electric power of $46.0\,\text{MW}$.

          What is the efficiency of the plant?

          %
          Answer

          92.0 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          power of the water flowing into the turbine $P_\text{o} = 50.0\,\text{MW}$, electric power $P_\text{s} = 46.0\,\text{MW}$

          Find

          the efficiency $\eta$ as a percentage

          Principle

          Efficiency is the ratio of the useful power obtained to the power taken into the device. Here the useful power is the electric power and the power taken in is the power the water brings to the plant.

          Formula

          $\eta = \dfrac{P_\text{s}}{P_\text{o}}$

          Conversions

          Both powers are in megawatts, so the unit cancels. A decimal number is changed to a percentage by multiplying by one hundred.

          Substitution

          $\eta$$= \dfrac{46.0\,\text{MW}}{50.0\,\text{MW}}$
          $= 0.920$
          $= 92.0\,\%$

          Answer

          $92.0\,\%$

          Three significant figures like the powers $46.0\,\text{MW}$ and $50.0\,\text{MW}$.

          Reasonableness

          The efficiency is below $100\,\%$, as it should be, and the efficiency of a large hydropower plant is typically about $90\,\%$. The losses are $4.0\,\text{MW}$, that is $8\,\%$ of the power taken in.

        4. The area swept by the rotor of a wind turbine is $1500\,\text{m}^2$. The wind speed is $8.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the density of air is $1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

          What is the power of the wind flowing through the rotor?

          kW
          Answer

          480.0 kW

          Show the solution

          Given

          area swept by the rotor $A = 1500\,\text{m}^2$, wind speed $v = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$, density of air $\rho = 1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$

          Find

          the power of the wind $P_\text{wind}$ in $\text{kW}$

          Principle

          In time $t$ a cylinder of air flows through the rotor, with mass $m = \rho A v t$ and kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. The power of the wind is this kinetic energy divided by the time. The efficiency is not needed, because we are asked for the power of the wind, not the electric power.

          Formula

          $P_\text{wind}$$= \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2}{t}$
          $= \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v t\, v^2}{t}$

          so $P_\text{wind} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so the power comes out in watts; finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.

          Substitution

          $P_\text{wind}$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 1500\,\text{m}^2 \cdot \left(8.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^3$
          $= 480\,000\,\text{W}$
          $= 480\,\text{kW}$

          Answer

          $480\,\text{kW}$

          Or $4.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{kW}$, two significant figures like the wind speed $8.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          Every second $1.25 \cdot 1500 \cdot 8\,\text{kg} = 15\,000\,\text{kg}$ of air flows through the rotor, and each kilogram has kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 8^2\,\text{J} = 32\,\text{J}$. The product is $480\,000\,\text{J}$ per second, the same result. Less than half of this would become electricity.

        5. A wind turbine produces electricity at a steady power of $3.00\,\text{MW}$ for $6.00$ hours on a stormy day.

          How much electric energy does the turbine produce in this time? Give the answer in megawatt-hours.

          MWh
          Answer

          18.0 MWh

          Show the solution

          Given

          electric power $P = 3.00\,\text{MW}$, time $t = 6.00\,\text{h}$

          Find

          the electric energy $E$ in $\text{MWh}$

          Principle

          Power tells how much energy is converted per unit of time. When the power stays constant, the energy is power times time.

          Formula

          $P = \dfrac{E}{t}$, so $E = Pt$

          Conversions

          When the power is in megawatts and the time in hours, the energy comes out directly in megawatt-hours, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 3.00\,\text{MW} \cdot 6.00\,\text{h}$
          $= 18.0\,\text{MWh}$

          Answer

          $18.0\,\text{MWh}$

          Three significant figures like the power $3.00\,\text{MW}$ and the time $6.00\,\text{h}$.

          Reasonableness

          In joules the energy is

          $3.00 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W} \cdot 6.00 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
          $\approx 6.5 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{J}$

          A detached house with electric heating uses about $20\,\text{MWh}$ a year, so this is almost one house's consumption for a whole year.

        1. Core

          The head of a planned hydropower plant is $12.0\,\text{m}$ and its efficiency $0.880$. What flow rate is needed for an electric power of $10.0\,\text{MW}$?

          m³/s
          Answer

          96.53106 m³/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          electric power $P = 10.0\,\text{MW}$, head $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, efficiency $\eta = 0.880$; density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the flow rate $Q$ in $\text{m}^3\text{/s}$

          Principle

          Falling water loses potential energy, and the plant converts the fraction $\eta$ of it into electricity. We solve the flow rate that gives the desired power from the formula for the power of a hydropower plant.

          Formula

          $P = \eta\rho Q g h$, so $Q = \dfrac{P}{\eta\rho g h}$

          Conversions

          $P$$= 10.0\,\text{MW}$
          $= 10.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}$

          Substitution

          The quantities are in SI units, and

          $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot \text{m/s}^2 \cdot \text{m}}$
          $= \tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}}$

          so

          $Q$$= \dfrac{10.0 \cdot 10^{6}}{0.880 \cdot 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 12.0}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}}$
          $= 96.53\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}}$

          Answer

          $96.5\,\text{m}^3\text{/s}$

          Three significant figures like the power $10.0\,\text{MW}$, the head $12.0\,\text{m}$ and the efficiency $0.880$.

          Reasonableness

          A cubic metre of water gives about $120\,\text{kJ}$ in a $12\,\text{m}$ drop and about $105\,\text{kJ}$ of electricity. So a power of $10\,\text{MW}$ needs about $10\,000 : 105 \approx 95$ cubic metres per second.

        2. Core

          According to the manufacturer's power curve, a wind turbine with a rotor diameter of $82\,\text{m}$ gives an electric power of $758\,\text{kW}$ at a wind speed of $8.0\,\text{m/s}$. The density of air is $1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

          What fraction of the power of the wind flowing through the rotor is converted into electricity? Give the answer as a percentage.

          %
          Answer

          44.854048 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          rotor diameter $d = 82\,\text{m}$, wind speed $v = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$, electric power $P = 758\,\text{kW}$, density of air $\rho = 1.25\,\text{kg/m}^3$

          Find

          the fraction converted into electricity, that is the efficiency $\eta$, as a percentage

          Principle

          The power of the wind flowing through the rotor is the kinetic energy of the air per second, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3$. The turbine converts the fraction $\eta$ of it into electricity, so the fraction follows from the formula for the power of a wind turbine.

          Formula

          $P = \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v^3$ and $A = \pi r^2$, so $\eta = \dfrac{2P}{\rho A v^3}$

          Conversions

          The radius is $r = 82\,\text{m} : 2 = 41\,\text{m}$ and

          $P$$= 758\,\text{kW}$
          $= 758\,000\,\text{W}$

          Substitution

          $A$$= \pi \cdot (41\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 5281.0\,\text{m}^2$

          and

          $\eta$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 758\,000\,\text{W}}{1.25\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 5281.0\,\text{m}^2 \cdot \left(8.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^3}$
          $= 0.4485$

          Answer

          about $45\,\%$, two significant figures like the diameter $82\,\text{m}$ and the wind speed $8.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The fraction is below the theoretical upper limit of $59\,\%$, and the fraction of a real wind turbine is less than half, as it should be. The power of the wind is about $1.7\,\text{MW}$, and the electric power $0.76\,\text{MW}$ is a little less than half of it.

          Answer

          5.725901 MWh

          Show the solution

          Given

          head $h = 9.50\,\text{m}$, flow rate $Q = 3.20\,\text{m}^3\text{/s}$, efficiency $\eta = 0.800$, time $t = 24.0\,\text{h}$; density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          the electric energy $E$ in $\text{MWh}$

        1. The mean wind speed at a turbine increases from $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ to $9.0\,\text{m/s}$ when the turbine is built on a taller tower. The efficiency stays the same, and the turbine does not reach its rated power.

          How many times as large is the electric power of the turbine in the stronger wind?

          Answer

          3.375

          Show the solution

          Given

          wind speed before $v_1 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$ and after $v_2 = 9.0\,\text{m/s}$; the rotor and the efficiency are the same, and the turbine does not reach its rated power

          Find

          how many times as large the electric power $P_2$ is as $P_1$, the ratio $x$

          Principle

          The electric power is the fraction $\eta$ of the kinetic energy per second of the air flowing through the rotor. When the wind is faster, more air flows through each second in proportion to $v$, and each kilogram has more kinetic energy in proportion to $v^2$. So the power is proportional to the cube $v^3$, when $\eta$, $\rho$ and $A$ stay the same.

          Formula

          $P = \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v^3$, so

          $x$$= \dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$
          $= \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v_2^3}{\tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v_1^3}$
          $= \left(\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^3$

          Conversions

          Both speeds are in the same unit, so the unit cancels and no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $x$$= \left(\dfrac{9.0\,\text{m/s}}{6.0\,\text{m/s}}\right)^3$
          $= 1.5^3$
          $= 3.375$

          Answer

          about $3.4$ times as large, two significant figures like the wind speeds $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ and $9.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          $1.5$ times as much air flows through, and each kilogram has $1.5^2 = 2.25$ times as much kinetic energy: $1.5 \cdot 2.25 \approx 3.4$. The factor is larger than the ratio of the speeds, $1.5$, as follows from the third power.

        2. The rotor of an old wind turbine has a diameter of $82\,\text{m}$, and that of a new one $162\,\text{m}$.

          How many times as much electric power does the new turbine get at the same wind speed, when the efficiency is the same and neither reaches its rated power?

          Answer

          3.903034

          Show the solution

          Given

          diameter of the old rotor $d_1 = 82\,\text{m}$, of the new one $d_2 = 162\,\text{m}$; the wind speed, the density of air and the efficiency are the same, and neither reaches its rated power

          Find

          how many times as large the electric power of the new turbine $P_2$ is as that of the old one $P_1$, the ratio $x$

          Principle

          At the same wind speed, a cylinder of air flows through each rotor every second, and its mass is proportional to the area swept by the rotor, $A = \pi r^2$. Each kilogram of air has the same kinetic energy, so the power is proportional to the area and therefore to the square of the diameter.

          Formula

          $P = \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\rho A v^3$ and $A = \pi\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)^2$, so

          $x$$= \dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$
          $= \dfrac{A_2}{A_1}$
          $= \left(\dfrac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2$

          Conversions

          The diameters are in the same unit, so the unit cancels and no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $x$$= \left(\dfrac{162\,\text{m}}{82\,\text{m}}\right)^2$
          $= 1.9756^2$
          $= 3.903$

          Answer

          about $3.9$ times as large, two significant figures like the diameter $82\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The diameter is about double, so the area is about $2^2 = 4$ times as large, and the result is a little below four. This is why new turbines have ever longer blades: the area grows with the square of the diameter.

        3. The wind blows at $10.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          From what height would a kilogram of water have to fall for its potential energy to decrease by as much as a kilogram of this wind has kinetic energy?

          m
          Answer

          5.09684 m

          Show the solution

          Given

          wind speed $v = 10.0\,\text{m/s}$, mass $m = 1\,\text{kg}$ for both the air and the water; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the height of the fall $h$ in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          The kinetic energy of a kilogram of air is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, and the potential energy of a kilogram of water decreases by $mgh$ in a fall. We set these equal. The mass cancels, so the result does not depend on how many kilograms are compared.

          Formula

          $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $h = \dfrac{v^2}{2 \cdot g}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $h$$= \dfrac{\left(10.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}}$
          $= 5.097\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $5.10\,\text{m}$

          Three significant figures like the wind speed $10.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          A kilogram of wind has kinetic energy of only $50\,\text{J}$, while in the $24\,\text{m}$ drop at Imatra a kilogram of water gives about $235\,\text{J}$. So wind has little energy per kilogram, and air is also light, which is why the rotor of a wind turbine has to be large. Unit: $\tfrac{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{\text{m}/\text{s}^2} = \text{m}$.
