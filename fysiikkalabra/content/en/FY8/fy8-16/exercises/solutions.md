## fy8-16-L1-01
**Given:** wavelength of the $\text{H}\beta$ line in the laboratory $\lambda_0 = 486.1\,\text{nm}$ and in the spectrum of the galaxy $\lambda = 492.4\,\text{nm}$
**Asked:** the redshift $z$, a pure number without a unit
**Principle:** In the light of the galaxy we see the same hydrogen line as in the laboratory, but shifted to a longer wavelength. Redshift is the change in wavelength relative to the laboratory wavelength of the line.
**Formula:** $z = \dfrac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$
**Conversions:** None needed: both wavelengths are in nanometres, and the unit cancels.
**Substitution:** $z = \dfrac{492.4\,\text{nm} - 486.1\,\text{nm}}{486.1\,\text{nm}} = \dfrac{6.3\,\text{nm}}{486.1\,\text{nm}} = 0.01296$
**Answer:** $0.013$, two significant figures, as in the wavelength difference $6.3\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The line shifted by about $6\,\text{nm}$, a little over one percent of its wavelength, so $z \approx 0.01$ is of the right order of magnitude. The redshift is positive because the line shifted to a longer wavelength.

## fy8-16-L1-02
**Given:** redshift $z = 0.0250$, speed of light $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s}$
**Asked:** the recession speed $v$ in kilometres per second
**Principle:** When the redshift is small, about $z < 0.1$, the recession speed of a galaxy is the product of the redshift and the speed of light. Here $z = 0.0250$ is small, so the equation holds.
**Formula:** $v = c\,z$
**Conversions:** None needed: the speed of light is already in kilometres per second, and the redshift is a pure number.
**Substitution:** $v = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s} \cdot 0.0250 = 7495\,\text{km/s}$
**Answer:** $7.50 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{km/s}$, three significant figures, as in the redshift $0.0250$.
**Reasonableness:** A redshift of $0.025$ means that the speed is $2.5\,\%$ of the speed of light: $0.025 \cdot 300\,000\,\text{km/s} = 7500\,\text{km/s}$. The speed is much smaller than the speed of light, as it should be for a small redshift.

## fy8-16-L1-03
**Given:** distance of the galaxy $d = 120\,\text{Mpc}$, Hubble constant $H_0 = 70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$
**Asked:** the recession speed $v$ in kilometres per second
**Principle:** According to the Hubble–Lemaître law, the recession speed of a galaxy is directly proportional to its distance, because the expansion of space increases all distances in the same proportion. The own motion of the galaxy is ignored.
**Formula:** $v = H_0\,d$
**Conversions:** None needed: the distance is in megaparsecs, so the megaparsec cancels and the speed comes out in kilometres per second.
**Substitution:** $v = 70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}} \cdot 120\,\text{Mpc} = 8400\,\text{km/s}$
**Answer:** $8.4 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{km/s}$, two significant figures, as in the Hubble constant $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$.
**Reasonableness:** For every megaparsec the speed grows by $70\,\text{km/s}$, so at $100\,\text{Mpc}$ the speed is $7000\,\text{km/s}$ and at $120\,\text{Mpc}$ a little more. The speed is less than $3\,\%$ of the speed of light.

## fy8-16-L1-04
**Given:** recession speed $v = 5250\,\text{km/s}$, Hubble constant $H_0 = 70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$
**Asked:** the distance of the galaxy $d$ in megaparsecs
**Principle:** According to the Hubble–Lemaître law, the speed is directly proportional to the distance. When the speed is known, the distance is obtained by dividing the speed by the Hubble constant. The own motion of the galaxy is ignored.
**Formula:** $v = H_0\,d$, so $d = \dfrac{v}{H_0}$
**Conversions:** None needed: kilometres per second cancel, and the distance comes out in megaparsecs.
**Substitution:** $d = \dfrac{5250\,\text{km/s}}{70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}} = 75\,\text{Mpc}$
**Answer:** $75\,\text{Mpc}$, two significant figures, as in the Hubble constant $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check by multiplying: $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}} \cdot 75\,\text{Mpc} = 5250\,\text{km/s}$, the same as the given speed. The distance is of the same order of magnitude as nearby galaxy clusters, tens of megaparsecs.

## fy8-16-L1-05
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 1.06\,\text{mm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the energy of the photon $E$ in the unit $10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$
**Principle:** The background radiation is electromagnetic radiation whose energy is quantised into photons (chapter *The photoelectric effect and the photon*). The energy of a photon is inversely proportional to its wavelength.
**Formula:** $E = hf$ and $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, so $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $1.06\,\text{mm} = 1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$. To change from joules to electronvolts, substitute $1\,\text{J} = \dfrac{1}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}} = 1.8740 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\text{J} = \dfrac{1.8740 \cdot 10^{-22}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV} = 1.1698 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $1.17 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$, three significant figures, as in the wavelength $1.06\,\text{mm}$.
**Reasonableness:** A photon of visible light has an energy of $1.7$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$ and a wavelength of about $500\,\text{nm}$. The wavelength of the background radiation is about $2000$ times longer, so the energy is about $2000$ times smaller: $2\,\text{eV} : 2000 = 1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$.

## fy8-16-L2-01
**Given:** the $\text{H}\alpha$ line in the laboratory $\lambda_0 = 656.3\,\text{nm}$ and in the spectrum of the galaxy $\lambda = 671.0\,\text{nm}$, $H_0 = 70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s}$
**Asked:** the distance of the galaxy $d$ in megaparsecs
**Principle:** The shift of the line gives the redshift $z$, and from it the recession speed $v = cz$, because the redshift is small. According to the Hubble–Lemaître law, the speed is proportional to the distance, so the distance is the speed divided by the Hubble constant.
**Formula:** $z = \dfrac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$, $v = cz$ and $v = H_0\,d$, so $d = \dfrac{c\,(\lambda - \lambda_0)}{H_0\,\lambda_0}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the nanometres cancel in the redshift, and the kilometres per second cancel when the speed is divided by the Hubble constant.
**Substitution:** $z = \dfrac{671.0\,\text{nm} - 656.3\,\text{nm}}{656.3\,\text{nm}} = \dfrac{14.7\,\text{nm}}{656.3\,\text{nm}} = 0.022398$, $v = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s} \cdot 0.022398 = 6715\,\text{km/s}$ and $d = \dfrac{6715\,\text{km/s}}{70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}} = 95.93\,\text{Mpc}$
**Answer:** $96\,\text{Mpc}$, two significant figures, as in the Hubble constant $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$.
**Reasonableness:** The redshift $z \approx 0.022$ is small, so $v = cz$ holds. With the estimate $v \approx 0.022 \cdot 300\,000\,\text{km/s} = 6600\,\text{km/s}$ and $6600 : 70 \approx 94$, close to the result.

## fy8-16-L2-02
**Given:** Hubble constant $H_0 = 67.4\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$, $1\,\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{km}$, $1\,\text{a} = 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the estimate of the age of the universe $t$ in billions of years
**Principle:** If the speed of the expansion has stayed the same, a galaxy that is now at distance $d$ and recedes at the speed $v = H_0\,d$ was in the same place as we were a time $t = \tfrac{d}{v}$ ago. The distance cancels, so the time is the same for all galaxies.
**Formula:** $t = \dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{d}{H_0\,d}$, so $t = \dfrac{1}{H_0}$
**Conversions:** For the megaparsec, substitute $1\,\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{km}$ so that the kilometres cancel, and change seconds into years by substituting $1\,\text{s} = \dfrac{1}{3.156 \cdot 10^{7}}\,\text{a}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{1\,\text{Mpc}}{67.4\,\text{km/s}} = \dfrac{3.086 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{km}}{67.4\,\text{km/s}} = 4.5786 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{s} = \dfrac{4.5786 \cdot 10^{17}}{3.156 \cdot 10^{7}}\,\text{a} = 1.4508 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{a}$
**Answer:** $14.5 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{a}$, that is, about $14.5$ billion years, three significant figures, as in the Hubble constant $67.4\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$.
**Reasonableness:** With $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$ the estimate is about $14.0$ billion years; a smaller Hubble constant means slower expansion and therefore a longer time. The accurate age of $13.8$ billion years is close, because the expansion first slowed down and then accelerated.

## fy8-16-L2-03
**Given:** present wavelength $\lambda_\text{now} = 1.06\,\text{mm}$, stretching factor $1100$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the energy of a photon $E$ when the radiation was released, in electronvolts
**Principle:** The expansion has stretched every wavelength by a factor of $1100$, so when it was released, the wavelength was $1100$ times shorter than now. The energy of a photon is obtained from the wavelength, as in the chapter *The photoelectric effect and the photon*.
**Formula:** $\lambda = \dfrac{\lambda_\text{now}}{1100}$ and $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so $E = \dfrac{1100\,hc}{\lambda_\text{now}}$
**Conversions:** $1.06\,\text{mm} = 1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$, and to change from joules to electronvolts, substitute $1\,\text{J} = \dfrac{1}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}}{1100} = 9.636 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$ and $E = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{9.636 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}} = 2.0615 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = \dfrac{2.0615 \cdot 10^{-19}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV} = 1.287\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $1.3\,\text{eV}$, two significant figures, as in the stretching factor $1100$.
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength $964\,\text{nm}$ is near-infrared, a little below the limit of visible light, $1.7\,\text{eV}$. A typical photon was therefore much weaker than the $13.6\,\text{eV}$ needed to ionise hydrogen. However, there are vastly more photons than atoms, so the atoms stayed ionised for as long as there were enough of the rare photons above $13.6\,\text{eV}$. When the temperature fell to about $3000\,\text{K}$, there were too few of them, the electrons bound to atoms and the light could travel freely.

## fy8-16-L2-04
**Given:** galaxy A: $d_\text{A} = 42.0\,\text{Mpc}$, $v_\text{A} = 2950\,\text{km/s}$; galaxy B: $v_\text{B} = 11\,300\,\text{km/s}$
**Asked:** the distance of galaxy B, $d_\text{B}$, in megaparsecs
**Principle:** According to the Hubble–Lemaître law, all galaxies have the same Hubble constant, so the ratio of speed to distance is the same: the number of times faster a galaxy recedes is the number of times farther away it is. The own motion of the galaxies is ignored.
**Formula:** $H_0 = \dfrac{v_\text{A}}{d_\text{A}} = \dfrac{v_\text{B}}{d_\text{B}}$, so $d_\text{B} = d_\text{A} \cdot \dfrac{v_\text{B}}{v_\text{A}}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the unit of the speeds cancels, and the distance comes out in megaparsecs.
**Substitution:** $d_\text{B} = 42.0\,\text{Mpc} \cdot \dfrac{11\,300\,\text{km/s}}{2950\,\text{km/s}} = 42.0\,\text{Mpc} \cdot 3.8305 = 160.88\,\text{Mpc}$
**Answer:** $161\,\text{Mpc}$, three significant figures, as in the given distance and speeds.
**Reasonableness:** Galaxy B recedes about four times faster, so it must be about four times farther away: $4 \cdot 42\,\text{Mpc} \approx 170\,\text{Mpc}$. The Hubble constant obtained from galaxy A is $2950 : 42.0 \approx 70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$, the known value.

## fy8-16-L3-01
**Given:** seven protons for every neutron, all neutrons in helium-4 nuclei (two protons and two neutrons), the masses of the proton and the neutron equal
**Asked:** the fraction of helium in the total mass of the nuclei, as a percentage
**Principle:** Because the masses of the proton and the neutron are assumed equal, the mass fraction is the same as the fraction of nucleons. For every two neutrons of a helium nucleus, two protons are needed in the helium nucleus, so helium contains twice as many nucleons as there are neutrons. The total number of nucleons does not change in fusion.
**Formula:** When there are $n$ neutrons and $p$ protons, $2n$ nucleons bind into helium, so the mass fraction is $Y = \dfrac{2n}{n + p}$
**Conversions:** None needed: we count numbers of nucleons, and finally the fraction is changed into a percentage by multiplying by $100\,\%$.
**Substitution:** Consider a group with $n = 1$ and $p = 7$: $Y = \dfrac{2 \cdot 1}{1 + 7} = \dfrac{2}{8} = 0.25 = 25\,\%$
**Answer:** $25\,\%$; the ratio $1 : 7$ is approximate, so the result is about a quarter.
**Reasonableness:** For example, of $16$ nucleons, $2$ are neutrons and $14$ protons: one helium nucleus ($4$ nucleons) and $12$ hydrogen nuclei form, and $\tfrac{4}{16} = 25\,\%$. The result matches the helium fraction observed in the oldest stars and gas clouds, about a quarter of the mass.

## fy8-16-L3-02
**Given:** mass of the helium-4 atom $m_\text{He} = 4.002603\,\text{u}$, mass of the carbon-12 atom $m_\text{C} = 12.000000\,\text{u}$, $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$
**Asked:** the energy $Q$ released in the reaction $3\,{}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \to {}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ in megaelectronvolts
**Principle:** In fusion, energy is released when the nucleus that forms is more tightly bound than the starting nuclei and therefore lighter than they are together (chapter *Mass defect and nuclear binding energy*). Atomic masses can be used, because there are six electrons on both sides.
**Formula:** $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$, where the starting materials are three helium atoms and the product is one carbon atom, so $Q = (3\,m_\text{He} - m_\text{C})\,c^2$
**Conversions:** The mass is not converted into kilograms: substitute $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ for the unit $\text{u}$, so that $c^2$ cancels.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final} = 3 \cdot 4.002603\,\text{u} - 12.000000\,\text{u} = 12.007809\,\text{u} - 12.000000\,\text{u} = 0.007809\,\text{u}$, so $Q = 0.007809 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 7.2741\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** $7.274\,\text{MeV}$, four significant figures, as in the mass difference $0.007809\,\text{u}$ and in the conversion factor $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$.
**Reasonableness:** $Q > 0$, so the reaction releases energy, as it must in fusion to the left of the peak of the binding energy curve. Per nucleon, the energy released is $7.274\,\text{MeV} : 12 \approx 0.6\,\text{MeV}$, which matches the rise of the curve from $7.07\,\text{MeV}$ for helium-4 to $7.68\,\text{MeV}$ for carbon-12.

## fy8-16-L3-03
**Given:** lines of the galaxy $447.2\,\text{nm}$, $500.9\,\text{nm}$ and $676.2\,\text{nm}$; laboratory lines $434.0\,\text{nm}$, $486.1\,\text{nm}$ and $656.3\,\text{nm}$; $H_0 = 70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s}$
**Asked:** the distance of the galaxy $d$ in megaparsecs
**Principle:** In redshift every wavelength grows in the same proportion, so the order of the lines is preserved: the shortest corresponds to the shortest and the longest to the longest. The pairs are checked with the ratio: $\tfrac{447.2}{434.0} = 1.030$, $\tfrac{500.9}{486.1} = 1.030$ and $\tfrac{676.2}{656.3} = 1.030$. The ratio is the same, so the pairs are correct, and $z = 0.030$ is small, so $v = cz$. The distance is obtained from the Hubble–Lemaître law. The most accurate $z$ comes from the longest line, whose shift is the greatest.
**Formula:** $z = \dfrac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$, $v = cz$ and $v = H_0\,d$, so $d = \dfrac{c\,(\lambda - \lambda_0)}{H_0\,\lambda_0}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the nanometres and the kilometres per second cancel.
**Substitution:** $z = \dfrac{676.2\,\text{nm} - 656.3\,\text{nm}}{656.3\,\text{nm}} = \dfrac{19.9\,\text{nm}}{656.3\,\text{nm}} = 0.030322$, $v = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s} \cdot 0.030322 = 9090.5\,\text{km/s}$ and $d = \dfrac{9090.5\,\text{km/s}}{70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}} = 129.9\,\text{Mpc}$
**Answer:** $1.3 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{Mpc}$, two significant figures, as in the Hubble constant $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$.
**Reasonableness:** The shifts $13.2\,\text{nm}$, $14.8\,\text{nm}$ and $19.9\,\text{nm}$ are not equal, but each is $3.0\,\%$ of its own wavelength, as it should be in redshift. Estimate: $0.03 \cdot 300\,000\,\text{km/s} = 9000\,\text{km/s}$ and $9000 : 70 \approx 130$.
