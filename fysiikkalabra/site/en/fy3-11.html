Work done by a gas and the heat engine

Why does a thermal power plant get less than half of the energy of its fuel as electricity, and where does the rest of the energy go?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the work done by an expanding gas and interpret it as an area in the pressure–volume graph.
  • explain why a heat engine turns heat into work only by releasing part of it to something colder.
  • calculate the efficiency of a heat engine.

Prior knowledge

  • The first law $\Delta U = Q + W$, where $W$ is the work done on the system by the surroundings, and conservation of energychapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy
  • Isobaric change of state: at constant pressure the volume of a gas grows when its temperature riseschapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law
  • Work is force times distance, and the area under an $(s, F)$ graphchapter Work and energy

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

In the cylinder of a museum locomotive, hot steam pushes a piston, and the piston turns the wheels of the locomotive. A large condensing power plant does basically the same thing: steam expands in a turbine and turns a generator. The plant is supplied with the energy of fuel at a rate of $1000\,\text{MW}$, but only about $400\,\text{MW}$ goes to the electricity grid. Energy is still conserved, as the first law says.

Could better technology turn all the energy of the fuel into electricity, and can the part that does not become electricity still be put to use?

A gas in a cylinder is heated so that its pressure stays constant (isobaric change of state, chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law).   • Besides heat, work also changes the internal energy. When the gas expands and pushes the piston, energy is transferred from it to the surroundings, so the internal energy grows less than the heat supplied: $\Delta U = 400\,\text{J} - 150\,\text{J} = 250\,\text{J}$.

  • Constant pressure does not mean constant internal energy. In isobaric heating the temperature of the gas rises (chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law), so its internal energy grows: $\Delta U = Q + W = 250\,\text{J}$.

  • Correct: $\Delta U = Q + W = 400\,\text{J} + (-150\,\text{J}) = 250\,\text{J}$. Part of the heat left as work when the gas pushed the piston. In this lesson the work done by the gas is written $W_\text{g}$, so here $W_\text{g} = 150\,\text{J}$ and $\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$. In this lesson you calculate how large that work is.

3Investigate

In the simulation a gas is heated in a cylinder whose piston is pressed down by two weights; the pressure of the gas is $200\,\text{kPa}$.

Simulation

How to use

Choose the heat $Q$ and press Heat: the piston rises, and the work done by the gas fills in the $(V, p)$ graph. Then press Return the piston. With the choice at the same pressure the weights push the piston back; with the choice cool first the gas first cools to the pressure $p_2$ (slider). Speed changes only the pace, not the results. The orange area is the work $W_\text{g}$ done by the gas, and the blue area is the work done on the gas; in the bars the same colour means the same energy.

Assumptions of the model

The readings are values of the model: the cylinder contains a monatomic ideal gas. The piston is frictionless and weightless, and there is no air above it, so the pressure is caused by the weights: $200\,\text{kPa}$ with two weights, and the lower weight alone gives the pressure $p_2$. The hot plate is always hotter than the gas and the cold water colder. No heat escapes elsewhere. The initial volume is $V_1 = 1.00\,\text{l}$.

How the calculation works

The change in internal energy is $\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}nR\Delta T = \tfrac{3}{2}\Delta(pV)$. At constant pressure $p_1$ the work done by the gas is $W_\text{g} = p_1\Delta V$ and $Q = \Delta U + W_\text{g} = \tfrac{5}{2}p_1\Delta V$, so $\Delta V = \dfrac{2Q}{5p_1}$. In the option cool first the upper weight is moved up onto a shelf and a new one is taken from below; when the piston is locked, the volume does not change. The net work of the cycle $W = (p_1 - p_2)\Delta V$ is the energy used to lift the weight, and $Q_1 - Q_2 = W$. The efficiency is $\eta = \dfrac{W}{Q_1}$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    When a gas expands and pushes a piston, it does work. The gas pushes the piston with a force $F = pA$, where $A$ is the area of the piston. If the piston moves a distance $s$ and the pressure stays the same, the work done by the gas is force times distance, $pAs$. The product $As$ is the change in the volume of the gas $\Delta V$, so the work done by the gas is $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$. In the ($V$, $p$) graph it is the area between the graph and the $V$ axis, just like the work in an ($s$, $F$) graph. When the gas is compressed, the surroundings do work on the gas, and $W_\text{g}$ is negative. A gas that does work gives up energy, so in this lesson the first law is written in the form $\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$.

    A heat engine repeats its motion

    A heat engine turns heat into work continuously, so it has to repeat its motion. The piston has to be brought back down. If it is pressed down at the same pressure, the return takes all the work done by the gas. The return takes less work if the gas is first cooled to a lower pressure, but then the gas gives up heat to a colder environment. In a steam power plant, water boils in the boiler, the hot steam expands in the turbine and turns the generator, and the steam coming out of the turbine condenses back into water in the condenser, which is cooled by seawater. The working substance goes round the same path again and again, so at the end of a cycle its internal energy is the same as at the start. Energy is conserved: the heat $Q_1$ from the hot source equals the work $W$ done by the engine plus the heat $Q_2$ given up to the colder environment.

    Experience shows that a heat engine cannot turn all the heat it receives into work; part of it must always be given up to a colder environment. This is the content of the second law of thermodynamics. The larger the temperature difference between the hot source and the cold environment compared with the temperature of the hot source, the larger the share of the heat the engine can turn into work. Cyclic processes are not calculated in this course; the simulation only shows why the heat $Q_2$ cannot be avoided.

    Efficiency

    Efficiency tells how large a share of the energy supplied to a machine is put to use, so its value depends on what is counted as useful. In the efficiency of a power plant's electricity production, only the electricity is useful. A Finnish condensing power plant that produces only electricity gets about $40\,\%$ of the energy of its fuel as electricity, and a nuclear power plant about $35\,\%$. Most of the rest is transferred to the sea through the condenser, and in a plant that burns fuel part also leaves into the air with the flue gases. The efficiency of a car engine is about $30\,\%$, and the rest of the energy leaves with the exhaust gases and the radiator.

    In combined production of electricity and heat, the heat $Q_2$ of the condenser is recovered: it heats the water of the district heating network, which carries it to the houses of the city. So the heat engine still gives up heat to something colder, but this heat is put to use. In the overall efficiency, the electricity and the district heating together count as useful, and it is 85–$90\,\%$. The efficiency of electricity production therefore does not rise; it is usually lower than when only electricity is produced. This answers the opening question: less than half becomes electricity, but the rest does not disappear; it is transferred as heat to the environment or to the district heating network.

    Refrigerator and heat pump

    A refrigerator and a heat pump work the opposite way to a heat engine: they use electrical energy to transfer heat from a cold place to a warm one. A refrigerator transfers heat from inside the cabinet to the room, and an air-source heat pump from the outdoor air to the inside. The heat given out indoors is the sum of the heat taken from outside and the electricity used, so a pump can heat a house with, for example, three times as much heating power as the electric power it uses. The ratio of the heating power to the electric power is called the coefficient of performance. The coefficient of performance is not an efficiency: it can be greater than 1, because part of the heat that comes indoors is transferred from the outdoor air and does not come from the electricity. The pump does not create energy; it transfers it.

    Work done by a gas and efficiency

    When the pressure of a gas stays constant, the work done by the gas is

    $$W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$$

    where $W_\text{g}$ is the work done by the gas (J), $p$ the pressure of the gas (Pa) and $\Delta V$ the change in the volume of the gas (m³). The work is positive when the gas expands and negative when the gas is compressed. The change in the internal energy of the gas is

    $$\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$$

    where $Q$ is the quantity of heat transferred to the gas (J). This is the first law $\Delta U = Q + W$ presented in the chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy, where $W$ is the work done on the gas by the surroundings: $W = -W_\text{g}$.

    During one cycle of a heat engine, the work done and the efficiency are

    $$W = Q_1 - Q_2$$

    $$\eta = \frac{W}{Q_1}$$

    where $Q_1$ is the heat transferred to the engine from the hot source (J), $Q_2$ the heat given up to the colder environment (J), $W$ the work done by the engine during the cycle (J) and $\eta$ the efficiency, which is often given as a percentage. Efficiency can be calculated in the same way from powers, for example the electric power divided by the fuel power.

    A heat pump gives out indoors a quantity of heat that is the sum of the heat taken from the outdoor air and the electrical energy used by the pump:

    $$Q_\text{indoors} = Q_\text{outdoors} + E_\text{el}$$

    The coefficient of performance tells how many times as much heat the pump gives out indoors as the electrical energy it uses:

    $$\varepsilon = \frac{Q_\text{indoors}}{E_\text{el}}$$

    where $Q_\text{indoors}$ is the heat given out indoors (J), $Q_\text{outdoors}$ the heat taken from the outdoor air (J), $E_\text{el}$ the electrical energy used by the pump (J) and $\varepsilon$ the coefficient of performance. The coefficient of performance can be calculated in the same way from powers.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that better technology could make a heat engine almost perfect, so that all the heat would turn into work. Reducing friction and other losses helps, but part of the heat must always be given up to a colder environment, so the efficiency of a heat engine stays clearly below $100\,\%$. The frictionless piston of the simulation shows the reason: for the piston to be brought down with less work than it took to raise it, the gas has to be cooled, and in the cooling heat is transferred to the cold water. The limit applies to a machine that turns heat into work, not to all devices: for example, in an electric heater practically all the electrical energy increases the internal energy of the resistor and is transferred as heat to the room.

    Another mistake is to think of a heat pump as a perpetual motion machine, because it gives out more heat than it uses electricity. The extra heat comes from the outdoor air, and energy is not created out of nothing. A third mistake is to think that a pressurised gas does work just because its pressure is high. Work is done only when the volume changes and the piston moves.

    Summary

    • A gas that expands at constant pressure does the work $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$, which is the area under the ($V$, $p$) graph. The internal energy changes by $\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$.
    • A piston machine can repeat its work only if the gas is cooled before the piston is returned; then heat is transferred to something colder.
    • A heat engine takes heat $Q_1$ from a hot source, does work $W$ and gives up heat $Q_2$ to a colder environment: $W = Q_1 - Q_2$.
    • Efficiency is $\eta = W/Q_1$. A heat engine cannot turn all the heat into work.
    • The efficiency of electricity production of a condensing power plant is about $40\,\%$. In combined production of electricity and heat the heat of the condenser is used as district heating, so the overall efficiency, in which electricity and heat both count as useful, is 85–$90\,\%$.
    • A refrigerator and a heat pump use work to transfer heat from cold to warm. They do not create energy. The coefficient of performance of a heat pump, $\varepsilon = Q_\text{indoors}/E_\text{el}$, is not an efficiency, and it can be greater than 1, because part of the heat comes from the outdoor air.

      Worked example

      Problem

      Wood chip energy is fed into the boiler of a biopower plant at a power of $500\,\text{MW}$. When the plant produces only electricity, the electric power is $190\,\text{MW}$. In winter the same plant works as a combined heat and power plant: it produces $150\,\text{MW}$ of electricity and $290\,\text{MW}$ of district heating. What is the efficiency in each case, and how much power is transferred to the environment as waste heat?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Fuel power $P_1 = 500\,\text{MW}$. When only electricity is produced, the electric power is $P_\text{e} = 190\,\text{MW}$; in combined production $P_\text{e} = 150\,\text{MW}$ and the district heating power is $P_\text{dh} = 290\,\text{MW}$. The efficiencies $\eta$ and the power of the waste heat $P_2$ are asked for in both cases.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Efficiency is the useful power divided by the power supplied; it is calculated from powers just like from energies, because both are energy in the same time. Energy is conserved, so the fuel power is divided into the useful power and the waste heat. In electricity production alone only the electricity is useful; in combined production both the electricity and the district heating are.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. Solve for the quantities asked for, with symbols before numbers:

      $$\begin{aligned} \eta &= \frac{P_\text{useful}}{P_1} \\ P_2 &= P_1 - P_\text{useful} \end{aligned}$$

      For electricity alone $P_\text{useful} = P_\text{e}$, and for combined production $P_\text{useful} = P_\text{e} + P_\text{dh}$.

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. All powers are given in megawatts, so no conversions are needed: in the efficiency the unit cancels, and the waste heat comes out in megawatts. A decimal number is changed to a percentage by multiplying by one hundred.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. For electricity alone and for combined production separately:

      $$\begin{aligned} \eta_1 &= \frac{P_\text{e}}{P_1} \\ &= \frac{190\,\text{MW}}{500\,\text{MW}} \\ &= 0.380 \\ P_{2,1} &= P_1 - P_\text{e} \\ &= 500\,\text{MW} - 190\,\text{MW} \\ &= 310\,\text{MW} \\[6pt] \eta_2 &= \frac{P_\text{e} + P_\text{dh}}{P_1} \\ &= \frac{150\,\text{MW} + 290\,\text{MW}}{500\,\text{MW}} \\ &= 0.880 \\ P_{2,2} &= P_1 - (P_\text{e} + P_\text{dh}) \\ &= 500\,\text{MW} - 440\,\text{MW} \\ &= 60\,\text{MW} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. With electricity production alone the efficiency is $\eta_1 = 0.380 = 38.0\,\%$ and $310\,\text{MW}$ of waste heat is transferred to the environment, most of it through the condenser to the sea. In combined production the overall efficiency is $\eta_2 = 0.880 = 88.0\,\%$ and there is only $60\,\text{MW}$ of waste heat. The powers are given in whole megawatts, so the differences $310\,\text{MW}$ and $60\,\text{MW}$ are known to the nearest megawatt. The efficiencies are quotients, so they have as many figures as the least precise power: $190\,\text{MW}$, $150\,\text{MW}$, $290\,\text{MW}$ and $500\,\text{MW}$ all have three figures, so the efficiencies are given with three figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Energy is conserved: $190\,\text{MW} + 310\,\text{MW} = 500\,\text{MW}$ and $150\,\text{MW} + 290\,\text{MW} + 60\,\text{MW} = 500\,\text{MW}$. The efficiency of electricity production stays clearly below $100\,\%$, because a heat engine must give up part of the heat to something colder. Even the overall efficiency is not $100\,\%$, because part of the heat leaves with the flue gases and other losses. Less electricity is obtained in combined production because the steam is condensed at a higher temperature so that its heat is suitable for district heating. The energy of the fuel is still put to much better use.

      • Condensing power plant: electricity is $40\,\%$ of the fuel power.
      • Electric heating: $1\,\text{MW}$ of electricity gives $1\,\text{MW}$ of heat.
      • Combined heat and power plant: electricity is $45\,\%$ and district heat $40\,\%$ of the fuel power.
      • Heat pump: coefficient of performance 3.0.

      The options:

      1. Condensing power plant and electric heating. The condensing power plant produces all the electricity, including that used for heating.
      2. Combined heat and power. The combined heat and power plant produces all the district heating, and at the same time more electricity is produced than the city needs.
      3. Combined heat and power and a heat pump. Part of the plant's electricity is used in a heat pump, which takes heat from purified waste water. The plant is sized so that the city gets exactly the electricity and heat it needs.

      The assumption for the combined heat and power plant corresponds to a plant that burns natural gas; in a plant that burns wood the share of electricity is smaller, as in the Worked example.

      First calculate the fuel power of options 1 and 2. How much fuel power does option 3 need? Give the answer in megawatts.

      MW
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Denote the fuel power of the combined heat and power plant by $P$ and the electric power of the heat pump by $P_\text{pump}$. What two conditions must the electricity and heat of the city meet?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The city is left with electricity $0.45P - P_\text{pump} = 300\,\text{MW}$, and heat comes from the plant and the pump: $0.40P + 3P_\text{pump} = 500\,\text{MW}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      From the first condition $P_\text{pump} = 0.45P - 300\,\text{MW}$. Substitute it into the second condition: $0.40P + 1.35P - 900\,\text{MW} = 500\,\text{MW}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      the electricity need of the city $P_\text{e} = 300\,\text{MW}$ and the district heating need $P_\text{h} = 500\,\text{MW}$; the efficiency of the condensing power plant $0.40$, the combined heat and power plant gives electricity $0.45P$ and heat $0.40P$ from the fuel power $P$, the coefficient of performance of the heat pump $3.0$; option 3 is examined

      Find

      the fuel power $P$ of option 3 in megawatts (for comparison, the fuel power of options 1 and 2)

      Principle

      In options 1 and 2 a single plant meets the demand, and the fuel power is the useful power divided by the efficiency. In option 3 part of the plant's electricity goes to the heat pump, so the balances of electricity and heat must be written separately. Energy is conserved: the pump gives out heat equal to three times its electric power, and the difference comes from the waste water.

      Formula

      Option 1:

      $P$$= \dfrac{P_\text{e} + P_\text{h}}{0.40}$

      Option 2: $P = \dfrac{P_\text{h}}{0.40}$. In option 3 the conditions are $0.45P - P_\text{pump} = P_\text{e}$ and $0.40P + 3P_\text{pump} = P_\text{h}$. Eliminating $P_\text{pump}$:

      $$P = \frac{P_\text{h} + 3P_\text{e}}{0.40 + 3 \cdot 0.45}$$

      Conversions

      All powers are in megawatts and the efficiencies are decimal numbers, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      Option 1:

      $P$$= \dfrac{300\,\text{MW} + 500\,\text{MW}}{0.40}$
      $= 2000\,\text{MW}$

      Option 2:

      $P$$= \dfrac{500\,\text{MW}}{0.40}$
      $= 1250\,\text{MW}$

      and the electricity produced is $0.45 \cdot 1250\,\text{MW} = 562.5\,\text{MW}$, which is $262.5\,\text{MW}$ more than needed. Option 3:

      $P$$= \dfrac{500\,\text{MW} + 3 \cdot 300\,\text{MW}}{0.40 + 3 \cdot 0.45}$
      $= \dfrac{1400\,\text{MW}}{1.75}$
      $= 800\,\text{MW}$

      Answer

      $800\,\text{MW}$

      Two significant figures. The limiting values are the efficiencies $0.40$ and $0.45$ (two figures); the needs of the city $300\,\text{MW}$ and $500\,\text{MW}$ and the coefficient of performance $3.0$ are design values of the problem that are read as having at least two figures ($3.0 \cdot 10^2$, $5.0 \cdot 10^2$), so the result is $8.0 \cdot 10^2\,\text{MW}$.

      Reasonableness

      Check the balance again: the electric power of the pump is $0.45 \cdot 800\,\text{MW} - 300\,\text{MW} = 60\,\text{MW}$ and it gives out heat $3 \cdot 60\,\text{MW} = 180\,\text{MW}$, of which $120\,\text{MW}$ comes from the waste water. The plant gives heat $0.40 \cdot 800\,\text{MW} = 320\,\text{MW}$, and together $320\,\text{MW} + 180\,\text{MW} = 500\,\text{MW}$, as needed. The losses of the plant, $0.15 \cdot 800\,\text{MW} = 120\,\text{MW}$, are made up by the $120\,\text{MW}$ taken from the waste water. Option 3 needs only $40\,\%$ of the fuel of option 1.

      the heat power taken from the waste water $P_\text{sewage}$ in megawatts

      Principle

      The coefficient of performance tells how many times as much heat power the pump gives to the network as its electric power. Energy is conserved: the power given to the network is the electric power of the pump and the power taken from the waste water together, because the pump only transfers heat.

      Formula

      $P_\text{indoors} = \varepsilon P_\text{pump}$ and $P_\text{indoors} = P_\text{sewage} + P_\text{pump}$, so

      4. Explain and transfer

      Your friend suggests: “Let’s build such a good power plant that it needs no condenser at all. Then all the energy of the fuel becomes electricity.” Explain why this is not possible, and say how the heat of the condenser can still be put to use.

      Model answer

      A heat engine turns heat into work only when part of the heat is given up to a colder environment; this is the second law of thermodynamics. The piston of the simulation showed the reason: for the piston to be brought back with less work than it took to raise it, the gas has to be cooled. In a power plant the condenser does the same: without it the steam would not condense back into water, and the cycle could not continue. Energy is conserved, $Q_1 = W + Q_2$, so the part $W$ that becomes electricity is always smaller than the heat $Q_1$ obtained from the fuel. Reducing friction raises the efficiency somewhat, but the heat $Q_2$ that has to be given up cannot be removed. The heat of the condenser can, however, be led to the district heating network instead of being released into the sea. A combined heat and power plant of this kind gets 85–$90\,\%$ of the energy of the fuel as useful electricity and heat together. This is the overall efficiency: the heat $Q_2$ is still given up, but it is put to use, and no more electricity is obtained.

          kJ
          Answer

          $12.0\,\text{kJ}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the steam pressure stays constant, $p = 480.0\,\text{kPa}$, change in volume $\Delta V = 0.0250\,\text{m}^3$

          Find

          the work $W_\text{g}$ done by the steam on the piston in kilojoules

          Principle

          The pressure stays constant, so the force $F = pA$ that the steam exerts on the piston is the same throughout the motion, and the work is force times distance. Since the change in volume is the area of the piston times the distance, $\Delta V = A s$, the work is the pressure times the change in volume.

          Formula

          $W_\text{g} = F s = pA s$, so $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$

          Conversions

          $480.0\,\text{kPa} = 480\,000\,\text{Pa}$. The volume is already in cubic metres, so $\text{Pa} \cdot \text{m}^3 = \text{J}$ without further conversion. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $W_\text{g}$$= 480\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0250\,\text{m}^3$
          $= 12\,000\,\text{J}$
          $= 12.0\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $12.0\,\text{kJ}$

          Three significant figures. Both values, the pressure $480.0\,\text{kPa}$ and the change in volume $0.0250\,\text{m}^3$, are given with at least three figures.

          Reasonableness

          The unit is correct:

          $\text{Pa} \cdot \text{m}^3$$= \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot \text{m}^3$
          $= \text{N} \cdot \text{m}$
          $= \text{J}$

          Estimate: $500\,\text{kPa} \cdot 0.025\,\text{m}^3 \approx 12\,\text{kJ}$. The work is positive because the steam expands and pushes the piston. It would be enough to lift a load of about 1.2 tonnes by a metre, and the pistons of a locomotive do this much work many times a second.

          Given

          volume of fuel $V = 6.00\,\textsf{l}$ over a distance $s = 100\,\text{km}$ (the reference distance for consumption), speed $v = 80.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$; density of petrol $\rho = 0.750\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\textsf{l}}$ and heat of combustion $H = 43.0\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$; efficiency $\eta = 28.0\,\% = 0.280$

          Find

          the average mechanical power $P$ of the engine in kilowatts, and justifications for where the energy goes and why an electric car needs less

          Principle

          The engine is a heat engine: the energy $Q_1$ released when petrol burns is divided into work $W = \eta Q_1$ and waste heat. Power is work divided by the time taken for the distance, and the speed is steady, so the power is constant.

          Formula

          $Q_1 = \rho V H$ and $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$, so

          $P$$= \dfrac{W}{t}$
          $= \dfrac{\eta \rho V H}{t}$

          Conversions

          $t$$= \dfrac{100\,\text{km}}{80.0\,\text{km/h}}$
          $= 1.25\,\text{h}$
          $= 4500\,\text{s}$
          $43.0\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$$= 43.0 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

          Finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.

          Substitution

          The work obtained from the energy of the fuel is

          $W$$= 0.280 \cdot 0.750\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\textsf{l}} \cdot 6.00\,\textsf{l} \cdot 43.0 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 5.418 \cdot 10^7\,\text{J}$

          so

          $$\begin{aligned} P &= \dfrac{5.418 \cdot 10^7\,\text{J}}{4500\,\text{s}} \\ &= 12\,040\,\text{W} \\ &= 12.04\,\text{kW} \end{aligned}$$

          Answer

          $12.0\,\text{kW}$

          Three significant figures. All the values ($6.00\,\textsf{l}$, $0.750\,\text{kg/l}$, $43.0\,\text{MJ/kg}$, $28.0\,\%$ and $80.0\,\text{km/h}$) are given with three figures; the distance $100\,\text{km}$ is the reference distance for consumption and does not limit the precision.

          Reasonableness

          The fuel power is $\tfrac{1.935 \cdot 10^8\,\text{J}}{4500\,\text{s}} = 43\,\text{kW}$, so $31\,\text{kW}$ leaves as waste heat through the exhaust gases and the radiator. The petrol used is $54\,\text{kWh}$ of energy, and the work is $28\,\%$ of that, $15\,\text{kWh}$. An electric car needs about $\tfrac{15\,\text{kWh}}{0.90} \approx 17\,\text{kWh}$ from the battery to do the same work, because its motor turns electricity into work with an efficiency of $90\,\%$ and no heat has to be turned into work.

          Example data: in a vertical cylinder there is air closed by a lightly moving piston.           Show the solution

          Given

          cross-sectional area $A = 200\,\text{cm}^2$, constant pressure $p = 125\,\text{kPa}$, power of the resistor $P = 260\,\text{W}$, time $t = 4.0\,\text{s}$, height of the piston $h_1 = 10.0\,\text{cm}$ at the start and $h_2 = 22.0\,\text{cm}$ at the end

          Find

          a) the heat $Q$ and the work $W_\text{g}$ done by the gas, b) the change in internal energy $\Delta U$ in kilojoules (for the answer box), c) the share that became work, and a justification of why a single expansion is not a heat engine

          Principle

          The energy of the resistor is transferred to the air as heat, $Q = Pt$. The pressure is constant, so the work done by the air is $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$, and the change in volume is obtained from the displacement of the piston: $\Delta V = A\,\Delta h$. Energy is conserved, so the first law is $\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$.

          Reading the graph

          From the table the height of the piston grows from $h_1 = 10.0\,\text{cm}$ to $h_2 = 22.0\,\text{cm}$, so $\Delta h = 12.0\,\text{cm}$. The height grows by about the same amount every second ($3.0\,\text{cm/s}$). The pressure is given as constant, and the steady rise fits heating with a constant power.

          Formula

          $Q = Pt$, $\Delta V = A\,\Delta h$, $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$ and $\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$

          Conversions

          $A$$= 200\,\text{cm}^2$
          $= 200 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 0.0200\,\text{m}^2$
          $\Delta h$$= 12.0\,\text{cm}$
          $= 0.120\,\text{m}$
          $p$$= 125\,\text{kPa}$
          $= 125\,000\,\text{Pa}$

          Substitution

          a)

          $$\begin{aligned} Q &= 260\,\text{W} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s} \\ &= 1040\,\text{J} \\ \Delta V &= 0.0200\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 0.120\,\text{m} \\ &= 2.40 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3 \\ W_\text{g} &= 125\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 2.40 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3 \\ &= 300\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

          b)

          $$\begin{aligned} \Delta U &= 1040\,\text{J} - 300\,\text{J} \\ &= 740\,\text{J} \\ &= 0.74\,\text{kJ} \end{aligned}$$

          c) The share that became work is

          $\tfrac{W_\text{g}}{Q}$$= \tfrac{300\,\text{J}}{1040\,\text{J}}$
          $= 0.288$
          $\approx 29\,\%$

          Answer

          b) $\Delta U = 0.74\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures, because the power $260\,\text{W}$ and the time $4.0\,\text{s}$ are given with two figures. This number goes in the answer box.

          Justification c)

          About $29\,\%$ of the heat became work, and the remaining about $71\,\%$ stayed as the internal energy of the air, so the air warmed up. This fits air: for a diatomic gas at constant pressure the share of the work is about $\tfrac{2}{7} \approx 29\,\%$ of the heat received. A single expansion is not a heat engine, because an engine repeats its work cycle after cycle. To bring the piston back to the start, the air has to be cooled and compressed, and in the cooling heat is transferred to the colder surroundings. The work share $29\,\%$ of one expansion is therefore not the efficiency of a cycle: that is $\eta = W/Q_1$, where the work is $W = Q_1 - Q_2$.

          Reasonableness

          The change in internal energy is positive and smaller than the heat, $0 < 740\,\text{J} < 1040\,\text{J}$, as it should be when a gas expands and receives heat. Energy balance: $740\,\text{J} + 300\,\text{J} = 1040\,\text{J}$. The speed of the piston, $3.0\,\text{cm/s}$, is slow, so the motion of a lightly moving piston is calm.
