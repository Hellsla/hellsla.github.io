Determining absolute zero

Why can the lowest possible temperature be found in a school laboratory, even though a thermometer does not come anywhere near it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • turn measurement data into a straight line and extend the line to zero pressure.
  • determine absolute zero experimentally by extrapolation.
  • estimate the uncertainty of a result using the steepest and the shallowest line that fit.

Prior knowledge

  • When the volume and the amount of gas stay the same, the pressure of a dilute gas is proportional to the temperature in kelvins, and absolute zero is the zero point of the kelvin scalechapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature
  • The temperature of a gas tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles, and pressure comes from particles colliding with the wallchapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature

1Think about it

On a frosty night, a car tyre is softer in the morning than it was the previous afternoon: when the air cools, its pressure decreases. Could the pressure keep decreasing if the cooling continued? In a school laboratory, you rarely get below $-20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and yet physics knows at which temperature the pressure of a gas would be zero.

How can the lowest possible temperature be determined from measurements made at quite ordinary temperatures?

Principle

The flask is sealed and rigid, so the amount and volume of the gas stay constant and the pressure is a linear function of temperature, $p = p_0 + k\theta$. So two points on the line are enough to determine the line, and the line can be extended (extrapolated) all the way to zero pressure.

Reading the graph

Points A and B are read far apart from the best-fit line, because a long distance reduces the effect of a reading error on the slope. The pressure of a point is read from the vertical axis at its temperature: A at $\theta = 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and B at $\theta = 90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The readings are given in the problem, and the vertical axis of the picture starts at $95\,\text{kPa}$, so the zero point is not read from the picture.

Formula

The slope, the pressure of the line at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the temperature at zero pressure:

$$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{p_B - p_A}{\theta_B - \theta_A} \\ p_0 &= p_A - k\theta_A \\ \theta_0 &= -\frac{p_0}{k} \end{aligned}$$

Conversions

No conversions are needed: the pressures are in kilopascals, so the kPa cancels in the quotient $p_0/k$ and the result is in degrees Celsius. The temperature difference $90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the same in kelvins.

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{(134.3 - 108.6)\,\text{kPa}}{(90.0 - 20.0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= \dfrac{25.7\,\text{kPa}}{70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= 0.36714\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

Then

$p_0$$= 108.6\,\text{kPa} - 0.36714\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
$= 101.26\,\text{kPa}$

and

$\theta_0$$= -\dfrac{101.26\,\text{kPa}}{0.36714\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
$= -275.8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Answer

$-276\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Three significant figures, because the pressure difference $25.7\,\text{kPa}$ and the temperature difference $70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are known to three figures; finally the subtraction $20.0 - 296$ gives the result in whole degrees.

Reasonableness

Estimate: the pressure rises by about $0.37\,\text{kPa}$ per degree, so $101\,\text{kPa}$ runs out after about $101/0.37 \approx 270$ degrees. The result is close to $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the difference is small compared with the extrapolation distance of $270\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    In a closed rigid container, the volume and the amount of gas do not change, so the pressure depends only on temperature. In a gas thermometer, a glass flask stands in a water bath. As the bath warms up, you read the bath temperature $\theta$ from a thermometer and the gas pressure $p$ from a pressure gauge.

    When you plot the measurements on a graph, the points lie almost on a straight line: the pressure rises by almost the same amount for every degree Celsius. In real data sets the points are usually not exactly on one line, because of measurement uncertainty. Measurement uncertainty describes the uncertainty attached to a measurement result. It does not usually mean an absolute maximum possible deviation from the true value. Here the uncertainty of the pressure reading is estimated as $\pm 1\,\text{kPa}$. The error limit that a manufacturer states for a gauge is a different thing: it is a property of the device, and the uncertainty of the measurement is also affected by, for example, reading the scale and the bath temperature settling. So you draw the line through the middle of the group of points, so that it passes through the uncertainty bars, not necessarily through every point.

    You extend the line beyond the measurement range towards colder temperatures until the pressure is zero. This is called extrapolation: the result comes from a region where nothing has been measured. According to the microscopic model, pressure comes from particles colliding with the wall. In the ideal gas model, the pressure would be zero at the temperature where the average kinetic energy of the particles is at its lowest. The temperature where the line meets zero pressure is the experiment's estimate of absolute zero. It is only an estimate: a real gas liquefies before that point, and the extension is a model.

    Temperature at zero pressure

    Within the measurement range, the pressure of a gas is a linear function of temperature:

    $$p = p_0 + k\theta$$

    where $p$ is the pressure (kPa), $p_0$ is the pressure of the line at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (kPa), $k$ is the slope of the line (kPa/°C) and $\theta$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius. You calculate the slope from two points read far apart from the drawn line:

    $$k = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$$

    where $(\theta_1, p_1)$ and $(\theta_2, p_2)$ are points on the line. Zero pressure is reached when $p = 0$. The temperature is then $\theta_0$, and the equation gives:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0 &= p_0 + k\theta_0 \\ \theta_0 &= -\frac{p_0}{k} \end{aligned}$$

    You get the same result from any point $(\theta_1, p_1)$ on the line by substituting $p = 0$ into the equation $p = p_1 + k(\theta - \theta_1)$:

    $$\theta_0 = \theta_1 - \frac{p_1}{k}$$

    You estimate the uncertainty by drawing two limit lines that still pass through all the uncertainty bars: the steepest and the shallowest. For each you calculate the temperature at zero pressure, and the smaller and larger values give an interval. You give the result using the midpoint and half of the interval:

    $$\Delta\theta_0 = \frac{\theta_{0,\text{max}} - \theta_{0,\text{min}}}{2}$$

    where $\theta_{0,\text{max}}$ is the larger (less negative) and $\theta_{0,\text{min}}$ the smaller temperature at zero pressure. The result has the form $\theta_0 \pm \Delta\theta_0$. The interval is an estimate, not a guaranteed limit: it takes into account the uncertainty of the points but not whether the dependence stays linear outside the measurement range.

    The picture is drawn to scale. The limit lines run close to each other in the measurement range but move apart when you extrapolate, so the uncertainty grows as you extend the line. A more precise pressure gauge, more measurement points and a wider measurement range reduce the uncertainty. Once the result is known, the zero of the kelvin scale is placed there: the temperature in kelvins is $T = \theta - \theta_0$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that absolute zero has been measured because it is on the graph. Only the seven points in the range 10–$100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are measured; the rest is an extension of the line, and the extension is an assumption that the pressure behaves in the same way at colder temperatures. Another mistake is to consider the result a failure if it is not exactly $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result $-276\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ differs from the accepted value by about 3 degrees when the uncertainty is 18 degrees, so it agrees with the value. A third mistake is to draw a wiggly line through every point: the variation of the points up and down is caused by measurement uncertainty, and the line should be drawn through the middle of the group of points.

    Summary

    • In a closed rigid container, the pressure of a dilute gas is a linear function of temperature within the measurement range: $p = p_0 + k\theta$. You turn the measurement data into a straight line by plotting $p$ as a function of temperature and drawing the line through the middle of the group of points.
    • Measurement uncertainty describes the uncertainty attached to a measurement result, and it is not usually an absolute maximum possible deviation from the true value. The line does not have to hit every point, only pass through the uncertainty bars.
    • Extrapolation extends the line beyond the measurement range: the temperature at zero pressure is $\theta_0 = -p_0/k$.
    • You estimate the uncertainty from the limit lines: $\Delta\theta_0 = (\theta_{0,\text{max}} - \theta_{0,\text{min}})/2$. The result $\theta_0 \pm \Delta\theta_0$ agrees with the accepted value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ if that value lies in the interval.

      • A change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, so the slope is the same with the unit kPa/K and kPa/°C. The error is elsewhere.

      • Correct. The formula $\theta_0 = -p_0/k$ assumes that the temperature of the point is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. When the point is elsewhere, the temperature $\theta_1$ must be included: $\theta_0 = \theta_1 - p_1/k$.

      • Kilopascals work, because the unit cancels: the pressure is divided by the slope, whose unit contains the same kilopascal.

          $\theta_{0,\text{min}}$$= -\dfrac{102\,\text{kPa}}{0.350\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
          $= -291.43\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          So

          $\Delta\theta_0$$= \dfrac{-263.16\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-291.43\,{}^\circ\text{C})}{2}$
          $= 14.14\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $14\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Two significant figures, because the zero points $-263\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $-291\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are known to three figures, that is, in whole degrees, and their difference $28\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is only a two-figure number; the divisor $2$ is exact.

          Reasonableness

          The interval is $-277 \pm 14\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, since the midpoint is

          $\dfrac{-263.16 + (-291.43)}{2}$
          $= -277.3\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          The accepted value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ lies in the interval, so the result is consistent. The order of magnitude is the same as in the worked example of the chapter ($18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), when the points and the uncertainty of the pressure reading are similar.

        3. In the experiment, a pressure gauge was used that shows the gauge pressure compared with the surrounding atmospheric pressure. The atmospheric pressure was $101.0\,\text{kPa}$. The gauge reading was $9.0\,\text{kPa}$ at $30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $31.0\,\text{kPa}$ at $90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          At what temperature would the absolute pressure of the gas be zero, when the pressure depends linearly on temperature? Give your answer in degrees Celsius.

          °C
          Answer

          −270 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          atmospheric pressure $p_\text{air} = 101.0\,\text{kPa}$; the gauge pressure from the gauge $p_\text{g,1} = 9.0\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $\theta_1 = 30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $p_\text{g,2} = 31.0\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $\theta_2 = 90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Find

          the temperature $\theta_0$ in degrees Celsius at which the absolute pressure of the gas is zero

          Principle

          The pressure of a gas means the absolute pressure, and only that is linear with respect to temperature in such a way that the zero of the line predicts a vacuum. The gauge shows the gauge pressure compared with atmospheric pressure, so you must add the atmospheric pressure before fitting the line.

          Formula

          $p = p_\text{g} + p_\text{air}$. Then

          $k$$= \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$

          and for zero pressure $0 = p_1 + k(\theta_0 - \theta_1)$, so $\theta_0 = \theta_1 - \dfrac{p_1}{k}$

          Conversions

          The gauge pressures are converted to absolute pressures:

          $p_1$$= 9.0\,\text{kPa} + 101.0\,\text{kPa}$
          $= 110.0\,\text{kPa}$

          and

          $p_2$$= 31.0\,\text{kPa} + 101.0\,\text{kPa}$
          $= 132.0\,\text{kPa}$

          The units are already kilopascals and degrees Celsius.

          Substitution

          $k$$= \dfrac{(132.0 - 110.0)\,\text{kPa}}{(90.0 - 30.0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= \dfrac{22.0\,\text{kPa}}{60.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= 0.36667\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

          and

          $\theta_0$$= 30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{110.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.36667\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
          $= 30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= -270\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $-270\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Three significant figures, because the pressure difference $22.0\,\text{kPa}$ and the temperature difference $60.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are known to three figures; finally the subtraction $30.0 - 300$ gives the result in whole degrees.

          Reasonableness

          The result is close to $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, as it should be. If the atmospheric pressure were not added, the line of the gauge pressure would reach zero already at $30.0 - 9.0/0.36667 = 5.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, which is impossible, because a gas at room temperature is nowhere near a vacuum.

        4. In the experiment, the reading precision of the temperature is $\pm 0.50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and of the pressure $\pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$. The slope of the line through the measurement points is $0.36\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$. On the line, the temperature uncertainty $\pm 0.50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ corresponds to a certain change in pressure.

          What percentage of the pressure uncertainty $1.0\,\text{kPa}$ is this change in pressure?

          %
          Answer

          18 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          the reading precision of the temperature $\Delta\theta = 0.50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the slope of the line $k = 0.36\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$ and the uncertainty of the pressure $\Delta p = 1.0\,\text{kPa}$

          Find

          the change in pressure $\Delta p_\theta$ corresponding to the temperature uncertainty, as a percentage of the uncertainty $\Delta p$

          Principle

          The measurement points lie on the line $p = p_0 + k\theta$, so an error $\Delta\theta$ in temperature moves the point horizontally, and at the line it corresponds to a vertical deviation $k\,\Delta\theta$. Once it is converted to a pressure, you can compare it directly with the uncertainty of the pressure reading.

          Formula

          $\Delta p_\theta = k\,\Delta\theta$ and the proportion

          $s$$= \dfrac{\Delta p_\theta}{\Delta p} \cdot 100\,\%$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the degrees Celsius cancel in the product $k\,\Delta\theta$, and the kilopascals cancel in the quotient. Multiplying by $100\,\%$ turns the ratio into a percentage.

          Substitution

          $\Delta p_\theta$$= 0.36\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot 0.50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 0.18\,\text{kPa}$

          and

          $s$$= \dfrac{0.18\,\text{kPa}}{1.0\,\text{kPa}} \cdot 100\,\%$
          $= 18\,\%$

          Answer

          $18\,\%$

          Two significant figures, because the slope $0.36\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$ is given to two figures; the uncertainties $0.50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $1.0\,\text{kPa}$ also have two figures.

          Reasonableness

          The temperature error corresponds to only about a fifth of the uncertainty of the pressure reading, so the precision of the result is limited by the pressure gauge, not by the thermometer. Limiting case: the proportion would be $100\,\%$ if the temperature error were $1.0/0.36 = 2.8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the thermometer would have to be much less precise than it is now.

        1. The measurement range is 20.0–$100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. At $20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the pressure is $106.0 \pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$ and at $100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ it is $137.0 \pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$. The steepest limit line passes through the points ($20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $105.0\,\text{kPa}$) and ($100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $138.0\,\text{kPa}$), and the shallowest through the points ($20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $107.0\,\text{kPa}$) and ($100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $136.0\,\text{kPa}$). Determine the uncertainty of absolute zero $\Delta\theta_0$ and deduce why limit lines based on the end points alone give a larger uncertainty than the seven-point data.

          °C
          Answer

          20.3 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          the end points $106.0 \pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $137.0 \pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The steepest limit line passes through the points $(20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $105.0\,\text{kPa})$ and $(100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $138.0\,\text{kPa})$, the shallowest through the points $(20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $107.0\,\text{kPa})$ and $(100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $136.0\,\text{kPa})$

          Find

          the uncertainty $\Delta\theta_0$ in degrees Celsius and the reason why the result is larger than with the seven-point data

          Principle

          The steeper or shallower a line you allow, the wider the interval of zero points. The zero-pressure point of each limit line is calculated from two points of the line, and the uncertainty is half of the interval between these two values.

          Formula

          For each line

          $k$$= \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$

          and $\theta_0 = \theta_1 - \dfrac{p_1}{k}$, and then

          $\Delta\theta_0$$= \dfrac{\theta_{0,\text{max}} - \theta_{0,\text{min}}}{2}$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the pressures are in kilopascals and the temperatures in degrees Celsius, and the temperature difference $100.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the same in kelvins.

          Substitution

          Steepest:

          $k$$= \dfrac{(138.0 - 105.0)\,\text{kPa}}{80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= 0.4125\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

          and

          $\theta_0$$= 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{105.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.4125\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
          $= -234.55\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Shallowest:

          $k$$= \dfrac{(136.0 - 107.0)\,\text{kPa}}{80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= 0.3625\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

          and

          $\theta_0$$= 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{107.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.3625\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
          $= -275.17\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          So

          $\Delta\theta_0$$= \dfrac{-234.55 - (-275.17)}{2}\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 20.31\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          (exactly $20.3\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), two significant figures, because the zero points $-235\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $-275\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are known to three figures, that is, in whole degrees, and their difference $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is a two-figure number. A third figure would require initial values to the hundredth, which does not fit the uncertainty $\pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$.

          Reasonableness

          The interval $-275.2\ldots -234.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ contains the accepted value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result is larger than the $18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ of the seven-point data, as it should be: a line through two points has no other constraints, so the steepest line may rise at one end and fall at the other to the edge of an uncertainty bar. In the seven-point data every line must also pass through the bars of the five intermediate points, which narrows the range of allowed slopes.

        2. The pressure of nitrogen gas in a rigid container is $100.0\,\text{kPa}$ at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins.

          What pressure would the line give at $77.0\,\text{K}$, where nitrogen boils at normal pressure? At such a low pressure, nitrogen gas condenses into a liquid only at about $67\,\text{K}$. Justify why the line cannot be extended all the way to absolute zero. Give your answer as a pressure in kilopascals.

          kPa
          Answer

          28.2 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          $p_0 = 100.0\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the target temperature $T = 77.0\,\text{K}$ and $p \propto T$ in kelvins; at this pressure nitrogen condenses into a liquid only at about $67\,\text{K}$

          Find

          the pressure $p$ at the temperature $77.0\,\text{K}$ in kilopascals, and a justification of why the line cannot be extended all the way to zero

          Principle

          In a rigid closed container, the amount and volume of the gas stay constant, so the pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins and $\dfrac{p}{T}$ is constant. The proportionality holds only in kelvins, because only the zero of the kelvin scale is absolute zero. It also requires that the gas stays a gas.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{p}{T} = \dfrac{p_0}{T_0}$, so $p = p_0\,\dfrac{T}{T_0}$

          Conversions

          The initial temperature is converted to kelvins:

          $T_0$$= 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 273.15\,\text{K}$

          The temperature $77.0\,\text{K}$ is already in kelvins. In the ratio $T/T_0$ the kelvins cancel, so the pressure stays in kilopascals.

          Substitution

          $p$$= 100.0\,\text{kPa} \cdot \dfrac{77.0\,\text{K}}{273.15\,\text{K}}$
          $= 28.19\,\text{kPa}$

          Answer

          $28.2\,\text{kPa}$

          Three significant figures, because the temperature $77.0\,\text{K}$ is given to three figures ($273.15\,\text{K}$ is a definition and does not limit). The line cannot be extended all the way to zero, because nitrogen condenses into a liquid at about $67\,\text{K}$. Then the amount of gas decreases, the pressure falls faster than the line predicts, and the condition "amount of substance constant" no longer holds.

          Reasonableness

          The temperature $77.0\,\text{K}$ is about $0.28$ times the temperature $273.15\,\text{K}$, and the pressure is in the same proportion: about $0.28 \cdot 100 = 28\,\text{kPa}$. At the temperature $67\,\text{K}$ the line would give a pressure of $100 \cdot 67/273.15 = 24.5\,\text{kPa}$, but at about that temperature the gas starts to condense into a liquid, and after that the pressure no longer follows the line. So extrapolation is reliable only as far as the assumption of a gas holds true.

        3. The result of group A's measurement is $\theta_0 = -262.00 \pm 6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The accepted value is $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          How many times does the uncertainty $\Delta\theta_0$ fit between the result and the accepted value? Deduce a reason why the result does not agree with the accepted value.

          kertaa
          Answer

          1.86 kertaa

          Show the solution

          Given

          group A's result $\theta_0 = -262.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and uncertainty $\Delta\theta_0 = 6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; the accepted value $\theta_\text{acc} = -273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Find

          the ratio $n$ of the deviation to the uncertainty (how many times $\Delta\theta_0$ fits between the result and the accepted value) and the reason for the disagreement

          Principle

          In this chapter a result is considered consistent with the accepted value if the value lies in the uncertainty interval $\theta_0 \pm \Delta\theta_0$, that is, when the deviation is at most one uncertainty. The ratio $n$ tells you how many times larger the deviation is than the uncertainty. The uncertainty is not an absolute limit, so a deviation alone does not prove an error, but the larger $n$ is, the more likely it is that the measurement has a systematic error or that the uncertainty was estimated too small.

          Formula

          $n$$= \dfrac{|\theta_0 - \theta_\text{acc}|}{\Delta\theta_0}$

          Conversions

          All temperatures are in degrees Celsius, and temperature differences are the same in kelvins. The ratio $n$ is dimensionless, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $n$$= \dfrac{|-262.00\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C})|}{6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= \dfrac{11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= 1.858$

          Answer

          $1.86$

          Times, three significant figures, because the difference $11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the uncertainty $6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are given to three figures (the result $-262.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the accepted value are given to the hundredth).

          Reasonableness

          The deviation $11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is almost two uncertainties ($2 \cdot 6.00 = 12.0$). The uncertainty interval $-268\ldots -256\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ does not contain the accepted value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so by this rule the result is not consistent with it. The cause may be a systematic error, for example an imprecise thermometer, an air leak or a change in the volume of the flask, or an uncertainty that was estimated too small. Check: $1.858 \cdot 6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
