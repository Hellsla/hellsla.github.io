The wave-mechanical model of the atom and orbitals

Why is the electron of an atom drawn in a chemistry book as a fuzzy cloud and not as a small ball going round the nucleus along an orbit?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how standing waves lead to the allowed states of an electron in an atom.
  • interpret an orbital as a region inside which the electron is detected with a chosen probability.
  • calculate the number of orbitals and electrons in a shell and the energy of the photon produced in a transition.

Prior knowledge

  • Electrons sent one at a time each hit one spot behind a double slit, but many hits build up an interference patternchapter Light and electrons as waves and particles
  • The energy levels of the hydrogen atom are $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, and the energy of the photon produced in a transition is the difference between the levelschapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model

1Think about it

On a page of a chemistry book, the electrons of a carbon atom do not go round the nucleus along neat orbits. They are drawn as blue clouds and two-lobed shapes, and the caption speaks of a region where the electron is likely to be detected. In physics, however, the energy levels of the hydrogen atom were calculated with the Bohr model, in which the electron goes round the nucleus on a circular orbit.

What does the picture of an orbital actually show, if it is not the path of the electron?

Assumptions of the model

The model is a hydrogen atom whose electron is in the $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$ or $2\text{p}$ state. Each measurement is made in a new atom in the same state, and the result is drawn from the probability distribution of the state. The picture is a see-through projection: the three-dimensional measurement results and the boundary are drawn in one plane, so some of the grey dots drawn at the boundary are in reality in front of or behind it. In the $2\text{s}$ state there is a gap around the nodal sphere that does not belong to the region, so its boundary is drawn as three circles. The lobes of the $2\text{p}$ state are vertical. In a real experiment the position of the electron of a single atom is not measured as directly as this; the model illustrates the probability interpretation.

How the calculation works

The share inside the boundary is $k : N$, where $k$ is the number of blue dots. The boundary is a surface on which the probability density is the same everywhere and which encloses the probability $p$. The greatest distance $R$ of the boundary is measured from the nucleus in the direction in which the region extends farthest; in the $2\text{p}$ state it is the tip of a lobe. The most common distance is the midpoint of the tallest bar of the histogram. The lengths have been calculated using the Bohr radius $0.0529\,\text{nm}$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    From an orbit to a wave

    The Bohr model gave the energy levels of the hydrogen atom correctly (chapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model), but its picture of a small ball on a circular orbit does not stand closer examination. The model does not tell why only certain orbits are allowed, and it does not work for atoms with several electrons.

    In the chapter Light and electrons as waves and particles we also saw that electrons sent one at a time form an interference pattern behind a double slit: the motion of an electron must be described by a wave. In the wave-mechanical model of the atom, the state of an electron in an atom is described by a wave that spreads around the nucleus, not by an orbit.

    The allowed states are standing waves

    A guitar string is fixed at both ends, so only those standing waves arise in it in which the ends of the string are nodes. There are therefore only certain ways of vibrating: one antinode, two antinodes, three antinodes and so on. The electric attraction of the nucleus binds the electron, and its wave must likewise fit around the nucleus. Lasting states arise only for certain wave shapes, and each shape has its own energy. The quantisation of energy is thus not a separate assumption as in the Bohr model, but follows from the fact that a bound wave can only be a standing wave. In a string, an extra node raises the frequency of the vibration. In the hydrogen atom the energy of a state is greater the more nodes the wave has; in an atom with many electrons this holds for states of the same shape (s, p, …).

    In an atom the wave is three-dimensional, so its nodes are surfaces, and the electron is not detected on a nodal surface. The wave of the lowest state, the $1\text{s}$ state, has no node. The states of the shell $n = 2$ have one node: in the $2\text{s}$ state it is a spherical surface around the nucleus, and in the $2\text{p}$ state a plane through the nucleus. The number in the name of the state is the principal quantum number $n$, and there are $n - 1$ nodes altogether. The letter tells the shape of the wave: an s state is spherically symmetric, and a p state has two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus.

    The probability interpretation

    The wave does not tell where the electron is but where it is likely to be detected. When the position of the electron is measured, the result is always one point, and a single result cannot be predicted. The square of the amplitude of the wave tells how likely the electron is to be detected in a small volume around each place. This is the probability interpretation of the wave model. The distribution becomes visible only when the measurement is repeated in many atoms in the same state. If $k$ of $N$ measurements land in some region, the probability of detecting the electron there is approximately

    $$P \approx \frac{k}{N}$$

    where $P$ is the probability, $k$ the number of measurements that landed in the region and $N$ the number of all measurements. The estimate becomes more accurate as $N$ grows. Between measurements the electron has no orbit that could be drawn.

    An orbital is a region of probability

    When the measurement results are marked as dots, an electron cloud is formed that is densest where the electron is most likely to be detected. The cloud has no sharp edge. That is why an orbital is drawn as a region inside which the electron is detected with a chosen probability, usually with a probability of $90\,\%$. The boundary is an agreed threshold, not a wall: about a tenth of the measurements land outside it. The $90\,\%$ boundary of the $1\text{s}$ orbital of the hydrogen atom is a sphere with a radius of $0.14\,\text{nm}$. The word orbital also means the state itself, for example the $2\text{p}$ orbital.

    The cloud is densest at the nucleus, but the most common distance from the nucleus is not zero. A thin spherical shell farther from the nucleus holds more volume than a small sphere right around the nucleus, so a $1\text{s}$ electron is detected most often at a distance of about $0.053\,\text{nm}$. This is the same as the radius of the smallest orbit of the Bohr model: in the wave model the Bohr orbit is the most common distance, not a path travelled by the electron.

    Shells, orbitals and energy levels

    In the hydrogen atom the energy of a state depends only on the principal quantum number $n$, and the wave model gives the same energy levels as the Bohr model:

    $$E_n = -\frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$$

    where $E_n$ is the energy of the electron (eV) in the shell $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ States with the same principal quantum number form an electron shell. The shell $n$ has $n$ subshells, s, p, d and f, which have $1$, $3$, $5$ and $7$ orbitals, so the number of orbitals in a shell is $n^2$.

    The number of orbitals in a subshell depends on the shape of the wave. An s orbital is a sphere, so there can be only one. The pair of lobes of a p orbital can point in three mutually perpendicular directions ($x$, $y$ and $z$), so there are three p orbitals; the figures of this chapter show one of them, whose lobes are vertical. The d and f subshells have five and seven different orbitals, respectively. An electron also has spin, its own property with only two possible values. Each orbital can therefore hold at most two electrons, and they have opposite spins (Pauli exclusion principle). So at most

    $$N_\text{max} = 2n^2$$

    electrons fit in a shell, where $N_\text{max}$ is the greatest number of electrons and $n$ the principal quantum number of the shell.

    Shell $n$ Subshells Orbitals $n^2$ Electrons $2n^2$
    $1$ s $1$ $2$
    $2$ s, p $4$ $8$
    $3$ s, p, d $9$ $18$
    $4$ s, p, d, f $16$ $32$

    When an electron moves from a higher state to a lower one, the atom emits a photon whose energy is the difference between the energies of the states (chapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms). In the hydrogen atom the states of the same shell have the same energy, so, for example, the transitions $3\text{d} \to 2\text{p}$ and $3\text{s} \to 2\text{p}$ produce the same spectral line.

    Connection to the orbitals of chemistry

    In an atom with many electrons the electrons repel each other, so the subshells of the same shell have different energies: $2\text{s}$ is lower than $2\text{p}$ and $3\text{s}$ lower than $3\text{p}$. In the ground state the electrons fill the orbitals starting from the lowest energy, at most two in each orbital. The filling order of light atoms is $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$, $3\text{s}$, $3\text{p}$. The six electrons of carbon are placed like this: $1\text{s}^2\,2\text{s}^2\,2\text{p}^2$, where the superscript is the number of electrons in the subshell.

    The electrons of the outermost shell, the valence electrons, are on average farthest from the nucleus and most weakly bound, and they decide what kind of bonds the atom forms. The periodic table and the orbital pictures of chemistry thus rest on the same wave model.

    Common misconception

    Many think that an orbital is an orbit along which the electron goes round, or a shell inside which the electron stays. In the wave model the electron has no orbit, and the boundary of an orbital is an agreed probability boundary: outside the $90\,\%$ boundary the electron is detected on average in every tenth measurement. Another mistake is to think that the cloud arises because the electron whizzes so fast that its orbit blurs like the blades of a rotating fan. The cloud describes the distribution of the measurement results: a single measurement always gives one point, and the position of the electron between measurements cannot be described as a path.

    Summary

    • In the wave-mechanical model of the atom, the state of an electron is described by a standing wave that spreads around the nucleus. Only certain wave shapes are possible, so the energy is quantised; in the hydrogen atom, the more nodes, the greater the energy.
    • Probability interpretation: the wave tells how likely the electron is to be detected at each place. One measurement gives one random point, and the distribution is seen only from many measurements: $P \approx \tfrac{k}{N}$.
    • An orbital is a region inside which the electron is detected with a chosen probability, for example $90\,\%$. The $90\,\%$ boundary of the $1\text{s}$ orbital of the hydrogen atom is a sphere with a radius of $0.14\,\text{nm}$.
    • The shell $n$ has $n^2$ orbitals and at most $2n^2$ electrons. The energy levels of the hydrogen atom are $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$.
    • Orbitals fill starting from the lowest energy ($1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$, $3\text{s}$, $3\text{p}$), and this is the basis of the electron configurations and the periodic table of chemistry.

      Worked example

      Problem

      The electron of a hydrogen atom moves from the $3\text{d}$ orbital to the $2\text{p}$ orbital, and the atom emits a photon. What is the wavelength of the photon?

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. The initial state $3\text{d}$ belongs to the shell $n = 3$ and the final state $2\text{p}$ to the shell $n = 2$. The energy levels of the hydrogen atom are $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, the Planck constant is $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ and the speed of light is $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. The wavelength $\lambda$ of the photon is asked.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Energy is conserved: the energy of the atom decreases by the difference between the energies of the states, and this energy leaves as a photon whose energy is $hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. In the hydrogen atom the energy of a state depends only on the principal quantum number, so the letters d and p do not change the energy; they tell the shape of the wave.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} \frac{hc}{\lambda} &= E_3 - E_2 \\ \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_3 - E_2} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The energy difference in electronvolts is

      $E_3 - E_2$$= -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{9} + \tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{4}$
      $= 1.8889\,\text{eV}$

      Extra figures are kept in the intermediate results, and rounding is done only at the end. The energy difference is converted to joules by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$:

      $E_3 - E_2$$= 1.8889 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
      $= 3.0260 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate the numerator

      $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
      $= 1.98647 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

      and divide it by the energy difference:

      $$\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_3 - E_2} \\ &= \frac{1.98647 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{3.0260 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \\ &= 6.5647 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m} \\ &= 656.47\,\text{nm} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The wavelength of the photon is $656\,\text{nm}$, with three significant figures like the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The wavelength is in the range of visible light, and $656\,\text{nm}$ is the red line of the spectrum of hydrogen (chapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms). The photon energy $1.9\,\text{eV}$ lies between the energies of photons of visible light, $1.6$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$. The transition $3\text{s} \to 2\text{p}$ would give the same line, because the energy of the initial state is the same.

      measurements $N = 6000$, threshold of the boundary $p = 90\,\% = 0.90$; the $2\text{p}$ orbital has two similar lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus

      the expected number $k_\text{upper}$ of measurements that land inside the boundary in the upper lobe

      According to the probability interpretation, in many measurements the share of those that land in a region is approximately the probability of the region, $P \approx \tfrac{k}{N}$.       2. Nodes and energy

      The wave of the state of an electron in a hydrogen atom has two nodal surfaces. Which claim is true?

      • It is the other way round: in a string, each extra node raises the frequency of the vibration, and in the hydrogen atom an extra node raises the energy of the state. The $1\text{s}$ state, which has no nodes, is the lowest state of the hydrogen atom.

      • The states of the shell $n$ have $n - 1$ nodes: the $1\text{s}$ state has none and the states of the shell $n = 2$ have one. Two nodes belong to the states of the shell $n = 3$.

      • On a nodal surface the amplitude of the wave is zero, so the electron is not detected there at all. The electron is detected most often at places where the amplitude of the wave is greatest.

      • Correct. There are $n - 1$ nodes, so $n = 3$. In the hydrogen atom, the more nodes, the greater the energy: $E_3 = -1.51\,\text{eV}$ is greater than $E_2 = -3.40\,\text{eV}$.

      3. Energy of a transition

      The electron of a hydrogen atom moves from the $2\text{p}$ orbital to the $1\text{s}$ orbital, and the atom emits a photon.

      What is the energy of the photon in electronvolts?

      eV
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which shells do the initial and final states belong to?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The energy of the photon is the difference between the energies of the states: $E = E_2 - E_1$, where $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $E_2 = -3.40\,\text{eV}$ and $E_1 = -13.6\,\text{eV}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      initial state $2\text{p}$, so $n = 2$; final state $1\text{s}$, so $n = 1$; energy levels of the hydrogen atom $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$

      Asked

      the energy $E$ of the photon in electronvolts

      Principle

      Energy is conserved: when the electron moves to a lower state, the energy of the atom decreases by the difference between the energies of the states, and the same energy leaves as a photon. In the hydrogen atom the energy of a state depends only on the principal quantum number, so the letters p and s have no effect.

      Formula

      $E$$= E_2 - E_1$
      $= -\dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{2^2} + \dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{1^2}$

      Conversions

      None are needed, because the energy levels and the asked energy are in electronvolts.

      Substitution

      $E$$= -\dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{4} + 13.6\,\text{eV}$
      $= -3.40\,\text{eV} + 13.6\,\text{eV}$
      $= 10.2\,\text{eV}$

      Answer

      $10.2\,\text{eV}$

      Three significant figures like the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

      Reasonableness

      The energy is positive and smaller than the binding energy of the ground state, $13.6\,\text{eV}$, because the electron is not released but moves from one shell to another. The energy of the photon is about $3$–$6$ times the energies of photons of visible light, $1.6$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$, so the radiation is ultraviolet, like the shortest lines of the spectrum of hydrogen.

When the position is measured, the result is one random point. The cloud appears only when the measurement is repeated in many atoms in the same state and the results are marked in the same picture. The cloud thins out outwards, but it has no sharp edge. That is why the boundary of an orbital is drawn at an agreed probability, usually so that the electron is detected inside it with a probability of $90\,\%$; about every tenth measurement lands outside the boundary.

        Finally, two quick questions. Answer them yourself first and only then open the answer.

        1. Write the electron configuration of a nitrogen atom (seven electrons) in the ground state.
          Answer

          $1\text{s}^2\,2\text{s}^2\,2\text{p}^3$: two electrons in the $1\text{s}$ orbital, two in the $2\text{s}$ orbital and three in the $2\text{p}$ subshell, seven altogether.

        2. Why can a guitar string vibrate only in certain ways, and what does the same idea mean for the electron of an atom?
          Answer

          The ends of the string stay in place, so the nodes of a standing wave must fall on the ends, and only certain wave shapes fit the string. The wave of an electron bound by the nucleus can likewise be only in certain standing shapes, and each shape has its own energy, so the energy of the electron is quantised.

        1. Core

          In heavy elements the electrons are in shells farther and farther out. What is the greatest number of electrons that fit in the shell $n = 5$?

          electrons
          Answer

          $50$

          Show the solution

          Given

          principal quantum number of the shell $n = 5$

          Asked

          the greatest number of electrons $N_\text{max}$ that fit in the shell

          Principle

          The shell $n$ has $n^2$ orbitals, because its subshells s, p, d, … have $1$, $3$, $5$, … orbitals. Each orbital can hold at most two electrons with opposite spins (Pauli exclusion principle), so the greatest number is twice the number of orbitals.

          Formula

          $N_\text{max} = 2n^2$

          Conversions

          None are needed, because a count is asked.

          Substitution

          $N_\text{max}$$= 2 \cdot 5^2$
          $= 2 \cdot 25$
          $= 50$

          Answer

          $50$

          Electrons; the result is an exact integer, because it is calculated from counts.

          Reasonableness

          The result continues the series $2, 8, 18, 32$ of the table: the differences $6$, $10$ and $14$ of successive shells grow by four, so the next difference is $18$ and $32 + 18 = 50$. So more electrons fit in the shell $n = 5$ than in the shell $n = 4$, as they should.

        2. Core

          In a computer simulation, the position of the $2\text{s}$ electron of a hydrogen atom is measured $8000$ times. Of the measurements, $421$ land inside the nodal sphere of the $2\text{s}$ orbital. Based on the measurements, estimate the probability with which the $2\text{s}$ electron is detected inside the nodal sphere. Give the answer as a percentage.

          %
          Answer

          $5.26\,\%$

          Show the solution

          Given

          measurements $N = 8000$, hits inside the nodal sphere $k = 421$

          Asked

          the probability $P$ of detecting the $2\text{s}$ electron inside the nodal sphere, as a percentage

          Principle

          According to the probability interpretation, only probabilities can be calculated from the wave, and from measurements made in many atoms in the same state the probability is estimated as the share of the hits that land in the region. The more measurements there are, the more accurate the estimate.

          Formula

          the estimate of the probability is $P = \dfrac{k}{N}$

          Conversions

          The share is converted to a percentage by multiplying by one hundred: $1 = 100\,\%$.

          Substitution

          $P$$= \dfrac{421}{8000}$
          $= 0.052\,625$
          $= 5.2625\,\%$

          Answer

          $5.26\,\%$

          Three significant figures like the number of hits $421$.

          Reasonableness

          About every twentieth measurement lands inside the nodal sphere, since $8000 : 20 = 400$, which is close to the number $421$. The share is small, because the radius of the nodal sphere is only about $0.11\,\text{nm}$ and most of the probability of the $2\text{s}$ electron is outside it.

        3. The position of the $1\text{s}$ electron of a hydrogen atom is measured $3000$ times, and the $90\,\%$ boundary of the $1\text{s}$ orbital is drawn in the picture.

          How many measurements do you expect to land outside the boundary?

          hits
          Answer

          $300$

          Show the solution

          Given

          measurements $N = 3000$, boundary of the orbital $90\,\%$

          Asked

          the expected number $k_\text{out}$ of measurements that land outside the boundary

          Principle

          The boundary is drawn so that the electron is detected inside it with probability $0.90$, so the probability of landing outside is $1 - 0.90 = 0.10$. In many measurements the share of those landing outside is approximately this probability, $P \approx \tfrac{k}{N}$.

          Formula

          $k_\text{out} = (1 - p)\,N$

          Conversions

          $p = 90\,\% = 0.90$

          Substitution

          $k_\text{out}$$= (1 - 0.90) \cdot 3000$
          $= 0.10 \cdot 3000$
          $= 300$

          Answer

          about $300$ measurements; the number is an expected value, and the real number varies from one series to another.

          Reasonableness

          Every tenth measurement lands outside the boundary, and a tenth of $3000$ is $300$. If the boundary were a wall, there would be no hits outside, but the boundary of an orbital is only an agreed probability boundary.

        4. The electron of a hydrogen atom is in the $3\text{p}$ state.

          At least how much energy is needed to release the electron from the atom? Give the answer in electronvolts.

          eV
          Answer

          $1.51\,\text{eV}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          electron in the $3\text{p}$ state, so $n = 3$; energy levels of the hydrogen atom $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$

          Asked

          the smallest energy $E_\text{i}$ needed for release, in electronvolts

          Principle

          The electron is free of the atom when its energy is at least $0$. In the hydrogen atom the energy of a state depends only on the principal quantum number, so the energy of the $3\text{p}$ state is the energy of the shell $n = 3$, and the energy needed is the difference $0 - E_3$.

          Formula

          $E_\text{i}$$= 0 - E_3$
          $= \dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{3^2}$

          Conversions

          None are needed, because the energy levels and the asked energy are in electronvolts.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{i}$$= \dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{9}$
          $= 1.5111\,\text{eV}$

          Answer

          $1.51\,\text{eV}$

          Three significant figures like the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

          Reasonableness

          On the shell $n = 3$ the electron is farther from the nucleus and more weakly bound than in the ground state, so release takes only a ninth of the ground-state energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$. The energy is positive, because it is brought into the atom.

        5. The electron of a hydrogen atom is in the shell $n = 5$. What is its energy in electronvolts?

          eV
          Answer

          $-0.544\,\text{eV}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          shell $n = 5$; energy levels of the hydrogen atom $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$

          Asked

          the energy $E_5$ of the electron in electronvolts

          Principle

          In the hydrogen atom the energy of the state of the electron depends only on the principal quantum number, and the energy levels are given by the formula. The energy is negative, because a bound electron needs energy to be released.

          Formula

          $E_5 = -\dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{5^2}$

          Conversions

          None are needed, because the energy is asked in electronvolts.

          Substitution

          $E_5$$= -\dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{25}$
          $= -0.544\,\text{eV}$

          Answer

          $-0.544\,\text{eV}$

          Three significant figures like the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

          Reasonableness

          The energy is close to zero, because the electron of the shell $n = 5$ is weakly bound. It is greater than $E_4 = -0.85\,\text{eV}$, as it should be, because the energy increases as the number of the shell increases.

        1. Core

          The electron of a hydrogen atom moves from the $3\text{p}$ orbital to the $1\text{s}$ orbital, and the atom emits a photon.

          What is the wavelength of the photon? Give the answer in nanometres.

          nm
          Answer

          $103\,\text{nm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          initial state $3\text{p}$, so $n = 3$; final state $1\text{s}$, so $n = 1$; $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

          Asked

          the wavelength $\lambda$ of the photon in nanometres

          Principle

          Energy is conserved: the energy of the atom decreases by the difference between the energies of the states, and the same energy leaves as a photon, whose energy is $hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. In the hydrogen atom the energy of a state depends only on the principal quantum number.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = E_3 - E_1$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{E_3 - E_1}$

          Conversions

          The energy difference in electronvolts is

          $E_3 - E_1$$= -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{9} + 13.6\,\text{eV}$
          $= 12.089\,\text{eV}$

          Substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$:

          $E_3 - E_1$$= 12.089 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
          $= 1.9366 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J}$

          Finally $1\,\text{nm} = 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          First

          $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

          and then

          $\lambda$$= \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{1.9366 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J}}$
          $= 1.0257 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 102.57\,\text{nm}$

          Answer

          $103\,\text{nm}$

          Three significant figures like the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

          Reasonableness

          The energy of the photon is about $12\,\text{eV}$, more than the energy $10.2\,\text{eV}$ of the transition $2\text{p} \to 1\text{s}$, so the wavelength must be shorter than the $122\,\text{nm}$ of that transition. The radiation is ultraviolet, like all the lines of hydrogen that end in the ground state.

        2. Core

          An argon atom has $18$ electrons, and in the ground state they fill the orbitals in the order $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$, $3\text{s}$, $3\text{p}$.

          How many electrons are in the outermost shell of argon?

          electrons
          Answer

          $8$

          Show the solution

          Given

          argon has $18$ electrons; filling order $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$, $3\text{s}$, $3\text{p}$; at most two electrons per orbital

          Asked

          the number of electrons $N_\text{outer}$ in the outermost shell

          Principle

          In the ground state the electrons fill the orbitals starting from the lowest energy. At most $2n^2$ electrons fit in the shell $n$, so first the shells $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ fill, and the remaining electrons are in the shell $n = 3$, which is the outermost shell of argon.

          Formula

          $N_\text{outer} = N - 2 \cdot 1^2 - 2 \cdot 2^2$

          Conversions

          None are needed, because a count is asked.

          Substitution

          $N_\text{outer} = 18 - 2 - 8 = 8$

          Answer

          $8$

          Electrons; the result is an exact integer.

          Reasonableness

          On the shell $n = 3$ the electrons are in the $3\text{s}$ and $3\text{p}$ subshells: $2 + 6 = 8$, so both subshells are full. The configuration $1\text{s}^2\,2\text{s}^2\,2\text{p}^6\,3\text{s}^2\,3\text{p}^6$ contains $18$ electrons. Full outermost s and p subshells fit the fact that argon is a noble gas that hardly forms bonds.

        3. Two boundaries are drawn for the $1\text{s}$ orbital of a hydrogen atom: the $50\,\%$ boundary, whose radius is $0.071\,\text{nm}$, and the $90\,\%$ boundary, whose radius is $0.14\,\text{nm}$. The position of the electron is measured $2400$ times.

          How many measurements do you expect to land between the boundaries?

          hits
          Answer

          $960$

          Show the solution

          Given

          measurements $N = 2400$; $50\,\%$ boundary $0.071\,\text{nm}$ and $90\,\%$ boundary $0.14\,\text{nm}$

          Asked

          the expected number $k$ of measurements that land between the boundaries

          Principle

          The $50\,\%$ boundary lies entirely inside the $90\,\%$ boundary, so the probability $0.90 - 0.50 = 0.40$ remains in the shell between the boundaries. In many measurements the share of those landing in a region is approximately the probability of the region. The radii of the boundaries are not needed.

          Formula

          $k = (p_{90} - p_{50})\,N$

          Conversions

          $p_{90} = 90\,\% = 0.90$ and $p_{50} = 50\,\% = 0.50$

          Substitution

          $k$$= (0.90 - 0.50) \cdot 2400$
          $= 0.40 \cdot 2400$
          $= 960$

          Answer

          about $960$ measurements; the number is an expected value, and the real number varies from one series to another.

          Reasonableness

          Half, that is $1200$ measurements, land inside the $50\,\%$ boundary and $2160$ measurements inside the $90\,\%$ boundary; their difference is $960$. There are fewer hits between the boundaries than inside the inner boundary, although the shell has a greater volume, because the cloud thins out outwards.

        4. The electron of a hydrogen atom is in the shell $n = 2$.

          What is the greatest wavelength at which a photon can still release the electron from the atom? Give the answer in nanometres.

          nm
          Answer

          $365\,\text{nm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          electron in the shell $n = 2$; $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

          Asked

          the greatest wavelength $\lambda_\text{max}$ at which a photon releases the electron, in nanometres

          Principle

          The energy of the photon must be at least the energy $0 - E_2$ needed for release. The energy of a photon, $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, decreases as the wavelength increases, so the greatest suitable wavelength corresponds to exactly this energy.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_\text{max}}$$= 0 - E_2$

          so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{hc}{-E_2}$

          Conversions

          $-E_2$$= \tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{4}$
          $= 3.40\,\text{eV}$

          Substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$:

          $-E_2$$= 3.40 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
          $= 5.4468 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

          Finally $1\,\text{nm} = 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          First

          $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

          and then

          $\lambda_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{5.4468 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}$
          $= 3.6470 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 364.70\,\text{nm}$

          Answer

          $365\,\text{nm}$

          Three significant figures like the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

          Reasonableness

          The energy $3.4\,\text{eV}$ is slightly above the energy of a photon of violet light, so the wavelength is just below the visible range, near ultraviolet. Visible light with a longer wavelength does not release an electron from the shell $n = 2$.

        1. An ultraviolet photon with a wavelength of $97.3\,\text{nm}$ hits a hydrogen atom in the ground state, and the atom absorbs it.

          How many orbitals does the shell to which the electron moves have?

          orbitals
          Answer

          $16$

          Show the solution

          Given

          wavelength of the photon $\lambda = 97.3\,\text{nm}$; atom in the ground state, $n = 1$; $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

          Asked

          the number of orbitals in the shell $n$ to which the electron moves

          Principle

          An atom can absorb a photon only if the energy of the photon is exactly the difference between the energies of two states. The energy of the photon gives the energy of the upper state, $E_n = E_1 + \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, from that the number of the shell $n$, and the shell $n$ has $n^2$ orbitals.

          Formula

          $E_n = E_1 + \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so

          $n$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{-E_n}}$

          and the number of orbitals is $n^2$

          Conversions

          $\lambda$$= 97.3\,\text{nm}$
          $= 97.3 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$

          With

          $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

          the energy of the photon is

          $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$$= \tfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{97.3 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
          $= 2.0416 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J}$

          It is converted to electronvolts by substituting $1\,\text{J} = \tfrac{1}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$:

          $\tfrac{2.0416 \cdot 10^{-18}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$
          $= 12.744\,\text{eV}$

          Substitution

          $E_n$$= -13.6\,\text{eV} + 12.744\,\text{eV}$
          $= -0.856\,\text{eV}$
          $n$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{0.856\,\text{eV}}}$
          $= \sqrt{15.9}$
          $= 3.99$
          $\approx 4$

          so there are $4^2 = 16$ orbitals

          Answer

          $16$

          Orbitals (shell $n = 4$: $4\text{s}$, three $4\text{p}$, five $4\text{d}$ and seven $4\text{f}$); $n$ is an integer, so the rounding $3.99 \to 4$ is reliable.

          Reasonableness

          The energy of the shell $n = 4$, $-\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{16} = -0.85\,\text{eV}$, equals the calculated $-0.856\,\text{eV}$ to the precision of three figures of the wavelength. The energies of the shells $n = 3$ ($-1.51\,\text{eV}$) and $n = 5$ ($-0.54\,\text{eV}$) are clearly farther away, so no other shell fits.

        2. In light atoms, the electrons fill the orbitals in the ground state in the order $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$, $3\text{s}$, $3\text{p}$, and at most two electrons fit in each orbital. In an atom of a certain element the $3\text{p}$ subshell is exactly half full.

          How many electrons does the atom have?

          electrons
          Answer

          $15$

          Show the solution

          Given

          filling order $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$, $3\text{s}$, $3\text{p}$; at most two electrons per orbital; the $3\text{p}$ subshell is half full

          Asked

          the number of electrons $N$ in the atom

          Principle

          In the ground state the electrons fill the orbitals starting from the lowest energy, so electrons go to the $3\text{p}$ subshell only when $1\text{s}$, $2\text{s}$, $2\text{p}$ and $3\text{s}$ are full. An s subshell has one orbital and a p subshell three, so they hold $2$ and $6$ electrons. A half-full $3\text{p}$ subshell contains half of its maximum number.

          Formula

          $N$$= N_\text{1s} + N_\text{2s} + N_\text{2p} + N_\text{3s} + \dfrac{N_\text{3p,max}}{2}$

          Conversions

          None are needed, because a count is asked.

          Substitution

          $N$$= 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + \dfrac{6}{2}$
          $= 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 3$
          $= 15$

          Answer

          $15$

          Electrons (the element is phosphorus, configuration $1\text{s}^2\,2\text{s}^2\,2\text{p}^6\,3\text{s}^2\,3\text{p}^3$); the result is an exact integer.

          Reasonableness

          The full shells $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ hold $2 + 8 = 10$ electrons, and the shell $n = 3$ has $2 + 3 = 5$ electrons, $15$ altogether. In the periodic table the atomic number $15$ is phosphorus, which is in the middle of the third period, as a half-full $3\text{p}$ subshell leads one to expect.

        3. The electron of a hydrogen atom is in the $2\text{s}$ state, and its position is measured $5000$ times. The $90\,\%$ boundary of the $2\text{s}$ orbital is drawn in the picture. On average $5.3\,\%$ of the measurements land inside the nodal sphere, and this inner part belongs practically entirely to the region of the orbital.

          How many measurements do you expect to land in the region of the orbital outside the nodal sphere?

          hits
          Answer

          $4240$

          Show the solution

          Given

          measurements $N = 5000$; boundary of the orbital $90\,\%$; $5.3\,\%$ of the measurements land inside the nodal sphere, and this inner part belongs practically entirely to the region of the orbital

          Asked

          the expected number $k$ of measurements that land in the region of the orbital outside the nodal sphere

          Principle

          The probability $0.90$ of the region of the orbital divides into two parts: the inner part of the nodal sphere and the shell outside the nodal sphere. Since the inner part belongs entirely to the region, the probability of the outer part is the difference $0.90 - 0.053$. In many measurements the share of those landing in a region is approximately the probability of the region.

          Formula

          $k = (p - p_\text{in})\,N$

          Conversions

          $p = 90\,\% = 0.90$ and $p_\text{in} = 5.3\,\% = 0.053$

          Substitution

          $k$$= (0.90 - 0.053) \cdot 5000$
          $= 0.847 \cdot 5000$
          $= 4235$

          Answer

          about $4240$ measurements; the number is an expected value that a real series fulfils only approximately, and the share of the inner part, $5.3\,\%$, limits the precision to about two or three significant figures.

          Reasonableness

          Altogether about $0.90 \cdot 5000 = 4500$ measurements land in the region, and of these about $0.053 \cdot 5000 = 265$ inside the nodal sphere; the difference $4235$ is clearly the largest part. This fits the fact that the most common distance of the $2\text{s}$ electron is outside the nodal sphere.
