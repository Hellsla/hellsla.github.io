## fy3-2-L1-01
**Given:** $F = 80\,\text{N}$ (in the direction of motion), $s = 25\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the force in kilojoules
**Principle:** The pushing force is in the direction of motion and constant, so it does work equal to the product of force and displacement. If the force were at an angle or changed along the way, another method would be needed.
**Formula:** $W = F s$, so the work is already solved for: $W = F s$
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the distance in metres, so the product is directly in joules ($1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$). Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 80\,\text{N} \cdot 25\,\text{m} = 2000\,\text{J} = 2.000\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the given values $80\,\text{N}$ and $25\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Rough estimate: $80\,\text{N} \approx 8\,\text{kg}$ in weight, and $8\,\text{kg}\cdot 10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot 25\,\text{m} = 2000\,\text{J}$. The same work would lift a $20\,\text{kg}$ object about $10\,\text{m}$: $20\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot 10\,\text{m} \approx 2.0\,\text{kJ}$. $W$ is positive because the force is in the direction of motion.

## fy3-2-L1-03
**Given:** work $W = 36\,\text{kJ}$, time $t = 12\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the power $P$ of the motor in kilowatts
**Principle:** Power tells you how fast work is done, that is, work per unit time. The motor does the work steadily in the given time, so the average power is the total work divided by the time.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$, so the power is already solved for: $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$
**Conversions:** $36\,\text{kJ} = 36 \cdot 1000\,\text{J} = 36\,000\,\text{J}$, so that the result comes out in watts ($1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s}$). Finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = \dfrac{36\,000\,\text{J}}{12\,\text{s}} = 3000\,\text{W} = 3.000\,\text{kW}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{kW}$, two significant figures like the time $12\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The units kilojoule and second give kilowatts directly: $\tfrac{36\,\text{kJ}}{12\,\text{s}} = 3\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{s}} = 3\,\text{kW}$. With this power you could lift a load of about $300\,\text{kg}$ at a steady one metre per second: $300\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot 1\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \approx 2.9\,\text{kW}$. This suits the motor of a lift.

## fy3-2-L1-04

**Find:** the total work $W$ done by the force in joules
**Principle:** The work is the area between the $(s,F)$ graph and the $s$-axis. The force is not constant over the whole distance, so the formula $W = F s$ does not apply directly to the whole distance. The area splits into two rectangles in which the force is constant.
**Reading the graph:** The first horizontal segment is on the grid line $F_1 = 25\,\text{N}$ and extends from $s = 0$ to $s = 6\,\text{m}$, where it drops vertically. The second segment is on the line $F_2 = 10\,\text{N}$ and extends from $s = 6\,\text{m}$ to $s = 10\,\text{m}$. The width of the second rectangle is $s_2 = 10\,\text{m} - 6\,\text{m} = 4\,\text{m}$.
**Formula:** $W = W_1 + W_2$, so $W = F_1 s_1 + F_2 s_2$
**Conversions:** The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed: $\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = \text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 25\,\text{N} \cdot 6\,\text{m} + 10\,\text{N} \cdot 4\,\text{m} = 150\,\text{J} + 40\,\text{J} = 190\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $190\,\text{J}$, two significant figures, because the values of the graph were read from the grid lines as whole numbers.
**Reasonableness:** The average force over the whole $10\,\text{m}$ is $\dfrac{190\,\text{J}}{10\,\text{m}} = 19\,\text{N}$, which lies between the smallest ($10\,\text{N}$) and the largest ($25\,\text{N}$) value of the force. If you calculated $25\,\text{N} \cdot 10\,\text{m} = 250\,\text{J}$ for the whole distance, you would get too large a result, because the force drops partway through.

## fy3-2-L2-04
**Given:** force $F_1 = 10\,\text{N}$ constant over the distance $s_1 = 2.0\,\text{m}$ ($0\text{–}2.0\,\text{m}$); then the force increases steadily from $F_1 = 10\,\text{N}$ to $F_2 = 30\,\text{N}$ over the distance $s_2 = 4.0\,\text{m}$ ($2.0\text{–}6.0\,\text{m}$)
**Find:** the total work $W$ in joules
**Principle:** The force changes, so the formula $W = F s$ does not work for the whole distance. The work is the area under the $(s,F)$ graph, and it can be calculated in parts: the constant-force part is a rectangle and the steadily increasing part is a trapezium.
**Reading the graph:** The graph has a horizontal segment at the height $10\,\text{N}$ from $0\ldots 2.0\,\text{m}$ and, continuing from it, a straight segment from the height $10\,\text{N}$ to the height $30\,\text{N}$ from $2.0\ldots 6.0\,\text{m}$. The rectangle has height $10\,\text{N}$ and width $2.0\,\text{m}$. The parallel sides of the trapezium are $10\,\text{N}$ and $30\,\text{N}$, and its height (the width along the $s$-axis) is $4.0\,\text{m}$.
**Formula:** $W = A_\text{rectangle} + A_\text{trapezium}$, so $W = F_1 s_1 + 0.5 \cdot (F_1 + F_2)\, s_2$
**Conversions:** The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $W = 10\,\text{N} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (10\,\text{N} + 30\,\text{N}) \cdot 4.0\,\text{m} = 20\,\text{J} + 80\,\text{J} = 100\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $100\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the given values (for example $2.0\,\text{m}$).
**Reasonableness:** The average force over the whole $6.0\,\text{m}$ is $\dfrac{100\,\text{J}}{6.0\,\text{m}} \approx 17\,\text{N}$, which lies between the smallest ($10\,\text{N}$) and greatest ($30\,\text{N}$) values of the force. Rough bounds: at least $10\,\text{N} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} = 60\,\text{J}$ and at most $30\,\text{N} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} = 180\,\text{J}$, and the result lies between them.

## fy3-2-L3-01
**Given:** peak force $F = 40\,\text{N}$; the force increases from zero to $F$ over the distance $s_1 = 0.50\,\text{m}$, stays constant over the distance $s_2 = 1.0\,\text{m}$ and decreases to zero over the distance $s_3 = 0.50\,\text{m}$; total distance $s = 2.0\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the average magnitude of the force with respect to distance, $F_\text{avg}$, in newtons
**Principle:** The force changes, so the work is the area under the $(s,F)$ graph. The average force is the constant force that would do the same work over the same distance, so $W = F_\text{avg}\, s$.
**Reading the graph:** The graph is a trapezium: a rising triangle ($0\ldots 0.50\,\text{m}$, height $40\,\text{N}$), a horizontal part as a rectangle ($0.50\ldots 1.5\,\text{m}$, height $40\,\text{N}$) and a falling triangle ($1.5\ldots 2.0\,\text{m}$, height $40\,\text{N}$). The work is the sum of these three areas.
**Formula:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2} F s_1 + F s_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} F s_3$ and $W = F_\text{avg}\, s$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{W}{s}$
**Conversions:** The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 40\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} + 40\,\text{N} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 40\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} = 10\,\text{J} + 40\,\text{J} + 10\,\text{J} = 60\,\text{J}$; $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{60\,\text{J}}{2.0\,\text{m}} = 30\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $30\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the distance $2.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The average is smaller than the peak value $40\,\text{N}$, because the force is small at the beginning and at the end, but greater than half of the peak, because the force is at its peak for half of the distance. The trapezium formula gives the same work: $0.5 \cdot (2.0\,\text{m} + 1.0\,\text{m}) \cdot 40\,\text{N} = 60\,\text{J}$. The average of a force increasing steadily from zero would be $20\,\text{N}$.

## fy3-2-L3-02

**Substitution:** $W_1 = 2.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} = 29.43\,\text{J}$; $\Delta U = 20 \cdot 29.43\,\text{J} = 588.6\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $590\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and the height $1.5\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Rough estimate: the weight is about $20\,\text{N}$, so one drop gives about $20\,\text{N} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} = 30\,\text{J}$ and 20 drops about $600\,\text{J}$. The result is the same as lifting an object of about $60\,\text{kg}$ by a metre, a small amount of energy, so the temperature of the water changed only a little and the measurement needed accuracy. That is why Joule repeated the drops many times.

$$\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum t &= 8.1\,\text{s} + 7.8\,\text{s} + 8.4\,\text{s} \\ &= 24.3\,\text{s} \\ t_\text{avg} &= \dfrac{24.3\,\text{s}}{3} = 8.1\,\text{s} \\ h &= 18 \cdot 0.17\,\text{m} = 3.06\,\text{m} \\ W &= 62\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 3.06\,\text{m} \\ &= 1861.2\,\text{J} \\ P &= \dfrac{1861.2\,\text{J}}{8.1\,\text{s}} \\ &= 229.8\,\text{W} \end{aligned}$$
**Answer:** $230\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the mass $62\,\text{kg}$ and the time $8.1\,\text{s}$.

The range of the three time measurements $7.8\ldots 8.4\,\text{s}$ corresponds to a power of $222\ldots 239\,\text{W}$, so the result $230\,\text{W}$ is within this range. The power of a person climbing stairs quickly is hundreds of watts.

## fy3-2-L3-04
**Given:** from the table $(s, F)$: $(2.0\,\text{cm};\ 1.9\,\text{N})$, $(4.0\,\text{cm};\ 4.2\,\text{N})$, $(6.0\,\text{cm};\ 5.8\,\text{N})$, $(8.0\,\text{cm};\ 8.1\,\text{N})$, $(10.0\,\text{cm};\ 10.0\,\text{N})$; extension $s_\text{max} = 10.0\,\text{cm}$; mass to be lifted $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** a) how the force depends on the extension, b) the work $W$ done in stretching, c) the lifting height $h$ in centimetres
**Principle:** The force changes with the extension, so the work is the area under the $(s,F)$ graph. If the force is proportional to the extension, the graph is a straight line through the origin and the area is a triangle. Energy is conserved without losses: the work done in stretching the spring can be used to lift an object slowly, so the lifting work $m g h$ is equal to $W$.
**Reading the graph:** The force grows with the extension so that the quotient $\tfrac{F}{s}$ is about $1.0\,\text{N/cm}$ at all points: for example $\tfrac{1.9}{2.0} = 0.95$ and $\tfrac{10.0}{10.0} = 1.0$. Because of measurement uncertainty the measured points are usually not exactly on one line, so a line through the origin is fitted among them; the origin belongs on the line, because the extension is measured from the free length. On the line, at $s = 10.0\,\text{cm}$ the force is $F = 10.0\,\text{N}$. Because the force grows steadily from zero, the graph is a straight line through the origin and the area under it is a triangle; therefore the work is the area of a triangle whose base is $s$ and whose height is $F$.
**Formula:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2} F s$ and $W = m g h$, so $h = \dfrac{W}{m g}$
**Conversions:** $10.0\,\text{cm} = 0.100\,\text{m}$, so that the work comes out in joules. Finally $h$ is converted to centimetres: $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:**

$$\begin{aligned} W &= 0.5 \cdot 10.0\,\text{N} \cdot 0.100\,\text{m} \\ &= 0.500\,\text{J} \\ h &= \dfrac{0.500\,\text{J}}{0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}} = 0.2548\,\text{m} \\ &= 25.48\,\text{cm} \end{aligned}$$

**Reasonableness:** If the force were $10.0\,\text{N}$ over the whole distance, the work would be $10.0\,\text{N} \cdot 0.100\,\text{m} = 1.0\,\text{J}$; the work of the triangle is half of this, because the average force is $5.0\,\text{N}$. $$\begin{aligned} G &= 450\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \\ &= 4414.5\,\text{N} \\ P &= 4414.5\,\text{N} \cdot 0.815\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 3598\,\text{W} \\ &= 3.598\,\text{kW} \\ P t &= 3598\,\text{W} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s} \\ &= 28\,780\,\text{J} \\ m g h &= 4414.5\,\text{N} \cdot 6.5\,\text{m} \\ &= 28\,690\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$
