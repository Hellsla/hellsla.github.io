Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model

Why does the light of a lamp containing hydrogen split on a grating into just four clearly visible coloured lines, and why exactly at the wavelengths $656\,\text{nm}$, $486\,\text{nm}$, $434\,\text{nm}$ and $410\,\text{nm}$?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the energy and wavelength of the photon produced in a transition from the energy levels of the hydrogen atom.
  • explain why hydrogen emits and absorbs only certain wavelengths.
  • evaluate the successes and shortcomings of the Bohr model in the development of atomic models.

Prior knowledge

1Think about it

In a darkened classroom a thin glass tube containing hydrogen gas glows pinkish violet when a voltage is connected to it. When the light is viewed through a grating, instead of a smooth rainbow only four narrow lines are seen: red, cyan, blue and violet. The positions of the lines are always the same, whichever laboratory the tube is in.

What in the hydrogen atom decides that it emits exactly these four wavelengths, and can they be calculated?

Optional Recall: the energy of a photon
The hydrogen spectrum has a red line at $656\,\text{nm}$ and a violet line at $410\,\text{nm}$. Which statement about their photons is correct?

  • The same atom can emit photons of different energies. The energy of a photon depends on its frequency: $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so the violet photon, with the shorter wavelength, has the greater energy.

  • Correct. $E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon): the energy of a red photon is about $1.89\,\text{eV}$ and that of a violet photon about $3.02\,\text{eV}$. Today we find out where these energies come from.

  • A longer wavelength means a lower frequency and a lower energy: $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. The energy of a red photon is about $1.89\,\text{eV}$ and that of a violet photon about $3.02\,\text{eV}$.

How the calculation works

In emission the energy and wavelength of the photon are

$$hf = E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}},$$

where the energy difference is converted to joules by multiplying by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}$. In absorption the model looks for a level $m > n_\text{l}$ for which $E_m - E_\text{l} = hf$. If $hf \geq -E_\text{l}$, the electron is freed, and its kinetic energy is $E_\text{k} = hf + E_\text{l}$.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    The energy of a hydrogen atom is quantised

    A hydrogen atom has one proton and one electron. The energy of the atom, the sum of the electron's kinetic energy and its electric potential energy, can have only certain values, the energy levels. The levels are numbered with an integer $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ The zero of energy is chosen to be the situation in which the electron is at rest far from the proton, that is, free of the atom. The energy of a bound electron is therefore negative: freeing the electron requires energy.

    The lowest level $n = 1$ is the ground state, with an energy of $-13.6\,\text{eV}$. The higher levels $n = 2, 3, \ldots$ are excited states. They crowd together upwards and approach zero. The energy needed to free the electron from the ground state is the ionisation energy of hydrogen, $13.6\,\text{eV}$. An atom usually stays in an excited state for only about $10^{-8}\,\text{s}$ and then returns to a lower level.

    A photon is created or disappears in a transition

    When an electron moves from a higher level to a lower one, the energy of the atom decreases and the difference leaves as a single photon. Energy is conserved, so the energy of the photon is the difference between the levels. Since there are only certain levels, there are also only certain differences: hydrogen emits only certain wavelengths, and its spectrum is a line spectrum.

    Transitions to the same lower level form a series. The transitions ending on level $n = 1$ (the Lyman series) are big jumps in energy, and their photons are ultraviolet. The transitions ending on level $n = 2$ (the Balmer series) produce the four bright visible lines of hydrogen; the following lines ($397\,\text{nm}$, $389\,\text{nm}$, …) are faint and lie at the violet edge of visible light, where the eye is insensitive. The transitions ending on level $n = 3$ (the Paschen series) are infrared.

    Absorption is the reverse process. An atom absorbs a photon only if the energy of the photon is exactly the difference between some pair of levels: the electron then moves to the higher level. A photon of any other energy passes the atom without being absorbed. The exception is ionisation: a photon whose energy is at least the ionisation energy can free the electron, and the excess energy stays as the kinetic energy of the free electron.

    The Bohr model as a historical stage

    In 1913 the Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed a model in which the electron circles the proton on a circular orbit, but only on certain allowed orbits. On an allowed orbit the electron does not radiate, although according to classical physics a circling charge should lose energy as radiation. Light is produced only when the electron moves from one orbit to another, and the energy of the photon is the difference between the energies of the orbits. The radius of the orbit grows as $n^2$: the radius of the ground state is about $0.053\,\text{nm}$, and the radius of orbit $n = 2$ is four times and that of orbit $n = 3$ nine times as large.

    The model was a great step forward. The hydrogen energy levels obtained from it are the same as those measured from the spectral lines, and it explained the size of the hydrogen atom and its ionisation energy. The model has its limits, however. It works only for atoms and ions with a single electron, and it does not tell why some spectral lines are brighter than others. In reality the electron does not have an orbit either: in the chapter The wave-mechanical model of the atom and orbitals the position of the electron is described with probabilities. The energy levels remain in the new model, but the orbits are dropped.

    Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the photon of a transition

    The energy levels of the hydrogen atom are

    $$E_n = -\frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$$

    where $E_n$ is the energy of level $n$ and $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ is the number of the level. The expression is given information here, and it corresponds to the measured hydrogen spectrum.

    When an electron moves from a level with energy $E_\text{u}$ to a lower level with energy $E_\text{l}$, the energy and wavelength of the photon produced are

    $$\begin{aligned} hf &= E_\text{u} - E_\text{l} \\ \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}} \end{aligned}$$

    where $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ is the Planck constant, $f$ the frequency of the photon, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ the speed of light and $\lambda$ the wavelength. In absorption the same formulas give the photon that raises the electron from level $E_\text{l}$ to level $E_\text{u}$. The energies are first calculated in electron volts, and the difference is converted to joules by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

    Lyman $n = 1$ ultraviolet $91.2$–$122$
    Balmer $n = 2$ visible and near UV $365$–$656$
    Paschen $n = 3$ infrared $821$–$1876$

    The longest wavelength of each series comes from the transition from the nearest upper level, and the shortest, the limit of the series, from the transition that starts from a free electron. Since the levels crowd together upwards, the lines of a series crowd together towards the limit.

    Common misconception

    Many calculate the energy of a photon from the energy of a single level, for example for the transition $4 \to 2$ from the value $|E_4| = 0.85\,\text{eV}$. The wavelength would then come out as about $1460\,\text{nm}$, although the transition produces the cyan line $486\,\text{nm}$. A photon carries not the energy of a level but the difference between levels $E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$. The negative energy of a level does not mean that energy is missing either: it tells how much energy freeing the electron requires.

    Another mistake is to take the Bohr orbits as real orbits on which the electron circles like a planet. The Bohr model gives the right energies for hydrogen, but the orbit of the electron cannot be observed, and in the present atomic model it does not exist.

    Summary

    • The energy of a hydrogen atom is quantised: $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$. The ground state is $n = 1$, the excited states are $n \geq 2$, and the ionisation energy from the ground state is $13.6\,\text{eV}$.
    • In a transition from a higher level to a lower one a photon is produced whose energy is the difference between the levels: $hf = E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$ and $\lambda = \tfrac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}}$.
    • An atom absorbs only a photon whose energy is the difference between levels, or is ionised by a photon whose energy is at least the ionisation energy.
    • The transitions ending on level $n = 2$ (the Balmer series) give the visible lines of hydrogen, $656\,\text{nm}$, $486\,\text{nm}$, $434\,\text{nm}$ and $410\,\text{nm}$; those ending on level $n = 1$ are ultraviolet.
    • The Bohr model (1913) explained the spectrum of hydrogen with allowed orbits, but it works only for atoms with one electron. The wave-mechanical model keeps the energy levels and rejects the orbits.

      Worked example

      Problem

      The red line of a hydrogen lamp is produced when the electron of a hydrogen atom moves from level $n = 3$ to level $n = 2$. Calculate the wavelength of the photon produced.

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. Upper level $n = 3$, lower level $n = 2$, energies of the levels $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ and $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. The wavelength $\lambda$ of the photon is asked.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Energy is conserved: the energy of the atom decreases by the difference between the levels, and exactly this energy is carried away by one photon. The energy of a photon is $hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The energy of the photon is written as the difference between the levels and the wavelength is solved:

      $$\begin{aligned} \frac{hc}{\lambda} &= E_3 - E_2 \\ \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_3 - E_2} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The energies of the levels are in electron volts, but $h$ is in $\text{J}\,\text{s}$. The energy difference is therefore converted to joules by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First the energy difference is calculated, and then the wavelength:

      $$\begin{aligned} &E_3 - E_2 \\ &= -\frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{9} + \frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{4} \\ &= 1.8889\,\text{eV} \\ &= 1.8889 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \\ &= 3.0260 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \\[6pt] hc &= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \\ &\quad \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s} \\ &= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m} \\[6pt] \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_3 - E_2} \\ &= \frac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{3.0260 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \\ &= 6.5647 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The wavelength is $\lambda \approx 6.56 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m} = 656\,\text{nm}$. The result is given to three significant figures, because the energy of the ground state, $13.6\,\text{eV}$, is known to three figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The wavelength $656\,\text{nm}$ is red light, and exactly such a line is seen in the hydrogen spectrum. The photon energy $1.89\,\text{eV}$ lies within the range of visible-light photons, $1.7$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$. The smallest energy difference of the Balmer series gives the longest wavelength of the series, as it should.

      Optional Find the error
      A student calculates the wavelength of the hydrogen transition $4 \to 2$ like this:

      $$\begin{aligned} E_4 &= -\frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{16} = -0.850\,\text{eV} \\ \lambda &= \frac{hc}{0.850\,\text{eV}} \approx 1460\,\text{nm} \end{aligned}$$

      The student concludes that the transition produces infrared radiation. Where is the error?

      • Correct. The energy of the photon is $E_4 - E_2 = -0.850\,\text{eV} + 3.40\,\text{eV} = 2.55\,\text{eV}$, and $\lambda = \tfrac{hc}{2.55\,\text{eV}} = 486\,\text{nm}$, the cyan line of visible light. The energy of level $n = 2$ cannot be left out, because the transition ends on it.

      • The conversion is necessary when $h$ is in $\text{J}\,\text{s}$, but it does not fix this error: even converted correctly, $0.850\,\text{eV}$ gives $1460\,\text{nm}$. The error is in which energy is substituted.

      • The expression divides by the square of $n$: $E_4 = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{16} = -0.850\,\text{eV}$ is correct. The value $-3.40\,\text{eV}$ is the energy of level $n = 2$.

      • The transition $4 \to 3$ is infrared ($1876\,\text{nm}$), but the transition $4 \to 2$ is visible light ($486\,\text{nm}$) and $4 \to 1$ is ultraviolet ($97\,\text{nm}$). The wavelength depends on both levels.

      Given

      atom in state $n = 4$, $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, wavelength of the light $\lambda = 656\,\text{nm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ and $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

      Asked

      the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ of the freed electron in electron volts

      Principle

      In ionisation the electron rises from the level $E_4$ to the energy zero, and since a free electron has no levels, the energy of the photon does not have to be a difference between levels; the excess stays as kinetic energy. Energy is conserved, so $hf + E_4 = E_\text{k}$.

      Formula

      $hf + E_4 = E_\text{k}$ and $hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so

      $$E_\text{k} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} + E_4$$

      Conversions

      $\lambda$$= 656\,\text{nm}$
      $= 656 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$

      the energy of the photon is converted from joules to electron volts by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

      $hf$$= \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{656 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
      $= 3.0282 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

      $= -0.850\,\text{eV}$

      $= 1.0402\,\text{eV}$

      $1.04\,\text{eV}$

      Three significant figures, as in the wavelength $656\,\text{nm}$ and the value $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

      The kinetic energy is positive and smaller than the energy of the photon, $1.89\,\text{eV}$, as it should be.       1. Concept

      The energy of the ground state of the hydrogen atom is $-13.6\,\text{eV}$. What does the negative sign mean?

      • Correct. The zero of energy is a free electron at rest. The energy of a bound electron is lower than this, and the difference $13.6\,\text{eV}$ is the ionisation energy.

      • Kinetic energy cannot be negative. The energy of a level is the sum of kinetic and potential energy relative to a free electron, and it is negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus.

      • The sign of the energy does not come from the charge. It comes from the choice of zero: the energy of a free electron is zero, and the energy of a bound electron is lower than that.

      • A negative value does not mean a shortage but the chosen zero: energy is measured from a free electron. The ground state is a stable state, not a deficient one.

      Optional 3. The Bohr model

      The Bohr model is called a stage in the history of atomic models.

      What did the model explain correctly, and why has it been given up? Answer in three or four sentences.

      Model answer

      The Bohr model explained why hydrogen emits and absorbs only certain wavelengths: the electron can only be on allowed orbits, and the energy of a photon is the difference between the energies of the orbits.           $f_\text{ion}$$= \dfrac{2.4209 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}$
          $= 3.653 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$
          $= 365.3\,\text{THz}$

          Answer

          $365\,\text{THz}$

          That is $3.65 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, three significant figures, as in the value $13.6\,\text{eV}$; the uncertainty of the wavelengths, $\pm 1\,\text{nm}$, is less than $0.1\,\%$. The limit corresponds to the wavelength $\tfrac{c}{f_\text{ion}} \approx 821\,\text{nm}$, which is near infrared.

          Reasonableness

          The threshold wavelength $821\,\text{nm}$ is shorter than any of the observed lines ($1094$–$1876\,\text{nm}$), as it should be for a series: the shortest possible wavelength of a series corresponds to a transition from an infinitely high level. The line $4 \to 3$ is the longest of the series and has the smallest energy, $0.661\,\text{eV}$, so this also agrees.

        5. Exercise 3-20

          Hydrogen gas in the ground state is illuminated with monochromatic ultraviolet light. Each atom absorbs one photon and is excited to the same higher level, from which it returns to the ground state emitting photons either directly or through intermediate levels. The energy levels of the hydrogen atom are $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$. The table shows the lines of the light emitted by the gas; the light of the source itself has been filtered out before the measurement. The uncertainty of the wavelengths is at most $1\,\text{nm}$ ($h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$).

          a) Calculate the energies of the photons of the lines and identify the initial and final level of each line.

          b) Deduce to which level the atoms were excited. Explain why the excitation level cannot be higher.

          c) Calculate the wavelength of the ultraviolet source in nanometres.

          Enter in the box the result of part c).

          Emission lines of the gas (example data)
          Line$\lambda\,(\text{nm})$
          a102.6
          b121.6
          c486.1
          d656.3
          e1875
          nm
          Answer

          $97.3\,\text{nm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          lines $\lambda = 102.6$; $121.6$; $486.1$; $656.3$ and $1875\,\text{nm}$ ($\pm 1\,\text{nm}$), atoms in the ground state, $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

          Asked

          the wavelength $\lambda_\text{L}$ of the ultraviolet source in nanometres

          Principle

          The energy of the photon of each line is the difference between levels, and energy is conserved. Once the lines have been identified, the highest initial level is the level to which the atoms were excited; the photon raised the atom from the ground state to this level, so the energy of the photon of the source is the difference between its energy and that of the ground state. If the excitation level were higher, the gas would also emit lines starting from it.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_\text{L}}$$= E_\text{u} - E_1$

          so

          $\lambda_\text{L}$$= \dfrac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_1}$

          Conversions

          The energies of the photons are converted from joules to electron volts by dividing by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$; finally $1\,\text{m} = 10^{9}\,\text{nm}$.

          Substitution

          The product $hc = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$. The energies of the lines are $12.09$; $10.20$; $2.551$; $1.889$ and $0.661\,\text{eV}$. They correspond to the differences between pairs of levels $E_3 - E_1 = 12.09\,\text{eV}$, $E_2 - E_1 = 10.20\,\text{eV}$, $E_4 - E_2 = 2.55\,\text{eV}$, $E_3 - E_2 = 1.889\,\text{eV}$ and $E_4 - E_3 = 0.661\,\text{eV}$. So the lines are the transitions $3 \to 1$, $2 \to 1$, $4 \to 2$, $3 \to 2$ and $4 \to 3$. The highest initial level is $n = 4$. From level $n = 5$ there would be lines $\lambda = 95.0$; $434$; $1282$ or $4052\,\text{nm}$, but none of them is in the table, so the atoms were not excited higher than level $n = 4$. The direct transition $4 \to 1$ emits the wavelength of the source, $97.3\,\text{nm}$, which was filtered out, so it is not in the table. The energy of the photon of the source is

          $E_4 - E_1$$= 13.6\,\text{eV} - \dfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{16}$
          $= 12.75\,\text{eV}$
          $= 12.75 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
          $= 2.0426 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J}$
          $\lambda_\text{L}$$= \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{2.0426 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J}}$
          $= 9.725 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{m}$
          $= 97.25\,\text{nm}$

          Answer

          $97.3\,\text{nm}$

          Three significant figures, as in the value $13.6\,\text{eV}$.

          Reasonableness

          The energy of the photon of the source, $12.75\,\text{eV}$, is smaller than the ionisation energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$, so the atoms are not ionised, and the wavelength is shorter than the shortest observed line, $102.6\,\text{nm}$. The sum of the energies of the photons of the cascade $4 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1$ is $0.661\,\text{eV} + 1.889\,\text{eV} + 10.20\,\text{eV} = 12.75\,\text{eV}$, so on returning exactly the absorbed energy is released.
