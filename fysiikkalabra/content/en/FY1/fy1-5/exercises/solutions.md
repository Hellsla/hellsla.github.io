## fy1-5-L1-01
**Given:** speed $v = 5.5\,\text{m/s}$, time $t = 20\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the zero point, so $s_0 = 0$
**Find:** distance travelled $s$ in metres
**Principle:** The speed is constant, so the motion is uniform, and the distance is directly proportional to time.
**Formula:** $s = s_0 + vt$, and since $s_0 = 0$, $s = vt$
**Conversions:** None needed: the speed is in m/s and the time in seconds, so the product is in metres.
**Substitution:** $s = 5.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 110\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $110\,\text{m}$, two significant figures as in the speed $5.5\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** About $5\,\text{m}$ every second for twenty seconds is about $5 \cdot 20 = 100\,\text{m}$, and the unit $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m}$ is the unit of distance.

## fy1-5-L1-02

**Find:** speed $v$ in m/s
**Principle:** In uniform motion, the speed is the distance travelled divided by the time it took.
**Formula:** $s = vt$, so $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{360\,\text{m}}{45\,\text{s}} = 8.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
**Answer:** $8.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the time $45\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check backwards: $8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 45\,\text{s} = 360\,\text{m}$. The speed corresponds to about $29\,\text{km/h}$, which matches the pace of a good runner in a 400-metre race.

## fy1-5-L1-03
**Given:** a graph in which the position $s$ of a walker as a function of time $t$ is a straight line from the origin; the motion continues uniformly beyond the graph; distance $s = 150\,\text{m}$
**Find:** time $t$ in seconds when the distance is $150\,\text{m}$
**Principle:** A straight line from the origin means uniform motion, and the slope of the line is the speed. When the speed is known, the time beyond the graph is obtained by dividing the distance by the speed.
**Reading the graph:** The line passes through the grid crossing where $t_1 = 0\,\text{s}$ and $s_1 = 0\,\text{m}$ (the origin), and through the crossing where $t_2 = 8\,\text{s}$ and $s_2 = 20\,\text{m}$. Other crossings are for example $(4\,\text{s};\,10\,\text{m})$ and $(10\,\text{s};\,25\,\text{m})$. The reading is exact from the grid, so an answer that deviates by at most $3\,\%$ is accepted.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$ and $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{20\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{8\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} = 2.5\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$ and $t = \dfrac{150\,\text{m}}{2.5\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}} = 60\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $60\,\text{s}$, that is one minute; the speed was read with two significant figures, so the answer is given with the same precision.
**Reasonableness:** The speed $2.5\,\text{m/s}$ is about $9\,\text{km/h}$, a brisk walk or a slow jog. In the graph $10\,\text{s}$ takes $25\,\text{m}$, so $150\,\text{m}$, a distance six times as long, takes six times as long, $60\,\text{s}$.

Part 2: $v_2 = \dfrac{60\,\text{m} - 30\,\text{m}}{40\,\text{s} - 10\,\text{s}} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$. Position at $35\,\text{s}$: $s = 30\,\text{m} + 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 25\,\text{s} = 55\,\text{m}$. Average speed: $v_\text{avg} = \dfrac{55\,\text{m} - 15\,\text{m}}{35\,\text{s} - 5.0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{40\,\text{m}}{30\,\text{s}} = 1.33\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$.
**Answer:** $1.3\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the data. The mean of the speeds of the parts would be $(3.0 + 1.0)\,\text{m/s} : 2 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, but that would be wrong, because the parts last for different times: the speed $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ is in effect for only $5.0\,\text{s}$ and the speed $1.0\,\text{m/s}$ for as long as $25\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The partial distances are $3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 15\,\text{m}$ and $1.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 25\,\text{s} = 25\,\text{m}$, together $40\,\text{m}$, the same as above. The average speed $1.3\,\text{m/s}$ is closer to the slower speed $1.0\,\text{m/s}$ than to the faster speed $3.0\,\text{m/s}$, as it should be, because most of the time is spent riding slowly.

## fy1-5-L3-05
**Given:** imaging interval $\Delta t = 0.010\,\text{s}$, positions of the bullet $s = 2.0\,\text{m}$, $5.0\,\text{m}$, $8.0\,\text{m}$ and $11.0\,\text{m}$ at the moments $0.000\,\text{s}$, $0.010\,\text{s}$, $0.020\,\text{s}$ and $0.030\,\text{s}$; scale of the drawing: $1.0\,\text{s}$ is $10\,\text{cm}$ and $1.0\,\text{m}$ is $1.0\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** speed $v$ of the bullet in m/s, and the rise of the graph and the length of the imaging interval in centimetres
**Principle:** The position grows by the same amount in every imaging interval ($3.0\,\text{m}$), so the motion is uniform and the speed is the slope of the graph, $\Delta s : \Delta t$. An object has only one position at every moment, so every value of time corresponds to one value of position. A vertical line would have $\Delta t = 0$ and $\Delta s \neq 0$, and then the quotient would not be any number.
**Reading the graph:** The points of the table lie on the same straight line, and between two consecutive points the position changes by $\Delta s = 5.0\,\text{m} - 2.0\,\text{m} = 3.0\,\text{m}$ in the time $\Delta t = 0.010\,\text{s}$.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$; in the drawing, the length is the value times the scale
**Conversions:** Not needed for calculating the speed. In the drawing, $\Delta s = 3.0 \cdot 1.0\,\text{cm} = 3.0\,\text{cm}$ and $\Delta t = 0.010 \cdot 10\,\text{cm} = 0.10\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{3.0\,\text{m}}{0.010\,\text{s}} = 300\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$. In one imaging interval the line rises $3.0\,\text{cm}$ and advances horizontally only $0.10\,\text{cm}$, that is, $1.0\,\text{mm}$.
**Answer:** $300\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the difference of positions $3.0\,\text{m}$. The line rises $3.0\,\text{cm}$ and advances $0.10\,\text{cm}$, so it is about $30$ times as steep as it is wide and looks almost vertical. It is still not vertical: the imaging interval always takes a time $\Delta t > 0$, and the bullet is at exactly one position at every moment. A vertical line would require the bullet to be at two positions at the same moment, that is, the speed would be infinite.
**Reasonableness:** The speed $300\,\text{m/s}$ is of the same order of magnitude as the speed of sound $343\,\text{m/s}$ and suits a fast bullet. Check: $300\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.010\,\text{s} = 3.0\,\text{m}$, the same as in the table. A stopped object would be drawn as a horizontal line, because $\Delta s = 0$.

## fy1-5-L2-08
**Find:** which of the four graphs shows the position $s$ of the walker as a function of time $t$, when she first stands at $6.0\,\text{m}$ and then walks steadily at a speed of $1.0\,\text{m/s}$ back to the starting point.
**Principle:** The height of a $(t, s)$ graph is the position, and the slope $\Delta s : \Delta t$ is the speed. When an object is at rest, $\Delta s = 0$ and the line is horizontal. In uniform motion the line is straight; if the position increases, the line goes up, and if the position decreases, the line goes down. The speed is the steepness of the line.
**Model answer:** The correct one is graph C. At first the position is $s = 6.0\,\text{m}$ all the time, so the line is horizontal at this height. The way back of $6.0\,\text{m}$ takes $\Delta t = 6.0\,\text{m} : 1.0\,\text{m/s} = 6.0\,\text{s}$, so the walker is at the starting point at the time $4.0\,\text{s} + 6.0\,\text{s} = 10\,\text{s}$. The position decreases, so the line goes down, and its steepness matches the speed $6.0\,\text{m} : 6.0\,\text{s} = 1.0\,\text{m/s}$. In graph A the line goes up, so the position would increase and the walker would move away. Graph B is a $(t, v)$ graph, which shows the speed and not the position. Graph D shows the distance walked $\Delta s$, which starts from zero although the position of the walker is $6.0\,\text{m}$ at the start.
**Reading the graph:** From the graphs we read where the line starts at the time $t = 0$, whether it is horizontal in the interval $0\,\text{s} - 4.0\,\text{s}$ and whether it then goes up or down. We also check the quantity on the vertical axis: in graph B it is the speed $v$. Only in graph C does the line start from $6.0\,\text{m}$, stay there until $4.0\,\text{s}$ and then go down to the point $(10\,\text{s};\ 0\,\text{m})$.
