## fy3-3-L1-01
**Given:** $\theta = 21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** temperature in kelvins, $T$
**Principle:** A kelvin and a degree Celsius are the same size, and the scales differ only in their zero point: $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is $273.15\,\text{K}$. So you get the reading in kelvins by adding $273.15$ to the Celsius reading.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{T}{\text{K}} = \tfrac{\theta}{{}^\circ\text{C}} + 273.15$, and this is already solved for the quantity to find, $T$
**Conversions:** The Celsius temperature is converted to kelvins in this formula; no other unit conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $T = (21 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 294.15\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $294\,\text{K}$, in whole kelvins, because the temperature $21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is given in whole degrees.
**Reasonableness:** Room temperature is about $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the estimate is $20 + 273 \approx 293\,\text{K}$, and the result is close to it. Check by converting back: $294.15 - 273.15 = 21$, so the conversion returns the starting value. A temperature in kelvins is positive, as it should be.

## fy3-3-L1-02
**Given:** $T = 350\,\text{K}$
**Find:** temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta$
**Principle:** The intervals of the Kelvin and Celsius scales are the same size, and the difference between the zero points is $273.15$. So you get the Celsius reading by subtracting $273.15$ from the reading in kelvins.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{T}{\text{K}} = \tfrac{\theta}{{}^\circ\text{C}} + 273.15$, so $\tfrac{\theta}{{}^\circ\text{C}} = \tfrac{T}{\text{K}} - 273.15$
**Conversions:** The temperature in kelvins is converted to degrees Celsius with this formula; no other unit conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\theta = (350 - 273.15)\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 76.85\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $77\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, in whole degrees, because $350\,\text{K}$ is given in whole kelvins.
**Reasonableness:** The boiling point of water at normal pressure is $373\,\text{K}$, or $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and $350\,\text{K}$ is $23\,\text{K}$ below it, so the estimate is $100 - 23 = 77\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result is also above zero, because $350\,\text{K} > 273\,\text{K}$. Check by converting back: $76.85 + 273.15 = 350$.

**Substitution:** $\Delta T = (45 - 20)\,\text{K} = 25\,\text{K}$

## fy3-3-L1-06
**Find:** whether the gas in a sealed cylinder is a closed or an open system.
**Principle:** A system is closed if no matter can leave it and none can enter. Energy can still cross the boundary. The piston is part of the boundary of the system: when it moves, the volume changes, but no matter crosses the boundary.
**Model answer:** The gas is a closed system, because no matter crosses its boundary, even though energy can be transferred and the volume can change. A system would become open only if matter could get out or in, for example if the cylinder leaked. Heating and compression change the state variables, that is, the temperature, the pressure and the volume, but they do not make the system open. The temperature of a closed system is not constant either.

## fy3-3-L1-08
**Find:** which quantity of the gas stays the same when a closed rigid container is heated.
**Principle:** The walls of a rigid container do not move, so the volume of the gas is the volume of the container and stays the same. Heating raises the temperature of the gas, and the average kinetic energy of the particles grows. The particles hit the wall more often and harder, so the pressure rises.
**Model answer:** The volume stays the same, because the walls of a rigid container do not move when it is heated. The temperature and the pressure rise. The average kinetic energy of the particles grows with the temperature, because in a dilute gas it is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins. The container being closed only means that the number of particles, that is, the amount of substance, stays the same. The container does not prevent warming or the rise in pressure.

## fy3-3-L2-02
**Given:** $(\theta_1,\, p_1) = (0\,{}^\circ\text{C},\ 90.0\,\text{kPa})$ and $(\theta_2,\, p_2) = (100\,{}^\circ\text{C},\ 123\,\text{kPa})$; the pressure depends linearly on the temperature
**Find:** pressure $p$ at the temperature $\theta = 35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ in kilopascals
**Principle:** The container is closed and rigid, so the amount of gas and the volume stay the same and the pressure changes only with the temperature. The problem allows a linear model, so the points determine a straight line, and you read the pressure at $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ from the line.
**Formula:** slope $k = \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$ and line $p = p_1 + k\theta$, because $\theta_1 = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; so $p = p_1 + \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}\,\theta$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed: the pressures are in the same unit, and the temperature difference $\theta_2 - \theta_1$ is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins. The unit of the slope is $\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{(123 - 90.0)\,\text{kPa}}{(100 - 0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}} = 0.33\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$, and $p = 90.0\,\text{kPa} + 0.33\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot 35\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 101.55\,\text{kPa}$
**Answer:** $102\,\text{kPa}$. The pressure difference $\Delta p = 123 - 90.0 = 33\,\text{kPa}$ has two figures, so $k = 0.33\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$ and $k\theta \approx 12\,\text{kPa}$; the sum $90.0 + 12 = 102\,\text{kPa}$ is rounded to whole kilopascals.
**Reasonableness:** The temperature $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is $35\,\%$ of the way from $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the pressure must be $35\,\%$ of the way through the range $90.0\ldots123\,\text{kPa}$: $90.0 + 0.35 \cdot 33 = 101.55\,\text{kPa}$. The result is between the pressures of the points and below the mean of the range, $106.5\,\text{kPa}$.

**Reasonableness:** All the differences are positive, so the mean must lie between the smallest and the largest difference: $1.3 < 1.5 < 1.7$. The corrected reading is lower than the reading of the thermometer, because the thermometer reads too high, and $37.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is a normal bath water temperature.

**Find:** final temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta_2$
**Principle:** According to the task, the average kinetic energy of the particles of the gas is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins; this is so in a dilute gas whose particles interact with each other only when they collide. The proportionality applies to the temperature in kelvins, because only the zero of the Kelvin scale is absolute zero. When the energy is halved, the temperature in kelvins is also halved. You must not halve the Celsius temperature.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{E_2}{E_1} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so $T_2 = \dfrac{T_1}{2}$, and $\tfrac{\theta_2}{{}^\circ\text{C}} = \tfrac{T_2}{\text{K}} - 273.15$
**Conversions:** The initial temperature is converted to kelvins: $T_1 = (20.0 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 293.15\,\text{K}$. At the end $T_2$ is converted back to degrees Celsius by subtracting $273.15$.
**Substitution:** $T_2 = \dfrac{293.15\,\text{K}}{2} = 146.575\,\text{K}$, and $\theta_2 = (146.575 - 273.15)\,{}^\circ\text{C} = -126.575\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $-127\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, three significant figures like the value $20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Reasonableness:** The gas must cool, so the result is below $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and it is above absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Check: $\tfrac{146.575\,\text{K}}{293.15\,\text{K}} = 0.5$. Halving the Celsius temperature would give $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, but then the temperature in kelvins would be only $283\,\text{K}$, or $97\,\%$ of the initial value, not half.

## fy3-3-L3-04
**Given:** the readings of five measurements (example data): ear thermometer $36.9$, $37.1$, $36.8$, $37.0$, $37.2$; forehead thermometer $36.2$, $36.7$, $36.0$, $36.5$, $36.6$; mouth thermometer (reference) $37.0$, $37.0$, $37.1$, $36.9$, $37.0$, all in degrees Celsius; fever limit $37.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and a mouth measurement of $37.6\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** the difference between the mean of the forehead thermometer and the reference method, $\Delta\theta = \theta_\text{mouth,avg} - \theta_\text{forehead,avg}$, in kelvins (part b)
**Principle:** The mean is calculated from the repeated measurements. The difference to the reference method shows the direction and size of the error of the method, and the spread of the readings shows the order of magnitude of the random variation. If the difference is larger than the spread and all the readings deviate in the same direction, the error is systematic. A simple estimate of the spread is half of the range.
**Formula:** $\theta_\text{avg} = \dfrac{\sum \theta_i}{n}$, estimate of the spread $s = \dfrac{\theta_\text{max} - \theta_\text{min}}{2}$ and $\Delta\theta = \theta_\text{mouth,avg} - \theta_\text{forehead,avg}$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed, because a temperature difference is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins.
**Substitution:**
$$\begin{aligned}
\sum\theta_\text{ear} &= 36.9 + 37.1 + 36.8 \\
&\quad + 37.0 + 37.2 \\
&= 185.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\
\theta_\text{ear,avg} &= 185.0 : 5 = 37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\
\sum\theta_\text{forehead} &= 36.2 + 36.7 + 36.0 \\
&\quad + 36.5 + 36.6 \\
&= 182.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\
\theta_\text{forehead,avg} &= 182.0 : 5 = 36.4\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\
\sum\theta_\text{mouth} &= 37.0 + 37.0 + 37.1 \\
&\quad + 36.9 + 37.0 \\
&= 185.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\
\theta_\text{mouth,avg} &= 185.0 : 5 = 37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}
\end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta\theta &= 37.0 - 36.4 = 0.6\,\text{K} \\
s_\text{ear} &= (37.2 - 36.8) : 2 = 0.2\,\text{K} \\
s_\text{forehead} &= (36.7 - 36.0) : 2 = 0.35\,\text{K} \\
s_\text{mouth} &= (37.1 - 36.9) : 2 = 0.1\,\text{K} 
\end{aligned}$$
$$\theta_\text{forehead,corr} = 37.6 - 0.6 = 37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$$
**Answer:** $0.6\,\text{K}$, one significant figure, because the readings are given to the accuracy of $0.1\,\text{K}$. The difference is systematic, because every forehead reading ($36.0\ldots 36.7\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) is lower than every mouth reading ($36.9\ldots 37.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), and the difference $0.6\,\text{K}$ is larger than the spread of the forehead thermometer, $0.35\,\text{K}$. When the mouth shows $37.6\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the forehead thermometer would show $37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ on average, which is below the fever limit, so the fever would go undetected although the person has a fever.
**Reasonableness:** The mean of the ear thermometer is the same as that of the reference, and its spread of $0.2\,\text{K}$ is small, so the ear thermometer is the most reliable in this data. The mean of the forehead thermometer, $36.4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, is lower than the reference, and all five forehead readings are lower than the lowest reference reading, $36.9\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Repeating reduces the random error of the mean but does not remove the systematic error, so even the mean of five measurements is too low for assessing a fever.

## fy3-3-L3-05
**Given:** gauge pressure $p_\text{g1} = 600\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $\theta_1 = 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, atmospheric pressure $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, the gauge pressure at which the valve opens $p_\text{g,max} = 900\,\text{kPa}$, $\theta_2 = 60.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (example data)
**Find:** the temperature $\theta_3$ in degrees Celsius at which the safety valve opens (part b); in addition the total pressure and the gauge pressure at the temperature $\theta_2$ and the increase in kinetic energy
**Principle:** The tank is sealed and rigid, so the amount of air and the volume are constant. Then the total pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins. The proportionality applies to the total pressure, not to the gauge pressure, so the gauge pressures are first converted to total pressures by adding the atmospheric pressure. In a dilute gas the average kinetic energy of the particles is also directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins.
**Formula:** $p = p_\text{g} + p_0$
$$p_2 = p_1\,\frac{T_2}{T_1}$$
$$\frac{\Delta E_\text{k}}{E_\text{k,1}} = \frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1}$$
$$T_3 = T_1\,\frac{p_3}{p_1}$$
$$\tfrac{\theta_3}{{}^\circ\text{C}} = \tfrac{T_3}{\text{K}} - 273.15$$
**Conversions:** $T_1 = (20.0 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 293.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = (60.0 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 333.15\,\text{K}$. Total pressures: $p_1 = 600 + 101 = 701\,\text{kPa}$ and $p_3 = 900 + 101 = 1001\,\text{kPa}$.
**Substitution:**
$$\begin{aligned}
p_2 &= 701\,\text{kPa} \cdot \frac{333.15\,\text{K}}{293.15\,\text{K}} \\
&= 796.7\,\text{kPa} \\
p_{\text{g},2} &= 796.7 - 101 = 695.7\,\text{kPa} \\
T_3 &= 293.15\,\text{K} \cdot \frac{1001\,\text{kPa}}{701\,\text{kPa}} \\
&= 418.61\,\text{K} \\
\theta_3 &= 418.61 - 273.15 = 145.46\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\
\frac{\Delta E_\text{k}}{E_\text{k,1}} &= \frac{333.15 - 293.15}{293.15} = 0.136
\end{aligned}$$
So the kinetic energy increases by $13.6\,\%$.
**Answer:** $145\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (exactly $145.46\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), three significant figures like the atmospheric pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$. Other results: the total pressure is $797\,\text{kPa}$ and the gauge pressure $696\,\text{kPa}$ at $60.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (the valve does not open), and the kinetic energy increases by $13.6\,\%$. The total pressure increases by a factor of $796.7 : 701 = 1.136$, or $13.6\,\%$, the same as the kinetic energy, but the gauge pressure increases by a factor of $695.7 : 600 = 1.16$, or $16\,\%$. The total pressure is proportional to the temperature in kelvins, because it arises from the collisions of the particles; the gauge pressure is the difference between the total pressure and the atmospheric pressure.
**Reasonableness:** When the valve opens, the total pressure increases by the ratio $1001 : 701 = 1.43$, so the temperature in kelvins must increase by the same factor: $293 \cdot 1.43 \approx 419\,\text{K}$, which matches the order of magnitude of the result $418.61\,\text{K} - 273.15 = 145.46\,{}^\circ\text{C} \approx 145\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The $60\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ inside a car is clearly below $145\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the valve does not open. If the gauge pressure were taken as proportional, the wrong result would be $293.15 \cdot 1.5 - 273.15 \approx 167\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

## fy3-3-L1-09
**Find:** the unit of the constant $c$ when $p = c\,T$, the pressure is in the unit $\text{kPa}$ and the temperature in the unit $\text{K}$
**Principle:** Both sides of the equation $p = c\,T$ must have the same unit. On the left is the pressure, so the unit of the product $c\,T$ must be $\text{kPa}$. The unit of temperature is $\text{K}$, so the unit of $c$ must cancel the kelvin.
**Model answer:** Solve for $c$:

$c = \dfrac{p}{T}$

Substitute the units:

$[c] = \dfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}}$

Check: $\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}} \cdot \text{K} = \text{kPa}$, so $c\,T$ is a pressure. The answer is therefore $\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}}$. The option $\text{kPa} \cdot \text{K}$ multiplies the units although $c$ is obtained by dividing. The option $\tfrac{\text{K}}{\text{kPa}}$ is the division upside down, and the option $\text{kPa}$ forgets the unit of temperature. With the example values $p = 120\,\text{kPa}$ and $T = 300\,\text{K}$ we get $c = 120\,\text{kPa} : 300\,\text{K} = 0.40\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}}$.
