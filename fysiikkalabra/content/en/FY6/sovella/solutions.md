### b)
**Given:** the line of part a, from which the points $(0.10\,\text{A};\ 4.48\,\text{V})$ and $(0.60\,\text{A};\ 3.88\,\text{V})$ are read
**Find:** the source voltage $E$ and the internal resistance $R_\text{int}$
**Principle:** According to the model the terminal voltage is $U = E - R_\text{int} I$. The point where the line meets the vertical axis is at current zero, when there is no voltage drop inside the battery, so the intercept is $E$. The slope tells how much the terminal voltage falls as the current increases, so it is $-R_\text{int}$.
**Reading the graph:** The slope is read from two points of the drawn line that are far apart, not from adjacent measurement points: $(0.10\,\text{A};\ 4.48\,\text{V})$ and $(0.60\,\text{A};\ 3.88\,\text{V})$. Between them $\Delta I = 0.50\,\text{A}$ and $\Delta U = -0.60\,\text{V}$ (the slope triangle of the graph). The line meets the vertical axis at $4.60\,\text{V}$.
**Formula:** $R_\text{int} = -\dfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta I}$ and $E = U + R_\text{int} I$ from any point of the line.
**Conversions:** Not needed: the voltage is in volts and the current in amperes, and $1\,\text{V/A} = 1\,\Omega$.
**Substitution:** $R_\text{int} = -\dfrac{-0.60\,\text{V}}{0.50\,\text{A}} = 1.2\,\Omega$ and $E = 4.48\,\text{V} + 1.2\,\Omega \cdot 0.10\,\text{A} = 4.48\,\text{V} + 0.12\,\text{V} = 4.60\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $E \approx 4.6\,\text{V}$ and $R_\text{int} \approx 1.2\,\Omega$, two significant figures, because the slope is read from a graph and cannot be read more precisely than to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The source voltage is slightly larger than the rated voltage of the battery, $4.5\,\text{V}$, which suits a new battery. The internal resistance is of the order of an ohm, as in flat flashlight batteries. The short-circuit current would be $\dfrac{E}{R_\text{int}} \approx 3.8\,\text{A}$.

### c)
**Given:** $E = 4.60\,\text{V}$, $R_\text{int} = 1.2\,\Omega$ and the external resistor $R = 8.0\,\Omega$
**Find:** the power $P$ transferred to the resistor and the fraction of the whole electric power of the battery that is transferred as heat in the internal resistance of the battery
**Principle:** In the circuit the battery, the internal resistance and the resistor are in series, so the same current flows through all of them. According to the loop rule the source voltage is divided between the internal resistance and the resistor: $E - R_\text{int} I - R I = 0$. Of the electric power, heat is produced both in the resistor and inside the battery, and the whole power of the battery is the sum of these, $E I$.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{E}{R + R_\text{int}}$, $P = R I^2$, $P_\text{int} = R_\text{int} I^2$ and the fraction $\dfrac{P_\text{int}}{P + P_\text{int}} = \dfrac{R_\text{int}}{R + R_\text{int}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed, all quantities are in base units.
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{4.60\,\text{V}}{8.0\,\Omega + 1.2\,\Omega} = \dfrac{4.60\,\text{V}}{9.2\,\Omega} = 0.50\,\text{A}$, $P = 8.0\,\Omega \cdot (0.50\,\text{A})^2 = 2.0\,\text{W}$ and $P_\text{int} = 1.2\,\Omega \cdot (0.50\,\text{A})^2 = 0.30\,\text{W}$. The fraction is $\dfrac{0.30\,\text{W}}{2.0\,\text{W} + 0.30\,\text{W}} = 0.13$.
**Answer:** The power transferred to the resistor is $P \approx 2.0\,\text{W}$, and about $13\,\%$ of the electric power of the battery is transferred as heat inside the battery. The result is given to two significant figures like the initial data.
**Reasonableness:** The terminal voltage is $R I = 4.0\,\text{V}$, which is the same as $E - R_\text{int} I = 4.60\,\text{V} - 0.60\,\text{V}$, and on the graph the voltage at a current of $0.50\,\text{A}$ is about $4.0\,\text{V}$. Since the external resistance is more than seven times the internal resistance, only a small part of the power stays inside the battery.

### d)
**Find:** the effect of discharging and warming on the result for the source voltage and the internal resistance, the uncertainty of $R_\text{int}$ caused by the scatter, and an improvement of the measurement
**Principle:** The model $U = E - R_\text{int} I$ assumes that $E$ and $R_\text{int}$ stay constant throughout the measurement. If the battery discharges or warms up, they change during the measurement, and the line of the graph describes a kind of average. The scatter tells about random errors, that is, how much the fitted line could turn.
**Model answer:** *Systematic error.* The current was increased all the time in the same direction, and for each reading the circuit was connected for about a minute. The discharging of the battery lowers the voltage over time, so the voltages measured later, that is, those corresponding to the largest currents, are too small. Since time and current increase together, the effect of time is mixed with the slope: the line falls too steeply and $R_\text{int}$ comes out too large. Warming also changes $R_\text{int}$ during the measurement (usually it makes it smaller), so the direction of its effect is uncertain. The effect on the source voltage is small, because the smallest current was measured first with a fresh battery.

*The size of the drift.* The new measurement at a current of $0.10\,\text{A}$ gave $4.45\,\text{V}$, that is $0.03\,\text{V}$ less than at the beginning of the series. At worst the last point is therefore about $0.03\,\text{V}$ too low, and the slope is too large by at most $\dfrac{0.03\,\text{V}}{0.50\,\text{A}} = 0.06\,\Omega$.

*Uncertainty.* The points deviate from the fitted line by at most $0.02\,\text{V}$. If one end of the line rises and the other falls by this much, the slope changes by $\dfrac{2 \cdot 0.02\,\text{V}}{0.50\,\text{A}} = 0.08\,\Omega$, that is, $R_\text{int} = (1.20 \pm 0.08)\,\Omega$. The uncertainty has been rounded to one significant figure.

*Improvement.* The currents are measured in random order, or the series is repeated in reverse order, and the circuit is connected only for the time of the reading. If the results differ from each other, the difference tells the size of the effect of discharging.

*Conclusion.* The effect of the drift ($0.06\,\Omega$) is of the same order of magnitude as that of the scatter ($0.08\,\Omega$), so $R_\text{int}$ is about $1.1\ldots1.2\,\Omega$. The value $1.2\,\Omega$ is rather an upper limit, because the systematic error increases it.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a the vertical axis of the graph may start from zero as long as the points and $E$ can be distinguished; then the line may also be extended to the horizontal axis, where $I_\text{sc} = \dfrac{E}{R_\text{int}} \approx 3.8\,\text{A}$.
- In part b a slope reading of $-1.1\ldots-1.3\,\text{V/A}$ (answer $R_\text{int}$ about $1.1\ldots1.3\,\Omega$) and a source voltage of $4.5\ldots4.7\,\text{V}$ are accepted. A fit made with a calculator or a spreadsheet is also accepted.
- In part d the uncertainty may also be estimated by fitting the extreme lines and reading their slopes.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The points are joined into a broken line, or the line is forced through the origin even though $U$ is not zero when the current is zero.
- The slope is read from two consecutive measurement points instead of points of the line that are far apart.
- The sign of the slope is ignored, or $R_\text{int}$ is calculated as $U : I$ from a single measurement, which gives the value of the external resistance, not the internal resistance.
- In part c the whole source voltage $E$ is taken as the voltage of the resistor and the voltage drop in the internal resistance is forgotten.
- The uncertainty is written as the reading precision of the meter, $0.01\,\text{V}$, as such, without taking into account the scatter of the points or the systematic error.

## fy6-s2

### a)
**Given:** source voltage $E = 12.0\,\text{V}$, resistance of the cable $R_\text{c} = 0.80\,\Omega$, lamp A $12\,\text{V},\ 24\,\text{W}$ and lamp B $12\,\text{V},\ 12\,\text{W}$
**Find:** the current $I$ from the battery bank when both lamps are lit
**Principle:** A lamp is treated as a constant resistance whose value is obtained from the rated values: at the rated voltage the power is $P = \dfrac{U^2}{R}$. Lamps A and B are in parallel, so they have the same voltage, and the cable is in series with them, so the whole current flows through the cable. Then the total resistance is the sum of the resistance of the cable and the equivalent resistance of the parallel connection.
**Formula:** $R_\text{A} = \dfrac{U_\text{n}^2}{P_\text{A}}$, $R_\text{B} = \dfrac{U_\text{n}^2}{P_\text{B}}$, $\dfrac{1}{R_\text{par}} = \dfrac{1}{R_\text{A}} + \dfrac{1}{R_\text{B}}$, $R = R_\text{c} + R_\text{par}$ and $I = \dfrac{E}{R}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: $1\,\text{V}^2/\text{W} = 1\,\Omega$.
**Substitution:** $R_\text{A} = \dfrac{(12\,\text{V})^2}{24\,\text{W}} = 6.0\,\Omega$ and $R_\text{B} = \dfrac{(12\,\text{V})^2}{12\,\text{W}} = 12\,\Omega$. Parallel connection: $\dfrac{1}{R_\text{par}} = \dfrac{1}{6.0\,\Omega} + \dfrac{1}{12\,\Omega} = \dfrac{3}{12\,\Omega}$, so $R_\text{par} = 4.0\,\Omega$. The total resistance is $R = 0.80\,\Omega + 4.0\,\Omega = 4.8\,\Omega$ and the current $I = \dfrac{12.0\,\text{V}}{4.8\,\Omega} = 2.5\,\text{A}$.
**Answer:** $I = 2.5\,\text{A}$, two significant figures as in the initial data.
**Reasonableness:** If the lamps got the rated voltage, their currents would be $2.0\,\text{A}$ and $1.0\,\text{A}$, a total of $3.0\,\text{A}$. The cable takes part of the voltage, so the current is smaller than this. The current is also below the limits of the fuse, $3.15\,\text{A}$, and of the cable, $4.0\,\text{A}$.

### b)
**Given:** $E = 12.0\,\text{V}$, $R_\text{c} = 0.80\,\Omega$, $R_\text{A} = 6.0\,\Omega$ and the current of part a, $I = 2.5\,\text{A}$
**Find:** the actual power $P_\text{A}$ of lamp A and its comparison with the rated power $24\,\text{W}$
**Principle:** According to the loop rule the voltage of the battery bank is divided between the voltage drop in the cable and the voltage of the lamps: $E - R_\text{c} I - U_\text{L} = 0$. The voltage of the lamps is therefore lower than the rated voltage, and the power is calculated from this actual voltage.
**Formula:** $U_\text{L} = E - R_\text{c} I$ and $P_\text{A} = \dfrac{U_\text{L}^2}{R_\text{A}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed.
**Substitution:** $U_\text{L} = 12.0\,\text{V} - 0.80\,\Omega \cdot 2.5\,\text{A} = 12.0\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V} = 10.0\,\text{V}$ and $P_\text{A} = \dfrac{(10.0\,\text{V})^2}{6.0\,\Omega} = 16.7\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $P_\text{A} \approx 17\,\text{W}$, about $70\,\%$ of the rated $24\,\text{W}$, two significant figures as in the initial data ($0.80\,\Omega$, $24\,\text{W}$, $12\,\text{V}$).
**Reasonableness:** The lamp shines dimmer than at the rated voltage, because its voltage is only $10.0\,\text{V}$. Check: the power of lamp B is $\dfrac{(10.0\,\text{V})^2}{12\,\Omega} = 8.3\,\text{W}$ and the heat transferred to the cable is $0.80\,\Omega \cdot (2.5\,\text{A})^2 = 5.0\,\text{W}$. The sum $16.7\,\text{W} + 8.3\,\text{W} + 5.0\,\text{W} = 30\,\text{W}$ is equal to the power of the battery bank, $12.0\,\text{V} \cdot 2.5\,\text{A} = 30\,\text{W}$.

### c)
**Find:** how the brightness of lamp A, the current from the battery bank and the warming of the cable change when lamp B is removed from the circuit, and a check of the power of lamp A by calculation
**Principle:** The battery bank gives the circuit a constant voltage of $12.0\,\text{V}$. When one parallel branch is removed, the equivalent resistance of the parallel connection increases and the total resistance increases, so the current decreases. The cable is in series with lamp A, so its voltage drop depends on the whole current.
**Model answer:** *Current.* The total resistance is now $R = 0.80\,\Omega + 6.0\,\Omega = 6.8\,\Omega$ and the current $I = \dfrac{12.0\,\text{V}}{6.8\,\Omega} \approx 1.8\,\text{A}$, previously $2.5\,\text{A}$.

*Brightness.* The smaller current reduces the voltage drop in the cable: $0.80\,\Omega \cdot 1.76\,\text{A} \approx 1.4\,\text{V}$, previously $2.0\,\text{V}$. A larger voltage $U_\text{A} \approx 10.6\,\text{V}$ is therefore left for lamp A, and its power is $\dfrac{(10.6\,\text{V})^2}{6.0\,\Omega} \approx 18.7\,\text{W}$, previously $16.7\,\text{W}$. *Conclusion.* Lamps connected in parallel are independent of each other only if the voltage across them stays constant. The resistance of the cable makes the lamps slightly dependent on each other: if the resistance of the cable were zero, the brightness of lamp A would not change at all.

## fy6-s3

### a)
**Given:** potential difference $U = 60\,\text{kV} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{V}$, distance $d = 20\,\text{cm}$, charge $q = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{C}$ and mass $m = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{kg}$
**Find:** the electric force $F$ acting on the droplet, the significance of the weight and the possibility of sparking
**Principle:** Between the plates there is a uniform electric field whose strength is the potential difference divided by the distance. A force in the direction of the field acts on the positively charged droplet, so the droplet moves towards the lower potential, that is, the grounded workpiece. The significance of the weight is assessed by comparing it with the electric force, and sparking by comparing the strength of the field with the dielectric strength of air.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$ and $F = qE$
**Conversions:** $20\,\text{cm} = 0.20\,\text{m}$ and $60\,\text{kV} = 60 \cdot 1000\,\text{V} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{V}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{6.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{V}}{0.20\,\text{m}} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m}$ and $F = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{C} \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m} = 1.8 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $F \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{N}$ in the direction of the field towards the workpiece, two significant figures as in the initial data.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the droplet is $mg = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 3.9 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{N}$, only about $2\,\%$ of the electric force, so the weight can be ignored. The strength of the field, $3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m} = 0.30\,\text{MV/m}$, is about a tenth of the dielectric strength of air, $3\,\text{MV/m}$, so no sparking occurs.

### b)
**Given:** $q = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{C}$, $U = 6.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{V}$, $m = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{kg}$ and the droplet leaves from rest
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the droplet when it hits the workpiece, when air resistance is not taken into account
**Principle:** The work done by the electric field on the droplet is the charge times the potential difference, $W = qU$, and it increases the kinetic energy of the droplet. Weight and air resistance are left out, so there are no other forces. In this way it is enough to know the potential difference, and the strength of the field or the acceleration is not needed.
**Formula:** $q U = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 q U}{m}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed, all quantities are in base units; $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $qU = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{C} \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{V} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{J}$ and $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 3.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{J}}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{180\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 13.4\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 13\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the initial data.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration is $\dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{1.8 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{N}}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{kg}} = 450\,\text{m/s}^2$, so over a distance of $0.20\,\text{m}$ the speed would be $\sqrt{2 \cdot 450\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.20\,\text{m}} = 13.4\,\text{m/s}$, which confirms the result. The work done by the weight over the whole distance would be only $7.8 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{J}$, about $2\,\%$ of the work done by the electric field.

### c)
**Find:** why the measured impact speed $5.0\,\text{m/s}$ is much smaller than the result of part b and where the work done by the electric field goes
**Principle:** The model of part b left out air resistance. Air resistance acts against the direction of motion of the droplet, and the work it does is negative. When the speed of the droplet increases, air resistance increases, and the droplet moves at a speed at which air resistance and the electric force are approximately equal. According to the conservation of energy, the work done by the electric field is not lost but goes elsewhere.
**Model answer:** *Explanation.* The speed $13\,\text{m/s}$ of part b would be possible only in a vacuum. In air, air resistance limits the speed to a much smaller value, so the result of part b is the greatest possible speed of the droplet, an upper limit.

*Calculation.* The actual kinetic energy of the droplet is $\dfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{kg} \cdot (5.0\,\text{m/s})^2 = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{J}$. The work done by the electric field is $3.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{J}$, so only $\dfrac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{J}}{3.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{J}} \approx 14\,\%$ ends up as kinetic energy.

*The fate of the energy.* The rest, $3.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{J} - 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{J} = 3.1 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{J}$, is transferred to the air. The internal energy of the air increases, and the energy is transferred further as heat to the surroundings. So energy is not lost.

*Conclusion.* The assumption of part b (no air resistance) makes the speed too large, so the result is the greatest possible speed. Energy is not lost but is transferred to the air.

### d)
**Given:** $q_1 = q_2 = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{C}$, $r = 1.0\,\text{mm}$ and $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$
**Find:** the force $F$ between the droplets and its significance for the behaviour of the cloud of droplets

## fy6-s4

### a)
**Given:** $C = 330\,\mu\text{F}$ and $U = 300\,\text{V}$
**Find:** the energy $E$ stored in the capacitor before the flash
**Principle:** Energy is stored in a charged capacitor, because charge has been moved onto its plates against the voltage. The energy depends on the capacitance and the square of the voltage.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{1}{2} C U^2$
**Conversions:** $330\,\mu\text{F} = 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F}$
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F} \cdot (300\,\text{V})^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F} \cdot 90\,000\,\text{V}^2 = 14.85\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $E \approx 15\,\text{J}$ ($14.85\,\text{J}$), two significant figures, because the precision of the voltage $300\,\text{V}$ and the capacitance $330\,\mu\text{F}$ is at most two or three figures.
**Reasonableness:** The energy is of the order of a joule, as in the capacitor of a small flash unit. If this energy were used to lift an object with a weight of one kilogram, it would rise about $1.5\,\text{m}$.

### b)
**Given:** $C = 330\,\mu\text{F}$, the voltage before the flash $U_1 = 300\,\text{V}$ and after it $U_2 = 220\,\text{V}$
**Find:** the energy $\Delta E$ transferred to the flash, its fraction of the stored energy and the reason for the size of the fraction
**Principle:** The energy transferred to the flash is the change in the energy of the capacitor: it is the energy before the flash minus the energy after the flash. The voltage is squared in the energy formula, so each energy is calculated separately; the change in voltage $\Delta U$ alone is not enough.
**Formula:** $\Delta E = \dfrac{1}{2} C U_1^2 - \dfrac{1}{2} C U_2^2$
**Conversions:** $330\,\mu\text{F} = 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F}$
**Substitution:** $E_1 = 14.85\,\text{J}$ and $E_2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F} \cdot (220\,\text{V})^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F} \cdot 48\,400\,\text{V}^2 = 7.99\,\text{J}$, so $\Delta E = 14.85\,\text{J} - 7.99\,\text{J} = 6.86\,\text{J}$. The fraction is $\dfrac{6.86\,\text{J}}{14.85\,\text{J}} = 0.46$.
**Answer:** The energy transferred to the flash was $\Delta E \approx 6.9\,\text{J}$, about $46\,\%$ of the stored energy, two significant figures as in part a.
**Reasonableness:** The voltage decreased by only $\dfrac{80\,\text{V}}{300\,\text{V}} \approx 27\,\%$, but $46\,\%$ of the energy was transferred, because energy is proportional to the square of the voltage: $1 - \left(\dfrac{220}{300}\right)^2 = 0.46$. The transferred energy is smaller than the initial energy, as it should be.

### c)
**Given:** $C = 330\,\mu\text{F}$, change in voltage $\Delta U = 300\,\text{V} - 220\,\text{V} = 80\,\text{V}$ and duration $\Delta t = 1.0\,\text{ms}$
**Find:** the average current $I$ during the flash
**Principle:** Current is the rate at which charge is transferred. The charge of the capacitor is $Q = C U$, so when the voltage decreases by $80\,\text{V}$, a charge $\Delta Q = C\,\Delta U$ has left the capacitor through the flash tube.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}$ and $\Delta Q = C\,\Delta U$
**Conversions:** $330\,\mu\text{F} = 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F}$ and $1.0\,\text{ms} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $\Delta Q = 330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F} \cdot 80\,\text{V} = 0.0264\,\text{C}$ and $I = \dfrac{0.0264\,\text{C}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}} = 26.4\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $I \approx 26\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the precision of the duration $1.0\,\text{ms}$.
**Reasonableness:** The average power is $\dfrac{6.86\,\text{J}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}} \approx 6.9\,\text{kW}$ and the current $26\,\text{A}$: these are much larger than a small battery could supply in such a short pulse. That is why the energy is first stored in the capacitor, which releases it quickly.

### d)
**Find:** whether there is any danger in the capacitor after the battery is removed, how it is discharged safely and how large a current would flow through the body
**Principle:** A capacitor stores charge and energy on its plates, and its voltage does not disappear when the battery that charges it is removed. The danger depends on the stored energy and the voltage, not on the battery. The size of the current through the body is obtained from the voltage and the resistance.
**Model answer:** *Danger.* After the battery is removed, the capacitor is still at $300\,\text{V}$ and holds about $15\,\text{J}$. The device does not get its voltage from the small battery but from the capacitor, which can discharge through the body of the repairer.

*Current.* If the resistance of the body is $1.0\,\text{k}\Omega$, the initial current would be $I = \dfrac{U}{R} = \dfrac{300\,\text{V}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\Omega} = 0.30\,\text{A}$. This is many times the few tens of milliamperes that can already be dangerous. The current decreases as the capacitor discharges, but the initial current is enough for a dangerous electric shock.

*Safe discharge* (only for understanding: do not try to discharge a capacitor yourself). A suitable resistor, for example $10\,\text{k}\Omega$, is connected between the terminals of the capacitor with insulated tools. The initial current is then $\dfrac{300\,\text{V}}{10\,\text{k}\Omega} = 30\,\text{mA}$, and the energy of the capacitor is transferred to the internal energy of the resistor and from there as heat to the surroundings. At first the power of the resistor is $\dfrac{(300\,\text{V})^2}{10\,\text{k}\Omega} = 9\,\text{W}$, so the power rating of the resistor must be suitable. The terminals are not short-circuited with a screwdriver, because the current is then very large and a spark is produced. Finally the voltage is checked with a meter.

*Conclusion.* The device must not be opened right away. The danger comes from the energy and voltage stored in the capacitor, not from the size of the battery. The resistance of the body varies, for example with the moisture of the skin, so the calculated current is an order-of-magnitude estimate.
