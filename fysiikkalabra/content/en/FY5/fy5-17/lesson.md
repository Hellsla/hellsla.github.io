## Key idea
### An echo tells the depth
Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above $20\,\text{kHz}$, that is, too high for a person to hear. In medical imaging frequencies of about $2\text{–}15\,\text{MHz}$ are used. The probe sends a short ultrasound pulse into the body and then listens for echoes. When the pulse meets the boundary of two tissues, part of it is reflected back and the rest continues deeper (chapter *A wave meets an obstacle or a gap*). So every boundary gives an echo of its own. The machine measures how long it takes for each echo to return. Because the pulse travels to the boundary and back, during the travel time of the echo it covers twice the depth of the boundary. The machine assumes that sound travels in soft tissue at an average speed of $1540\,\text{m/s}$, and calculates the depth of every echo.

When the probe is tilted or it has a row of small transmitters, pulses are sent in many directions. From the depths and strengths of the echoes a two-dimensional greyscale image, the B-scan, is formed. The simplest display is the A-scan, the echo curve: its horizontal axis shows the time from sending the pulse and its vertical axis the strength of the echo. A peak in the echo curve means a boundary, and the position of the peak tells how deep the boundary is.

### Why gel is needed
How large a part of the wave is reflected depends on how different the substances at the boundary are as far as the propagation of sound is concerned. Soft tissues resemble each other, so only about one per cent is reflected from their boundaries. Therefore most of the pulse continues deeper and produces echoes also from deeper boundaries. From the boundary of bone and soft tissue about $40\,\%$ is reflected, and bone also strongly attenuates sound, so behind a bone there is a shadow that gives no echoes. From the boundary of air and skin about $99.9\,\%$ is reflected. If even a thin layer of air is left between the probe and the skin, almost the whole pulse is reflected back already at the skin. Gel displaces the air: from the boundary of gel and skin only about $0.2\,\%$ is reflected.

### Frequency: a sharp image or an image that reaches deep
The wavelength is obtained from the equation $v = f\lambda$ (chapter *How waves form and travel*). At the frequency $5\,\text{MHz}$ the wavelength of ultrasound in soft tissue is about $0.3\,\text{mm}$. The smallest details that can be distinguished are roughly a few wavelengths in size, so a high frequency and a short wavelength give a sharper image. On the other hand, tissue turns part of the energy of the sound wave into internal energy, and this attenuation grows approximately in direct proportion to the frequency. A high-frequency pulse therefore weakens quickly, and its echo does not return from deep down strongly enough. Organs deep down, such as the heart and the liver, are imaged with a low frequency, and structures near the surface, such as tendons and the thyroid gland, with a high one.

### Echolocation in nature and technology (extra)
Bats and dolphins locate their prey on the same principle. A bat sends out short ultrasonic calls whose frequency, depending on the species, is about $20$–$100\,\text{kHz}$, and works out the distance from the delay of the echoes. At the frequency $50\,\text{kHz}$ the wavelength in air is about $7\,\text{mm}$. From an object much smaller than the wavelength only a very weak echo returns, because the wave bends around it (chapter *A wave meets an obstacle or a gap*). That is why bats that hunt insects use high frequencies. The sonar of a ship measures the depth of the water in the same way and detects shoals of fish; sound travels in water at about $1400\text{–}1500\,\text{m/s}$, depending on the temperature.

### Earthquake waves
In an earthquake the bedrock breaks, and earthquake waves set out from the point of fracture. Two kinds of waves travel through the interior of the Earth. The P wave is a longitudinal wave in which the rock is compressed and rarefied in the direction of propagation. It is the fastest and arrives at a station first. The S wave is a transverse wave in which the rock moves sideways. It is slower than the P wave. In the upper part of the crust the speed of the P wave is typically about $6\,\text{km/s}$ and that of the S wave about $3.5\,\text{km/s}$. Surface waves, which travel along the surface, are slower still, but their amplitude is the greatest, and they cause most of the damage.

A transverse wave needs a medium that resists a change of shape: when a layer of rock is displaced sideways, the neighbouring layers pull it back. A liquid does not resist a change of shape in the same way, so the S wave does not travel in a liquid. The P wave also travels in a liquid, like sound in water. When earthquakes were detected on different sides of the globe, it was noticed that S waves are not detected on the half opposite the earthquake, when the angular distance, the angle between the earthquake and the station measured at the centre of the Earth, is more than about $103^\circ$. A shadow zone is left for S waves because the outer core of the Earth is liquid.

### Tsunami (extra)
An earthquake on the sea floor can suddenly raise or lower a large mass of water, and a tsunami sets out from it. Its wavelength is tens or hundreds of kilometres, much greater than the depth of the ocean. Then the water moves from the bottom all the way to the surface, and the speed of the wave depends only on the depth of the water: in the deep ocean a tsunami travels at the speed of an aeroplane. When the wave comes into shallow water near the shore, it slows down, its wavelength shortens and its energy is concentrated in a smaller amount of water. That is why the height of the wave grows many times over near the coast.

## Formula: Distance from the travel time of a wave
When a pulse travels to a boundary and back along the same path at a constant speed, the distance of the boundary is
```latex
d = \frac{v t}{2}
```
where $d$ is the distance of the boundary (m), $v$ the speed of sound in the medium (m/s) and $t$ the travel time of the echo, that is, the time from sending the pulse to the return of the echo (s). In an ultrasound machine $v = 1540\,\text{m/s}$. The formula also holds for the echoes of a bat and a sonar, when the speed is the speed of sound in air or water. An A-scan is read by taking the time of each peak from the time axis and converting it to a depth with this formula. The thickness of a layer between two boundaries is obtained from the time difference of the echoes with the same formula.

A seismograph station does not know when the earthquake happened, but it measures the difference $\Delta t$ in the arrival times of the P wave and the S wave. When the waves cover the same distance $d$ at constant speeds, the S wave falls behind the P wave the more, the longer the distance is:
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta t = \frac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \frac{d}{v_\text{P}} \\[6pt]
&d = \frac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t
\end{aligned}
```
where $d$ is the distance of the station from the point where the earthquake started (km), $v_\text{P}$ and $v_\text{S}$ the speeds of the P wave and the S wave (km/s) and $\Delta t$ the difference between the arrival times of the S wave and the P wave (s). When $v_\text{P} = 6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and $v_\text{S} = 3.5\,\text{km/s}$, every second of time difference corresponds to a distance of $8.4\,\text{km}$. When the distance is known from three stations, the location of the earthquake is at the intersection of the circles drawn around them.

When the wavelength is much greater than the depth of the water, the speed of a shallow-water wave, such as a tsunami, is
```latex
v = \sqrt{g h}
```
where $v$ is the speed of the wave (m/s), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ the acceleration of free fall and $h$ the depth of the water (m). The equation is given here without derivation. At a depth of four kilometres the speed is about $198\,\text{m/s}$, or about $710\,\text{km/h}$, but in shallow water $10\,\text{m}$ deep only about $9.9\,\text{m/s}$.

### Sources of the values
The speed of sound $1540\,\text{m/s}$ is the standard assumption of ultrasound machines for soft tissue (for example Hoskins, Martin and Thrush: *Diagnostic Ultrasound*, 3rd edition, 2019). The speeds of waves in the crust are approximations from the Earth speed model IASP91 (Kennett and Engdahl 1991), in which the values for the uppermost crust are $5.8\,\text{km/s}$ and $3.36\,\text{km/s}$. The percentages of the reflected fractions have been calculated from the typical acoustic properties of the tissues, and they are approximations.

## Worked example
A seismograph station records an earthquake. The clock of the station showed $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 18\,\text{s}$ when the P wave arrived and $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 43\,\text{s}$ when the S wave arrived. The speed of the P wave in the crust is $6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and that of the S wave $3.5\,\text{km/s}$. How far from the station did the earthquake occur? Assume that the waves travel along the same straight path at constant speeds.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Given and find.** $v_\text{P} = 6.0\,\text{km/s}$, $v_\text{S} = 3.5\,\text{km/s}$, arrival times $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 18\,\text{s}$ and $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 43\,\text{s}$. We are asked for the distance $d$ to the point where the earthquake started.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Principle.** Each wave covers the same distance $d$ at a constant speed, so the travel time is the distance divided by the speed. The moment of the earthquake is not known, but the difference $\Delta t$ in the arrival times is the difference of the travel times of the S wave and the P wave.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Formula solved.** Take $d$ as a common factor and solve:
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta t = d\left(\frac{1}{v_\text{S}} - \frac{1}{v_\text{P}}\right) \\[6pt]
&d = \frac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Unit conversions.** The hours and minutes are the same, so the difference in arrival times is $43\,\text{s} - 18\,\text{s} = 25\,\text{s}$. The speeds are in km/s and the time in seconds, so the distance comes out directly in kilometres.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Substitution.**
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&d = \frac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t \\
&= \frac{6.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 3.5\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}}{6.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} - 3.5\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}} \cdot 25\,\text{s} \\
&= 8.4\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 25\,\text{s} \\
&= 210\,\text{km}
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Result.** The earthquake occurred about $210\,\text{km}$ from the station. The result is given with two significant figures, because the speeds $6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and $3.5\,\text{km/s}$ are known to two figures.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Reasonableness.** Let us check the travel times: the P wave covers $210\,\text{km} : 6.0\,\text{km/s} = 35\,\text{s}$ and the S wave $210\,\text{km} : 3.5\,\text{km/s} = 60\,\text{s}$. Their difference is $25\,\text{s}$, as measured. So the earthquake occurred $35\,\text{s}$ before the arrival of the P wave, that is, when the clock of the station showed $12\,\text{h}\ 03\,\text{min}\ 43\,\text{s}$.

## Common misconception
The most common calculation error is to forget that the echo travels there and back. If an ultrasound echo returns after $65\,\mu\text{s}$, the product $v t$ is $10\,\text{cm}$, but the boundary is only at a depth of $5.0\,\text{cm}$. Another error is to think that the highest frequency is always best, because it gives the sharpest image. High-frequency ultrasound is attenuated quickly, so deep structures are imaged with a lower frequency and sharpness has to be compromised. A third error is to think of ultrasound as radiation like X-rays. Ultrasound is mechanical wave motion that needs a medium, and it does not ionise tissue.

## Summary
- Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above $20\,\text{kHz}$; medical imaging uses frequencies of about $2\text{–}15\,\text{MHz}$.
- Part of the pulse is reflected from every boundary. The depth of a boundary is obtained from the travel time of the echo, $d = \tfrac{vt}{2}$, when $v = 1540\,\text{m/s}$.
- Gel displaces the air between the probe and the skin; from the boundary of air and skin almost the whole pulse would be reflected.
- A high frequency gives a short wavelength and a sharp image, but it is attenuated quickly; deep structures are imaged with a low frequency.
- The P wave is longitudinal and the fastest, the S wave transverse and slower. The S wave does not travel in a liquid, which shows that the outer core is liquid.
- The distance of an earthquake is obtained from the difference in the arrival times of the S wave and the P wave: $d = \tfrac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\Delta t$.
- The speed of a tsunami depends on the depth of the water, $v = \sqrt{gh}$; near the coast the wave slows down and grows tall.
