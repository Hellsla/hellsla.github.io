## fy7-s1

### a)
**Find:** the forces acting on a boron-11 ion travelling straight, and which plate of the selector is positive
**Principle:** In the selector the ion is acted on by a magnetic force, because it moves in a magnetic field, and by an electric force, because there is an electric field between the plates. Gravity is negligibly small. The ion travels straight at constant velocity only if the sum of the forces is zero, that is, the forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The magnetic force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, and its direction is given by the right-hand rule. The electric force on a positive charge is in the direction of the electric field.

**Model answer:** The ion moves to the right and the magnetic field points into the page. By the right-hand rule, the magnetic force $\overline{F}_\text{m}$ on a positive ion points upwards.
- For the ion to travel straight, the electric force $\overline{F}_\text{s}$ must point downwards and be equal in magnitude to the magnetic force. The force arrows are drawn equally long.
- The velocity $\overline{v}$ is drawn separately in its own colour, because it is not a force.

The electric force on a positive ion is in the direction of the electric field, so the electric field $\overline{E}$ points downwards. The electric field points from the positive plate to the negative one, so **the upper plate is positive**. The choice does not depend on the mass of the ion: the same polarity suits all positive ions.

### b)
**Given:** accelerating voltage $U = 30.0\,\text{kV}$, charge of the ion $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ and mass $m_{11} = 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$; the kinetic energy of the ion before acceleration is negligibly small
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the ion after the acceleration
**Principle:** The electric field in the accelerating gap does work $W = eU$ on the ion. Because the kinetic energy of the ion before acceleration is negligibly small and there are no other forces, the work becomes entirely the kinetic energy of the ion (the energy principle).
**Formula:** $eU = \tfrac{1}{2} m_{11} v^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m_{11}}}$
**Conversions:** $U = 30.0\,\text{kV} = 30.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}$
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 30.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}}{1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{5.258 \cdot 10^{11}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 7.25 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$, three significant figures like the accelerating voltage.
**Reasonableness:** The speed is about $0.24\,\%$ of the speed of light, so the classical expression for kinetic energy is sufficient. The kinetic energy of the ion, $4.8 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$, is small, but the ion is very light, so the speed is high.

### c)
**Given:** $v = 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$ (part b), $B_\text{s} = 0.200\,\text{T}$ and the distance between the plates $d = 2.00\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** the voltage $U_\text{s}$ between the plates
**Principle:** The ion travels straight when the electric force $eE$ and the magnetic force $evB_\text{s}$ are equal in magnitude (part a). The electric field between the plates is uniform, so its strength is the voltage between the plates divided by the distance between the plates.
**Formula:** $eE = evB_\text{s}$, so $E = vB_\text{s}$. When $E = U_\text{s} : d$, we get $U_\text{s} = Ed = vB_\text{s}d$.
**Conversions:** $d = 2.00\,\text{cm} = 0.0200\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $E = 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.200\,\text{T} = 1.450 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m}$ and $U_\text{s} = 1.450 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m} \cdot 0.0200\,\text{m} = 2900\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $U_\text{s} \approx 2.90\,\text{kV}$, three significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** The units are consistent, because $1\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 1\,\text{T} = 1\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$. The voltage is about a tenth of the accelerating voltage, which is easy to achieve with a plate separation of $2\,\text{cm}$.

### d)
**Given:** $v = 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$, $m_{11} = 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, $m_{10} = 1.6627 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ and the radius of the path of the boron-11 ions $r = 40.0\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** the magnetic flux density $B$ of the analysing magnet and the distance of the point of impact of the boron-10 ions from the slit of the boron-11 ions
**Principle:** The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does not change the speed but turns the direction of motion. In a uniform field the ion moves in uniform circular motion, and the magnetic force is its centripetal force. After the selector all the ions have the same velocity, so the radius of the path is directly proportional to the mass of the ion. After the semicircle the ion hits the wall at the far end of a diameter $2r$ from the point of entry, so the distance between the points of impact of the two kinds of ion is the difference of their diameters.
**Formula:** $evB = \dfrac{m v^2}{r}$, so $B = \dfrac{m_{11} v}{e r}$. At the same speed and in the same field $r_{10} = \dfrac{m_{10} v}{eB} = \dfrac{m_{10}}{m_{11}}\,r$, and the distance between the points of impact is $\Delta x = 2r - 2r_{10} = 2(r - r_{10})$.
**Conversions:** $r = 40.0\,\text{cm} = 0.400\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $mv = 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg} \cdot 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s} = 1.3256 \cdot 10^{-20}\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$, so $B = \dfrac{1.3256 \cdot 10^{-20}\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 0.400\,\text{m}} = 0.2069\,\text{T}$. The radius of the boron-10 ions is $r_{10} = \dfrac{1.6627}{1.8281} \cdot 0.400\,\text{m} = 0.3638\,\text{m}$, so $\Delta x = 2 \cdot (0.400\,\text{m} - 0.3638\,\text{m}) = 0.0724\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $B \approx 0.207\,\text{T}$ and $\Delta x \approx 7.2\,\text{cm}$: the boron-10 ions would hit the wall about $7\,\text{cm}$ closer to the point of entry of the ions than the slit. The field has three significant figures like the data; the distance is the difference of two almost equal numbers, so it is left with two.
**Reasonableness:** The field is of the same order of magnitude as that of an ordinary electromagnet. The masses differ by about $9\,\%$, so the radii also differ by about $9\,\%$, that is, about $3.6\,\text{cm}$, and the diameters by twice that. The difference is much larger than the size of the slit, so the magnet certainly separates the isotopes.

### e)
**Find:** whether the analysing magnet can be left out when the kinetic energy of the ions varies by as much as $10\,\%$ after the acceleration
**Principle:** In the balance of forces on an ion passing through the selector, $eE = evB_\text{s}$, the charge cancels and the mass does not appear at all. The selector therefore lets straight through every ion whose speed is $E : B_\text{s}$. The speed after the acceleration depends on both the mass and the kinetic energy.
**Model answer:** *Without variation in energy.* A boron-10 ion that has received the full acceleration is lighter, so its speed is greater: $v_{10} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m_{10}}}$. Substituting the values of part b and the mass $m_{10} = 1.6627 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$ gives $v_{10} \approx 7.60 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$. The magnetic force at that speed is greater than the electric force, so the ion curves upwards in the selector and does not get through. Here the engineer is right.

*With varying energy.* A boron-10 ion passes through the selector if its speed is the same as that of the boron-11 ions, that is, $7.25 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$. Its kinetic energy is then $\tfrac{1}{2} m_{10} v^2$, while that of a boron-11 ion is $\tfrac{1}{2} m_{11} v^2$, so the ratio is $m_{10} : m_{11} = 1.6627 : 1.8281 \approx 0.91$. A boron-10 ion whose kinetic energy is about $9\,\%$ smaller than the full value therefore passes straight through. When the energy varies by as much as $10\,\%$, there are certain to be such ions.

*Conclusion.* The suggestion does not work. The selector selects speed, not mass, and when the energy varies, boron-10 ions also get through it. Only the analysing magnet separates ions travelling at the same speed according to their mass, because the radius of the path is then directly proportional to the mass.

### Hyväksytään myös
- The speed in part b can also be calculated through the kinetic energy: first $W = eU = 4.806 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$ and then $v = \sqrt{2W : m_{11}}$.
- The distance in part d can also be calculated by first finding the radius of the boron-10 ions directly from the equation $r_{10} = \dfrac{m_{10} v}{eB}$. The answer $7.2$–$7.3\,\text{cm}$ is accepted.
- In part e it is also enough to give a justification that calculates the accelerating voltage at which a boron-10 ion would get the right speed: $0.91 \cdot 30.0\,\text{kV} \approx 27.3\,\text{kV}$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The magnetic force is drawn in the direction of the velocity or the field, or its direction is deduced with the rule for a negative charge.
- The electric force is drawn regardless of the plates, and from the upper plate it is concluded that the electric field points towards the positive plate.
- The velocity is drawn in the force diagram as a force or in the same colour as the forces.
- The kilovolts of the accelerating voltage are forgotten when converting to volts, so the speed is about $32$ times too small.
- In part c the voltage is calculated with the formula $U = Ed$ without taking into account that $E$ must first be solved from the balance of forces.
- In part d the difference of the radii is given as the distance between the points of impact instead of the difference of the diameters.

## fy7-s2

### a)
**Given:** the graph of the voltage of the dynamo with the light off
**Find:** the frequency $f$ and the effective value $U$ of the voltage
**Principle:** The voltage varies sinusoidally, so the period is the duration of one complete oscillation and the frequency is its reciprocal. The effective value is the direct voltage that would produce the same average power in a resistor. For a sinusoidal voltage it is the peak value divided by $\sqrt{2}$.
**Reading the graph:** The voltage starts from zero and returns to zero, changing in the same direction, at $30\,\text{ms}$, $60\,\text{ms}$ and $90\,\text{ms}$: three cycles last $90\,\text{ms}$, so $T = 90\,\text{ms} : 3 = 30\,\text{ms}$. Reading three cycles is more accurate than reading one. The peaks are at $7.5\,\text{ms}$, $37.5\,\text{ms}$ and $67.5\,\text{ms}$ on the grid line $14\,\text{V}$, so $e_0 = 14\,\text{V}$.
**Formula:** $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ and $U = \dfrac{e_0}{\sqrt{2}}$
**Conversions:** $T = 30\,\text{ms} = 0.030\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $f = \dfrac{1}{0.030\,\text{s}} = 33.3\,\text{Hz}$ and $U = \dfrac{14\,\text{V}}{\sqrt{2}} = 9.90\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $f \approx 33\,\text{Hz}$ and $U \approx 9.9\,\text{V}$, two significant figures like the values read from the graph.
**Reasonableness:** The frequency is lower than the $50\,\text{Hz}$ of the mains, which suits a wheel that turns only a few revolutions per second. The effective value is about $70\,\%$ of the peak value, as it should be for a sinusoidal voltage.

### b)
**Given:** $f = 33.3\,\text{Hz}$ (part a), $14$ cycles of the voltage per revolution of the wheel and the diameter of the wheel $D = 0.70\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the cyclist
**Principle:** The magnet ring has $14$ pairs of north and south poles. When one pair of poles passes the coil, the flux through the coil makes one complete oscillation, so the voltage has $14$ cycles per revolution of the wheel. The rotation frequency of the wheel is therefore $14$ times smaller than the frequency of the voltage. When the wheel rolls without slipping, in one revolution it advances the length of its circumference $\pi D$.
**Formula:** $f_\text{w} = \dfrac{f}{14}$ and $v = \pi D f_\text{w} = \pi D \cdot \dfrac{f}{14}$
**Conversions:** The answer is converted at the end: $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
**Substitution:** $f_\text{w} = \dfrac{33.3\,\text{Hz}}{14} = 2.381\,\text{Hz}$ and $v = \pi \cdot 0.70\,\text{m} \cdot 2.381\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} = 5.236\,\text{m/s} = 5.236 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} = 18.8\,\text{km/h}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 5.2\,\text{m/s} \approx 19\,\text{km/h}$, two significant figures like the diameter and the period.
**Reasonableness:** The speed is a normal commuting cycling speed. The wheel turns only about two and a half revolutions per second, but $28$ poles still make the frequency of the voltage more than $30\,\text{Hz}$.

### c)
**Given:** the effective voltage with no load $U = 9.90\,\text{V}$ at the speed $v = 5.236\,\text{m/s}$ (parts a and b), rated voltage of the light $U_\text{lamp} = 6.0\,\text{V}$
**Find:** the lowest speed $v_\text{min}$ at which the effective voltage is at least $6.0\,\text{V}$
**Principle:** The peak value of the voltage of a generator is $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. The number of turns of the coil, the field of the magnets and the area stay the same, so the peak value, and with it the effective value, is directly proportional to the frequency. The frequency in turn is directly proportional to the rotation frequency of the wheel and to the speed. The effective voltage is therefore directly proportional to the speed: the more slowly one rides, the more slowly the magnets pass the coil and the more slowly the flux changes.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{v_\text{min}}{v} = \dfrac{U_\text{lamp}}{U}$, so $v_\text{min} = v \cdot \dfrac{U_\text{lamp}}{U}$
**Conversions:** The answer is converted at the end to kilometres per hour: $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
**Substitution:** $v_\text{min} = 5.236\,\text{m/s} \cdot \dfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{9.90\,\text{V}} = 3.17\,\text{m/s} = 3.17 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} = 11.4\,\text{km/h}$
**Answer:** $v_\text{min} \approx 3.2\,\text{m/s} \approx 11\,\text{km/h}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** The result is slow cycling but brisker than walking, so at walking speed the light stays dim. The assumption is rough, however: when the light is on, a current flows in the coil and part of the voltage is left in the coil itself, so the light gets less than the no-load voltage. The real lowest speed is therefore somewhat greater.

### d)
**Find:** whether the dynamo brakes the wheel when the light is on and when it is off
**Principle:** When the coil is in a closed circuit, the changing flux produces an induced current in it. By Lenz's law, the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change that produces it. By conservation of energy, the energy radiated by the light must come from somewhere. A changing magnetic field also produces eddy currents in conducting metal parts.
**Model answer:** *Light on.* An induced current flows in the coil, and its magnetic field opposes the change in flux: it pushes an approaching magnetic pole away and pulls a receding one back. The rotating magnet ring is therefore acted on by a braking magnetic force, even though nothing touches anything else. The same follows from conservation of energy: the light radiates energy that comes from the work done by the cyclist. If there were no braking force, the light would get energy from nothing. The first part of the claim is true.

*Light off.* The circuit is open, so no current flows in the coil and the coil does not brake. The changing field of the magnet ring does, however, produce eddy currents in the metal parts of the dynamo, for example in the iron core of the coil and in the metal hub. The eddy currents heat the metal and brake the rotation in accordance with Lenz's law. In addition, the magnetisation of the iron core reverses continuously, which takes a little energy.

*Conclusion.* The second part of the claim is not exactly true: the dynamo brakes the wheel a little even when the light is off. The braking is then much smaller than when the light is on, because the iron core is made of thin insulated sheets, in which the eddy currents stay small.

### e)
**Given:** power of the light $P_\text{lamp} = 3.0\,\text{W}$, efficiency of the dynamo $\eta = 60\,\% = 0.60$, speed $v = 5.236\,\text{m/s}$ (part b) and air resistance and rolling resistance together about $10\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the extra braking force $F$ of the dynamo and its significance
**Principle:** The dynamo converts the cyclist's work into electrical energy. The light receives the fraction of the power taken by the dynamo given by the efficiency, so the power taken by the dynamo is $P_\text{lamp} : \eta$. When the braking force acts on a wheel moving at speed $v$, the power of the work it does is $P = Fv$, as in an eddy-current brake.
**Formula:** $P_\text{in} = \dfrac{P_\text{lamp}}{\eta}$ and $P_\text{in} = Fv$, so $F = \dfrac{P_\text{lamp}}{\eta v}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the power is in watts and the speed in metres per second.
**Substitution:** $P_\text{in} = \dfrac{3.0\,\text{W}}{0.60} = 5.0\,\text{W}$ and $F = \dfrac{5.0\,\text{W}}{5.236\,\text{m/s}} = 0.955\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $F \approx 0.95\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the power of the light and the efficiency.
**Reasonableness:** Air resistance and rolling resistance at this speed total about $10\,\text{N}$, so the light adds about a tenth to the resistance. The difference can be felt, but it is small; for example, a $1\,\%$ uphill gradient adds about $9\,\text{N}$ for a $90\,\text{kg}$ cyclist and bicycle. When riding more slowly the same power requires a larger force, so the light is felt most on an uphill.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a the period can also be read from a single cycle, for example between two successive peaks: $37.5\,\text{ms} - 7.5\,\text{ms} = 30\,\text{ms}$.
- The answers to parts b and c can be given in metres per second; in part c, $11$–$12\,\text{km/h}$ is accepted.
- In part c the peak values can be compared: the light needs a peak value of $6.0\,\text{V} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 8.5\,\text{V}$, and $v_\text{min} = v \cdot 8.5\,\text{V} : 14\,\text{V}$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The period is read as the interval between zero crossings, which is only half a cycle, so the frequency is twice too large.
- The effective value is calculated by multiplying the peak value by $\sqrt{2}$ instead of dividing by it.
- In part b the frequency of the voltage is used as the rotation frequency of the wheel, so the speed comes out at over $250\,\text{km/h}$.
- The circumference is taken to be the product of the radius and $\pi$, or the product of the diameter and $2\pi$.
- In part d the braking effect is explained by friction, or it is said that the magnet attracts iron all the time in the same direction.
- In part e the power of the light is multiplied by the efficiency instead of divided by it.

## fy7-s3

### a)
**Given:** power fed into the line by the substation $P = 6.0\,\text{MW}$, transmission voltage $U = 20\,\text{kV}$, length of the line $25\,\text{km}$, two conductors, cross-sectional area $A = 150\,\text{mm}^2$ and resistivity $\rho = 2.82 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the power loss $P_\text{h}$ of the line and its share of the power fed into the line
**Principle:** The current flows to the mine along one conductor and returns along the other, so in the simplified two-wire model the length of the conductors in the circuit is $2 \cdot 25\,\text{km}$. The resistance is obtained from the resistivity, the length and the cross-sectional area. The transmitted power is the product of the voltage and the current, so the current is the power divided by the voltage. The power converted into heat in the resistance of the line is the product of the resistance and the square of the current.
**Formula:** $R = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}$, $I = \dfrac{P}{U}$ and $P_\text{h} = R I^2$
**Conversions:** $l = 2 \cdot 25\,\text{km} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m}$, $A = 150\,\text{mm}^2 = 150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$, $P = 6.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}$ and $U = 20 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}$
**Substitution:** $R = 2.82 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m} \cdot \dfrac{5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m}}{150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2} = 9.40\,\Omega$, $I = \dfrac{6.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}}{20 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}} = 300\,\text{A}$ and $P_\text{h} = 9.40\,\Omega \cdot (300\,\text{A})^2 = 846\,000\,\text{W}$. The share is $846\,\text{kW} : 6000\,\text{kW} = 0.141 = 14.1\,\%$.
**Answer:** $P_\text{h} \approx 850\,\text{kW}$, that is, about $14\,\%$ of the power fed into the line, two significant figures like the power and the voltage.
**Reasonableness:** The losses of the whole electricity grid in Finland are only a few per cent in total, so a loss of $14\,\%$ in a single line is far too large. ### b)
**Given:** the data of part a and the resistance $R = 9.40\,\Omega$, transmission voltage $U = 110\,\text{kV}$
**Find:** the power loss $P_\text{h}$
**Principle:** The same power is transmitted at $110 : 20 = 5.5$ times the voltage, so the current is $5.5$ times smaller. The power loss is proportional to the square of the current, so it decreases by a factor of $5.5^2 \approx 30$. The conductors are the same, so the resistance is the same as in part a.
**Formula:** $P_\text{h} = R I^2 = R\left(\dfrac{P}{U}\right)^2$

**Answer:** $P_\text{h} \approx 28\,\text{kW}$, that is, about $0.5\,\%$, two significant figures like the power.
**Reasonableness:** $846\,\text{kW} : 28.0\,\text{kW} \approx 30$, as the square of the ratio of the voltages predicted. The loss is now a fraction of a per cent, which is usual in electricity transmission.

### c)
**Given:** in the $20\,\text{kV}$ line the current $I = 300\,\text{A}$ and the resistance $R = 9.40\,\Omega$ (part a); permitted deviation $10\,\%$
**Find:** the voltage $U_\text{mine}$ left at the mine and whether it is acceptable
**Principle:** The substation maintains a voltage of $20\,\text{kV}$ at the start of the line. There is a voltage drop across the resistance of the conductors, which is the product of the resistance and the current. What is left at the mine is the difference between the transmission voltage and the voltage drop.
**Formula:** $U_\text{mine} = U - RI$
**Conversions:** $U = 20\,\text{kV} = 20\,000\,\text{V}$
**Substitution:** $RI = 9.40\,\Omega \cdot 300\,\text{A} = 2820\,\text{V}$ and $U_\text{mine} = 20\,000\,\text{V} - 2820\,\text{V} = 17\,180\,\text{V}$. The deviation is $2820\,\text{V} : 20\,000\,\text{V} \approx 14\,\%$.
**Answer:** $U_\text{mine} \approx 17.2\,\text{kV}$, which is about $14\,\%$ below the rated value. It is not acceptable, because the deviation is more than $10\,\%$. Three significant figures, so that the deviation can be compared with the limit.
**Reasonableness:** The product of the voltage drop and the current, $2820\,\text{V} \cdot 300\,\text{A} = 846\,\text{kW}$, is equal to the power loss of part a, as it should be. In the $110\,\text{kV}$ line the voltage drop would be only $9.40\,\Omega \cdot 54.5\,\text{A} \approx 0.51\,\text{kV}$, that is, less than $0.5\,\%$.

### d)
**Find:** whether the loss of the $20\,\text{kV}$ line can be made as small by using thicker conductors
**Principle:** The power loss is $P_\text{h} = RI^2$. At a voltage of $20\,\text{kV}$ the current is always $300\,\text{A}$, so the only way to reduce the loss is to reduce the resistance. The resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area, and the mass of the conductors is directly proportional to their volume.
**Model answer:** *Required thickness.* For the loss to be the same as in the $110\,\text{kV}$ line, the resistance must be $846\,\text{kW} : 28.0\,\text{kW} \approx 30$ times smaller. The cross-sectional area must therefore be $30.25 \cdot 150\,\text{mm}^2 \approx 4500\,\text{mm}^2$. The diameter of a solid round conductor would be $2\sqrt{4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2 : \pi} \approx 7.6\,\text{cm}$.

*Amount of aluminium.* The mass of the present conductors is $2700\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m} \cdot 150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2 \approx 20\,\text{t}$. The mass of the thick conductors would be $30$ times as much, about $610\,\text{t}$. The poles would have to carry all this, and the aluminium would cost many times the price of a transformer.

*Why it pays to raise the voltage.* Raising the voltage reduces the current in the same ratio, and the loss depends on the square of the current: $5.5$ times the voltage reduces the loss by a factor of about $30$. A thicker conductor reduces the loss only in the same ratio as the cross-sectional area increases, so to get the same result the cross-sectional area of the conductor has to be made $30$ times as large.

*Conclusion.* The suggestion would work in principle, but the line would be enormously heavy and expensive. That is why long transmission is done at high voltage and the voltage is stepped down with a transformer only near the place of use.

### e)
**Given:** distance between the conductors $r = 1.5\,\text{m}$, current in normal operation $I = 300\,\text{A}$ (part a) and in a short circuit $I_\text{sc} = 8.0\,\text{kA}$, $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$, cross-sectional area of a conductor $A = 150\,\text{mm}^2$ and density of aluminium $\rho_\text{Al} = 2700\,\text{kg/m}^3$
**Find:** whether the conductors attract or repel each other, the force per metre of conductor $F : l$ in both situations and its significance
**Principle:** The current flows to the mine along one conductor and returns along the other, so currents of equal magnitude flow in the conductors in opposite directions. Each conductor is a long straight conductor, and its magnetic field at the other conductor is obtained from the formula for the field of a straight conductor, because the conductors are much longer than the distance between them. The field is perpendicular to the other conductor, so a force $F = IlB$ acts on it. By the right-hand rule, currents in opposite directions repel each other. With alternating current the currents change direction at the same time, so they are always in opposite directions and the conductors repel each other all the time. The weight of a metre of conductor is obtained from its mass, which is the product of the density and the volume.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$ and $F = IlB$, so $\dfrac{F}{l} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I^2}{2\pi r}$. For the weight, $\dfrac{G}{l} = \rho_\text{Al} A g$.
**Conversions:** $I_\text{sc} = 8.0\,\text{kA} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{A}$ and $A = 150\,\text{mm}^2 = 150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$

**Answer:** The conductors repel each other. The force is about $0.012\,\text{N}$ in normal operation and about $8.5\,\text{N}$ in a short circuit for each metre of conductor, two significant figures like the distance and the short-circuit current. In normal operation the force does not matter, but in a short circuit it is about twice the weight of the conductor.
**Reasonableness:** In normal operation the force is only about $0.3\,\%$ of the weight of the conductor, about $4.0\,\text{N}$ per metre. In a short circuit the current is about $27$ times as large and the force grows as the square of the current, so it is about $710$ times as large: $8.53 : 3.97 \approx 2.1$ times the weight of the conductor. Conductors hanging between poles then swing apart and may swing back and slap together. That is why the spacing of the conductors is designed for a short circuit and the short circuit is interrupted in a fraction of a second. A current this large is possible only near the substation, where the resistance of the line up to the fault is small.

### b)
**Find:** why the slab glows orange-red although the peak of the spectrum is in the infrared
**Principle:** The spectrum of thermal radiation is continuous: the object radiates at all wavelengths, but a different amount at each. The eye sees only wavelengths of about $0.38$–$0.75\,\mu\text{m}$, and the colour of the glow depends on how the radiation is distributed in this range.
**Model answer:** In the graph the curve begins to rise already in the range of visible light, although it looks almost zero there. Visible light is therefore only a small part of the total radiation, but it is there. In the visible range the intensity increases steeply with increasing wavelength, so there is much more red and orange light than green or blue. That is why the glow looks orange-red and not white.

As a whole, the hot slab radiates with an enormous power, so even a small visible fraction is bright to the eye. The peak of the spectrum of an object at room temperature is at about $10\,\mu\text{m}$, and its share of visible light is vanishingly small, so it does not glow. If the slab were heated further, the peak would shift to shorter wavelengths and the glow would become yellower and brighter.

### d)
**Find:** whether the result of part a conflicts with the thermocouple reading $1210\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and what the difference may be due to
**Principle:** Two measurements conflict only if the difference between them is greater than their uncertainty. In addition, one must consider whether the methods measure the same thing and whether the assumptions of the model hold.
**Model answer:** *Uncertainty.* The peak of the spectrum is flat, so its position can be read from the graph to an accuracy of about $\pm 0.05\,\mu\text{m}$. The temperature is inversely proportional to the wavelength of the peak, so the relative uncertainty is the same: $0.05 : 2.0 = 2.5\,\%$, that is, $0.025 \cdot 1449\,\text{K} \approx 36\,\text{K}$. The difference from the thermocouple is $1210\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 1176\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 34\,\text{K}$, so it is within the reading accuracy.

*What the methods measure.* The pyrometer measures the radiation of the surface, the thermocouple the temperature of the interior of the slab. *Conclusion.* The results do not conflict: the difference is within the reading accuracy and is also explained by the cooling of the surface. The pyrometer cannot be said to read wrongly. For a more accurate comparison the pyrometer should be calibrated with an object at a known temperature.

### b)
**Given:** the graph of part a and its line
**Find:** the line density of the grating, that is, the number of lines per millimetre
**Principle:** According to the model, the gradient of the line is $\tfrac{1}{d}$, where $d$ is the grating spacing, that is, the distance between adjacent slits. The gradient is read from the line using two points far from each other; one of them can be the origin, because the line passes through it. The line density is a millimetre divided by the grating spacing.
**Reading the graph:** The point $(1300\,\text{nm};\ 0.780)$, which is far from the origin, is read from the line. The gradient is $0.780 : 1300\,\text{nm} = 6.00 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{nm}}$.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{1}{d} = \text{gradient}$, so $d = \dfrac{1}{\text{gradient}}$, and the line density is $N = \dfrac{1\,\text{mm}}{d}$.
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{mm} = 10^{6}\,\text{nm}$
**Substitution:** $d = \dfrac{1}{6.00 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{nm}}} = 1667\,\text{nm}$ and $N = \dfrac{10^{6}\,\text{nm}}{1667\,\text{nm}} = 600$
**Answer:** $d \approx 1.67\,\mu\text{m}$, so the grating has about $600$ lines per millimetre, three significant figures like the point read from the graph.
**Reasonableness:** Gratings for teaching use typically have $100$–$600$ lines per millimetre, so the result is a common one. The grating spacing is about three times the wavelength of visible light, so the first-order maxima are at angles of about $15$–$25^\circ$, as measured.

### c)
**Given:** the first-order maximum of the yellow line at the angle $\theta = 20.7^\circ$ and the grating spacing $d = 1667\,\text{nm}$ (part b)
**Find:** the wavelength $\lambda$ of the yellow line
**Principle:** The same grating and the same condition for maxima apply to all light, so an unknown wavelength is obtained from the measured angle when the grating spacing is known.
**Formula:** $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so when $k = 1$, $\lambda = d\sin\theta$.
**Conversions:** Not needed: the grating spacing is in nanometres, so the wavelength is obtained in nanometres.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = 1667\,\text{nm} \cdot \sin 20.7^\circ = 1667\,\text{nm} \cdot 0.3535 = 589.1\,\text{nm}$
**Answer:** $\lambda \approx 589\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures like the angle.
**Reasonableness:** The angle is between those of the red ($23.2^\circ$) and blue-green ($17.0^\circ$) lines, and the wavelength is correspondingly between theirs, in the range of yellow light. The same result is obtained from the graph: with $\sin 20.7^\circ \approx 0.354$, $k\lambda \approx 590\,\text{nm}$ is read from the line.

### d)
**Find:** whether the conclusion about sodium is justified and how the measurement could be improved
**Principle:** Two alternatives can be told apart by a measurement only if the difference between them is greater than the uncertainty of the measurement. The uncertainty of the angle is carried over to the wavelength through the grating equation.
**Model answer:** *Uncertainty.* The reading accuracy of the angle is $0.1^\circ$. We calculate the wavelengths from the angles $20.6^\circ$ and $20.8^\circ$: $1667\,\text{nm} \cdot \sin 20.6^\circ \approx 586.5\,\text{nm}$ and $1667\,\text{nm} \cdot \sin 20.8^\circ \approx 592.0\,\text{nm}$. The uncertainty of the wavelength is therefore about $\pm 3\,\text{nm}$, that is, $\lambda = (589 \pm 3)\,\text{nm}$. The grating spacing also has a small uncertainty, which only adds to this.

*Evaluating the conclusion.* The helium line $587.56\,\text{nm}$ and the sodium lines $588.99\,\text{nm}$ and $589.59\,\text{nm}$ all lie between $586$ and $592\,\text{nm}$. The difference between helium and sodium, about $1.4\,\text{nm}$, is smaller than the uncertainty. In terms of angles the difference is only $20.64^\circ$ against $20.70^\circ$, that is, about $0.05^\circ$, which is below the reading accuracy of the goniometer. The measurement therefore does not distinguish sodium from helium, and the group's conclusion is not justified by this measurement. The lamp may contain sodium, but that does not follow from this measurement.

*Improvements.* From the condition for maxima $\sin\theta = \dfrac{k\lambda}{d}$ we see that when the wavelength changes, the sine of the angle changes $k$ times as much. In the second order the maxima of helium and sodium are at $44.84^\circ$ and $44.98^\circ$, so the difference is about $0.14^\circ$, almost three times that in the first order, and the uncertainty in the wavelength is about $\pm 1\,\text{nm}$. The second order would make it easier to tell them apart, but on its own it is barely sufficient: the angular difference is only slightly larger than the reading accuracy of $0.1^\circ$, and $\pm 1\,\text{nm}$ is almost as large as the $1.4\,\text{nm}$ separation of the lines. A grating with more lines, in which the angles are larger, or a more accurate goniometer is therefore also needed. In addition, the angle can be measured on both sides of the central maximum and half of the angle between them used, which removes the error caused by tilting of the grating.

### e)
**Find:** whether either observation is wrong when in a prism violet and in a grating red is deviated the most
**Principle:** A grating and a prism separate colours by different phenomena. In a grating the direction is determined by the interference of waves, in a prism by refraction, which depends on the refractive index.
**Model answer:** *Grating.* A maximum forms in the direction in which the waves from adjacent slits meet in the same phase, that is, their path difference $d\sin\theta$ is a multiple of the wavelength. A longer wavelength requires a larger path difference and therefore a larger angle, so red light is deviated the most and violet the least, as in the table.

*Prism.* Light is refracted when it enters the glass and when it leaves it. The refractive index of glass depends on the wavelength (dispersion): it is greater for violet light than for red, because violet light travels more slowly in glass. Violet is therefore refracted the most.

*Conclusion.* The claim is wrong: both observations are correct. The colours are separated by different phenomena, in the grating by interference and in the prism by refraction, so the order of the colours can be the opposite.

### Yleisiä virheitä

- The angles are plotted in the graph in degrees, although the model is linear in the sine of the angle.
- The gradient is read from a single measured point instead of the fitted line.
- The line density is confused with the grating spacing, or nanometres are converted incorrectly to millimetres.
- In part d the result $589\,\text{nm}$ is accepted as sodium without comparing the difference between helium and sodium with the uncertainty of the measurement.
