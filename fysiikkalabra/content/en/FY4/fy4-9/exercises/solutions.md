## fy4-9-L1-01
**Given:** mass of the puck $m = 0.17\,\text{kg}$, velocity $v = 30\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the momentum of the puck $p$ in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Momentum is the product of mass and velocity. It is a vector with the direction of the velocity; here its magnitude is asked.
**Formula:** $p = mv$
**Conversions:** The mass and the velocity are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $p = 0.17\,\text{kg} \cdot 30\,\text{m/s} = 5.1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $5.1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.17\,\text{kg}$ and the velocity $30\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate with round numbers: $0.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 30\,\text{m/s} = 6\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. A light puck has a small momentum even though its velocity is large: the momentum of a walking person is about $100\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.

## fy4-9-L1-02
**Given:** pushing force $F = 12\,\text{N}$, time of action $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the impulse of the pushing force $I$ in $\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Principle:** The impulse of a constant force is the product of the force and the time it acts. The impulse has the direction of the force, here the direction of the push.
**Formula:** $I = F\,\Delta t$
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the time in seconds, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $I = 12\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{s} = 6.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, two significant figures like the force $12\,\text{N}$ and the time $0.50\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Half of $12\,\text{N}$ is $6\,\text{N}$, so the product is correct. If the mass of the cart were $15\,\text{kg}$ and there were no friction, its velocity would increase by $6.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s} : 15\,\text{kg} = 0.40\,\text{m/s}$, which fits a short push.

## fy4-9-L1-05
**Given:** impulse $I = 0.15\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, contact time $\Delta t = 0.0010\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the average force of the racket $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** Impulse is force times time. When the force changes during contact, the impulse divided by the contact time gives the average force.
**Formula:** $I = F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{I}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** The time is in seconds, so no conversions are needed; $\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{s}} = \text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{0.15\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{0.0010\,\text{s}} = 150\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $150\,\text{N}$, that is, $1.5 \cdot 10^2\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the impulse $0.15\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$ and the time $0.0010\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Dividing by a thousandth of a second multiplies the number by a thousand: $0.15 \cdot 1000 = 150$. The force is large compared with a light shuttlecock, but it acts for only a thousandth of a second, as in hits generally.

## fy4-9-L2-01
**Given:** mass of the car $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 54\,\text{km/h}$, final velocity $v = 0$, stopping time $\Delta t = 3.0\,\text{s}$; air resistance is neglected
**Find:** the average braking force $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{kN}$
**Principle:** The road is horizontal, so the weight of the car and the normal force of the road cancel each other, and the horizontal net force is the braking friction force of the road. By the impulse–momentum principle its impulse equals the change in the car's momentum. The positive direction is the direction of motion of the car.
**Formula:** $F\,\Delta t = mv - mv_0 = -mv_0$, so the magnitude of the force is $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{mv_0}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $v_0 = 54\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{54\,\text{m}}{3.6\,\text{s}} = 15\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{1200\,\text{kg} \cdot 15\,\text{m/s}}{3.0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{18\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{3.0\,\text{s}} = 6000\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the velocity $54\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $3.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The sign of the force is negative, that is, it points against the motion, as a braking force should. The force is about half of the weight of the car, $1200\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 12\,\text{kN}$, which corresponds to ordinary braking on a dry road.

**Answer:** $58\,\text{km/h}$, two significant figures like the mass $75\,\text{kg}$ and the time $0.12\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The velocity corresponds to an ordinary speed on a country road or a main city street, and seat belt tests use velocities of the same order of magnitude. **Substitution:** $\Delta F = \dfrac{0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m/s}}{0.020\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{1.2\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{0.020\,\text{s}} = 60\,\text{N}$

## fy4-9-L1-06
**Find:** the sign and direction of the momentum $\overline{p}$ of the cart when the cart moves to the left and the positive direction is to the right
**Principle:** The momentum $\overline{p} = m\overline{v}$ is a vector with the direction of the velocity, because mass is always positive. In one dimension the direction is expressed by the sign: a momentum pointing in the positive direction is positive, and one pointing in the opposite direction is negative.
**Model answer:** The correct statement is the one saying that the momentum is negative, because its direction is the direction of the velocity, that is, against the chosen direction. The cart moves to the left, that is, against the positive direction, so $v < 0$ and $p = mv < 0$. The statement about a positive momentum forgets that the sign of the velocity carries over to the product: the mass is positive, but the velocity is not. The statement that the momentum always points in the chosen positive direction is wrong, because the direction follows the velocity and not the choice of direction. The statement about zero is wrong, because the momentum is zero only when the velocity is zero; whether the motion is horizontal does not affect it.

**Given:** mass of the ball $m = 0.60\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; times from the table (example data): release $0.000\,\text{s}$, hits the floor $0.500\,\text{s}$, leaves the floor $0.512\,\text{s}$, highest point $0.912\,\text{s}$; air resistance is neglected

**Principle:** In the air the acceleration of the ball is $g$ downwards, so the velocity before the hit follows from the falling time and the velocity after the bounce from the rising time, because the ball is at rest at the highest point. The positive direction is up. During the contact the force of the floor $N$ upwards and the weight $G = mg$ downwards act on the ball, so the net force is $N - G$. By the impulse–momentum principle its impulse equals the change in the ball's momentum.
**Formula:** $v_1 = -g\,t_\text{fall}$ and $v_2 = g\,t_\text{rise}$; $(N_\text{avg} - mg)\,\Delta t = mv_2 - mv_1$, so $N_\text{avg} = mg + \dfrac{m(v_2 - v_1)}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** The falling time is $0.500\,\text{s}$, the contact time $\Delta t = 0.512\,\text{s} - 0.500\,\text{s} = 0.012\,\text{s}$ and the rising time $0.912\,\text{s} - 0.512\,\text{s} = 0.400\,\text{s}$.

Explanation for c: the change in momentum of the clay ball is $2.9\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, but the momentum of the rebounding ball also changes upwards to $+2.4\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, so its change $5.3\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ is greater and a greater force is needed in the same time.

## fy4-9-L1-09
**Find:** the order of magnitude of the average force $F_\text{avg}$ when the roof stops a $20\,\text{g}$ hailstone from a speed of $20\,\text{m/s}$ in a time of $1.0\,\text{ms}$
**Principle:** During the impact the force of the roof acts on the hailstone, and the effect of the weight is ignored, so the force of the roof is the net force. According to the impulse principle, its impulse equals the change in the momentum of the hailstone. The hailstone stops and does not bounce, so the magnitude of the change in momentum is $m v$. For the order of magnitude, the mass and the time still have to be converted to SI units.
**Model answer:** Conversions: $m = 20\,\text{g} = 0.020\,\text{kg}$ and $\Delta t = 1.0\,\text{ms} = 0.0010\,\text{s}$.

From the impulse principle $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = m v$, so

$F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{m v}{\Delta t}$

$m v = 0.020\,\text{kg} \cdot 20\,\text{m/s}$, that is $m v = 0.40\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$

$F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{0.40\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{0.0010\,\text{s}}$, that is $F_\text{avg} = 400\,\text{N}$

The nearest option is $400\,\text{N}$. Reasonableness: the weight of the hailstone is $0.020\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.20\,\text{N}$, about $2000$ times smaller than the force of the roof, so ignoring the weight was justified. The other options come from errors: $0.4\,\text{N}$ when milliseconds are not converted to seconds, $4000\,\text{N}$ when the kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = 4.0\,\text{J}$ is used instead of the momentum, and $400\,000\,\text{N}$ when the mass is substituted in grams.

## fy4-9-L2-08
**Given:** from the graph the force as a function of time; the greatest force $F_\text{max} = 900\,\text{N}$ and the contact ends at $\Delta t = 12\,\text{ms}$
**Find:** the average force $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The force changes, so the impulse is read from the area between the graph and the time axis. The average force is the constant force that would give the same impulse in the same time, so $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = I$.
**Reading the graph:** The force rises steadily from zero to $900\,\text{N}$ by $6\,\text{ms}$ and falls to zero at $12\,\text{ms}$. The region is a triangle with base $12\,\text{ms}$ and height $900\,\text{N}$.
**Formula:** $I = \tfrac{1}{2} F_\text{max}\,\Delta t$ and $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{I}{\Delta t}$, so $F_\text{avg} = \tfrac{1}{2} F_\text{max}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta t = 12\,\text{ms} = 0.012\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $I = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 900\,\text{N} \cdot 0.012\,\text{s} = 5.4\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$ and $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{5.4\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{0.012\,\text{s}} = 450\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $450\,\text{N}$, two significant figures ($4.5\cdot 10^2\,\text{N}$), because the peak value $900\,\text{N}$ and the duration $12\,\text{ms}$ are read from the graph to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The average is half the peak, as it should be for a force that rises and falls steadily. It is smaller than $900\,\text{N}$ and larger than zero.
