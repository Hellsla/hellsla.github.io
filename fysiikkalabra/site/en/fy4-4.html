FY4 · Chapter 5

Newton's second law

Why does a fully loaded truck accelerate away from the traffic lights more slowly than an empty one, even though the engine is the same?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how the acceleration depends on the net force and the mass.
  • work out the direction of the net force from the acceleration, also in slowing-down motion.
  • calculate an acceleration or a force using the free-body diagram and the equation of motion $\Sigma F = ma$.

Prior knowledge

  • Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit of time, $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion
  • When the forces acting on an object cancel each other, the object is at rest or moves at constant velocitychapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

Two identical timber trucks stand side by side at the traffic lights. One is empty, the other is full of logs. The light turns green, and both drivers accelerate with equal effort. Within a few seconds the empty truck is clearly ahead.

What decides how quickly the velocity of an object changes, and why does the same engine not get the loaded truck up to speed as quickly?

Optional Recall: uniform motion
A puck slides straight ahead at constant velocity on horizontal ice. Friction and air resistance are so small that they are ignored.Which forces act on the puck?

  • Nothing pushes the puck while it slides, so there is no forward force. The puck keeps moving without a force, because the velocity changes only when the net force is not zero.

  • The puck does not sink through the ice, so the ice supports it upward. If nothing cancelled the weight, the puck would accelerate downward.

  • Correct. Only the Earth and the ice act on the puck. In the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws you learned that uniform motion needs no forward force: when the net force is zero, the velocity stays the same.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    The net force changes the velocity

    In the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws you learned that an object's velocity stays unchanged when the forces acting on it cancel each other. The object is then at rest or moves in a straight line at constant velocity. When the forces do not cancel, the velocity changes: the object speeds up, slows down or turns. What matters is not any single force but the combined effect of all the forces acting on the object, the net force $\Sigma\overline{F}$. It is the vector sum of the forces, not just one force added to another.

    On the left of the figure a box rests on the floor. The weight $\overline{G}$ and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the floor are equal and opposite, so the net force is zero. On the right the box is pulled by a rope. The vertical forces still cancel, but horizontally the tension $\overline{T}$ in the rope is greater than the friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the floor. The net force points to the right, and the box accelerates to the right.

    Mass resists a change in velocity

    Measurements with a cart reveal two regularities. When the net force doubles, the acceleration doubles: the acceleration is directly proportional to the net force. When the same net force acts on a cart with twice the mass, the acceleration is halved. Mass therefore tells us about the inertia of an object, that is, how hard it is to change its velocity. In the graph each mass gives a straight line through the origin, and the line of the heavier cart is half as steep.

    This gives the answer to the opening question. The engine turns the driving wheels, and the road pushes the tyres forward. When this force is equally large for the loaded and the empty truck, the much greater mass of the loaded truck makes its acceleration small. The weight of the load does not pull the truck backward as such, because the normal force from the road cancels it. The heavy load does increase the rolling resistance of the tyres, but the main reason for the slow acceleration is the large mass.

    Newton's second law

    Newton's second law, also called the fundamental law of dynamics, gathers the observations into one equation:

    $$\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$$

    where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the vector sum of the forces acting on the object, the net force ($\text{N}$), $m$ is the mass of the object ($\text{kg}$) and $\overline{a}$ is its acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). The unit of force is defined by this law: one newton is the force that gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of $1\,\text{m/s}^2$. So $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$.

    The net force points in the direction of the acceleration

    The law is a vector equation: the net force and the acceleration always point in the same direction. The direction of the velocity can be another one. In the figure a crane holds a crate on a rope. When the crate rises while speeding up, the force of the rope is greater than the weight and the net force points up. When the crate rises while slowing down, the force of the rope is smaller than the weight: the net force and the acceleration point down, even though the crate still moves upward. At rest and when rising at constant velocity, the force of the rope and the weight are equal.

    First the forces, then the equation

    When the law is used in a calculation, the steps are always taken in the same order. Choose the object and draw its free-body diagram, in which every force has a cause: the Earth, a rope, a surface or a puller. Choose a positive direction, usually the direction of the acceleration. Only then write the net force from the forces, with signs, and set it equal to $ma$. For the crate of the crane, when up is positive, we get $T - G = ma$. In the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces the same method extends to slanted forces.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a force always acts in the direction of motion and that the net force points in the direction of motion. The net force, however, points in the direction of the acceleration. When air resistance is ignored, a ball thrown upward keeps rising even though only its weight, which points down, acts on it, and that is why it slows down. In a slowing motion the net force points against the velocity. A second mistake is to think that a constant force keeps up a constant velocity. When the net force is constant and not zero, the velocity changes steadily. A third mistake is to confuse mass and weight. On a horizontal track the weight does not resist the motion, because the normal force cancels it: it is the mass that makes the acceleration smaller.

    Summary

    • When the net force is zero, the velocity does not change. When it is not zero, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.
    • Newton's second law is $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$. The acceleration is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass.
    • The net force points in the direction of the acceleration, which is not always the direction of the velocity: in a slowing motion it points against the velocity.
    • First the free-body diagram is drawn and a positive direction is chosen, then $\Sigma F = ma$ is written with signs.
    • The unit of force is $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$.

      5Examples

      Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

      Example 1: A crane lifting a panel

      A construction crane lifts a concrete slab of mass $480\,\text{kg}$. The tension in the rope is $5.20\,\text{kN}$. What is the acceleration of the slab, and in which direction does it point? Air resistance is ignored.

      Step 1/8

      Given and find. $m = 480\,\text{kg}$, $T = 5.20\,\text{kN}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the acceleration $a$ and its direction.

      Step 2/8

      Principle. Choose the concrete slab as the object. The Earth (weight) and the rope (tension) act on it. By Newton's second law, the acceleration is determined by the sum of these forces. The force of the rope alone does not accelerate the slab, because most of it is used to hold up the weight.

      Step 3/8

      Free-body diagram. The slab is drawn as a rectangle. The weight $\overline{G}$ starts from the centre and points down, and the tension $\overline{T}$ starts from the top surface and points up. The positive direction is chosen to be up.

      Step 4/8

      Rearranged formula. From the diagram we write the net force $T - mg$ and solve for the acceleration:

      $$\begin{aligned} &T - mg = ma \\ &a = \frac{T - mg}{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 5/8

      Unit conversions. The tension is converted to newtons:

      $5.20\,\text{kN}$$= 5.20\cdot 1000\,\text{N}$
      $= 5200\,\text{N}$

      Step 6/8

      Substitution. First we calculate the weight and then the acceleration:

      $$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{T - mg}{m} \\ mg &= 480\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \\ \phantom{mg} &= 4708.8\,\text{N} \\ a &= \frac{5200\,\text{N} - 4708.8\,\text{N}}{480\,\text{kg}} \\ \phantom{a} &\approx 1.02\,\text{m/s}^2 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 7/8

      Result. The acceleration is $a \approx 1.02\,\text{m/s}^2$ upward. It has three significant figures, like the tension $5.20\,\text{kN}$ and the free-fall acceleration $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The whole number $480\,\text{kg}$ is taken as an exact value, so it does not limit the precision.

      Step 8/8

      Reasonableness. The force of the rope is only about ten percent greater than the weight $4.71\,\text{kN}$, so the acceleration should also be about a tenth of the free-fall acceleration: $0.1\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 1\,\text{m/s}^2$. If the weight were forgotten, the result would be $5200\,\text{N} : 480\,\text{kg} \approx 10.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. That would be wrong, because besides the force of the rope the weight acts on the element downward, and the acceleration is set by the sum of these forces. For example, when the element rises at a constant velocity, the rope pulls with a force equal to the weight and the forces cancel, but a calculation that forgets the weight would still give an acceleration of about $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

      Example 2: Choose the correct free-body diagram

      A crane lowers a crate downward on a rope. Just before the ground the crate slows down and stops.In which diagram is the free-body diagram of the crate correct during the slowing down? Air resistance is ignored. Each wrong diagram has one mistake.

      • Motion does not cause a force. The force $\overline{F}$ has no cause: only the Earth and the rope act on the crate. Otherwise the diagram would be correct.

      • Here the net force points down, in the direction of the velocity. The velocity of the crate would then increase. During the slowing down the acceleration and the net force point up, against the motion.

      • Arrows of equal length mean that the net force is zero. The crate would then move at constant velocity and would not slow down. The acceleration of a slowing crate points up, so the force of the rope must be greater than the weight.

      • Correct. The crate moves down but slows down, so its acceleration points up. The net force must point in the direction of the acceleration: $T - G = ma$, so $T > G$.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      A passenger car of mass $1400\,\text{kg}$ is driving on a horizontal road at a velocity of $72\,\text{km/h}$. The driver brakes, and the car stops, slowing down uniformly, in $4.0\,\text{s}$.

      What is the net force acting on the car during the braking? Give the answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      The net force is obtained from the law $\Sigma F = ma$. What do you need to know first, and where do you get it?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time: $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. First convert the velocity to $\text{m/s}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $72\,\text{km/h} = 20\,\text{m/s}$, so

      $a$$= \dfrac{0 - 20\,\text{m/s}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= -5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Multiply by the mass.

      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 1400\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 72\,\text{km/h}$, final velocity $v = 0$, time $\Delta t = 4.0\,\text{s}$, the car slows down uniformly

      Find

      the magnitude of the net force $\Sigma F$ in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The velocity of the car changes steadily, so its acceleration is constant. By Newton's second law the net force is the mass times the acceleration, and it points in the direction of the acceleration, that is backward.

      Free-body diagram

      The car as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of motion. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the road points up from the tyres, equally long. The friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the road points backward from the contact point of the tyres. There is no force pushing forward.

      Formula

      $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$ and $\Sigma F = ma$, so

      $\Sigma F$$= \dfrac{m(v - v_0)}{\Delta t}$

      Conversions

      $72\,\text{km/h}$$= 72 : 3.6\,\text{m/s}$
      $= 20\,\text{m/s}$

      Substitution

      $\Sigma F$$= \dfrac{1400\,\text{kg}\cdot(0 - 20\,\text{m/s})}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= 1400\,\text{kg}\cdot(-5.0\,\text{m/s}^2)$
      $= -7000\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $7000\,\text{N}$

      Or $7.0\,\text{kN}$ backward, two significant figures like the time $4.0\,\text{s}$ and the velocity $72\,\text{km/h}$. The minus sign tells us that the force points against the motion.

      Reasonableness

      The weight of the car is about $14\,\text{kN}$, so the braking force is about half of the weight. The deceleration $5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ is about half of the free-fall acceleration, which is an ordinary firm braking on a dry road. The force points backward even though the car moves forward, because the car is slowing down.

        Line 1: $\Sigma F = F - F_\mu$ is correct, because friction points against the pushing force.

        Line 2: $45\,\text{N} - 9.0\,\text{N} = 36\,\text{N}$ is correct.

        Line 3: $G = 18\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, that is $G = 176.58\,\text{N} \approx 177\,\text{N}$, is calculated correctly, although the weight is not needed.

        Line 4 is wrong, because the net force is divided by the weight instead of the mass. The correct calculation:

        $a = \dfrac{\Sigma F}{m}$

        $a = \dfrac{36\,\text{N}}{18\,\text{kg}}$, that is $a = 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

        The acceleration is $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the direction of the pushing force, two significant figures like the forces and the mass. Check with units: $\text{N} : \text{kg} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2 : \text{kg}$, that is $\text{m/s}^2$, but the division $\text{N} : \text{N}$ on line 4 does not give the unit of acceleration at all.       1. Exercise 5-17

        A student of mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$ stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator. The scale measures the force with which it supports the student and shows it in kilograms, that is, divides the force by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. When the elevator starts to move, the scale shows $54.0\,\text{kg}$ for a moment.

        What is the acceleration of the elevator, and in which direction does it point?

        m/s²
        Answer

        $0.98\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Show the solution

        Given

        mass of the student $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$, reading of the scale $54.0\,\text{kg}$, so the normal force of the scale is $N = 54.0\,\text{kg}\cdot g$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Find

        the acceleration $a$ of the elevator (and the student) in $\text{m/s}^2$ and its direction

        Principle

        The scale does not measure mass but the normal force with which it pushes on the student. The student accelerates with the elevator, so by Newton's second law the difference between the normal force and the weight is $ma$.

        Free-body diagram

        The student as a rectangle, the positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the scale points up from the bottom surface. The reading is smaller than the mass, so $\overline{N}$ is shorter than $\overline{G}$ and the net force points down.

        Formula

        $N - mg = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{N - mg}{m}$

        Conversions

        The reading of the scale is converted to a force:

        $N$$= 54.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
        $= 529.74\,\text{N}$

        Substitution

        $a$$= \dfrac{529.74\,\text{N} - 60.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{60.0\,\text{kg}}$
        $= \dfrac{529.74\,\text{N} - 588.6\,\text{N}}{60.0\,\text{kg}}$
        $= -0.981\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Answer

        $0.98\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Downward, two significant figures, because the difference of the readings, $6.0\,\text{kg}$, is known to two figures.

        Reasonableness

        The scale shows ten percent too little, so the acceleration is a tenth of the free-fall acceleration, $0.1\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The minus sign means an acceleration downward: the elevator starts moving down. The same reading would also be shown if an elevator moving upward were slowing down.

      2. Exercise 5-18

        An empty timber truck of mass $10\,\text{t}$ accelerates uniformly from rest to a velocity of $36\,\text{km/h}$ in $8.0\,\text{s}$. Fully loaded, its mass is $25\,\text{t}$. Assume that the net force during the acceleration is the same as for the empty truck.

        How long does it take the loaded truck to accelerate from rest to $36\,\text{km/h}$?

        s
        Answer

        $20\,\text{s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        mass of the empty truck $m_1 = 10\,\text{t}$, of the loaded truck $m_2 = 25\,\text{t}$, change in velocity $\Delta v = 36\,\text{km/h}$, time of the empty truck $\Delta t_1 = 8.0\,\text{s}$, the same net force

        Find

        the acceleration time $\Delta t_2$ of the loaded truck in $\text{s}$

        Principle

        The net force is obtained from the acceleration of the empty truck by Newton's second law. The same net force gives a greater mass a smaller acceleration, and with a smaller acceleration the same change in velocity takes longer.

        Formula

        $\Sigma F$$= m_1 \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t_1}$
        $\Sigma F$$= m_2 \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t_2}$

        so

        $\Delta t_2$$= \dfrac{m_2 \Delta v}{\Sigma F}$

        Conversions

        $36\,\text{km/h}$$= 36 : 3.6\,\text{m/s}$
        $= 10\,\text{m/s}$

        $10\,\text{t} = 10\,000\,\text{kg}$ and $25\,\text{t} = 25\,000\,\text{kg}$

        Substitution

        $\Sigma F$$= 10\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot\dfrac{10\,\text{m/s}}{8.0\,\text{s}}$
        $= 12\,500\,\text{N}$
        $\Delta t_2$$= \dfrac{25\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot 10\,\text{m/s}}{12\,500\,\text{N}}$
        $= 20\,\text{s}$

        Answer

        $20\,\text{s}$

        Two significant figures like the time $8.0\,\text{s}$.

        Reasonableness

        The mass grows $2.5$ times, so the acceleration becomes $2.5$ times smaller and the time becomes $2.5$ times longer: $2.5\cdot 8.0\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{s}$. This explains why a loaded truck is left behind at the traffic lights.

      3. Exercise 5-19

        A child slides on a sled on horizontal snow. The total mass of the sled and the child is $30\,\text{kg}$, and the velocity is $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ at the start. Nobody pulls or pushes the sled. The friction force from the snow is $18\,\text{N}$, and air resistance is not taken into account.

        How long does it take before the sled stops?

        s
        Answer

        $6.7\,\text{s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        $m = 30\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, final velocity $v = 0$, friction force $F_\mu = 18\,\text{N}$

        Find

        the time $\Delta t$ in $\text{s}$ after which the sled stops

        Principle

        The sled is neither pulled nor pushed, so the only horizontal force is the friction, which points against the motion. The friction is constant, so by Newton's second law the deceleration is constant, and the stopping time follows from the change in velocity.

        Free-body diagram

        The sled and the child as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of motion. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the snow points up from the bottom surface, equally long. The friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the snow points backward from the back corner of the bottom surface. There is no force pushing forward.

        Formula

        $-F_\mu = ma$ and $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta t = \dfrac{m(v_0 - v)}{F_\mu}$

        Conversions

        The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{kg}\cdot(4.0\,\text{m/s} - 0)}{18\,\text{N}}$
        $= \dfrac{120\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{18\,\text{N}}$
        $= 6.67\,\text{s}$

        Answer

        $6.7\,\text{s}$

        Two significant figures like the data $4.0\,\text{m/s}$, $18\,\text{N}$ and $30\,\text{kg}$.

        Reasonableness

        The deceleration is $18\,\text{N} : 30\,\text{kg} = 0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the speed decreases by $0.6\,\text{m/s}$ every second, and $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ takes about seven seconds. The sled keeps moving forward the whole time even though the net force points backward: it just slows down.

      4. Exercise 5-20

        Students investigate how the acceleration $a$ of a cart pulled along a table depends on the horizontal pulling force $F$. The mass of the cart stayed the same. A constant friction force from the surface acts on the moving cart. The table is an example data set.

        a) Draw the $(F, a)$ graph of the measurement points and fit a straight line to the points. Justify why the line does not pass through the origin.

        b) Determine the mass of the cart from the slope of the line.

        c) Determine the magnitude of the friction force from the point where the line crosses the $F$-axis. Enter the magnitude of the friction force in the answer box.

        Acceleration of the cart with different pulling forces (example data)
        $F\,(\text{N})$$a\,(\text{m/s}^2)$
        2.00.40
        3.00.91
        4.01.42
        5.01.88
        6.02.41

        Answer

        $1.2\,\text{N}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        the points $(F, a)$ of the table for $F = 2.0 \ldots 6.0\,\text{N}$; the mass is constant, the friction force is constant

        Find

        the magnitude of the friction force $F_\mu$ in $\text{N}$ (and also the mass $m$ of the cart)

        Principle

        On the moving cart the pulling force acts in the direction of motion and friction against it, so by Newton's second law $F - F_\mu = ma$, that is, $a = \dfrac{1}{m}F - \dfrac{F_\mu}{m}$. The graph is a straight line whose slope is $\dfrac{1}{m}$. The line does not pass through the origin, because part of the pulling force is used to overcome friction: the acceleration is zero when $F = F_\mu$, so the line crosses the $F$-axis at the value of the friction force.

        Reading the graph

        A straight line is fitted between the points. From the line we read two points far from each other: $(2.0\,\text{N}, 0.41\,\text{m/s}^2)$ and $(6.0\,\text{N}, 2.40\,\text{m/s}^2)$. The slope is calculated from them, and the crossing point on the $F$-axis is obtained from the first point with the slope.

        Formula

        $k$$= \dfrac{\Delta a}{\Delta F}$
        $= \dfrac{1}{m}$

        so $m = \dfrac{1}{k}$, and at the crossing point $a = 0$, so $F_\mu = F_1 - \dfrac{a_1}{k}$

        Conversions

        The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $k$$= \dfrac{2.40\,\text{m/s}^2 - 0.41\,\text{m/s}^2}{6.0\,\text{N} - 2.0\,\text{N}}$
        $= 0.4975\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}}$
        $m$$= \dfrac{1}{0.4975\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}}}$
        $= 2.01\,\text{kg}$
        $F_\mu$$= 2.0\,\text{N} - \dfrac{0.41\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.4975\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}}}$
        $= 1.18\,\text{N}$

        Answer

        $1.2\,\text{N}$

        ($1.18\,\text{N}$ before rounding), two significant figures. The mass of the cart is about $2.0\,\text{kg}$. The points are not exactly on the line because of the random variation of the measurement, so the result is an estimate.

        Reasonableness

        When $F = 4.0\,\text{N}$, the net force is

        $4.0\,\text{N} - 1.18\,\text{N}$$= 2.82\,\text{N}$
        $a$$= 2.82\,\text{N} : 2.01\,\text{kg}$
        $= 1.40\,\text{m/s}^2$

        which matches the value $1.42\,\text{m/s}^2$ of the table. Friction is small compared with the weight $2.01\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 19.7\,\text{N}$, about $6\,\%$ of it, which fits a smooth surface.

      5. Exercise 5-21

        A person of mass $70\,\text{kg}$ stands in an elevator. The elevator starts upward from rest and moves in three phases. The accelerations of phases 1 and 2 have been measured with an acceleration sensor (example data), and the positive direction is up.

        In phase 3 the elevator slows down uniformly and stops at the moment $t = 13.0\,\text{s}$.

        a) Calculate the greatest velocity of the elevator.

        b) Calculate the acceleration of the elevator in phase 3.

        c) Draw the free-body diagram of the person in phase 3 and calculate the normal force from the floor on the person. Compare it with the weight of the person and justify why the normal force differs from the weight. Enter the normal force of part c) in the answer box.

        Acceleration of the elevator in phases 1 and 2 (example data)
        Phase$t_{\text{start}}\,(\text{s})$$t_{\text{end}}\,(\text{s})$$a\,(\text{m/s}^2)$
        10.03.01.0
        23.09.00.0
        N
        Answer

        $630\,\text{N}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        $m = 70\,\text{kg}$, phase 1: $0 \ldots 3.0\,\text{s}$, $a_1 = 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$; phase 2: $3.0 \ldots 9.0\,\text{s}$, $a_2 = 0$; phase 3: $9.0 \ldots 13.0\,\text{s}$, uniform slowing down to a stop; initial velocity $0$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Find

        the greatest velocity $v_{\max}$, the acceleration $a_3$ and the normal force $N$ from the floor in phase 3

        Principle

        In uniformly accelerated motion the change of velocity is $\Delta v = a\Delta t$. The elevator accelerates only in phase 1, so the greatest velocity is at the end of phase 1 and it is kept in phase 2. In phase 3 the elevator slows down to a stop. The forces on the person are from the Earth (weight) and from the floor (normal force), and by Newton's second law their sum is $ma$.

        Free-body diagram

        The person as a rectangle, the positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the floor points up from the bottom surface.         $v_{\max}$$= a_1\Delta t_1$
        $a_3$$= \dfrac{0 - v_{\max}}{\Delta t_3}$

        $N - mg = ma_3$, so $N = m(g + a_3)$

        Conversions

        The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The duration of phase 3 is

        $\Delta t_3$$= 13.0\,\text{s} - 9.0\,\text{s}$
        $= 4.0\,\text{s}$

        Substitution

        $v_{\max}$$= 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{s}$
        $= 3.0\,\text{m/s}$
        $a_3$$= \dfrac{0 - 3.0\,\text{m/s}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$

        $= 634.2\,\text{N}$

        $630\,\text{N}$

        ($634\,\text{N}$ before rounding), two significant figures like the mass $70\,\text{kg}$ and the acceleration. The greatest velocity is $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the acceleration in phase 3 is $0.75\,\text{m/s}^2$ downward.         Reasonableness

        The elevator stops: $3.0\,\text{m/s} - 0.75\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 4.0\,\text{s} = 0$. The difference between the weight and the normal force is

        $686.7\,\text{N} - 634.2\,\text{N}$
        $= 52.5\,\text{N}$

      2. Concept task

      A ball has been thrown straight up, and it is still rising. Air resistance is ignored.Which statement about the net force on the ball is correct during the rise?

      3. Calculation task

      A shopping trolley of mass $18\,\text{kg}$ is pushed on a horizontal floor with a force of $30\,\text{N}$. The rolling resistance of the wheels and other friction slow the trolley with a total force of $12\,\text{N}$.

      What is the acceleration of the trolley? Give the answer in $\text{m/s}^2$.

      m/s²
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Draw the free-body diagram of the trolley. Which forces are horizontal, and which cancel each other in the vertical direction?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Choose the direction of the push as the positive direction. In the horizontal direction $F - F_\mu = ma$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $a$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{N} - 12\,\text{N}}{18\,\text{kg}}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 18\,\text{kg}$, pushing force $F = 30\,\text{N}$ horizontally, slowing forces in total $F_\mu = 12\,\text{N}$

      Find

      the acceleration $a$ of the trolley in $\text{m/s}^2$

      Principle

      By Newton's second law the acceleration of the trolley is determined by the net force. In the vertical direction the trolley does not accelerate, so only the horizontal forces remain in the net force.

      Free-body diagram

      The trolley as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of the push. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the floor points up from the bottom surface, equally long. The pushing force $\overline{F}$ points forward and the slowing force $\overline{F}_\mu$ points backward from the edge of the bottom surface, shorter than the pushing force.

      Formula

      $F - F_\mu = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{F - F_\mu}{m}$

      Conversions

      All quantities are in SI units, and $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} = \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $a$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{N} - 12\,\text{N}}{18\,\text{kg}}$
      $= \dfrac{18\,\text{N}}{18\,\text{kg}}$
      $= 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Answer

      $1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

      In the direction of the push, two significant figures like the data $30\,\text{N}$, $12\,\text{N}$ and $18\,\text{kg}$.

      Reasonableness

      Without the slowing forces the acceleration would be $30\,\text{N} : 18\,\text{kg} \approx 1.7\,\text{m/s}^2$, so friction makes it smaller, as it should. An acceleration of $1\,\text{m/s}^2$ means that the velocity of the trolley increases by $1\,\text{m/s}$ every second, which fits a brisk push.
