## fy4-1-L1-01
**Given:** $\Delta x = 120\,\text{m}$, $\Delta t = 15\,\text{s}$; the motion is uniform and in the direction chosen as positive
**Find:** the velocity $v$ of the runner in the unit $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The motion is uniform, so the velocity does not change along the whole distance. Then the velocity is the change in position divided by the time it took. The runner moves straight in the positive direction, so the displacement is equal to the distance.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$. The formula is already solved for the velocity: $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** The distance is in metres and the time in seconds, so the result comes directly in the unit $\text{m/s}$. No conversion is needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{120\,\text{m}}{15\,\text{s}} = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $8.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the time $15\,\text{s}$ (the distance $120\,\text{m}$ does not limit the accuracy further).
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $\dfrac{120\,\text{m}}{15\,\text{s}}$ is $8\,\text{m}$ per second, and in $15\,\text{s}$ $8\,\text{m/s} \cdot 15\,\text{s} = 120\,\text{m}$, so the calculation can be checked backwards. The velocity $8.0\,\text{m/s} \approx 29\,\text{km/h}$ is the speed of a sprinter, so over a distance of $120\,\text{m}$ it is possible. The sign is positive, because the motion is in the positive direction.

## fy4-1-L1-02
**Given:** initial position $x_1 = 12\,\text{m}$, final position $x_2 = -5.0\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the displacement $\Delta x$ with its sign in the unit $\text{m}$
**Principle:** Displacement is the change in position, not the distance travelled. It is always calculated as final position minus initial position, so the sign tells the direction: a negative displacement is motion in the negative direction.
**Formula:** $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$. The formula is already solved for the displacement: $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$
**Conversions:** Both positions are in metres, so no units need to be converted.
**Substitution:** $\Delta x = -5.0\,\text{m} - 12\,\text{m} = -17\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $-17\,\text{m}$, two significant figures. The position $12\,\text{m}$ is known to the accuracy of a metre, so the difference has that accuracy.
**Reasonableness:** The cart moved in the negative direction, so the displacement must be negative. The cart first went $12\,\text{m}$ to the origin and then $5.0\,\text{m}$ past it, altogether $12\,\text{m} + 5.0\,\text{m} = 17\,\text{m}$. Here the distance and the magnitude of the displacement are equal, as long as the cart does not turn around.

## fy4-1-L1-03
**Given:** $v = 3.5\,\text{m/s}$, $\Delta t = 12\,\text{s}$; the motion is uniform and in the positive direction
**Find:** the displacement $\Delta x$ in the unit $\text{m}$
**Principle:** In uniform motion the velocity stays constant, so the position changes by the same amount every second. The displacement is therefore the velocity multiplied by the time.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta x = v\,\Delta t$
**Conversions:** The velocity is in the unit $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the seconds cancel and the result is in metres. No conversion is needed.
**Substitution:** $\Delta x = 3.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12\,\text{s} = 42\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $42\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like both initial values ($3.5\,\text{m/s}$ and $12\,\text{s}$).
**Reasonableness:** The unit is correct: $\text{m/s} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m}$. Estimate: about $3\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12\,\text{s} = 36\,\text{m}$, so $42\,\text{m}$ is of the right order of magnitude. The velocity $3.5\,\text{m/s}$ corresponds to about $13\,\text{km/h}$, that is jogging, and in $12\,\text{s}$ a jogger covers a few tens of metres. The displacement is positive, because the motion is in the positive direction.

## fy4-1-L1-04
**Given:** points of the line $(t_1;\, x_1) = (0\,\text{s};\, 4.0\,\text{m})$ and $(t_2;\, x_2) = (10\,\text{s};\, 24\,\text{m})$
**Find:** the velocity $v$ of the cart in the unit $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** In uniform motion the velocity is constant, so the $(t, x)$ graph is a straight line. The slope of the line, that is the rise divided by the horizontal run, is the change in position divided by the change in time, that is the velocity.
**Reading the graph:** We use the two given points of the line: the position of the first is $x_1 = 4.0\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t_1 = 0\,\text{s}$ and the position of the second is $x_2 = 24\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t_2 = 10\,\text{s}$. The vertical rise is $x_2 - x_1$ and the horizontal run is $t_2 - t_1$. Their ratio is the velocity.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$, where $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$. So $v = \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so no conversions are needed. The starting position $4.0\,\text{m}$ is not zero, but it cancels in the difference.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{24\,\text{m} - 4.0\,\text{m}}{10\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{20\,\text{m}}{10\,\text{s}} = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures, because the position $24\,\text{m}$ is given to the accuracy of a metre ($4.0\,\text{m}$ to a decimetre), so the difference $20\,\text{m}$ has two figures; the time $10\,\text{s}$ also has two.
**Reasonableness:** The graph rises, so the velocity is positive. Check: $4.0\,\text{m} + 2.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 10\,\text{s} = 24\,\text{m}$, which is the other point of the line. If the velocity were calculated as $\dfrac{24\,\text{m}}{10\,\text{s}} = 2.4\,\text{m/s}$, the starting position would be forgotten.

## fy4-1-L1-05
**Given:** $x_0 = 15\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t = 0\,\text{s}$, $v = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$, the moment asked for $t = 20\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the position $x$ at the moment $t = 20\,\text{s}$, in the unit $\text{m}$
**Principle:** The motion is uniform, so the position changes by the product of the velocity and the time. The position is the initial position plus this displacement, because the walker did not start from the origin.
**Formula:** $x = x_0 + v\,t$. The formula is already solved for the position: $x = x_0 + v\,t$
**Conversions:** The position is in metres, the velocity in the unit $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $x = 15\,\text{m} + 1.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 15\,\text{m} + 30\,\text{m} = 45\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $45\,\text{m}$, two significant figures, because the velocity $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ and the time $20\,\text{s}$ have two figures and the sum $15\,\text{m} + 30\,\text{m}$ is to the accuracy of a metre.
**Reasonableness:** The walker moves $30\,\text{m}$ forward, so the position must be greater than $15\,\text{m}$, and $45\,\text{m}$ is. The velocity $1.5\,\text{m/s} \approx 5.4\,\text{km/h}$ is a normal walking speed. The product $v\,t = 30\,\text{m}$ alone would be the displacement, not the position. The limiting case $t = 0$ gives $x = x_0 = 15\,\text{m}$, as it should.

## fy4-1-L2-01
**Given:** $v = 90\,\text{km/h}$, $\Delta t = 12\,\text{s}$; the motion is uniform and straight
**Find:** the displacement $\Delta x$ in metres; the car drives along a straight road in one direction, so the distance $s = v\,\Delta t$ is equal
**Principle:** The car drives at a uniform velocity along a straight road in one direction, so the displacement is the velocity times the time. The velocity and the time must first be put into the same system of units, so that the result comes in metres.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta x = v\,\Delta t$
**Conversions:** One $\text{km/h}$ is $\dfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{1}{3.6}\,\text{m/s}$, so $90\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{90}{3.6}\,\text{m/s} = 25\,\text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta x = 25\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12\,\text{s} = 300\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $300\,\text{m}$ or $3.0 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$, two significant figures, because the velocity $90\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $12\,\text{s}$ have two figures; the conversion factor $3.6$ is exact and does not limit the accuracy.
**Reasonableness:** The car travels $25\,\text{m}$ every second, which is about the length of six cars. In ten seconds the distance would be $250\,\text{m}$ and in twelve a little more, so $300\,\text{m}$ fits. If one calculated directly $90 \cdot 12 = 1080$, one would mix up the units: that would be $\text{km/h} \cdot \text{s}$, not metres.

## fy4-1-L2-02
**Given:** $v = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, $\Delta x = 1.2\,\text{km}$; the motion is uniform and straight
**Find:** the time $\Delta t$ in minutes
**Principle:** The velocity is constant, so the distance is covered at the same rate all the time. The time can be solved from the definition of velocity when the distance and the velocity are known.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta t = \dfrac{\Delta x}{v}$
**Conversions:** $1.2\,\text{km} = 1.2 \cdot 1000\,\text{m} = 1200\,\text{m}$, so that the units are $\text{m}$ and $\text{m/s}$. The time comes out in seconds, and $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$, so $\Delta t_\text{min} = \dfrac{\Delta t}{60}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta t = \dfrac{1200\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{m/s}} = 300\,\text{s} = \dfrac{300}{60}\,\text{min} = 5.0\,\text{min}$
**Answer:** $5.0\,\text{min}$, two significant figures like the velocity $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the distance $1.2\,\text{km}$.
**Reasonableness:** The velocity $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ is $14.4\,\text{km/h}$, so a kilometre takes about $4.2\,\text{min}$, and $1.2\,\text{km}$ takes about $1.2 \cdot 4.2\,\text{min} \approx 5\,\text{min}$, so $5.0\,\text{min}$ fits. The time is positive, as it should be.

## fy4-1-L2-03
**Given:** $(t_1;\, x_1) = (2.0\,\text{s};\, 18\,\text{m})$ and $(t_2;\, x_2) = (7.0\,\text{s};\, 3.0\,\text{m})$; the motion is uniform
**Find:** the velocity $v$ with its sign in the unit $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The motion is uniform, so the average velocity between two moments is the same as the velocity at all times. The velocity is calculated from the change in position divided by the change in time, and the sign tells the direction.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$, where $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$. So $v = \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so no conversions are needed. The differences are always calculated as final minus initial.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{3.0\,\text{m} - 18\,\text{m}}{7.0\,\text{s} - 2.0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{-15\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s}} = -3.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $-3.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures, because the difference of the positions $3.0\,\text{m} - 18\,\text{m} = -15\,\text{m}$ is to the accuracy of a metre (two figures) and the change in time $5.0\,\text{s}$ also has two.
**Reasonableness:** The position decreases, so the velocity must be negative: the cart moves towards the origin. Check backwards: $18\,\text{m} + (-3.0\,\text{m/s}) \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 3.0\,\text{m}$, which is the other given position. The speed is $3.0\,\text{m/s}$.

## fy4-1-L2-04
**Given:** $x_0 = -24\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t = 0\,\text{s}$, $v = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$, the position asked for $x = 0$
**Find:** the time $t$ in seconds
**Principle:** The motion is uniform, so the position follows the equation $x = x_0 + v\,t$. The cart is at the origin when its position is zero, and the time can be solved from this condition.
**Formula:** $x = x_0 + v\,t$, so $v\,t = x - x_0$ and $t = \dfrac{x - x_0}{v}$
**Conversions:** The positions are in metres and the velocity in the unit $\text{m/s}$, so the time comes directly in seconds. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{0 - (-24\,\text{m})}{1.5\,\text{m/s}} = \dfrac{24\,\text{m}}{1.5\,\text{m/s}} = 16\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $16\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the initial values $24\,\text{m}$ and $1.5\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The cart is on the negative side of the origin and moves in the positive direction, so it arrives at the origin, and the time is positive. Check backwards: $-24\,\text{m} + 1.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 16\,\text{s} = -24\,\text{m} + 24\,\text{m} = 0$. Estimate: $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ is about $1\,\text{m}$ for every 0.7 seconds, and $24\,\text{m}$ takes about $16\,\text{s}$.

## fy4-1-L3-01
**Given:** east $s_1 = 6.0\,\text{km}$ in the time $\Delta t_1 = 20\,\text{min}$ and west $s_2 = 6.0\,\text{km}$ in the time $\Delta t_2 = 30\,\text{min}$
**Find:** the average speed $v_\text{speed}$ in the unit $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The average speed is the whole distance travelled divided by the elapsed time. Both parts are included in the distance, because the distance does not depend on the direction. The average velocity would be a different thing, because it would use the displacement, in which the direction matters.
**Formula:** $v_\text{speed} = \dfrac{s}{\Delta t}$, where $s = s_1 + s_2$ and $\Delta t = \Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2$. So $v_\text{speed} = \dfrac{s_1 + s_2}{\Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2}$
**Conversions:** $6.0\,\text{km} = 6000\,\text{m}$ and $20\,\text{min} = 1200\,\text{s}$, $30\,\text{min} = 1800\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $v_\text{speed} = \dfrac{6000\,\text{m} + 6000\,\text{m}}{1200\,\text{s} + 1800\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{12\,000\,\text{m}}{3000\,\text{s}} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures, because the times $20\,\text{min}$ and $30\,\text{min}$ (the sum $50\,\text{min}$) have two figures; the distances $6.0\,\text{km}$ also have two, and their sum $12.0\,\text{km}$ limits less.
**Reasonableness:** The speed on the way out is $\dfrac{6000\,\text{m}}{1200\,\text{s}} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the speed on the way back $\dfrac{6000\,\text{m}}{1800\,\text{s}} \approx 3.3\,\text{m/s}$, and the value lies between them, as an average speed should. The cyclist returned to the starting point, so the displacement is $0$ and the average velocity $0\,\text{m/s}$, even though the average speed is not zero. About $14\,\text{km/h}$ is relaxed cycling.

## fy4-1-L3-02
**Given:** Aino: $x_{0\text{A}} = 0$, $v_\text{A} = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$; Eeva: $x_{0\text{E}} = 90\,\text{m}$, $v_\text{E} = -1.2\,\text{m/s}$; both start at the same time and the motion is uniform
**Find:** the meeting position $x$ in the unit $\text{m}$
**Principle:** The motion of both is uniform, so the position of each is of the form $x_0 + v\,t$. They meet when their positions are the same at the same moment, and the time is solved first from this condition.
**Formula:** $x_\text{A} = x_{0\text{A}} + v_\text{A}\,t$ and $x_\text{E} = x_{0\text{E}} + v_\text{E}\,t$. From the condition $x_\text{A} = x_\text{E}$ it follows that $x_{0\text{A}} + v_\text{A}\,t = x_{0\text{E}} + v_\text{E}\,t$, so $(v_\text{A} - v_\text{E})\,t = x_{0\text{E}} - x_{0\text{A}}$ and

$$t = \dfrac{x_{0\text{E}} - x_{0\text{A}}}{v_\text{A} - v_\text{E}}$$

The position is $x = x_{0\text{A}} + v_\text{A}\,t$
**Conversions:** All quantities are in the units $\text{m}$ and $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed. Eeva's velocity is substituted as negative, because she walks in the negative direction.
**Substitution:**

$$\begin{aligned} t &= \dfrac{90\,\text{m} - 0}{1.5\,\text{m/s} - (-1.2\,\text{m/s})} \\ &= \dfrac{90\,\text{m}}{2.7\,\text{m/s}} \\ &= 33.33\ldots\,\text{s} \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} x &= 0 + 1.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 33.33\ldots\,\text{s} \\ &= 50\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$
**Answer:** $50\,\text{m}$ or $5.0 \cdot 10^1\,\text{m}$, two significant figures, because the velocities $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ and $-1.2\,\text{m/s}$ have two figures and so does their difference $2.7\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Eeva walks $1.2\,\text{m/s} \cdot 33.33\,\text{s} = 40\,\text{m}$, so her position is $90\,\text{m} - 40\,\text{m} = 50\,\text{m}$, the same as Aino's. The position is between the starting and finishing points, and because Aino is faster, the meeting is closer to Eeva's starting position than halfway ($45\,\text{m}$).

## fy4-1-L3-03
**Given:** parts of the $(t, x)$ graph: $x = 0$ at $0\,\text{s}$, $x = 30\,\text{m}$ at $10\,\text{s}$, $x = 30\,\text{m}$ at $20\,\text{s}$ and $x = 10\,\text{m}$ at $40\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the average velocity $v_\text{avg}$ over the whole $40\,\text{s}$ with its sign in the unit $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The average velocity is the whole displacement divided by the whole time. Only the initial and final positions affect the displacement, so the intermediate stages, that is moving forward, stopping and coming back, do not affect the calculation.
**Reading the graph:** From the given points we read the starting point $(0\,\text{s};\, 0\,\text{m})$ and the end point $(40\,\text{s};\, 10\,\text{m})$. Between $10\text{–}20\,\text{s}$ the graph is horizontal, because the robot stands still, and its points are not needed. The average velocity is the slope of the line between these two points.
**Formula:** $v_\text{avg} = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v_\text{avg} = \dfrac{10\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{40\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} = 0.25\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $0.25\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures, because the final position $10\,\text{m}$ and the time $40\,\text{s}$ are given with two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The robot went $30\,\text{m}$ forward and $20\,\text{m}$ back, so the displacement is $30\,\text{m} - 20\,\text{m} = 10\,\text{m}$ even though the distance was $50\,\text{m}$. The sign is positive, because at the end the robot is at a greater $x$ than at the beginning. The mean of the partial velocities $3.0\,\text{m/s}$, $0$ and $-1.0\,\text{m/s}$, $\dfrac{3.0 + 0 - 1.0}{3} = 0.67\,\text{m/s}$, would be wrong, because the sub-intervals are not of equal length.
