Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude

Why can the motion of a child on a swing be described with a few numbers, even though the swing is in a different place at every moment?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • read the amplitude, period and frequency of an oscillation from a $(t, x)$ graph.
  • calculate the displacement with the sine model $x = A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$.
  • fit a sine function to measured points and interpret the result of the fit.

Prior knowledge

  • The period $T$ is the duration of one revolution, and the rotation frequency $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$ is the number of revolutions per unit timechapter Moving in a circle: period and speed
  • A $(t, x)$ graph shows the position of an object as a function of timechapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

A weight hangs from a spring in a laboratory stand. When it is pulled down a little and released, it starts to bounce up and down. On the floor under the weight there is a motion sensor that measures the position of the weight twenty times a second. A wavy curve appears on the screen, and it seems to repeat itself.

With which numbers can such a motion be described, and how are they obtained from the curve?

Optional Recap: period and rotation frequency
A carousel makes one revolution in $4.0\,\text{s}$. What is its rotation frequency?

  • Correct. The rotation frequency is the reciprocal of the period, $f = \tfrac{1}{T} = \tfrac{1}{4.0\,\text{s}} = 0.25\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$ (chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed). Today the same quantities describe back-and-forth motion.

  • The number $0.25$ is right, but the unit is not: the rotation frequency is the number of revolutions per unit time, so its unit is $\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, not the second.

  • Four seconds is the duration of one revolution, that is, the period. The rotation frequency tells the number of revolutions per second: one revolution in four seconds means $\tfrac{1}{4.0}$ of a revolution in a second, that is, $0.25\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$.

How to use

Choose Data set and adjust the amplitude $A$ and the period $T$ of the model. The blue curve is your model $x = A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$, the black points are the simulated measurement. The reading Fit error $d$ tells how far the points are from the curve on average: the smaller $d$ is, the better the model fits. Start replays the measurement for $5\,\text{s}$: the weight moves as in the measurement, the blue triangle beside it shows the displacement of the model, and a dashed line marks the same moment in the graph. If the model fits, the triangle stays level with the weight all the time. The readings $N$ and $f$ concern the model. Speed changes only the pace of the replay.

Assumptions of the model

The weight oscillates vertically on a spring, and up is the positive direction. The oscillation does not die out, and timing starts when the weight passes the equilibrium position going up. Every data set is a simulated measurement: the points have been calculated from a sine model and a small random-looking error of at most $0.25\,\text{cm}$ has been added, as in a real measurement. This is why the points do not lie exactly on any sine curve and the fit error is never zero. All the numbers are values of the model and the simulation, not measured values.

How the calculation works

There are $51$ measured points, at intervals of a tenth of a second from $0$ to $5.0\,\text{s}$. The displacement of the model at the time $t$ is $x = A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$, and the frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$. The fit error $d$ is the mean of the vertical distances between the points and the model curve: for each point $|x_i - A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t_i}{T}\right)|$ is calculated, and the results are added together and divided by the number of points. $N$ is the number of full oscillations of the model between $0$ and $5.0\,\text{s}$, that is, the integer part of $\tfrac{5.0\,\text{s}}{T}$. The reading simulated measurement is the value of the nearest measured point.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Motion is periodic when it repeats in the same way at equal intervals of time. A uniformly rotating carousel (chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed), the pendulum of a pendulum clock, a swing and a weight bouncing on a spring all move periodically. The last three are also in oscillation: the object moves back and forth on both sides of the equilibrium position. The equilibrium position is the position where an object placed at rest stays at rest. A child on a carousel does not oscillate, although the motion is periodic, because the child goes round and does not move back and forth on both sides of one point.

    The position of an oscillating object is described by the displacement $x$, that is, the distance of the object from the equilibrium position, with a sign. At the equilibrium position $x = 0$, and one direction is chosen as positive, for the weight on a spring for example up. The greatest displacement is the amplitude $A$. It is measured from the equilibrium position to an extreme position, not from one extreme position to the other: the distance between the highest and the lowest position is $2A$. In this chapter the oscillation is assumed not to die out, so the amplitude stays the same.

    Period and frequency

    The period $T$ is the duration of one full oscillation (one cycle): the time after which the object is again in the same place and moving in the same direction. When the weight leaves the equilibrium position going up, it reaches the highest position, returns to the equilibrium position, reaches the lowest position and returns to the equilibrium position again. This is one oscillation. The frequency $f$ is the number of full oscillations per unit time. The period and the frequency correspond to the period and the rotation frequency in the chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed: one oscillation corresponds to one revolution. The period is measured most precisely from the duration of several oscillations: if $n$ oscillations take the time $t_n$, the period is $T = \tfrac{t_n}{n}$.

    An oscillation as a graph

    When the displacement is drawn as a function of time, the result is a $(t, x)$ graph (chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs). The graph of periodic motion repeats in the same way at intervals of one period. The period is read between two successive peaks or between two passes through the equilibrium position in which the object moves in the same direction. Successive passes are only half a period apart, because the object passes the equilibrium position once going up and once coming down. The amplitude is read from the level $x = 0$ of the equilibrium position to the level of a peak.

    Sinusoidal oscillation

    The frequency is the reciprocal of the period, because one oscillation takes the time $T$, and in a unit of time $\tfrac{1}{T}$ oscillations are completed:

    $$f = \frac{1}{T}$$

    where $f$ is the frequency ($\text{Hz}$) and $T$ the period ($\text{s}$). The unit of frequency is the hertz, $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$. When the period is $T = 0.50\,\text{s}$, the object makes $2.0$ oscillations per second, that is, $f = 2.0\,\text{Hz}$.

    The $(t, x)$ graph of many oscillating objects is a sine curve. This is so, for example, for a weight oscillating on a spring and for a pendulum swinging through small angles; the reason becomes clear later in the course from the forces acting on the oscillating object. When timing starts at the moment the object passes the equilibrium position in the positive direction, the displacement is

    $$x = A \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right) = A \sin(2\pi f t)$$

    where $x$ is the displacement from the equilibrium position, $A$ the amplitude (in the same unit as $x$), $t$ the time ($\text{s}$), $T$ the period ($\text{s}$) and $f$ the frequency ($\text{Hz}$). The argument of the sine is in radians, so the calculator must be set to radian mode.

    Why exactly $2\pi \tfrac{t}{T}$?

    The expression comes from circular motion. A point moves uniformly anticlockwise round a circle of radius $A$ with the period $T$, and it starts from the right-hand end of the horizontal radius, from which the angle $2\pi \tfrac{t}{T}$ is measured. In the time $t$ the point goes round the fraction $\tfrac{t}{T}$ of a revolution. A whole revolution is $2\pi$ radians, so the radius turns through the angle $2\pi \tfrac{t}{T}$, and the height of the point above the level of the centre is $A \sin\left(2\pi \tfrac{t}{T}\right)$. So the height of the point oscillates exactly like the displacement of an oscillating object. At the time $t = \tfrac{T}{4}$ the angle is $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ and $x = A$, at $\tfrac{T}{2}$ again $x = 0$, at $\tfrac{3T}{4}$ $x = -A$ and at $T$ again $x = 0$.

    Fitting a sine function

    In real series of measurements the measured points do not usually lie exactly on a sine curve because of measurement uncertainty. In fitting a sine function one looks for the values of $A$ and $T$ for which the curve $x = A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$ goes through the middle of the set of points. When fitting by hand, the amplitude is half of the difference between the levels of the highest and the lowest points. The period is read from the duration of several full oscillations and divided by the number of oscillations, because then the inaccuracy of reading is shared among many periods. In the simulated points of the figure three oscillations take $3.6\,\text{s}$, so $T = \tfrac{3.6\,\text{s}}{3} = 1.2\,\text{s}$, and the amplitude is about $3.0\,\text{cm}$.

    Graphical Analysis Pro fits a sine function from the same menu as a line: Curve Fit of the graph tools, from whose drop-down menu the sine function (Sine) is chosen. The model of the program is $y = A \sin(Bt + C) + D$. Here $A$ is the amplitude and $B = \tfrac{2\pi}{T}$, so the period is $T = \tfrac{2\pi}{B}$. The constant $D$ is the reading at the equilibrium position, for example the distance of the weight from the motion sensor when the weight is at the equilibrium position. The constant $C$ tells the phase at the time $t = 0$, that is, at which point of the oscillation the object was when timing started. When timing starts at a pass through the equilibrium position in the positive direction, $C = 0$; in the tasks of this chapter timing always starts like this.

    Common misconception

    Many take the amplitude to be the distance between the highest and the lowest position. The amplitude, however, is the greatest displacement from the equilibrium position, so it is half of the distance between the extreme positions. If the weight moves $12\,\text{cm}$ from the highest position to the lowest, the amplitude is $6.0\,\text{cm}$.

    A common error when reading a graph is to measure the period between two successive passes through the equilibrium position. Between them the object moves in opposite directions, so the interval is only half of the period. A full period is between two successive peaks.

    In calculations the period and the frequency are easily mixed up. The longer the period is, the lower the frequency is, because $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$. When the value of a sine is calculated, the calculator must be in radian mode: in the example, $\sin 2.618$ calculated in degrees is about $0.046$, and the displacement would wrongly come out as about $0.18\,\text{cm}$.

    Summary

    • Periodic motion repeats in the same way at equal intervals of time. In an oscillation an object moves back and forth on both sides of the equilibrium position.
    • The displacement $x$ is the distance from the equilibrium position, with a sign. The amplitude $A$ is the greatest displacement, and the distance between the extreme positions is $2A$.
    • The period $T$ is the duration of one full oscillation, and the frequency $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$ is the number of oscillations per unit time; the unit is $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$.
    • When timing starts at a pass through the equilibrium position in the positive direction, a sinusoidal oscillation is described by $x = A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right) = A \sin(2\pi f t)$, with the argument in radians.
    • In fitting a sine function the amplitude and the period are chosen so that the curve goes through the middle of the set of measured points; the period is read from the duration of several oscillations.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A weight oscillating on a spring reaches $4.0\,\text{cm}$ above the equilibrium position at its highest and just as much below it at its lowest. The weight makes $5$ full oscillations in $3.0\,\text{s}$. Timing starts when the weight passes the equilibrium position going up, and up is the positive direction. How far from the equilibrium position is the weight at the time $t = 0.25\,\text{s}$, and on which side?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Amplitude $A = 4.0\,\text{cm}$ (the greatest displacement from the equilibrium position), number of oscillations $n = 5$, their duration $t_n = 3.0\,\text{s}$ and the moment $t = 0.25\,\text{s}$. The displacement $x$ at the time $t$ is asked.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The weight oscillates periodically, and the oscillation is assumed to be sinusoidal and undamped. Timing starts at a pass through the equilibrium position in the positive direction, so the displacement is described by the model $x = A \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$. The period is the duration of one oscillation, so it is obtained by dividing the duration of five oscillations by five.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} T &= \frac{t_n}{n} \\ x &= A \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right) \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Conversions. None needed: the times are in seconds, so the argument of the sine becomes a dimensionless number in radians, and $x$ comes out in centimetres like the amplitude. The calculator is set to radian mode.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} T &= \frac{t_n}{n} \\ &= \frac{3.0\,\text{s}}{5} = 0.60\,\text{s} \\ x &= A \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right) \\ &= 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot \sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot 0.25\,\text{s}}{0.60\,\text{s}}\right) \\ &= 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot \sin 2.618 \\ &= 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 0.500 \\ &= 2.0\,\text{cm} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The weight is $2.0\,\text{cm}$ above the equilibrium position, because the displacement is positive. The result is given to two significant figures, like the amplitude $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the time $3.0\,\text{s}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The period is $0.60\,\text{s}$, so the weight is at its highest position at the time $\tfrac{T}{4} = 0.15\,\text{s}$ and returns to the equilibrium position at $\tfrac{T}{2} = 0.30\,\text{s}$. The moment $0.25\,\text{s}$ is between these, so the weight is on its way down from the highest position to the equilibrium position, and the displacement must be between zero and $4.0\,\text{cm}$. The result $2.0\,\text{cm}$ satisfies this.

      Apply to a new situation

      The deck of a light footbridge oscillates vertically when people run on the bridge in step. The amplitude of the oscillation is $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the frequency $0.50\,\text{Hz}$, and the oscillation is assumed to be sinusoidal and undamped.

      How far from the equilibrium position is the deck $0.40\,\text{s}$ after it passed the equilibrium position going up? Answer in centimetres.

      cm
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Timing starts at a pass through the equilibrium position going up, so you can use the sine model. Which form fits when you know the frequency?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $x = A \sin(2\pi f t)$. First calculate the argument $2\pi f t$ and check that the calculator is in radian mode.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $2\pi \cdot 0.50\,\text{Hz} \cdot 0.40\,\text{s} = 1.257$, so $x = 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot \sin 1.257$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      amplitude $A = 4.0\,\text{cm}$, frequency $f = 0.50\,\text{Hz}$, time from the pass through the equilibrium position $t = 0.40\,\text{s}$

      Find

      the displacement $x$ of the deck from the equilibrium position in centimetres

      Principle

      The deck oscillates sinusoidally, and the time is counted from the moment when it passed the equilibrium position going in the positive direction, up. Then the displacement is described by the sine model, where the frequency can be used in place of the period because $\tfrac{1}{T} = f$.

      Formula

      $$x = A \sin(2\pi f t)$$

      Conversions

      None needed: $\text{Hz} \cdot \text{s} = \tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s}$ is dimensionless, so the argument is in radians, and $x$ comes out in centimetres like $A$.

      Substitution

      $x$$= 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot \sin(2\pi \cdot 0.50\,\text{Hz} \cdot 0.40\,\text{s})$
      $= 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot \sin 1.257$
      $= 4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 0.9511$
      $= 3.804\,\text{cm}$

      Answer

      $3.8\,\text{cm}$

      Above the equilibrium position, two significant figures as in the amplitude $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the frequency $0.50\,\text{Hz}$.

      2. Frequency from a graph

      The graph shows the horizontal displacement of the bob of a pendulum as a function of time according to the sine model.

      Hz
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Read the duration of several full oscillations and divide by the number of oscillations: $T = \tfrac{t_n}{n}$. Then $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The curve rises through the equilibrium position at the times $0$, $1.8\,\text{s}$, $3.6\,\text{s}$ and $5.4\,\text{s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      from the graph, the displacement $x$ of the pendulum as a function of time from $0$ to $5.4\,\text{s}$

      Find

      the frequency $f$ of the pendulum in hertz

      Principle

      The period is the duration of one full oscillation. It is read most precisely from the duration of several oscillations and divided by their number, and the frequency is the reciprocal of the period.

      Reading the graph

      The curve starts from zero going up and passes the equilibrium position going up the next times at $1.8\,\text{s}$, $3.6\,\text{s}$ and $5.4\,\text{s}$.       $$f = \frac{n}{t_n}$$

      Conversions

      $f$$= \dfrac{3}{5.4\,\text{s}}$
      $= 0.5556\,\text{Hz}$

        5. A weight oscillates on a spring, and the amplitude of the oscillation is $4.0\,\text{cm}$. How far does the weight travel during one period?

          amplitude $A = 4.0\,\text{cm}$

          Principle

          The first weight passes the equilibrium position going up at intervals of a full period, at the times $0.60\,\text{s}$, $1.20\,\text{s}$, $1.80\,\text{s}$, … and the second at the times $0.90\,\text{s}$, $1.80\,\text{s}$, … We look for the shortest time that is a whole multiple of both periods: integers $k$ and $m$ for which $k T_1 = m T_2$.

          Formula

          $k T_1 = m T_2$, from which $\dfrac{k}{m} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, and $t = k T_1$ with the smallest integer $k$

          Conversions

          None needed: both periods are in seconds.

          Substitution

          $\dfrac{k}{m}$$= \dfrac{0.90\,\text{s}}{0.60\,\text{s}}$
          $= 1.5$
          $= \dfrac{3}{2}$

          so

          $k$$= 3$
          $t$$= 3 \cdot 0.60\,\text{s}$
          $= 1.80\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $1.8\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures as in the periods $0.60\,\text{s}$ and $0.90\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          In the time $1.8\,\text{s}$ the first makes $\tfrac{1.8}{0.60} = 3$ and the second $\tfrac{1.8}{0.90} = 2$ full oscillations, so both are again in the starting state. It does not happen earlier: at $0.90\,\text{s}$ the first has made only one and a half oscillations and passes the equilibrium position going down.
