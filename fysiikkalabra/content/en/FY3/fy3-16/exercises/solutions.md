## fy3-16-L1-01
**Given:** stones $m = 20\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; given in the problem $c = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the stones, in megajoules
**Principle:** The stones warm up but stay solid, so their state does not change and no latent heat is needed. Then the quantity of heat absorbed is proportional to the mass and to the change in temperature, and the constant of proportionality is the specific heat capacity $c$.
**Formula:** $Q = c\,m\,\Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins: $\Delta T = 300\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 280\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 20\,\text{kg} \cdot 280\,\text{K} = 4\,480\,000\,\text{J} = 4.48\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $4.5\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures, because the mass $20\,\text{kg}$ is known to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $800 \cdot 20 \cdot 300\,\text{J} \approx 4.8\,\text{MJ}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. $Q$ is positive because the stones warm up. Heating the same mass of water by the same amount would take about five times the energy, because the $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ of water is about five times larger.

## fy3-16-L1-02
**Given:** water $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, already at the boiling point $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** quantity of heat $Q$ needed for vaporisation, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The water is already at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and is vaporising, so its temperature does not change: all the energy goes into the change of state and none into raising the temperature. So we use the specific latent heat of vaporisation $r$, not $c\,\Delta T$.
**Formula:** $Q = r\,m$
**Conversions:** Not needed: $r$ is in kilojoules per kilogram and the mass is in kilograms, so the product comes directly in kilojoules.
**Substitution:** $Q = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.20\,\text{kg} = 452\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $450\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.20\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Vaporising a kilogram takes over two megajoules, so a fifth of a kilogram takes about $0.2 \cdot 2300\,\text{kJ} \approx 460\,\text{kJ}$. Heating the same amount of water from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ would take only $4190 \cdot 0.20 \cdot 80\,\text{J} \approx 67\,\text{kJ}$, so vaporisation takes about seven times as much.

## fy3-16-L1-03
**Given:** $U = 1.6\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}}$, $A = 0.60\,\text{m}^2$, $T_\text{in} = 2.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{out} = 30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** power $P$ conducted through the walls into the bag, in watts
**Principle:** Heat is conducted through the wall from warm to cold, and when the temperature difference stays the same, the power stays constant. The power is proportional to the area of the walls and to the temperature difference, and the constant of proportionality is the $U$-value.
**Formula:** $P = U A\,\Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{out} - T_\text{in}$
**Conversions:** The temperature difference is the same in kelvins: $\Delta T = 30\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 2.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 28\,\text{K}$. Nothing else is needed, because $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K} = \text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = 1.6\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 28\,\text{K} = 26.88\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $27\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the values $1.6$ and $0.60$ and the difference $28\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $1.6 \cdot 0.6 \approx 1\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{K}}$, and $28\,\text{K}$ gives about $28\,\text{W}$. The power is positive because heat comes in from outside. It is small because the wall insulates well, but in an hour the energy coming in is still $27\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} \approx 97\,\text{kJ}$, which would be enough to melt about $0.3\,\text{kg}$ of ice per hour.

## fy3-16-L1-05
**Given:** $c = 3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, circulating mass $\tfrac{m}{t} = 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$, rise in temperature $\Delta T = 8.0\,\text{K}$
**Find:** power $P$ transferred from the engine to the liquid, in kilowatts
**Principle:** The liquid warms up and does not boil, so its state does not change, and the quantity of heat absorbed per second is $c\,m\,\Delta T$ for the mass that passes through the engine in a second. Power is energy per unit time, $P = \tfrac{Q}{t}$.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{Q}{t} = \dfrac{c\,m\,\Delta T}{t} = c\,\dfrac{m}{t}\,\Delta T$
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is already in kelvins. Units: $\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{K} = \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = \text{W}$, and finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = 3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot 8.0\,\text{K} = 14\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = 14\,000\,\text{W} = 14\,\text{kW}$
**Answer:** $14\,\text{kW}$, two significant figures like the values $0.50\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$ and $8.0\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** Every second, $14\,\text{kJ}$ is transferred to the liquid. Estimate: $3500 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 8 = 14\,000$. The power carried by the coolant of a passenger car is typically tens of kilowatts, so the value is of the right order of magnitude.

## fy3-16-L2-01
**Given:** $U = 2.0\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}}$, $A = 0.50\,\text{m}^2$, outside $25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and inside $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so $\Delta T = 25\,\text{K}$, time $t = 6.0\,\text{h}$; from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** mass of the ice $m$ in kilograms
**Principle:** As long as there is ice, the inside stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the temperature difference and the power through the wall stay constant. The bag contains only ice, so all the energy $P\,t$ that has come through the wall is used to melt the ice: $P\,t = s\,m$.
**Formula:** $U A\,\Delta T\,t = s\,m$, so $m = \dfrac{U A\,\Delta T\,t}{s}$
**Conversions:** $t = 6.0\,\text{h} = 6.0 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 21\,600\,\text{s}$ and $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$, so that a watt times a second is a joule.
**Substitution:** $P = 2.0 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 25\,\text{W} = 25\,\text{W}$ and $m = \dfrac{25\,\text{W} \cdot 21\,600\,\text{s}}{334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = \dfrac{540\,000\,\text{J}}{334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = 1.617\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $1.6\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures, because the U-value $2.0$, the area $0.50\,\text{m}^2$, the temperature difference $25\,\text{K}$ and the time $6.0\,\text{h}$ all have two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The power is $25\,\text{W}$, so in six hours $540\,\text{kJ}$ comes into the bag. Check: $1.617\,\text{kg} \cdot 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} = 540\,\text{kJ}$. An ice pack of a little over one and a half kilograms is a common size, and twice the time would need twice the mass.

## fy3-16-L2-02
**Given:** water $m = 0.25\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** total quantity of heat $Q$ in kilojoules
**Principle:** The process has two stages, one after the other. First the water warms up to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($c\,m\,\Delta T$), then it vaporises at this temperature, which does not change ($r\,m$). There is no change in temperature during vaporisation, so the energies of the stages are calculated with different formulas and added together.
**Formula:** $Q = Q_\text{heating} + Q_\text{vaporisation} = c\,m\,\Delta T + r\,m$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$ and finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q_\text{heating} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg} \cdot 80\,\text{K} = 83\,800\,\text{J}$ and $Q_\text{vaporisation} = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg} = 565\,000\,\text{J}$, so $Q = 648\,800\,\text{J} = 648.8\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $650\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.25\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Vaporisation takes $\tfrac{565}{648.8} \approx 87\,\%$ of the total energy, even though the temperature does not change during it. Estimate: $0.25\,\text{kg}$ needs about $2.6\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ per kilogram, so about $650\,\text{kJ}$. If vaporisation were forgotten, the result would be only $84\,\text{kJ}$.

## fy3-16-L2-03
**Given:** $P = 24\,\text{kW}$, $c = 3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, greatest allowed rise in temperature $\Delta T = 8.0\,\text{K}$
**Find:** mass of liquid to be circulated per second $\tfrac{m}{t}$ in $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$
**Principle:** All the power transferred from the engine goes into warming the liquid, and the liquid does not boil, so its state does not change. The mass $\tfrac{m}{t}$ passing per second absorbs the quantity of heat $c\,\tfrac{m}{t}\,\Delta T$, and this is equal to the power $P$.
**Formula:** $P = c\,\dfrac{m}{t}\,\Delta T$, so $\dfrac{m}{t} = \dfrac{P}{c\,\Delta T}$
**Conversions:** $24\,\text{kW} = 24\,000\,\text{W} = 24\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}$. The change in temperature is already in kelvins.
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{m}{t} = \dfrac{24\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}}{3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 8.0\,\text{K}} = \dfrac{24\,000}{28\,000}\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} = 0.857\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$
**Answer:** $0.86\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$, two significant figures like the values $24\,\text{kW}$ and $8.0\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check: $3500 \cdot 0.857 \cdot 8.0\,\text{W} = 24\,\text{kW}$. With pure water ($c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$) about $0.72\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$ would be enough, so a liquid containing glycol, with a smaller $c$, needs a mass flow about a fifth larger. The direction is right: the worse the liquid stores energy, the more of it has to be circulated.

## fy3-16-L2-04
**Given:** lemonade $m_\text{j} = 0.33\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ (the value for water); from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** mass of melted ice $m_\text{ice}$ in grams
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: when no heat comes in from the surroundings, the energy that the lemonade gives out as it cools is all used to melt ice. There is enough ice, so the temperature settles at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the melted ice does not warm up.
**Formula:** $c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T = s\,m_\text{ice}$, so $m_\text{ice} = \dfrac{c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T}{s}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 22\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 22\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$, and finally $1\,\text{kg} = 1000\,\text{g}$.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{ice} = \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.33\,\text{kg} \cdot 22\,\text{K}}{334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = \dfrac{30\,419.4\,\text{J}}{334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = 0.0911\,\text{kg} = 91.1\,\text{g}$
**Answer:** $91\,\text{g}$, two significant figures like the values $0.33\,\text{kg}$ and $22\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** The mass of melted ice is $\tfrac{91}{330} \approx 28\,\%$ of the mass of the lemonade, because melting needs the energy of heating by about 80 kelvins per kilogram: $\tfrac{22}{80} \approx 0.28$. The mass is positive and smaller than that of the lemonade, as it should be.

## fy3-16-L3-01
**Given:** stones $m_\text{k} = 25\,\text{kg}$, $c_\text{k} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; water $m_\text{v} = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, warms to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and vaporises; from the table $c_\text{v} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** drop in the temperature of the stones $\Delta T_\text{k}$ in kelvins
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: when all the energy is taken from the stones, the energy given out by the stones is equal to the energy absorbed by the water. The water absorbs energy in two stages (warming up and vaporisation), and the stones only cool down, so their state does not change. The warming of the steam and the heating of the stove are not taken into account.
**Formula:** $c_\text{k}\,m_\text{k}\,\Delta T_\text{k} = m_\text{v}(c_\text{v}\,\Delta T_\text{v} + r)$, so $\Delta T_\text{k} = \dfrac{m_\text{v}(c_\text{v}\,\Delta T_\text{v} + r)}{c_\text{k}\,m_\text{k}}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T_\text{v} = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$.
**Substitution:** $c_\text{v}\,\Delta T_\text{v} = 4190 \cdot 80\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} = 335\,200\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$, $Q_\text{v} = 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot (335\,200 + 2\,260\,000)\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} = 519\,040\,\text{J}$ and $\Delta T_\text{k} = \dfrac{519\,040\,\text{J}}{800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{kg}} = 25.95\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $26\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the masses $0.20\,\text{kg}$ and $25\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Vaporisation takes $452\,\text{kJ}$, which is $87\,\%$ of the energy, and warming alone would lower the temperature of the stones by only about $3.4\,\text{K}$. The drop in the stones is small compared with their temperature of $300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, because there are a lot of stones. Warming the steam would make the drop slightly larger, and a stove that keeps heating the stones would make it smaller; $26\,\text{K}$ is the drop without heating by the stove.

## fy3-16-L3-02
**Given:** ice $m_\text{ice} = 0.500\,\text{kg}$, $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; water $m_\text{v} = 2.00\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{v} = 30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; the container is insulated; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** final temperature of the mixture $T$ in degrees Celsius
**Principle:** Energy is conserved, because the container is insulated: the energy given out by the water melts the ice and warms the melted water to the final temperature. This works only if all the ice melts. First check: as it cools to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the water can give out at most $c\,m_\text{v}\,T_\text{v} = 251\,400\,\text{J}$, and melting the ice needs only $s\,m_\text{ice} = 167\,000\,\text{J}$. Since $251\,400\,\text{J} > 167\,000\,\text{J}$, all the ice melts and the final temperature is above $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Formula:** $c\,m_\text{v}(T_\text{v} - T) = s\,m_\text{ice} + c\,m_\text{ice}\,T$, so $T = \dfrac{c\,m_\text{v}\,T_\text{v} - s\,m_\text{ice}}{c\,(m_\text{v} + m_\text{ice})}$
**Conversions:** The ice is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the change in temperature of the melted water is $T - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = T$ and the water cools by $T_\text{v} - T$; the differences are the same in kelvins, and $T$ comes directly in degrees Celsius. $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$.
**Substitution:** The numerator is $4190 \cdot 2.00 \cdot 30.0\,\text{J} - 334\,000 \cdot 0.500\,\text{J} = 251\,400\,\text{J} - 167\,000\,\text{J} = 84\,400\,\text{J}$ and the denominator is $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 2.50\,\text{kg} = 10\,475\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}$, so

$$T = \dfrac{84\,400\,\text{J}}{10\,475\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}} = 8.057\,{}^\circ\text{C}$$

**Answer:** $8.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures. The initial values $0.500\,\text{kg}$, $2.00\,\text{kg}$ and $30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ have three figures, but the subtraction in the numerator $251\,400 - 167\,000 = 84\,400\,\text{J}$ loses one figure, so two figures are left in the result.
**Reasonableness:** The result is between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and closer to the temperature of the ice, because melting also cools the water. Energy balance: the water gives out $4190 \cdot 2.00 \cdot (30.0 - 8.057)\,\text{J} = 183\,880\,\text{J}$, and the ice takes in $167\,000\,\text{J} + 4190 \cdot 0.500 \cdot 8.057\,\text{J} = 183\,880\,\text{J}$. If the energy of the water had been less than $167\,000\,\text{J}$, the result would be $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and some ice would be left.

## fy3-16-L3-03
**Given:** $U = 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}}$, $A = 0.50\,\text{m}^2$, outside $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and inside $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so $\Delta T = 30\,\text{K}$, $t = 6.0\,\text{h}$; drinks $m_\text{j} = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, $\Delta T_\text{j} = 24\,\text{K}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; ice $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C} \to 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c_\text{ice} = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** smallest mass $m$ of the ice pack in kilograms
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: during the 6.0 hours the ice has to absorb the energy coming through the wall and the energy given out by the drinks. The ice first warms from $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice}$) and then melts ($s$). By the assumption of the problem the inside is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the power through the wall stays constant.
**Formula:** $m\,(c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice} + s) = U A\,\Delta T\,t + c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T_\text{j}$, so $m = \dfrac{U A\,\Delta T\,t + c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T_\text{j}}{c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice} + s}$
**Conversions:** $t = 6.0\,\text{h} = 21\,600\,\text{s}$ and $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$. The change in the temperature of the ice is $\Delta T_\text{ice} = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}) = 10\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins.
**Substitution:**
Energy from the wall: $U A\,\Delta T\,t = 1.5 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 30 \cdot 21\,600\,\text{J} = 486\,000\,\text{J}$
Energy from the drinks: $c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T_\text{j} = 4190 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 24\,\text{J} = 50\,280\,\text{J}$
Energy taken by the ice per kilogram: $c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice} + s = 21\,000 + 334\,000 = 355\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
Smallest mass: $m = \dfrac{486\,000\,\text{J} + 50\,280\,\text{J}}{355\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = 1.511\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $1.5\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures, because the U-value $1.5$, the area $0.50\,\text{m}^2$, the mass of the drinks $0.50\,\text{kg}$ and the time $6.0\,\text{h}$ all have two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The wall brings in $486\,\text{kJ}$ and the drinks $50\,\text{kJ}$, about $536\,\text{kJ}$ in total. If the ice were already at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, we would need $\tfrac{536\,\text{kJ}}{334\,\text{kJ/kg}} \approx 1.6\,\text{kg}$. Warming freezer ice takes about $\tfrac{21}{334} \approx 6\,\%$ more energy per kilogram, so the mass needed is slightly smaller, $1.51\,\text{kg}$, which with two figures gives the smallest suitable mass $1.5\,\text{kg}$.
