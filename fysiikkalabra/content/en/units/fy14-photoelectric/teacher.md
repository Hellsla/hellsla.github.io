## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–7 min, Think about it.** The hook read aloud; the review question on Planck's packets. Ask: "What would you expect from a brighter lamp?"
- **7–12 min, Predict.** Both predictions answered individually and committed, each with a short reason.
- **12–35 min, Investigate.** Pairs on one device: experiment 1 (colour and stopping voltage, at least five rows), experiment 2 (intensity, three rows), then the pair conclusion with the word bank.
- **35–50 min, Theory summary.** Read the puzzle, the key idea and the formula together; the vocabulary match; the biographies (the comparison task is optional); back to the predictions.
- **50–62 min, Apply.** The worked example step by step; Planck's constant from the example table. The error-spotting task is optional.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the explanation task, the summary and the French corner (say the phrases in pairs).

## Organisation and preparation
- Pairs share one device for the simulation; both students keep their own predictions on their own device or on paper.
- The word bank and sentence starters support the pair conclusion: ask one student to speak and the other to write, then swap for the explanation task.
- If possible, show a real photocell or a zinc plate on an electroscope with an ultraviolet lamp (and a strong visible lamp that does nothing). Use UV safety glasses.
- The unit prepares the Fotoni lab visit, where students measure Planck's constant with a photocell and a mercury lamp. Keep the students' experiment 1 tables for the comparison.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade, use the same criteria: the conclusion shows the threshold, the effect of intensity on the current only, and the growth of the stopping voltage with frequency, each with numbers from the student's own table; the explanation compares one photon's energy with the work function and proposes ultraviolet light.
- Language: correct use of at least three key words (photon, work function, threshold frequency, stopping voltage, intensity) and a sentence starter used to argue from evidence.

## Common difficulties
- Students mix "more energy" (intensity) with "more energy per photon" (frequency). Ask: "Energy of what — of the whole beam, or of one photon?"
- Joules and electronvolts in one subtraction; the error-spotting task targets this.
- Reading the stopping voltage: the current must reach $0.0\ \text{nA}$; students who stop at a small current get values that are a little too small. The voltage slider moves in steps of $0.02\ \text{V}$, and the arrow keys help.
- The simulation is a model: its work functions are typical textbook values and its current is simplified (the same quantum efficiency for every wavelength).

## Extension ideas
- Plot all rows of experiment 1 as $U_\text{s}$ against $f$ (a spreadsheet or paper), fit a line and find $h$ and the work function of sodium from the slope and the intercept. Repeat for caesium.
- Discuss why Millikan, who did not believe in photons, is part of the story: what does it mean that a sceptic confirmed the equation?
- Applications: photomultipliers, light meters and why solar cells (a related, internal effect) need photons above a threshold energy.
