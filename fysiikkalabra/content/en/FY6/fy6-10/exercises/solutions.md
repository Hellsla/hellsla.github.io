## fy6-10-L1-01
**Given:** charge of the comb $Q = -6.4\,\text{nC}$, elementary charge $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the number of extra electrons $n$
**Principle:** Charge is always made of whole elementary charges. The comb is negative because it has more electrons than protons, and each extra electron brings a charge of $-e$. So the magnitude of the charge is the number of extra electrons times the elementary charge.
**Formula:** $Q = ne$, so $n = \dfrac{Q}{e}$, when $Q$ is the magnitude of the charge
**Conversions:** $Q = 6.4\,\text{nC} = 6.4 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$
**Substitution:** $n = \dfrac{6.4 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 3.995 \cdot 10^{10}$
**Answer:** $4.0 \cdot 10^{10}$ electrons, two significant figures like the charge $6.4\,\text{nC}$.
**Reasonableness:** One coulomb takes about $6 \cdot 10^{18}$ elementary charges, so a nanocoulomb takes about $6 \cdot 10^{9}$, and $6.4\,\text{nC}$ about six times as many, $4 \cdot 10^{10}$. The number is huge, but it is only a small part of all the electrons of the comb.

## fy6-10-L1-02
**Given:** charges $q_1 = +15\,\text{nC}$ and $q_2 = +25\,\text{nC}$, distance $r = 4.0\,\text{cm}$; the constant of Coulomb's law $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$
**Find:** the magnitude of the force between the spheres $F$ in millinewtons
**Principle:** The spheres are small compared with their distance, so they can be treated as point charges, and the magnitude of the force comes from Coulomb's law. The charges have the same sign, so the spheres repel each other.
**Formula:** $F = k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$
**Conversions:** $q_1 = 15 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$, $q_2 = 25 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$ and $r = 4.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.040\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.
**Substitution:** $F = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \dfrac{15 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 25 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.040\,\text{m})^2} = 2.107 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N} = 2.107\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $2.1\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures like the charges and the distance.
**Reasonableness:** The product of the charges is $3.75 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{C}^2$ and $r^2 = 1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2$, so the estimate $9 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 4 \cdot 10^{-16} : 1.6 \cdot 10^{-3} \approx 2 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$ is close. In the simulation two spheres of $20\,\text{nC}$ at a distance of $3.0\,\text{cm}$ repel each other with a force of $4.0\,\text{mN}$, so a force of a few millinewtons is believable.

**Given:** the charge that moved in the lightning $Q = 5.0\,\text{C}$, time $t = 0.20\,\text{ms}$
**Find:** the average electric current $I$ in kiloamperes
**Principle:** Electric current is moving charge: the current tells how large a charge moves in a unit of time. So the average current is the charge that moved divided by the time, even though the real current in lightning varies.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$
**Conversions:** $t = 0.20\,\text{ms} = 0.20 \cdot 0.001\,\text{s} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{s}$; finally $1\,\text{kA} = 1000\,\text{A}$.
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{5.0\,\text{C}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{s}} = 25\,000\,\text{A} = 25\,\text{kA}$
**Answer:** $25\,\text{kA}$, two significant figures like the charge and the time.
**Reasonableness:** The unit is right, $1\,\text{C/s} = 1\,\text{A}$. A charge of $5\,\text{C}$ is small, but it moves in under a millisecond, so the current is tens of thousands of amperes; the peak currents of lightning are typically of just this order of magnitude.

## fy6-10-L1-04

**Principle:** Charge is conserved: in contact electrons only move from one sphere to the other, so the common charge of the spheres is the same before and after. Identical metal spheres share their common charge equally, so each gets half.
**Formula:** $Q_1 + Q_2 = 2Q$, so $Q = \dfrac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: all the charges are in nanocoulombs, and the answer is asked in the same unit.
**Substitution:** $Q = \dfrac{12\,\text{nC} + (-4.0\,\text{nC})}{2} = \dfrac{8.0\,\text{nC}}{2} = 4.0\,\text{nC}$
**Answer:** $+4.0\,\text{nC}$ on each, two significant figures like the charge $4.0\,\text{nC}$.
**Reasonableness:** The common charge is $+8.0\,\text{nC}$ before and $2 \cdot 4.0\,\text{nC} = 8.0\,\text{nC}$ after, so the charge was conserved. The negative sphere has lost its extra electrons and more, so both became positive.

**Formula:** $F = k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$, so $r = \sqrt{\dfrac{k \lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{F}}$
**Conversions:** $q_1 = q_2 = 30 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$ and $F = 2.0\,\text{mN} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$; finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.

**Answer:** $6.4\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the charges and the force.
**Reasonableness:** Substituting back: $8.988 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 9.0 \cdot 10^{-16} : 0.0636^2\,\text{N} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$. In addition, spheres of $20\,\text{nC}$ at a distance of $6.0\,\text{cm}$ repel each other with a force of $1.0\,\text{mN}$, so with larger charges a force twice as large at the same distance is believable.

**Principle:** In Coulomb's law the charges and $k$ stay the same, so the force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance. When the law is written for both distances and divided, the unknown charges cancel.

**Conversions:** Not needed: in the ratio of the distances the centimetres cancel, and the force comes in the same unit as $F_1$.
**Substitution:** $F_2 = 3.6\,\text{mN} \cdot \left(\dfrac{4.0\,\text{cm}}{10\,\text{cm}}\right)^2 = 3.6\,\text{mN} \cdot 0.16 = 0.576\,\text{mN}$

**Reasonableness:** The distance becomes $2.5$ times as large, so the force drops to $2.5^2 = 6.25$ times smaller: $3.6\,\text{mN} : 6.25 = 0.58\,\text{mN}$. The force dropped clearly more than the distance grew, as follows from the square of the distance.

## fy6-10-L2-04
**Given:** the mass of each sphere $m = 0.50\,\text{g}$, distance $r = 5.0\,\text{cm}$, equal charges; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$
**Find:** the charge of a sphere $q$ in nanocoulombs
**Principle:** The electric force is equal to the weight of a sphere, $G = mg$. The spheres are small, so the force comes from Coulomb's law, and with equal charges the product of the charges is $q^2$. The charge is solved as a square root.
**Formula:** $k\,\dfrac{q^2}{r^2} = mg$, so $q = r\sqrt{\dfrac{mg}{k}}$
**Conversions:** $m = 0.50\,\text{g} = 0.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{kg}$ and $r = 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.050\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{C} = 10^{9}\,\text{nC}$.

**Answer:** $37\,\text{nC}$, two significant figures like the mass and the distance.
**Reasonableness:** Substituting back: $8.988 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot (3.69 \cdot 10^{-8})^2 : 0.050^2\,\text{N} = 4.9\,\text{mN}$, which is the weight of a sphere. The charge is of the same order of magnitude as the tens of nanocoulombs of a balloon charged by rubbing, so the electric force can well lift a light sphere.

## fy6-10-L3-01
**Given:** $q_1 = +20\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 0$, $q_2 = -20\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 10\,\text{cm}$, $q_3 = +5.0\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 5.0\,\text{cm}$; $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$
**Find:** the magnitude of the net force $F$ on the charge $q_3$ in millinewtons
**Principle:** The force of each charge is calculated separately with Coulomb's law, and the net force is their vector sum. The positive $q_1$ repels the positive $q_3$ in the positive $x$-direction, and the negative $q_2$ attracts $q_3$ towards itself, also in the positive $x$-direction. The forces are in the same direction, so their magnitudes are added. The distances and the magnitudes of the charges are the same, so the forces are equal.
**Formula:** $F_1 = k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_1 q_3 \rvert}{r^2}$, $F_2 = k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_2 q_3 \rvert}{r^2}$ and $F = F_1 + F_2$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0\,\text{cm} = 0.050\,\text{m}$, $1\,\text{nC} = 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$ and finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.
**Substitution:** $F_1 = F_2 = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \dfrac{20 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.050\,\text{m})^2} = 3.595 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$ and $F = 2 \cdot 3.595 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N} = 7.190 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N} = 0.7190\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $0.72\,\text{mN}$ in the positive $x$-direction, that is, towards the charge $q_2$, two significant figures like the charge $5.0\,\text{nC}$ and the distance.
**Reasonableness:** If $q_2$ were positive, the forces would be opposite and would cancel. Between charges of opposite signs the forces reinforce each other, so the net force is twice the force of one charge.

## fy6-10-L3-03
**Given:** $Q_1 = +4.0\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 0$, $Q_2 = +1.0\,\text{nC}$ at $d = 12\,\text{cm}$; the third charge is between them
**Find:** the point $x$ where no net force acts on the third charge, in centimetres
**Principle:** Both charges are positive, so between them they push the third charge in opposite directions. The net force is zero when the forces are equal. In Coulomb's law the magnitude of the third charge and $k$ are the same in both forces, so they cancel, and the result does not depend on the magnitude or the sign of the third charge.
**Formula:** $k\,\dfrac{Q_1 q}{x^2} = k\,\dfrac{Q_2 q}{(d - x)^2}$, so $\dfrac{x}{d - x} = \sqrt{\dfrac{Q_1}{Q_2}}$ and $x = \dfrac{d \sqrt{Q_1 : Q_2}}{1 + \sqrt{Q_1 : Q_2}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: in the ratio of the charges the nanocoulombs cancel, and the point comes in centimetres.
**Substitution:** $\sqrt{\dfrac{4.0\,\text{nC}}{1.0\,\text{nC}}} = 2$ and $x = \dfrac{12\,\text{cm} \cdot 2}{1 + 2} = 8.0\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $8.0\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the charges.
**Reasonableness:** The point is twice as far from the larger charge ($8.0\,\text{cm}$) as from the smaller one ($4.0\,\text{cm}$). ## fy6-10-L2-08
**Find:** how the magnitude of the electric force on ball B changes when the charge of ball A doubles and the distance triples
**Principle:** By Coulomb's law $F = k\,\tfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$ the force is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. The forces on balls A and B are a force and its reaction, so they are equal in magnitude. Therefore a change in the charge of ball A also changes the force on ball B.
**Model answer:** The force decreases to $\tfrac{2}{9}F$. At the start:

$F = k\,\dfrac{q \cdot 2q}{r^2} = k\,\dfrac{2q^2}{r^2}$

After the change:

$F' = k\,\dfrac{2q \cdot 2q}{(3r)^2} = k\,\dfrac{4q^2}{9r^2}$

The ratio:

$\dfrac{F'}{F} = \dfrac{4}{9} \cdot \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{2}{9}$

The product of the charges doubles, which doubles the force, and the square of the distance becomes nine times as large, which divides the force by nine. So the force is $\tfrac{2}{9}F \approx 0.22F$. The answer $\tfrac{2}{3}F$ would result if the force were inversely proportional to the distance and not to its square. The answer $\tfrac{1}{9}F$ would result if the change in the charge of ball A did not affect ball B, although the law contains the product of the charges. The answer $18F$ would result if the square of the distance were in the numerator.
