Changes of state

Why does the temperature of boiling water not rise above $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, even though the hob is on full and energy keeps flowing into the pan?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain where the energy goes when a pure substance changes state at constant pressure and temperature.
  • calculate the quantity of heat needed for melting and vaporisation.

Prior knowledge

  • The quantity of heat $Q = cm\Delta T$ and the specific heat capacity of water, $4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$chapter Heating and cooling of a substance
  • Power tells you how fast energy is transferred: $Q = Pt$chapter Work and energy

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 6.

1Think about it

You put ice cubes from the freezer into a pan, switch the hotplate on and push a thermometer in among the ice. The hotplate gives out the same power all the time. The thermometer reading starts below freezing and rises – but does it rise steadily all the time until the pan is empty?

Where does the energy from the hotplate go at the moments when the thermometer reading does not change?

Optional Review: quantity of heat
A pan contains $0.50\,\text{kg}$ of water at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What quantity of heat is needed to warm the water to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$? The specific heat capacity of water is $4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$.

  • Correct: $Q = cm\Delta T = 4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 100\,\text{K} \approx 210\,\text{kJ}$. You can compare this figure with the changes of state in this lesson.

  • This is the quantity of heat for warming by one kelvin. The temperature change is $\Delta T = 100\,\text{K}$, so $Q = cm\Delta T = 4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 100\,\text{K} \approx 210\,\text{kJ}$.

  • The mass was left out. The specific heat capacity gives the quantity of heat per kilogram, so it is multiplied by the mass: $Q = cm\Delta T = 4190 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 100\,\text{J} \approx 210\,\text{kJ}$.

Prediction 3: why does the temperature not rise?

During melting, the temperature of the mixture of ice and water stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ even though the heater works all the time. Three students explain the phenomenon.Whose explanation fits the fact that the duration of the constant part grows with the mass?

  • This explanation fits the observations: the temperature always stops at the melting point, and the duration of the constant part is directly proportional to the mass, because the melting of every kilogram increases the interaction energy between the particles by the same amount. The model in the simulation is built on this explanation, so the simulation alone does not prove it; what matters is that the explanation predicts the length of the constant part correctly. The water molecules stay intact: only the interactions between the molecules change, not the chemical bonds inside the molecules.

  • The thermometer's delay is a matter of seconds, but melting takes minutes, and the constant part is longer the more ice there is. The delay explains neither. Besides, temperature describes the motion of the particles: if the motion sped up, the temperature would rise. During the horizontal part, the energy transferred increases the interaction energy between the particles.

  • If energy escaped as fast as it arrived, the horizontal part would never end. But it does end when all the ice has melted, and its duration grows with the mass: every kilogram needs its own energy. Losses slow down the warming, but they do not explain why the temperature stops exactly at the melting point.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid and gas. In the micro model, the particles of a solid vibrate in place, held together by each other. In a liquid, they move past each other but stay together. In a gas, they move freely and only rarely collide. In a change of state, the interactions between the particles change: in melting, the regular order of the solid breaks down, and in vaporisation, the particles move far apart from each other. The particles themselves do not change: when ice melts or water boils, the chemical bonds inside the water molecules do not break. The opposite changes are freezing and condensation. Ice can also sublimate straight into vapour, as in laundry drying in freezing weather.

    Where does the energy go?

    Melting and vaporisation need energy but do not raise the temperature. When ice is heated, its temperature rises up to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Then the temperature stays constant until all the ice has melted, even though energy keeps being transferred. During melting and vaporisation, the energy transferred to the substance changes the interactions between the particles and increases the interaction energy between them. When a pure substance changes state at constant pressure, its temperature stays constant, so the average kinetic energy of the particles does not increase.

    For the same reason, boiling water stays at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ even when the hob is on full: the energy transferred vaporises water and does not raise its temperature. Steam takes up about 1700 times as much space as water, so as it expands it pushes the surrounding air. A small part of the energy needed for vaporisation is used for this work, and most of it increases the interaction energy between the molecules. The greater the power, the faster the water boils away, but it does not get any hotter. The energy that was taken up when the water vaporised is released again when the vapour condenses, and that is why steam at 100 °C burns worse than water at 100 °C.

    The quantity of heat needed for melting and vaporisation is directly proportional to the mass. The coefficients are properties of the substance: the specific latent heat of fusion $s$ tells you how much energy it takes to melt one kilogram, and the specific latent heat of vaporisation $r$ how much it takes to vaporise one kilogram. For water, $s = 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$ and $r = 2260\,\text{kJ/kg}$. So vaporising takes almost seven times as much energy as melting, and more than five times as much as heating water from $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

    The boiling point depends on pressure. A phase diagram shows the state of a substance at each temperature and pressure: its curves join the melting, boiling and sublimation points at different pressures. On a mountain, where the atmospheric pressure is lower, water boils below $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and in a pressure cooker the pressure raises the boiling point above $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so food cooks faster.

    Latent heat of fusion and vaporisation

    The quantity of heat needed to melt or vaporise a mass $m$ is

    $$Q = s m \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q = r m$$

    where $Q$ is the quantity of heat (J), $s$ is the specific latent heat of fusion of the substance (J/kg), $r$ is the specific latent heat of vaporisation (J/kg) and $m$ is the mass of the substance that melts or vaporises (kg). The same quantity of heat is released when the substance freezes or condenses. At constant pressure the temperature does not change during a change of state, so the formula has no temperature change $\Delta T$.

    Water Value
    $s$ $334\,\text{kJ/kg}$
    $r$ $2260\,\text{kJ/kg}$
    $c$ (ice) $2100\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$
    $c$ (water) $4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$
    $c$ (steam) $2000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$

    In the table, $c$ is the specific heat capacity, which was introduced in chapter Heating and cooling of a substance.

    A heating curve shows the temperature as a function of the energy transferred. On the rising parts the substance warms up and $Q = cm\Delta T$ applies. On the horizontal parts the substance changes state and $Q = sm$ or $Q = rm$ applies.     Common misconception

    Many people think that adding heat always raises the temperature. When a pure substance changes state at constant pressure, the temperature stays constant even though a lot of energy is transferred: melting ice takes as much energy as heating the same water from $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $80\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Another mistake is to treat ice at 0 °C and water at 0 °C as equally good coolants. Ice cools a drink much more effectively, because as it melts it takes in a lot of energy before it starts to warm up. A third mistake is to think that water always boils at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$: the boiling point depends on pressure.

    Summary

    • The states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. In the micro model, a change of state changes the interactions between the particles: in melting and vaporisation the interaction energy between the particles increases, but the chemical bonds inside the molecules do not break.
    • At constant pressure, the temperature of a pure substance stays constant during a change of state. Melting needs $Q = sm$ and vaporising needs $Q = rm$; for water, $s = 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$ and $r = 2260\,\text{kJ/kg}$.
    • In a heating curve, the rising parts are warming ($Q = cm\Delta T$) and the horizontal parts are changes of state.
    • The boiling point depends on pressure; a phase diagram tells you the state of a substance from its temperature and pressure.
    • The specific latent heat of vaporisation can be measured by boiling water with an immersion heater on a balance: $r = Pt/\Delta m$, where $\Delta m$ is the change in the balance reading.

      Worked example

      Problem

      The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is determined experimentally. An open calorimeter containing boiling water stands on a balance, and the water contains a $1000\,\text{W}$ immersion heater. During five minutes ($300\,\text{s}$), the balance reading falls by $128\,\text{g}$ (an invented figure, not the result of a real measurement). What is the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water according to this reading?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Power $P = 1000\,\text{W}$, time $t = 300\,\text{s}$ and the mass vaporised, that is, the change in the balance reading, $\Delta m = 128\,\text{g}$. The water is boiling, so it is at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. We want the specific latent heat of vaporisation $r$. Assume that all the energy from the heater goes into the water.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The water boils all the time, so its temperature does not rise and all the energy $Q = Pt$ from the heater is used to vaporise water. The quantity of heat needed for vaporisation is proportional to the mass vaporised, $Q = r\,\Delta m$. Energy is conserved, so these two are equal.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. Set the quantities of heat equal and solve for $r$:

      $$\begin{aligned} Pt &= r\,\Delta m \\ r &= \frac{Pt}{\Delta m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Convert the change in the balance reading to kilograms: $128\,\text{g} = 0.128\,\text{kg}$. Power and time are already in base units, and $1\,\text{W} \cdot 1\,\text{s} = 1\,\text{J}$. Finally the result is converted to megajoules: $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} r &= \frac{Pt}{\Delta m} \\ &= \frac{1000\,\text{W} \cdot 300\,\text{s}}{0.128\,\text{kg}} \\ &= \frac{300\,000\,\text{J}}{0.128\,\text{kg}} \\ &= 2\,343\,750\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The specific latent heat of vaporisation is $2.34\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. The result is given to three significant figures, because the mass reading $128\,\text{g}$ is given to three figures and it limits the precision most.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The result $2.34\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ is slightly greater than the table value $2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, by about $3.7\,\%$. This is the expected direction: some of the heater's energy passes through the vessel into the surrounding air and does not vaporise water, so the assumption "all the energy goes into the water" gives too large an $r$. With the table value,

      $\dfrac{300\,\text{kJ}}{2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}}$
      $= 0.133\,\text{kg}$

      or $133\,\text{g}$, would vaporise, only $5\,\text{g}$ more than was measured, so the result is correct to within a few per cent.

      Optional Find the error
      A student calculates the quantity of heat needed to melt $0.20\,\text{kg}$ of ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C: $Q = cm\Delta T = 2100\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \cdot 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 0\,\text{K} = 0\,\text{J}$. The student concludes that ice melts without energy.Where is the error?

      • Correct. On the horizontal part of a heating curve $\Delta T = 0$, but energy is transferred all the time: it breaks the bonds between the particles. That is why melting has its own formula, $Q = sm$.

      • Changing the coefficient does not help: when $\Delta T = 0$, the product is still zero. The error is in the choice of formula itself.

      • A temperature change is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius, and here the initial and final temperatures are both $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so $\Delta T = 0$. The error is that melting needs the formula $Q = sm$.

      • Ice at 0 °C does not melt unless energy is transferred to it; in a cool bag ice melts precisely because it takes heat from its surroundings. Melting needs $Q = sm$.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      Water boils at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What happens to the water molecules when the water vaporises?

      • A change of state does not change the substance: steam is still water molecules. Only the interactions between the molecules change; the chemical bonds inside a molecule do not break.

      • Correct. In a liquid the molecules stay close to each other, pulled together by each other; in vaporisation they move far apart, and the vapour expands. This takes the energy $Q = rm$, even though the temperature does not rise. The water molecules themselves stay intact.

      • Temperature describes the motion of the particles, and during boiling it stays at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. So the average kinetic energy of the molecules does not increase; the energy transferred increases the interaction energy between the molecules as they move farther apart.

      • The molecules themselves do not grow. Steam takes up more space because the distances between the molecules increase.

      2. Boiling water

      In a pan, water is boiling steadily at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The power of the hotplate is turned up. What happens?

      • In an open pan, the temperature of boiling water does not rise above the boiling point. The extra energy does not increase the kinetic energy of the molecules but pulls the molecules apart from each other, so it is used to vaporise water.

      • The water absorbs all the energy transferred: vaporisation just becomes faster. No energy is left unused, but every extra joule vaporises more water.

      • Correct. The energy used in vaporisation is $Q = rm$, so a greater power vaporises a greater mass in the same time. The temperature stays constant until the water has boiled away.

      • The temperature really does stay the same, but the rate of vaporisation changes. The energy transferred does not disappear: it vaporises more water per second, so the water boils away faster.

      Optional 4. Explain and transfer

      Why does steam at 100 °C burn skin worse than the same mass of water at 100 °C? Use a change of state and energy in your explanation.

      Model answer

      When steam hits the skin, it condenses into water. As it condenses, the molecules bind to each other, and the energy that was taken up in vaporisation is released: $Q = rm$. After that the water that has formed cools to the temperature of the skin like ordinary hot water. One gram of steam gives out $2260\,\text{J}$ as it condenses and a further $264\,\text{J}$ or so as it cools from $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $37\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, about $2.5\,\text{kJ}$ in total. One gram of water at 100 °C gives out only the cooling part, about $264\,\text{J}$, which is almost ten times less.

        1. Crushed ice is heated. The temperature rises to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and then stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ until all the ice has melted, even though energy is transferred all the time. Use the micro model to explain where the energy goes.

Water boils in an open vessel on a balance, and the water contains an immersion heater whose power can be adjusted. When the heater power is $800\,\text{W}$, the balance reading falls by $196\,\text{g}$ in 10.0 minutes. When the power is $400\,\text{W}$, the reading falls by $90.0\,\text{g}$ in 10.0 minutes. In both measurements the water is at 100 °C, so the same amount of energy passes from the vessel to the surrounding air every second. Calculate the specific latent heat of vaporisation using the difference of the two measurements. Justify why the energy passing to the air does not affect the result and why the value calculated from each measurement separately is too large. Give your answer in the unit MJ/kg.

          MJ/kg
          Answer

          2.26 MJ/kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          $P_1 = 800\,\text{W}$, $\Delta m_1 = 196\,\text{g}$; $P_2 = 400\,\text{W}$, $\Delta m_2 = 90.0\,\text{g}$; in both $t = 10.0\,\text{min}$; the water is at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the power $P_\text{l}$ passing to the air is the same

          Find

          the specific latent heat of vaporisation $r$ in $\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Principle

          The water boils all the time, so its temperature does not change and the energy that stays in the water is used for vaporisation: $(P - P_\text{l})t = r\Delta m$. There are two equations and two unknowns ($r$ and $P_\text{l}$), so they can be solved together.

          Formula

          $(P_1 - P_\text{l})t = r\Delta m_1$ and $(P_2 - P_\text{l})t = r\Delta m_2$. Subtract the equations: $(P_1 - P_2)t = r(\Delta m_1 - \Delta m_2)$, from which

          $r$$= \dfrac{(P_1 - P_2)t}{\Delta m_1 - \Delta m_2}$

          Conversions

          $t = 10.0\,\text{min} = 600\,\text{s}$, $\Delta m_1 = 0.196\,\text{kg}$ and $\Delta m_2 = 0.0900\,\text{kg}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $r$$= \dfrac{(800\,\text{W} - 400\,\text{W}) \cdot 600\,\text{s}}{0.196\,\text{kg} - 0.0900\,\text{kg}}$
          $= \dfrac{240\,000\,\text{J}}{0.106\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 2\,264\,151\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 2.264\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Answer

          $2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Three significant figures: the difference $196\,\text{g} - 90.0\,\text{g} = 106\,\text{g}$ is accurate to the nearest gram and has three figures.

          Reasonableness

          The energy passing to the air, $P_\text{l}t$, is the same in both measurements and cancels in the difference. The individual measurements would give

          $\dfrac{800\,\text{W} \cdot 600\,\text{s}}{0.196\,\text{kg}}$$\approx 2.45\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          and

          $\dfrac{400\,\text{W} \cdot 600\,\text{s}}{0.0900\,\text{kg}}$$\approx 2.67\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          both too large, because the energy passing to the air would also be counted as vaporisation. The loss power is

          $800\,\text{W} - 2.264 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot \dfrac{0.196\,\text{kg}}{600\,\text{s}}$
          $\approx 60\,\text{W}$

          and the result agrees with the table value $2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.
