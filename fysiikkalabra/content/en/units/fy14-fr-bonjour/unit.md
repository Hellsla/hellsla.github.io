Imagine that your class visits **ITER**, the international fusion experiment that is under construction (as of November 2026) next to the
Cadarache research centre in Provence, in the south of France. You would meet engineers and researchers from many
countries there, and a few French phrases, said politely, open doors. This is a scenario for practice: no visit has
been booked.

## Tu or vous?

French has two words for *you*. Choosing the right one is part of being polite.

- **<span lang="fr">tu</span>** is for one person you know well: a friend, a family member, a classmate, a child. Young people of the
  same age usually say *<span lang="fr">tu</span>* to each other from the first moment.
- **<span lang="fr">vous</span>** is for one adult you do not know, or someone in a formal or work situation: a receptionist, an
  engineer, a professor. *<span lang="fr">Vous</span>* is also the only word for **two or more people**, whoever they are.

Finnish speakers know the difference from *sinä* and *te* (**teitittely**), but in France the polite *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* is much
more common than *te* is in Finland today. A safe rule for a visit: start with *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* with every adult. Change to *<span lang="fr">tu</span>*
only when the other person offers it, for example <span lang="fr">On peut se tutoyer ?</span> (Shall we say *<span lang="fr">tu</span>*?). People often move
from *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* to *<span lang="fr">tu</span>* quickly, for example among colleagues in science and technology; moving back from *<span lang="fr">tu</span>* to *<span lang="fr">vous</span>*
is rare.

The greeting follows the same idea. **<span lang="fr">Bonjour</span>** works with anyone during the day; add <span lang="fr">madame</span> or
<span lang="fr">monsieur</span> to be polite. **<span lang="fr">Bonsoir</span>** is for the evening, both when you meet and when you leave.
**<span lang="fr">Salut</span>** (hi, bye) is only for friends and people your age. In France you say *<span lang="fr">bonjour</span>* before anything else:
before you ask a question, when you enter a shop, when you meet the guide. Starting without it can sound rude.

The summary table further down puts the *<span lang="fr">tu</span>* and *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* forms of every question side by side.

## Sounds for Finnish speakers

In Finnish, you say every letter you write. In French you do not, and that is the first surprise. Four tips help you
most at the start. The pronunciation hints in italics are written with Finnish letters: capitals show the stressed
syllable (in French usually the last one), *õ*, *ã* and *ẽ* are nasal vowels, *è* is an open *e* and *ž* is the *s* of
English *pleasure*.

1. **Silent final letters.** Most consonants at the end of a word are not pronounced, and neither is a final *<span lang="fr">-e</span>*:
   <span lang="fr">salut</span> is *sa-LY*, <span lang="fr">vous</span> is *VU*, <span lang="fr">français</span> is *frã-SÈ*, <span lang="fr">appelles</span> is *a-PEL*. A final *c*, *r*, *f*
   or *l* is often heard, like the *r* of <span lang="fr">bonsoir</span>. This is why
   <span lang="fr">finlandais</span> (boy) and <span lang="fr">finlandaise</span> (girl) sound different: in *<span lang="fr">finlandaise</span>* the *s* is no longer at the
   end, so it is heard, as a *z*.
2. **The French r** is made at the back of the throat, a little like a soft gargle. It is not the rolled Finnish *r*.
   Practise with <span lang="fr">Bonjour</span>, <span lang="fr">merci</span> and <span lang="fr">au revoir</span>.
3. **Nasal vowels.** In *<span lang="fr">an, en, on, in, un</span>* at the end of a word or before another consonant, the air goes through
   the nose and you do **not** say the *n*: <span lang="fr">bonjour</span>, <span lang="fr">bien</span>, <span lang="fr">Finlande</span>, <span lang="fr">enchanté</span>, <span lang="fr">un peu</span>.
   A Finnish speaker easily says a clear *n* (*bon-ŽUR*); try *bõ-ŽUR* instead.
4. **Liaison.** A silent final consonant is linked to the next word when that word starts with a vowel or a silent
   *h*: <span lang="fr">vous‿êtes</span> is *vu-ZÈT*, <span lang="fr">vous‿habitez</span> is *vu-za-bi-TE*. After *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* before a verb the liaison is
   compulsory, and the *s* sounds like a *z*.

Some letters are friendly for a Finn: the French *u* (<span lang="fr">salut</span>) is the Finnish *y*, *<span lang="fr">ou</span>* (<span lang="fr">vous</span>) is the
Finnish *u*, *<span lang="fr">eu</span>* (<span lang="fr">un peu</span>) is close to *ö*, and *é* is a clear *e*. The *j* of <span lang="fr">je</span> and <span lang="fr">bonjour</span>
is like the *s* in English *pleasure*.

## Summary

The first minute of a meeting. Use *<span lang="fr">tu</span>* with friends and people your age, *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* with adults you do not know and with any group. *<span lang="fr">Comment ça va ?</span>* also works with *<span lang="fr">vous</span>*. With *<span lang="fr">tu</span>*, a question can simply rise at the end (*<span lang="fr">Tu parles anglais ?</span>*); the inverted form (*<span lang="fr">Parles-tu anglais ?</span>*) is more careful. With *<span lang="fr">vous</span>*, the table gives the inverted form.

| English | With *<span lang="fr">tu</span>* | With *<span lang="fr">vous</span>* |
|---|---|---|
| Hello! | <span lang="fr">Salut ! Bonjour !</span> | <span lang="fr">Bonjour, madame.</span> |
| Good evening! | <span lang="fr">Salut ! Bonsoir !</span> | <span lang="fr">Bonsoir, monsieur.</span> |
| What is your name? | <span lang="fr">Comment tu t'appelles ?</span> | <span lang="fr">Comment vous appelez-vous ?</span> |
| My name is … | <span lang="fr">Je m'appelle …</span> | <span lang="fr">Je m'appelle …</span> |
| Pleased to meet you! | <span lang="fr">Enchanté(e) !</span> | <span lang="fr">Enchanté(e) !</span> |
| How are you? | <span lang="fr">Comment ça va ?</span> | <span lang="fr">Comment allez-vous ?</span> |
| I'm fine, thanks. | <span lang="fr">Ça va bien, merci.</span> | <span lang="fr">Je vais bien, merci.</span> |
| And you? | <span lang="fr">Et toi ?</span> | <span lang="fr">Et vous ?</span> |
| Where are you from? | <span lang="fr">Tu viens d'où ?</span> | <span lang="fr">D'où venez-vous ?</span> |
| I come from Finland. | <span lang="fr">Je viens de Finlande.</span> | <span lang="fr">Je viens de Finlande.</span> |
| I am Finnish. | <span lang="fr">Je suis finlandais(e).</span> | <span lang="fr">Je suis finlandais(e).</span> |
| Where do you live? | <span lang="fr">Tu habites où ?</span> | <span lang="fr">Où habitez-vous ?</span> |
| I live in Espoo. | <span lang="fr">J'habite à Espoo.</span> | <span lang="fr">J'habite à Espoo.</span> |
| Do you speak English? | <span lang="fr">Tu parles anglais ?</span> | <span lang="fr">Parlez-vous anglais ?</span> |
| I speak a little French. | <span lang="fr">Je parle un peu français.</span> | <span lang="fr">Je parle un peu français.</span> |
| Bye! Goodbye! | <span lang="fr">Salut ! À bientôt !</span> | <span lang="fr">Au revoir, madame !</span> |

When you are not sure, choose **<span lang="fr">Bonjour</span>** and **<span lang="fr">vous</span>**: being a little too polite is rarely a mistake.
