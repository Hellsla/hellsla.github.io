## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–7 min, Think about it.** Read the hook (the Sun's core is "too cold" for classical fusion). The review question on closest approach. Pairs talk: impossible or only unlikely?
- **7–12 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, committed with a short reason.
- **12–32 min, Investigate.** *Electrons one at a time* and the three panels; the pair task *Particle or wave?* with the word bank. Then *Through the wall* and the factor task on the table.
- **32–45 min, Theory summary.** Key idea and the formula; the vocabulary match; *Tunnelling and the Sun* with the log–log graph (read it together: each step on an axis is ten times more); back to the predictions. The biography of de Broglie is optional.
- **45–60 min, Apply.** The worked example (electron microscope) step by step; the proton's wavelength in the Sun.
- **60–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the explanation for a younger student with the sentence starters, the summary and the French corner.

## Plan for fast pairs and for running short of time
- **Fast pairs:** the optional tennis-ball task; the de Broglie biography; then estimate how the Sun's typical proton energy compares with the barrier for deuterium and tritium (same charges, so the same order of magnitude) as a lead-in to chapter 3.
- **Short of time:** keep the predictions, both investigation sections, the Key idea and *Tunnelling and the Sun*. Do the worked example as a quick demonstration and give the proton task and the French corner as homework.

## Organisation and preparation
- Pairs for the investigations; predictions and checks individually.
- A classroom demonstration of interference: a laser pointer through a double slit (or a hair for diffraction) onto the wall. Do not look into the beam.
- A ripple tank video or a phone-camera image of interference helps students who have not seen wave interference.
- The double-slit station at the Fotoni lab visit repeats the experiment with single photons; this unit gives the background.
- Log scales are new to many students: point out that $10$, $100$ and $1000$ are equally spaced, and that a straight falling line means $E \propto 1/r$ here.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed with the criteria shown after writing. For a grade: *Particle or wave?* separates one electron (a dot at random) from the pattern of many (probability); the final explanation contrasts classical physics and tunnelling, uses the barrier and the large number of protons, and gives a number.
- Language: *probability, likely / unlikely, barrier, to tunnel, whereas, however*; explaining for a younger reader in simple sentences.

## Common difficulties
- "Tunnelling means the particle gains energy" or "finds a hole": stress that the energy is unchanged and most particles bounce back.
- "Doubling the width halves the chance": the factor task and check 2 target this.
- The table and the double-slit panels are model values and a simulation; the experiments themselves are real (Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi; Davisson and Germer; the scanning tunnelling microscope).
- keV and MeV: write $1\ \text{MeV} = 1000\ \text{keV}$ on the board; $720\ \text{keV}$ and $1.35\ \text{keV}$ differ by a factor of more than $500$.
- The Sun's "temperature of fusion" is not enough by itself: the slowness of fusion in the Sun (9 billion years per proton on average) also involves the weak force in the first step, which chapter 3 explains.

## Extension ideas
- The scanning tunnelling microscope: why does a change of the tip distance by $0.1\ \text{nm}$ change the current strongly? Use the factor of about $13$ per $0.25\ \text{nm}$ from the table.
- The simple estimate behind the table, $T \approx e^{-2\kappa d}$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{2m\,\Delta E}/\hbar$: for an electron and $\Delta E = 1\ \text{eV}$, $\kappa \approx 5.1\ \text{nm}^{-1}$.
- Alpha decay: why do some nuclei live for microseconds and others for billions of years? (Small changes in energy change the tunnelling chance enormously.)
