Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle

Why does a car slide off the road on a slippery bend?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • recognise uniform circular motion and determine the direction and magnitude of the centripetal acceleration.
  • draw the free-body diagram of an object moving on a circular path and apply Newton's second law to it.
  • explain which force turns a car in a bend and where the feeling of being pressed outwards comes from.

1Think about it

On a slippery winter day a car comes into a bend a little too fast. The driver turns the steering wheel, but the car ends up in a snowbank. The passenger felt in the bend as if something pressed him outwards against the door.

What force keeps a car in a bend, and is the force pressing outwards real?

Optional Review: velocity in circular motion
An object goes round a circular path so that its tangential speed stays the same all the time. Which statement about its velocity $\overline{v}$ is true?

  • The velocity is along the tangent of the path, that is perpendicular to the radius. If the velocity pointed to the centre, the object would move towards the centre and not along the circle.

  • Correct. As you learned in the chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed, the velocity is always along the tangent of the path, so its direction turns as the object goes round. This chapter finds out what the change of direction means for acceleration and forces.

  • The speed, that is the magnitude of the velocity, stays the same, but velocity is a vector that also has a direction. On a circular path the direction is always along the tangent of the path, so it keeps changing.

How to use

Choose the tangential speed $v$ and the radius $r$ of the path with the sliders, and the Mass of the car and the Surface of the road. Press Start and the car starts to go round the path. If the friction needed is greater than the maximum static friction, the car starts to slide. Reset puts the car back on the path. The readings update at once, also when the car is standing still. Animation speed slows down or speeds up the animation without changing the readings.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation. The road is horizontal, air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected, and the forces on the tyres are added together into one support force and one friction force. The coefficients of static friction are model values: dry asphalt $\mu_0 = 0.80$, wet asphalt $0.50$ and ice $0.10$. When the grip fails, in the model the road still gives its maximum friction $\mu_0 mg$ perpendicular to the velocity of the car, so the car turns less than the path. For clarity the car and the arrows are drawn larger than to scale, and the path is always the same size in the picture.

How the calculation works

The centripetal acceleration is $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$, and the car stays on the path if the friction of the road gives the net force $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre. In the vertical direction $N = mg$, and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The maximum static friction is $\mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$. If $m\tfrac{v^2}{r} \le \mu_0 mg$, the friction is $F_\mu = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$; otherwise $F_\mu = \mu_0 mg$, and the car slides off the path.

Show the solution

Given

Find

the greatest tangential speed $v$ in $\text{m/s}$

Principle

The car is in uniform circular motion, so by Newton's second law the net force is $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre. The only horizontal force is the static friction of the road, which can be at most $\mu_0 N$. At the limit speed the friction is at its maximum.

$= 19.81\,\text{m/s}$

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    The direction of the velocity changes, so the object accelerates

    In the chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed you learned that in uniform circular motion an object goes round a circular path at a constant speed. The magnitude of the velocity, the tangential speed $v$, stays the same, but the direction of the velocity keeps changing: the velocity $\overline{v}$ always points along the tangent of the path. Velocity is a vector, so a change of direction is also a change of velocity. When the velocity changes, the object has an acceleration. So in uniform circular motion the object accelerates even though its speed does not change.

    In the figure, the velocity of the object is first $\overline{v}_1$ and a moment later $\overline{v}_2$. When the arrows are drawn from the same point, the arrow between their tips is the change of velocity $\Delta\overline{v}$. It points towards the centre of the path. Acceleration is the change of velocity per unit of time, so the acceleration also points to the centre.

    Centripetal acceleration points to the centre

    In uniform circular motion the acceleration is perpendicular to the velocity and, at every point of the path, points towards the centre of the circle. It is called centripetal acceleration $\overline{a}_\text{n}$ (also known as normal acceleration). The subscript n comes from the fact that the acceleration lies along the normal of the path, the line perpendicular to the tangent. Since the acceleration has no component along the velocity, the speed neither increases nor decreases: the acceleration changes only the direction of the velocity.

    The net force points to the centre

    By Newton's second law, the net force $\Sigma\overline{F}$ has the same direction as the acceleration. So in uniform circular motion the sum of the forces acting on the object points towards the centre of the path. This net force pointing to the centre is called the centripetal force. Without it the object would continue in a straight line along the tangent of the path, as Newton's first law says.

    The centripetal force is not a new kind of force, and it is not drawn in the free-body diagram as an arrow of its own. It is the sum of the real forces, and the situation tells which forces make it up. When a car drives round a bend on a horizontal road and air resistance is neglected, the only horizontal force on the car is the friction of the road on the tyres, so the friction points towards the centre of the bend. For a stone whirled on a string, it is the tension of the string; for a rider on a fairground ride, the forces of the seat and the support.

    In the free-body diagram the car is drawn from behind, so the centre of the bend is on the left. In the vertical direction the car does not accelerate, so the support force $\overline{N}$ and the weight $\overline{G}$ cancel each other. The friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ is left as the net force. If the road is slippery, static friction is not enough for this. Then the car turns less than the road does and drifts towards the outside of the bend, although nothing pushes it outwards.

    Centripetal acceleration

    When an object moves in uniform circular motion, the magnitude of its centripetal acceleration is

    $$a_\text{n} = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

    where $a_\text{n}$ is the centripetal acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$), $v$ the tangential speed ($\text{m/s}$) and $r$ the radius of the path ($\text{m}$). The acceleration grows with the square of the speed: when the speed doubles, the acceleration becomes four times as large. At the same speed a tight bend, that is a small radius, gives a large acceleration. If the period $T$ is known, the tangential speed is first found from the formula $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$.

    Why exactly the square of the speed? The speed has an effect twice. At a greater speed the velocity arrow is longer, so the same turn of direction means a larger change of velocity $\Delta\overline{v}$. In addition, the object goes round the arc faster, so the velocity turns through the same angle in a shorter time. Both factors grow with the speed, so the acceleration grows with the square of the speed. At the same speed, in a bend of smaller radius the direction of the velocity turns faster, so the acceleration is larger.

    In the graph, with a radius of $50\,\text{m}$ the centripetal acceleration is only $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ at a speed of $10\,\text{m/s}$, but already $8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ at $20\,\text{m/s}$. With double the radius, $100\,\text{m}$, it is half as large at the same speed, $4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

    Newton's second law in circular motion

    By Newton's second law, in uniform circular motion the magnitude of the sum of the forces acting on an object is

    $$\Sigma F = m\,\frac{v^2}{r}$$

    where $\Sigma F$ is the net force pointing to the centre ($\text{N}$) and $m$ the mass of the object ($\text{kg}$). The calculation goes as in the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces: first draw the free-body diagram and choose one axis to point towards the centre of the path. Then the forces pointing towards the centre are positive, those pointing away are negative, and their sum is $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. Perpendicular to the plane of the path the object does not accelerate, so in that direction the sum of the forces is zero.

    Crest and dip

    When a car drives over the top of a hill at a steady speed, it is for a moment on a circular path whose centre is below the road. The acceleration then points down, so the weight is greater than the support force of the road: $G - N = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. The support force is smaller than the weight, and the passenger feels light. The greater the speed, the smaller the support force. If $\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ grows equal to $g$, the support force is zero. If it grows greater than $g$, the car leaves the road for a moment.

    At the bottom of a dip the centre is above the road, and the acceleration points up. Then the support force has to be greater than the weight: $N - G = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. That is why in a dip of a roller coaster the passenger is pressed into the seat and feels heavy. In both cases the weight is the same $mg$. Only the support force changes so that the net force points towards the centre of the arc.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that in circular motion a centrifugal force acts outwards on the object, keeping it on the path or throwing it out of the bend. There is no such force: no object pulls or pushes the car or the passenger outwards. When the car turns, the body of the passenger tends to continue straight on. The door presses against the passenger and pushes inwards, towards the centre of the bend, and this is felt as being pressed outwards. When the grip fails, the car does not fly out along the radius but continues straighter than the road bends. Another mistake is to draw the centripetal force in the free-body diagram as an arrow of its own. The centripetal force is the sum of real forces, such as friction or tension.

    Summary

    • In uniform circular motion the tangential speed is constant but the direction of the velocity changes, so the object accelerates.
    • The centripetal acceleration points towards the centre of the path, and its magnitude is $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$.
    • The sum of the forces on the object, the centripetal force, points towards the centre: $\Sigma F = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. It is the sum of real forces, not a force of its own.
    • In a bend the centripetal force is given by the friction of the road, on a string by the tension. At the top of a hill the support force is smaller than the weight, at the bottom of a dip greater.
    • There is no centrifugal force. When the force towards the centre is not enough, the object continues straighter than the path bends.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A car of mass $1200\,\text{kg}$ drives round a bend of a horizontal road at a steady speed of $54\,\text{km/h}$. The radius of the bend is $45\,\text{m}$. How large a friction force must the road exert on the car, and how large must the coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the road be at least? Air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected, and the forces on the tyres are added together.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, $v = 54\,\text{km/h}$, $r = 45\,\text{m}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the friction force $F_\mu$ of the road and the smallest coefficient of static friction $\mu_0$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Choose the car as the object. The forces on it come from the Earth (weight $\overline{G}$) and the road (support force $\overline{N}$ and friction $\overline{F}_\mu$). The car is in uniform circular motion, so by Newton's second law the net force points towards the centre of the bend and has the magnitude $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. The only horizontal force is friction, so it points towards the centre. The tyres do not slide sideways, so the friction is static friction, at most $\mu_0 N$. In the free-body diagram the car is seen from behind with the centre of the bend on the right, the $x$-axis towards the centre and the $y$-axis up.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The equations of motion are read from the diagram and the asked quantities are solved:

      $$\begin{aligned} &x\text{:}\ \ F_\mu = m\frac{v^2}{r} \\ &y\text{:}\ \ N - mg = 0 \\ &F_\mu \le \mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg \\ &\mu_0 \ge \frac{v^2}{gr} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The speed is converted to $\text{m/s}$:

      $v$$= 54\,\text{km/h}$
      $= \tfrac{54\cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
      $= 15\,\text{m/s}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} F_\mu &= m\frac{v^2}{r} \\ \phantom{F_\mu} &= 1200\,\text{kg}\cdot\frac{(15\,\text{m/s})^2}{45\,\text{m}} \\ \phantom{F_\mu} &= 6000\,\text{N} \\[4pt] \mu_0 &\ge \frac{v^2}{gr} \\ \phantom{\mu_0} &= \frac{(15\,\text{m/s})^2}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 45\,\text{m}} \\ \phantom{\mu_0} &\approx 0.510 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The friction force of the road is $F_\mu \approx 6.0\,\text{kN}$ towards the centre of the bend, and the coefficient of static friction must be at least $0.51$, with two significant figures like the speed $54\,\text{km/h}$ and the radius $45\,\text{m}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The centripetal acceleration is

      $a_\text{n}$$= \tfrac{(15\,\text{m/s})^2}{45\,\text{m}}$
      $= 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

      about half of the free-fall acceleration, so the friction must be about half of the weight, $11.8\,\text{kN}$. On dry asphalt the coefficient of static friction is about $0.8$, so the car stays in the bend. On wet asphalt, where the coefficient can be about $0.5$, the grip is at its limit, and on ice it would fail. The mass cancels, so the requirement on the coefficient is the same for a light and a heavy car.

      Find the error

      A car drives at a steady speed over the top of a hill. A student drew the free-body diagram of the car as in the picture and named the force starting from the top face the centrifugal force $\overline{F}_\text{cf}$.What is wrong in the diagram?

      • The weight $mg$ is the same on the hill and on a level road, because the gravitational pull of the Earth does not depend on the shape of the road. The support force changes. The error is the force $\overline{F}_\text{cf}$, which no object causes.

      • The centripetal force is not a force of its own but the sum of the forces. Here it is the difference of the weight and the support force, so it is not drawn separately. The error is the force $\overline{F}_\text{cf}$, which no object causes.

      • Then the sum of the forces would be zero, but at the top the car accelerates down towards the centre of the arc. The support force is rightly smaller than the weight. The error is the force $\overline{F}_\text{cf}$, which no object causes.

      • Correct. No object pulls the car upwards, so the centrifugal force is not drawn. At the top the car accelerates down towards the centre of the arc, so the sum of the forces cannot be zero: the weight is greater than the support force, $G - N = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$.

      Choose the right free-body diagram

      A car drives at a steady speed through the bottom of a dip.In which diagram is the free-body diagram of the car correct? Compare the arrows carefully: each wrong diagram has one error.

      • Here the support force is smaller than the weight, so the sum of the forces points down. That is the case at the top of a hill. At the bottom of a dip the centre is above, so the support force must be greater than the weight.

      • Here there is also a force $\overline{F}_\text{cf}$ pointing down, which no object causes. Without it the diagram would be correct. The centrifugal force is not drawn in a free-body diagram.

      • Here the support force and the weight are equal, so the sum of the forces is zero. At the bottom of a dip, however, the car accelerates up towards the centre of the arc, so the support force must be greater than the weight.

      • Correct. At the bottom of the dip the centre of the arc is above, so the car accelerates up. So the support force is greater than the weight, $N - G = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$, and the sum of the forces points up towards the centre.

      Show the solution

      Given

      Find

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      A child sits on a roundabout that turns steadily. In which direction does the sum of the forces on the child point?

      • The speed does not change, but the direction of the velocity does, so the child has an acceleration. Then the sum of the forces cannot be zero: it points towards the centre.

      • Motion does not need a force in the direction of the velocity to continue. The speed is constant, so there is no acceleration along the velocity. The direction of the velocity changes, and that takes a sum of forces pointing to the centre.

      • The body of the child tends to continue straight on, and that is why it feels as if he were pressed outwards. The seat and the support, however, push him inwards, and the sum of the forces points towards the centre.

      • Correct. The child is in uniform circular motion, so he has a centripetal acceleration towards the centre. By Newton's second law the sum of the forces points in the same direction.

        Exit ticket

        Finally three quick questions. Answer first yourself and open the answer only then.

        1. A car turns sharply to the left, and the passenger feels pressed against the door on the right. What force really acts on the passenger in the horizontal direction, and in which direction does it point?
          Answer

          The door (and the seat) pushes the passenger to the left, towards the centre of the bend. The body of the passenger tends to continue straight on, so the door comes against him and gives him the centripetal force. There is no centrifugal force pushing outwards: the feeling comes from the body continuing straight on if the door did not turn it.

        2. A car drives the same bend first at a speed of $40\,\text{km/h}$ and then at $120\,\text{km/h}$. How many times as large is the centripetal acceleration the second time?
          Answer

          The speed triples, and $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ grows with the square of the speed, so the acceleration is $3^2 = 9$ times as large. At the same time the friction of the road has to be nine times as large, and that is why a high speed easily takes the car out of the bend.

        3. Why does the passenger of a car feel light when the car drives fast over the top of a hill?
          Answer

          At the top the car and the passenger accelerate down towards the centre of the arc, so the support force of the seat must be smaller than the weight: $N = m\left(g - \tfrac{v^2}{r}\right)$. The weight does not change, but the seat presses the passenger less, and this is felt as lightness.

          Answer

          Given

          Find

          the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          The car drives round the bend at a steady speed, so it is in uniform circular motion. The direction of the velocity changes, and the acceleration points towards the centre of the bend. Its magnitude depends only on the tangential speed and the radius.

          Formula

          $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$

          Conversions

          The speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the radius in metres, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $a_\text{n}$$= \dfrac{(20\,\text{m/s})^2}{80\,\text{m}}$
          $= \dfrac{400\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{80\,\text{m}}$
          $= 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Reasonableness

          The acceleration is about half of the free-fall acceleration $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. A passenger feels it as a clear sideways push, but on a dry road the grip is enough for it. The unit $\tfrac{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{\text{m}} = \text{m/s}^2$ is the unit of acceleration.

          the net force $\Sigma F$ on the stone in $\text{N}$

          The stone is in uniform circular motion, so its acceleration is the centripetal acceleration towards the centre. By Newton's second law the net force is the product of the mass and the acceleration, and it points in the same direction.

          Formula

          $\Sigma F = m a_\text{n}$ and $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $\Sigma F = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$

          The weight of the stone is about $4.9\,\text{N}$, so the horizontal net force is slightly greater than the weight.         1. Core

          A cyclist rides through the bottom of a dip in an underpass at a steady speed of $8.0\,\text{m/s}$. The combined mass of the cyclist and the bicycle is $80\,\text{kg}$, and at the bottom of the dip the radius of the arc of the road is $16\,\text{m}$. Air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected.

          How large is the support force of the road on the bicycle at the bottom of the dip? Give the answer in kilonewtons.

          kN
          Answer

          $1.1\,\text{kN}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          combined mass $m = 80\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$, radius of the arc at the bottom of the dip $r = 16\,\text{m}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected

          Find

          the support force $N$ of the road in $\text{kN}$

          Principle

          At the bottom of the dip the cyclist and the bicycle are for a moment on a circular path whose centre is above the road. By Newton's second law the net force points up towards the centre and equals $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. In the vertical direction only the weight and the support force of the road act on them, so the support force must be greater than the weight.

          Force diagram

          The cyclist and the bicycle as one object, a rectangle on the road, $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards and the support force $\overline{N}$ of the road from the bottom face upwards, longer than the weight. The acceleration separately as a thin orange arrow up towards the centre of the arc.

          Formula

          $y$: $N - mg = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $N = m\left(g + \dfrac{v^2}{r}\right)$

          The quantities are in SI units; the result is finally converted to kilonewtons, $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.

          Substitution

          $N$$= 80\,\text{kg}\cdot\left(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + \dfrac{(8.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{16\,\text{m}}\right)$
          $= 80\,\text{kg}\cdot 13.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 1104.8\,\text{N}$
          $= 1.1048\,\text{kN}$

          Answer

          $1.1\,\text{kN}$

          Upwards, two significant figures like the speed $8.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the radius $16\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The weight is $80\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 785\,\text{N}$, and the support force is about $1.4$ times it, because $\tfrac{v^2}{r} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ is about $0.4$ times $g$.         1. An icy road bends horizontally, and the radius of the bend is $60\,\text{m}$. The coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the ice is $0.10$. Air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected.

          What is the greatest speed at which a car can go through the bend without sliding? Give the answer in $\text{km/h}$.

          km/h
          Answer

          $28\,\text{km/h}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          radius of the bend $r = 60\,\text{m}$, coefficient of static friction $\mu_0 = 0.10$, horizontal road; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected

          Find

          the greatest speed $v$ in $\text{km/h}$

          Principle

          In a bend the only horizontal force on the car is the static friction of the road, and by Newton's second law it must be $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre. Static friction can be at most $\mu_0 N$. At the greatest speed the friction is at its maximum.

          Force diagram

          The car seen from behind as a rectangle, the centre of the bend on the right, $x$ towards the centre and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the support force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom face upwards and equally long, and the maximum static friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the corner of the bottom face towards the centre, one tenth of the length of the weight. The acceleration separately towards the centre.

          Formula

          $y$: $N - mg = 0$ and $x$: $\mu_0 N = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $\mu_0 mg = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $v = \sqrt{\mu_0 g r}$

          Conversions

          The result is finally converted to $\text{km/h}$:

          $1\,\text{m/s}$$= \dfrac{0.001\,\text{km}}{\tfrac{1}{3600}\,\text{h}}$
          $= 3.6\,\text{km/h}$

          Substitution

          $v$$= \sqrt{0.10\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 60\,\text{m}}$
          $= \sqrt{58.86\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
          $= 7.672\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 7.672\cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h}$
          $= 27.62\,\text{km/h}$

          Answer

          $28\,\text{km/h}$

          Two significant figures like the radius $60\,\text{m}$ and the coefficient $0.10$.

          Reasonableness

          On ice a bend must be taken very slowly, and on dry asphalt ($\mu_0 \approx 0.8$) the same bend could be taken at $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.8$ times the speed, about $78\,\text{km/h}$. The mass cancelled, so even a heavy car does not stay in the bend any better.

        2. A puck of mass $0.40\,\text{kg}$ is tied with a string to a peg fixed to a table, and it slides on the smooth table along a horizontal circle of radius $0.80\,\text{m}$. The string breaks if its tension exceeds $25\,\text{N}$. Friction is neglected.

          What is the greatest rotation frequency the puck can have?

          Hz
          Answer

          $1.4\,\text{Hz}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 0.40\,\text{kg}$, $r = 0.80\,\text{m}$, greatest tension of the string $T_\text{max} = 25\,\text{N}$, the table is smooth and horizontal

          Find

          the greatest rotation frequency $f$ in $\text{Hz}$

          Principle

          The puck is in uniform circular motion. In the plane of the table only the tension of the string acts on it, so by Newton's second law the tension is $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the peg. The greater the speed, the greater the tension, so the greatest allowed tension gives the greatest speed. The rotation frequency $f$ is the number of revolutions per second, so in one second the puck travels the distance $f \cdot 2\pi r$, that is $v = 2\pi r f$.

          Force diagram

          The puck seen from the side as a rectangle on the table, the peg on the right, $x$ towards the peg and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the support force $\overline{N}$ of the table from the bottom face upwards and equally long, and the tension $\overline{T}$ of the string from the right side towards the peg. The acceleration separately towards the peg.

          Formula

          $x$: $T = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, where $T$ is the tension of the string, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T r}{m}}$; $v = 2\pi r f$, so $f = \dfrac{v}{2\pi r}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed; $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{1/s}$.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{25\,\text{N}\cdot 0.80\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{kg}}}$
          $= \sqrt{50\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
          $= 7.071\,\text{m/s}$
          $f$$= \dfrac{7.071\,\text{m/s}}{2\pi\cdot 0.80\,\text{m}}$
          $= 1.407\,\text{Hz}$

          Answer

          $1.4\,\text{Hz}$

          Two significant figures like the mass, the radius and the strength of the string.

          Reasonableness

          The puck makes a little over one revolution per second, and the period is about $0.71\,\text{s}$, which is possible when whirled by hand. The tension grows with the square of the speed, so a small increase in the frequency would soon break the string.

        3. A road designer dimensions a horizontal bend for a speed of $80\,\text{km/h}$. For safety, the friction of the road should not have to give the car a sideways force greater than $0.25$ times the weight of the car, so that the grip is enough also on a wet road. Air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected.

          How large must the radius of the bend be at least?

          m
          Answer

          $200\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          design speed $v = 80\,\text{km/h}$, sideways friction at most $0.25\,mg$, horizontal road; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected

          Find

          the smallest radius $r$ of the bend in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          In a bend the only horizontal force on the car is the friction of the road, and by Newton's second law it must be $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre. The smaller the radius, the greater the friction needed, so the smallest allowed radius is found when the friction is the greatest allowed.

          Force diagram

          The car seen from behind as a rectangle, the centre of the bend on the right, $x$ towards the centre and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the support force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom face upwards and equally long, and the friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the corner of the bottom face towards the centre, a quarter of the length of the weight. The acceleration separately towards the centre.

          Formula

          $y$: $N - mg = 0$ and $x$: $F_\mu = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$. The condition $F_\mu \le 0.25\,mg$ gives $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} \le 0.25\,mg$, so $r \ge \dfrac{v^2}{0.25\,g}$

          Conversions

          $v$$= 80\,\text{km/h}$
          $= \dfrac{80\cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $= 22.22\,\text{m/s}$

          Substitution

          $r$$= \dfrac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{0.25\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{493.8\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{2.4525\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 201.4\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $200\,\text{m}$

          Or $2.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the speed $80\,\text{km/h}$ and the coefficient $0.25$.

          Reasonableness

          The radii of the bends of country roads are hundreds of metres, so the result is believable. The radius grows with the square of the speed: for $120\,\text{km/h}$ a radius $2.25$ times as large would be needed, about $450\,\text{m}$. The mass cancelled, so the same radius is enough for all cars.
