## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** Read the hook; the review question links to the plasma unit. Ask the class: "What can a magnetic field push, and what can it not push?"
- **5–10 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually and committed, each with a short reason.
- **10–35 min, Investigate.** Pairs on one device. Two minutes of free exploration of the simulation, then experiment 1 (field and radius, at least three rows) and experiment 2 (the mirror, at least three pitch angles, watching each orbit for a few seconds). The pair conclusion with the word bank: one student speaks, the other asks questions; both write their own answer.
- **35–50 min, Theory summary.** Read the force, the formula and the mirror sections together; the second theory part (why a ring, one particle is not a plasma); the ordering task, explained aloud in pairs; back to the predictions.
- **50–62 min, Apply.** The worked example (check it in the simulation: deuteron, $1.0 \cdot 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$, $5.0\ \text{T}$ gives $r = 4.17\ \text{mm}$); the alpha-particle task individually; the misconception text.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the explanation task with the sentence starters, the summary and the French corner.

## Organisation and preparation
- Test the simulation on the classroom devices beforehand. The experiments set and lock the controls; if a pair gets lost, *Reset* in the experiment returns to its start.
- In experiment 2 the *Magnetic mirror* checkbox must be ticked (it is by default). The verdict "trapped" comes from the mirror condition; students should also watch the orbit turn back or leave.
- A demonstration helps: an electron beam tube (e/m apparatus, also at the Fotoni lab visit) with Helmholtz coils shows the circle shrinking as the coil current grows. A magnet held near an old CRT screen is not recommended.

## Plan for fast pairs and for running out of time
- **Fast pairs:** the optional particle experiment (why the deuteron and the alpha particle have almost the same circle; which particle turns the other way); the optional error-spotting task; then the *Electric field* checkbox: describe the drift and note that it goes the same way for both signs of charge.
- **Running out of time:** shorten experiment 2 to three angles (10°, 40°, 80°); set the French corner and check 3 as homework; read *One particle is not a plasma* aloud instead of individually.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade: the conclusion relates radius and field with numbers from the student's own table and separates trapped and escaping pitch angles; the explanation shows that particles circle around field lines but move freely along them, that a tube has ends, that a ring closes the field lines, and one remaining problem.
- Calculation: the alpha task shows the conversion from MeV to joules, the speed from the kinetic energy and $|q| = 2e$.
- Language: at least three key words used correctly (field, force, radius, pitch angle, trapped, escape, drift) and a sentence starter used to argue from evidence.

## Common difficulties
- "The magnetic force speeds the particle up." It is perpendicular to the velocity and does no work.
- The direction of the force: the right-hand rule for a positive charge; reverse it for an electron.
- The scale changes in the simulation: real gyroradii range from micrometres (electrons) to centimetres; the grid square changes so that the orbit fits. Ask students to read the radius from the readout, not from the picture.
- The mirror reflects large pitch angles, not small ones: the motion *along* the field is what must be stopped.
- The single-particle picture is not the plasma. Make sure every student can name one thing it leaves out (collisions, the plasma's own currents and fields, turbulence, instabilities).

## Extension ideas
- Use the *Electric field* checkbox and derive the drift speed $v_\text{d} = E/B$ from the condition that the electric and magnetic forces cancel for the centre of the circle.
- The Earth as a magnetic mirror: look up the Van Allen radiation belts (discovered in 1958) and explain why some particles reach the polar atmosphere and make the northern lights.
- History: magnetic mirror machines were developed at Livermore from the 1950s by Richard Post's team. Why were they finally given up in favour of rings?
