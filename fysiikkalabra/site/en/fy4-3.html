Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws

Why does a curling stone slide tens of metres along the ice although nobody is pushing it any more?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain with Newton's first law why motion continues without a forward force
  • identify a force and its reaction force and explain why they do not cancel each other
  • calculate an unknown force when an object is at rest or moves at constant velocity

Prior knowledge

  • Velocity is a vector, and in uniform motion it stays the same in magnitude and directionchapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 3.

1Think about it

A curler slides a few metres with the stone and then lets go. The stone carries on alone along the ice and stops only tens of metres away, in the target area. The sweepers do not touch the stone, and nothing else pushes it after the release. When the stone hits another stone, both stones change their motion.

Why does the stone keep moving although nothing pushes it any more, and what happens to each stone in the collision?

Optional Recall: uniform motion
The $(t, x)$ graph of an object is a rising straight line. What does it tell about the velocity of the object?

  • The position of an object at rest does not change, so its $(t, x)$ graph is a horizontal line. A rising line means motion at constant velocity (chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs).

  • The position increases, but by the same amount every second, so the velocity stays the same. An increasing velocity would show in the $(t, x)$ graph as a curve bending upwards (chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs).

  • Correct. The slope of the line is the velocity, and on a straight line it is the same everywhere. This chapter shows what uniform motion tells about the forces acting on an object.

The weight is $G = mg = 10\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 98.1\,\text{N}$, and the floor supports the box with an equally large normal force $N$. In the model values the sliding distance is $1.125\,\text{m}$ on the carpet, $2.25\,\text{m}$ on the wooden floor and $4.5\,\text{m}$ on the ice. Calculate the product $F_\mu\cdot s$ for each surface.

The product is $45\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ on every surface. Solve $s$ from the equation $5.0\,\text{N}\cdot s = 45\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$.

Show the solution

Given

model values at the initial speed $v_0 = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$: on the carpet $F_\mu = 40\,\text{N}$ and $s = 1.125\,\text{m}$, on the wooden floor $20\,\text{N}$ and $2.25\,\text{m}$, on the ice $10\,\text{N}$ and $4.5\,\text{m}$; new friction force $F_\mu = 5.0\,\text{N}$

Find

the sliding distance $s$ in $\text{m}$

Principle

The table shows a regularity: when the friction force is halved, the sliding distance doubles. So the product of the friction force and the sliding distance is the same on every surface. We assume that the same rule also holds for a smaller friction.

Formula

$F_\mu s = F_{\mu,1} s_1$, so $s = \dfrac{F_{\mu,1} s_1}{F_\mu}$, where $F_{\mu,1}$ and $s_1$ are the values of one row of the table

Conversions

The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

On the ice row

$F_{\mu,1} s_1$$= 10\,\text{N}\cdot 4.5\,\text{m}$
$= 45\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$

so

$s$$= 45\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} : 5.0\,\text{N}$
$= 9.0\,\text{m}$

Answer

$9.0\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the friction force $5.0\,\text{N}$.

Reasonableness

The friction is half of the friction on the ice, so the distance must be twice the $4.5\,\text{m}$ of the ice, and it is. As the friction decreases towards zero, the sliding distance grows without limit: on a frictionless surface the box does not stop at all, and that is exactly what Newton's first law says.

Conclusion

Using your measurements and the force diagram, explain why the box slides after the release although the hand no longer pushes it, and what would happen if there were no friction at all.

Model answer

During the push the hand acts on the box, but the force ends when the hand lets go. After that the force diagram has only the weight, the normal force and friction pointing backwards. The box continues its motion because it has a velocity, and only friction changes it. In the model values the box slid $1.125\,\text{m}$ on the carpet ($40\,\text{N}$), $2.25\,\text{m}$ on the wooden floor ($20\,\text{N}$) and $4.5\,\text{m}$ on the ice ($10\,\text{N}$): when the friction is halved, the sliding distance doubles. On the frictionless surface the net force is zero, and the speed stayed at $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ to the end of the track. Without friction the box would therefore slide at the same speed for ever, as Newton's first law says.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Force is an interaction

    When two objects act on each other, there is an interaction between them: a hand pushes a box, a rope pulls a sledge and the Earth pulls an apple. The strength of an interaction is described by a force, whose unit is the newton ($\text{N}$). A force is a vector with a magnitude and a direction, and it is written $\overline{F}$. Every force has a cause, and that cause is always another object. In contact forces the objects touch each other. The gravitational force of the Earth, the weight $\overline{G}$, and the forces of magnets also act at a distance.

    Motion continues without a force

    A curler pushes the stone only for a moment. When the hand lets go, nothing pushes the stone forward any more, but the stone still slides tens of metres. During the slide only the Earth and the ice act on the stone (air resistance is very small). In the vertical direction the weight and the normal force of the ice, the force that supports the stone upwards and is perpendicular to the surface, cancel each other. In the horizontal direction there is only the small friction of the ice, a force between the surfaces that opposes motion, points backwards and slows the stone down very gradually.

    If there were no friction at all, the speed of the stone would not change, and it would slide in the same direction for ever. This is Newton's first law, also called the law of inertia: if the sum of the forces acting on an object is zero, the object stays at rest or moves in a straight line at constant velocity. A force is needed to change motion, not to keep it going. The tendency of an object to keep its state of motion is called inertia.

    Everyday experience seems to say otherwise, because on Earth friction and air resistance slow down almost everything that moves. A bicycle stops when you stop pedalling because the road and the air brake it, not because the "force of motion" has been used up. The smaller the friction, the longer the motion continues: further on ice than on asphalt, and in space almost without limit.

    Action and reaction

    When skater A pushes skater B, both start to glide away from each other. B does not just receive the push, but acts on A at the same time. An interaction always works in both directions. This is Newton's third law, the law of action and reaction: when object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts an equally large but oppositely directed force on A. The two forces form an action–reaction pair. A force and its reaction force arise in the same interaction at the same time, and they are of the same kind: push and counter-push, attraction and counter-attraction.

    An adult and a child receive an equally large force from each other, although the adult is much heavier. The child still starts moving with a greater speed, because the same force changes the motion of the smaller mass more; we return to this in the chapter Newton's second law. The most important point is that a force and its reaction force act on different objects. That is why they never cancel each other, and why they are not drawn in the force diagram of the same object.

    Newton's first and third laws

    Newton's first law is a condition for the sum of the forces acting on an object:

    $$\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0} \ \Leftrightarrow\ \overline{v} = \text{constant}$$

    where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the vector sum of the forces acting on the object, the net force (N), and $\overline{v}$ is the velocity of the object (m/s). Constant velocity means that both the magnitude and the direction of the velocity stay the same; rest, $\overline{v} = \overline{0}$, is also constant velocity.

    A net force of zero does not mean that there are no forces, but that they cancel each other. On a car driving along a straight road at constant velocity, the weight and the normal force of the road act in the vertical direction, and the force of the road on the tyres forwards and the air resistance backwards. Because the velocity does not change, the forward force is as large as the air resistance when rolling resistance is ignored.

    Newton's third law is written for the forces between two objects:

    $$\overline{F}_{AB} = -\overline{F}_{BA}$$

    where $\overline{F}_{AB}$ is the force with which object A acts on object B (N), and $\overline{F}_{BA}$ is the force with which B acts on A (N). The minus sign tells that the forces are opposite in direction; the magnitudes are the same, $F_{AB} = F_{BA}$.

    In calculations the magnitude of the weight is often needed:

    $$G = mg$$

    where $G$ is the weight (N), $m$ is the mass of the object (kg) and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the acceleration due to gravity. The reaction force of the weight is the equally large gravitational force of the object on the Earth.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a force must act on a moving object in the direction of motion and that the motion ends when this force "wears out". According to the first law this is not so: no force acts forwards on a sliding curling stone, and it slows down only because of friction. Another common error is to think that a force and its reaction force cancel each other. They act on different objects: a horse pulls a cart and the cart pulls the horse, but the motion of the cart is decided only by the forces acting on the cart. A third error is to take the weight of a book resting on a table and the normal force of the table as a force and its reaction force. They are equally large because of the first law. The reaction force of the normal force is the force of the book on the table, and the reaction force of the weight is the gravitational force of the book on the Earth.

    Summary

    • A force describes the interaction of two objects. Every force has a cause, another object, and the unit of force is the newton.
    • Newton's first law: if the net force is zero, the object stays at rest or moves in a straight line at constant velocity.
    • A force is needed to change motion, not to keep it going. In everyday life motion slows down because of friction and air resistance.
    • Newton's third law: $\overline{F}_{AB} = -\overline{F}_{BA}$. A force and its reaction force are equally large, opposite in direction and of the same kind, and they act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A passenger aircraft flies horizontally in a straight line at a constant velocity of $850\,\text{km/h}$. Each of its two jet engines pushes air and exhaust gases backwards with a force of $48\,\text{kN}$. How large is the air resistance on the aircraft?

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. The force of one engine on the gases is $F_1 = 48\,\text{kN}$ backwards, there are two engines, and the velocity $850\,\text{km/h}$ stays the same in magnitude and direction. We are asked for the air resistance $F_\text{d}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. By Newton's third law the gases push each engine forwards with an equally large force $F_1$; this is the thrust of the engine. The aircraft flies straight at constant velocity, so by Newton's first law its net force is zero. In the horizontal direction only two thrusts $\overline{F}_1$ and $\overline{F}_2$ act forwards on the aircraft (both $48\,\text{kN}$), and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ acts backwards, so they cancel each other. In the vertical direction the lift of the wings and the weight cancel each other. In the force diagram the aircraft is a rectangle and the forces acting on it are arrows:

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. Choose the forward direction as positive and solve the air resistance:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Sigma F_x &= 2F_1 - F_\text{d} = 0 \\ F_\text{d} &= 2F_1 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The velocity $850\,\text{km/h}$ is not needed: it is enough to know that it does not change. The forces are in kilonewtons, so the result is obtained directly in kilonewtons: $48\,\text{kN} = 48\,000\,\text{N}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the force of the engine with its unit:

      $$\begin{aligned} F_\text{d} &= 2F_1 \\ &= 2\cdot 48\,\text{kN} \\ &= 96\,\text{kN} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The air resistance is $96\,\text{kN}$, or $9.6\cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N}$, and it points backwards. The precision is two significant figures like the force of the engine $48\,\text{kN}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. If the air resistance were smaller than the thrusts, the net force would point forwards and the aircraft would accelerate; if it were larger, the aircraft would slow down. Constant velocity is possible only when the air resistance is as large as the sum of the thrusts $2\cdot 48\,\text{kN}$. The result does not depend on the magnitude of the velocity: the same reasoning holds for all constant velocities.

      Optional Find the error
      A student reasons: “The horse cannot get the cart moving, because the cart pulls the horse backwards with an equally large force as the horse pulls the cart forwards.The forces cancel each other.” What is the error in the reasoning?

      • A force and its reaction force arise in the same interaction at the same moment. When the horse pulls the shafts, the shafts pull the horse immediately. The error is that forces acting on different objects are added together.

      • Correct. The pull of the horse acts on the cart and the pull of the cart on the horse. The cart starts to move when the pull of the horse is larger than the resistance of the road on the cart. The horse, in turn, starts to move when the ground pushes its hooves forward more than the cart pulls it backwards.

      • Newton's third law always holds, also in accelerating motion: the forces of the horse and the cart on each other are equally large. The error is that forces acting on different objects are added together.

      Apply to a new situation

      A car pulls a trailer along a straight road at constant velocity. The air resistance $420\,\text{N}$ and the rolling resistance $260\,\text{N}$ act backwards on the trailer.

      With what force does the trailer pull the tow hook of the car? Answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      First choose the trailer as the object. Which horizontal forces act on it, and what does the constant velocity tell about their sum?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      On the trailer, the forward force of the tow hook is as large as the resistances together. The force asked for, however, acts on the car: which law connects these two forces?

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      By Newton's third law the trailer pulls the hook backwards with the same force as the hook pulls the trailer forwards: $420\,\text{N} + 260\,\text{N}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      air resistance of the trailer $F_\text{d} = 420\,\text{N}$ and rolling resistance $F_\text{r} = 260\,\text{N}$ backwards, the velocity is constant and the road is straight

      Find

      the force of the trailer on the tow hook of the car $F_{\text{TC}}$ in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The trailer moves in a straight line at constant velocity, so by Newton's first law its net force is zero: the forward force $F_{\text{CT}}$ of the tow hook is as large as the resistances together. By Newton's third law the trailer pulls the hook backwards with an equally large force.

      Force diagram

      Choose the trailer as the object and draw it as a rectangle. Three forces act on it in the horizontal direction: the force of the tow hook $\overline{F}_{\text{CT}}$ forwards, and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{a}$ and rolling resistance $\overline{F}_\text{r}$ backwards. In the vertical direction there are also the weight and the normal force of the road, which cancel each other. The force of the car on the hook does not belong in the force diagram of the trailer, because it acts on the car. The positive direction is forwards.

      Formula

      $\Sigma F_x$$= F_{\text{CT}} - F_\text{d} - F_\text{r}$
      $= 0$

      and $F_{\text{TC}} = F_{\text{CT}}$, so $F_{\text{TC}} = F_\text{d} + F_\text{r}$

      Conversions

      The forces are in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $F_{\text{TC}}$$= 420\,\text{N} + 260\,\text{N}$
      $= 680\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $680\,\text{N}$

      Backwards, to the nearest ten newtons like the given forces.

      Reasonableness

      If the hook pulled the trailer more weakly than $680\,\text{N}$, the resistances would slow it down, and if it pulled harder, the trailer would accelerate. The force of the road on the wheels of the car must cancel, in addition to the car's own air resistance and rolling resistance, this $680\,\text{N}$ as well, so the trailer increases fuel consumption.

        Exit ticket

        Finally, two quick questions. First answer yourself and only then open the answer.

        1. A space probe travels far from stars and planets with its engine switched off. Is a force needed for the probe to keep going?
          Answer

          No. When no forces act on the probe, by Newton's first law it continues in a straight line at the same velocity. An engine is needed only when the magnitude or the direction of the velocity is to be changed.

        2. A mosquito hits the windscreen of a moving car. Which one experiences the larger force in the collision, the mosquito or the car?
          Answer

          The forces are equally large and opposite in direction, because they belong to the same interaction (Newton's third law). The consequences are different: the motion of the light mosquito changes drastically, but the motion of the heavy car hardly changes at all.

          The rope is at rest, so by Newton's first law the sum of the forces acting on it is zero. In the horizontal direction only the pulls of the teams act on the rope.

          Force diagram

          The rope as a long rectangle, positive direction to the right. The pull of team A $\overline{F}_A$ from the left end of the rope to the left and the pull of team B $\overline{F}_B$ from the right end to the right.

          Formula

          $F_B - F_A = 0$, so $F_B = F_A$

          Conversions

          The force is in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F_B = F_A = 850\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $850\,\text{N}$

          Three significant figures like the force $850\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          If B pulled more weakly, the rope would start to move to the left. A tug of war is won by the team that gets a larger friction force from the ground on its feet, not by the team that pulls the rope harder: the rope pulls both teams with an equally large force.

        3. A skater stands on the ice and pushes the boards of the rink with their hands with a force of $90\,\text{N}$.

          With what force do the boards push the skater?

          N
          Answer

          $90\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          force of the skater on the board $F_{\text{SB}} = 90\,\text{N}$ towards the board

          Find

          the force of the board on the skater $F_{\text{BS}}$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          Newton's third law: the skater and the board interact, so the board acts on the skater with an equally large but oppositely directed force. The law holds regardless of whether either of them moves.

          Force diagram

          Choose the skater as the object and draw the skater as a rectangle; the positive direction is away from the board. The skater's weight acts down, the normal force of the ice up and the force of the board $\overline{F}_{\text{BS}}$ away from the board, ending at the skater's side. The skater's own force on the board $\overline{F}_{\text{SB}}$ acts on the board, so it does not belong in the skater's diagram but is the reaction force of the force asked for.

          Formula

          $\overline{F}_{\text{BS}} = -\overline{F}_{\text{SB}}$, so in magnitudes $F_{\text{BS}} = F_{\text{SB}}$

          Conversions

          The force is in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F_{\text{BS}}$$= F_{\text{SB}}$
          $= 90\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $90\,\text{N}$

          On the skater, directed away from the board, two significant figures like the force $90\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          The skater starts to glide away from the board, so a force away from the board has acted on the skater. Its cause is the board. The board does not move, because it is fixed to the ice and the structures of the rink, which act on it with their own forces.

        4. A car drives along a straight road at constant velocity. The road pushes the driving wheels of the car forwards with a force of $620\,\text{N}$, and the air resistance on the car is $380\,\text{N}$. In addition, rolling resistance acts backwards on the car.

          How large is the rolling resistance?

          N
          Answer

          $240\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          force of the road on the tyres forwards $F = 620\,\text{N}$, air resistance $F_\text{a} = 380\,\text{N}$, the velocity is constant and the road is straight

          Find

          the rolling resistance $F_\text{r}$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The car moves in a straight line at constant velocity, so by Newton's first law the net force is zero also in the direction of motion: the forces acting forwards and backwards are equally large.

          Force diagram

          The car as a rectangle, positive direction forwards. In the horizontal direction the force of the road $\overline{F}$ forwards, and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{a}$ and rolling resistance $\overline{F}_\text{r}$ backwards. In the vertical direction the weight and the normal force of the road cancel each other.

          Formula

          $F - F_\text{a} - F_\text{r} = 0$, so $F_\text{r} = F - F_\text{a}$

          Conversions

          The forces are in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F_\text{r}$$= 620\,\text{N} - 380\,\text{N}$
          $= 240\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $240\,\text{N}$

          To the nearest ten newtons like the given forces, so two significant figures.

          Reasonableness

          The sum of the resistances $380\,\text{N} + 240\,\text{N} = 620\,\text{N}$ is as large as the force driving forwards, so the velocity does not change. The rolling resistance is smaller than the air resistance, which fits motorway speeds, at which air resistance is the largest resistance on a car.

        5. A book of mass $0.65\,\text{kg}$ rests on a table. The Earth pulls the book with a force equal to its weight.

          With what force does the book pull the Earth?

          N
          Answer

          $6.4\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the book $m = 0.65\,\text{kg}$, the force of the Earth on the book is the weight of the book; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the force of the book on the Earth $F$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The Earth and the book pull each other. By Newton's third law the book pulls the Earth with an equally large force as the Earth pulls the book, but in the opposite direction, that is, upwards. The force of the Earth on the book is the weight $G = mg$.

          Force diagram

          Choose the book as the object and draw it as a rectangle; the positive direction is up. The weight $\overline{G}$ acts down from the centre and the normal force of the table $\overline{N}$ up from the bottom surface. The weight is the force of the Earth on the book, and its reaction force is the force $\overline{F}$ of the book on the Earth, which acts on the Earth and does not belong in the diagram of the book.

          Formula

          $F = G$ and $G = mg$, so $F = mg$

          Conversions

          The mass is in kilograms, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F$$= 0.65\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 6.3765\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $6.4\,\text{N}$

          Upwards towards the book, two significant figures like the mass $0.65\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          The weight of a kilogram is about $10\,\text{N}$, so the weight of a book of $0.65\,\text{kg}$ is about $6\,\text{N}$. The Earth does not noticeably move because of this force, since its mass is enormous. The normal force of the table is not the reaction force of the weight, but belongs to the interaction between the book and the table.

        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          On a table there is a book of weight $8.0\,\text{N}$ and on top of it another book of weight $5.0\,\text{N}$. The books are at rest.

          With what force does the lower book press the table?

          N
          Answer

          $13\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          weight of the lower book $G_1 = 8.0\,\text{N}$, weight of the upper book $G_2 = 5.0\,\text{N}$, the books are at rest

          Find

          the force of the lower book on the table $F$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The upper book is at rest, so the lower book supports it with a force $G_2$ (first law), and by the third law the upper book presses the lower one down with an equally large force. The lower book is also at rest, so the normal force of the table on it is as large as the downward forces together. Finally, the lower book presses the table with a force as large as the normal force (third law).

          Force diagram

          The lower book as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}_1$ down from the centre, the force of the upper book $\overline{F}_2$ down from the top surface and the normal force of the table $\overline{N}$ up from the bottom surface.

          Formula

          $N - G_1 - F_2 = 0$ and $F_2 = G_2$, so $F = N = G_1 + G_2$

          Conversions

          The forces are in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F$$= 8.0\,\text{N} + 5.0\,\text{N}$
          $= 13\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $13\,\text{N}$

          Downwards. The sum is to the nearest tenth of a newton like the weights $8.0\,\text{N}$ and $5.0\,\text{N}$, so it has two significant figures.

          Reasonableness

          The table supports both books, so it must support their combined weight. The upper book does not touch the table, however: its weight is transmitted to the table through the lower book as a chain of force pairs.

        2. Core

          A swimmer swims straight in the horizontal direction at constant velocity. The swimmer's weight is $720\,\text{N}$ and the buoyancy of the water is equally large. She pushes the water backwards with the hands and feet with an average force of $65\,\text{N}$.

          How large is the average drag of the water on the swimmer?

          N
          Answer

          $65\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          force of the swimmer on the water $F_{\text{SW}} = 65\,\text{N}$ backwards, weight of the swimmer $720\,\text{N}$ and an equally large buoyancy of the water, the swimmer swims straight in the horizontal direction at constant velocity

          Find

          the drag of the water on the swimmer $F_\text{d}$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          By the third law the water pushes the swimmer forwards with an equally large force $F_{\text{WS}} = 65\,\text{N}$; this force moves the swimmer forwards. The swimmer moves at constant velocity, so by the first law the sum of the horizontal forces is zero. The weight and the buoyancy are vertical and do not affect the horizontal equilibrium, so they are not needed: the drag of the water backwards is as large as the push of the water forwards.

          Force diagram

          The swimmer as a rectangle, positive direction forwards. The push of the water $\overline{F}_{\text{WS}}$ forwards and the drag of the water $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ backwards. In the vertical direction the buoyancy and the weight cancel each other.

          Formula

          $F_{\text{WS}} - F_\text{d} = 0$ and $F_{\text{WS}} = F_{\text{SW}}$, so $F_\text{d} = F_{\text{SW}}$

          Conversions

          The force is in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F_\text{d}$$= F_{\text{SW}}$
          $= 65\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $65\,\text{N}$

          Backwards, two significant figures like the force $65\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          The swimmer moves forwards only because the water pushes the swimmer; without a reaction force, pulling with the hands would not move the swimmer anywhere. If the swimmer pushed the water harder, the forward force would increase and she would accelerate until the drag grew equally large.

        3. A skydiver whose mass with equipment is $85\,\text{kg}$ descends straight down at constant velocity with the parachute open.

          How large is the air resistance on the skydiver and the parachute together?

          N
          Answer

          $830\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass with equipment $m = 85\,\text{kg}$, the velocity is constant and straight down; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the air resistance $F_\text{d}$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The skydiver descends in a straight line at constant velocity, so by Newton's first law the net force is zero. The Earth (weight, down) and the air (resistance, up, against the motion) act on the skydiver.

          Force diagram

          The skydiver and the parachute as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ down from the centre and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ up, equally long. The velocity, directed down, is drawn separately and is not a force.

          Formula

          $F_\text{d} - G = 0$ and $G = mg$, so $F_\text{d} = mg$

          Conversions

          The mass is in kilograms, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F_\text{d}$$= 85\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 833.85\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $830\,\text{N}$

          Or $8.3\cdot 10^{2}\,\text{N}$, upwards, two significant figures like the mass $85\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          The weight of $85\,\text{kg}$ is about $850\,\text{N}$, and the air resistance is equally large. Motion downwards does not require a net force downwards: the skydiver moves down because of inertia, and the forces are in balance.

        4. The weight of an elevator car is $5.8\,\text{kN}$, and the car contains passengers whose total mass is $320\,\text{kg}$. The elevator rises at constant velocity.

          How large is the tension of the cable on the car? Answer in kilonewtons.

          kN
          Answer

          $8.9\,\text{kN}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          weight of the car $G_\text{c} = 5.8\,\text{kN}$, mass of the passengers $m = 320\,\text{kg}$, the elevator rises at constant velocity; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the tension of the cable $T$ in $\text{kN}$

          Principle

          The car and the passengers move together at constant velocity, so by the first law the sum of the forces acting on them is zero. When the car and the passengers are thought of as one object, the cable supports their combined weight.

          Force diagram

          The car with the passengers as a rectangle, positive direction up. The tension of the cable $\overline{T}$ up from the top surface, and the weight of the car $\overline{G}_\text{c}$ and the weight of the passengers $\overline{G}_\text{p}$ down.

          Formula

          $T - G_\text{c} - mg = 0$, so $T = G_\text{c} + mg$

          Conversions

          $5.8\,\text{kN} = 5800\,\text{N}$; at the end the newtons are converted to kilonewtons by dividing by $1000$.

          Substitution

          $T$$= 5800\,\text{N} + 320\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 5800\,\text{N} + 3139.2\,\text{N}$
          $= 8939.2\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $8.9\,\text{kN}$

          Two significant figures like the weight of the car $5.8\,\text{kN}$.

          Reasonableness

          The weight of the passengers is about $3\,\text{kN}$, and $5.8\,\text{kN} + 3\,\text{kN} \approx 9\,\text{kN}$. In steady rising the tension is exactly the same as in an elevator standing still, because the velocity does not change.

        Level 3 · challenging 3 exercises

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. A hot-air balloon descends straight down at constant velocity. The combined weight of the balloon, the hot air inside it, the basket and the passengers is $4.62\,\text{kN}$, and the buoyancy of the surrounding air is $4.35\,\text{kN}$.

          How large is the air resistance on the balloon, and in which direction does it point? Give the magnitude in kilonewtons and the direction in words; only the magnitude is checked.

          kN
          Answer

          $0.27\,\text{kN}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          combined weight $G = 4.62\,\text{kN}$, buoyancy $F_\text{b} = 4.35\,\text{kN}$, the balloon descends straight down at constant velocity

          Find

          the air resistance $F_\text{d}$ in $\text{kN}$ and its direction

          Principle

          The balloon moves at constant velocity, so by the first law the net force is zero. Air resistance opposes the motion, and the balloon moves down, so the resistance points up. The buoyancy alone is not enough to cancel the weight, so the air resistance supports the rest.

          Force diagram

          The balloon with its basket as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ down from the centre, the buoyancy $\overline{F}_\text{b}$ up and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ up. The velocity down is drawn separately and is not a force.

          Formula

          $F_\text{b} + F_\text{d} - G = 0$, so $F_\text{d} = G - F_\text{b}$

          Conversions

          All forces are in kilonewtons and the answer is asked in kilonewtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F_\text{d}$$= 4.62\,\text{kN} - 4.35\,\text{kN}$
          $= 0.27\,\text{kN}$

          Answer

          $0.27\,\text{kN}$

          Upwards. The difference has two significant figures, because the given forces are to the nearest hundredth of a kilonewton.

          Reasonableness

          If there were no air resistance, the difference of $0.27\,\text{kN}$ between the weight and the buoyancy, directed down, would change the motion of the balloon and it would descend faster and faster. Steady descent means that the air resistance has grown exactly as large as this difference. The air resistance points up, although the motion is down.

        2. Two identical ring magnets are threaded onto a vertical wooden stick standing on a table. The magnets repel each other, and the upper one floats at rest above the lower one without touching it. The weight of each magnet is $0.30\,\text{N}$, and friction against the stick is ignored.

          With what force does the table support the lower magnet? The force of the stick is ignored.

          N
          Answer

          $0.60\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          weight of each magnet $G = 0.30\,\text{N}$, the upper magnet floats at rest, friction and the force of the stick are ignored

          Find

          the normal force of the table on the lower magnet $N$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The upper magnet is at rest, so the repulsion of the lower magnet on it points up and is as large as its weight (first law). By the third law the upper magnet repels the lower one downwards with an equally large force, although the magnets do not touch each other. The lower magnet is at rest, so the normal force of the table cancels its weight and this force.

          Force diagram

          The lower magnet as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ down from the centre, the force of the upper magnet $\overline{F}_\text{m}$ down from the top surface and the normal force of the table $\overline{N}$ up from the bottom surface.

          Formula

          $N - G - F_\text{m} = 0$ and $F_\text{m} = G$, so $N = 2G$

          Conversions

          The forces are in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $N$$= 2\cdot 0.30\,\text{N}$
          $= 0.60\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $0.60\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the weight $0.30\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          The table carries the weight of both magnets, just as if the upper magnet were resting on the lower one. A floating magnet is not weightless: its weight is transmitted to the table through a magnetic pair of forces.

        3. Two spring scales are hung one below the other: the upper one is attached to the ceiling, and the lower one hangs from the hook of the upper one. An apple of weight $1.5\,\text{N}$ hangs from the hook of the lower scale. The weight of the lower scale itself is $0.40\,\text{N}$. Everything is at rest.

          What does the upper scale show?

          N
          Answer

          $1.9\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          weight of the apple $G_\text{a} = 1.5\,\text{N}$, weight of the lower scale $G_\text{s} = 0.40\,\text{N}$, everything is at rest

          Find

          the reading of the upper scale, that is, the force $F$ with which the lower scale pulls the hook of the upper one, in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The apple is at rest, so the hook of the lower scale pulls it up with a force of $1.5\,\text{N}$ (first law), and by the third law the apple pulls the hook down with an equally large force. The lower scale is at rest, so the hook of the upper scale must pull it up by the force of the apple plus the weight of the scale itself. By the third law the lower scale pulls the upper one down with an equally large force, and this is what the upper scale shows.

          Force diagram

          The lower scale as a rectangle, positive direction up. The force of the hook of the upper scale $\overline{F}$ up from the top surface, the weight $\overline{G}_\text{s}$ down from the centre and the force of the apple $\overline{F}_\text{a}$ down from the bottom surface.

          Formula

          $F - G_\text{s} - F_\text{a} = 0$ and $F_\text{a} = G_\text{a}$, so $F = G_\text{a} + G_\text{s}$

          Conversions

          The forces are in newtons, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $F$$= 1.5\,\text{N} + 0.40\,\text{N}$
          $= 1.9\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $1.9\,\text{N}$

          To the nearest tenth of a newton like the weight of the apple $1.5\,\text{N}$, so two significant figures.

          Reasonableness

          The lower scale shows the weight of the apple, $1.5\,\text{N}$, and the upper one also supports the lower scale, so its reading must be larger. The difference of the readings, $0.40\,\text{N}$, is exactly the weight of the lower scale.
