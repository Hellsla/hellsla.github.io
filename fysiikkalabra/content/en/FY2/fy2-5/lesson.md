# Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm

> **Why** does a kilogram of petrol release almost three times as much energy as a kilogram of air-dried firewood, even though both are just burning matter?

## Key idea
A fuel is a substance whose chemical energy is released by burning it. Chemical energy is energy stored in the structure of the atoms and molecules of a substance. When a fuel burns, the structure changes and energy is released as heat and light. Energy does not appear out of nothing: chemical energy is transformed into another form. The same is true of food: your body slowly "burns" food and releases energy from it.

Different fuels contain different amounts of chemical energy for their mass. The quantity that tells you this is the heat of combustion $H$. It is the energy released when one kilogram of fuel burns, and its unit is, for example, $\text{MJ/kg}$. The heat of combustion of petrol is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$, so about $43\,\text{MJ}$ is released from one kilogram (about $43 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}$). The heat of combustion of air-dried firewood (moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) is only about $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$, so to get the same energy you need almost three times the mass of wood.

Because the heat of combustion is energy per unit of mass, the energy released is directly proportional to the mass of fuel burnt: twice as much fuel releases twice as much energy. This gives the law used to calculate the energy released.

## Formula: Heat of combustion and released energy
The energy released when a fuel burns is
```latex
E = Hm
```
where $E$ is the energy released ($\text{J}$ or $\text{MJ}$), $H$ is the heat of combustion of the fuel ($\text{MJ/kg}$) and $m$ is the mass of fuel burnt ($\text{kg}$). The mass must be in kilograms, because the heat of combustion is energy per kilogram. For example, $3.0\,\text{kg}$ of petrol releases $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 3.0\,\text{kg} = 129\,\text{MJ} \approx 130\,\text{MJ}$, because the mass is known to two figures.

The table gives the heats of combustion of common fuels. They are net heats of combustion, meaning that the heat released when water vapour in the flue gas condenses is not counted, and energy statistics use them. The values are rounded: the real value varies from batch to batch of fuel.

### Moisture and fuel quality
Wet wood gives less energy per kilogram than dry wood, because part of the released energy is used to vaporise the water. This is why the firewood in the table is air-dried (moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) and the peat is milled peat with a moisture of $40\text{–}50\,\%$. Completely dry wood would have a heat of combustion of about $19\,\text{MJ/kg}$.

### The energy in food is the same idea
A nutrition label gives energy per hundred grams, for example $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per $100\,\text{g}$. When you convert the energy to megajoules and the mass to kilograms, the label gives the "heat of combustion" of the food. So the number on the package corresponds to the heat of combustion: it tells you the chemical energy of the food per unit of mass. It is the energy your body can use, so it is slightly smaller than the energy released in burning.

## Worked example
In a fireplace, $6.0\,\text{kg}$ of air-dried firewood with a heat of combustion of $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ is burnt. How much energy is released from the wood? Give the answer in megajoules and in kilowatt-hours.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Given and find.** The mass of the firewood is $m = 6.0\,\text{kg}$ and from the table the heat of combustion is $H = 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. We want the energy released, $E$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Principle.** The wood burns, so its chemical energy is transformed into heat. The heat of combustion tells you the energy per kilogram, so the energy released is found by multiplying the heat of combustion by the mass.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Rearranged formula.** The quantity we want, $E$, is already alone on the left side of the equation:
```latex
E = Hm
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Unit conversions.** The mass is already in kilograms, so you can calculate the megajoules directly. For kilowatt-hours we use $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$, from which $1\,\text{MJ} = 1\,\text{kWh} : 3.6$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Substitution.**
```latex
\begin{aligned}
E &= Hm \\
&= 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{kg} \\
&= 90\,\text{MJ}
\end{aligned}
```
In kilowatt-hours $E = 90\,\text{MJ} = 90 \cdot (1\,\text{kWh} : 3.6) = 25\,\text{kWh}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Result.** The wood releases $90\,\text{MJ}$ of energy, which is $25\,\text{kWh}$. The answer is given to two significant figures, because both the mass $6.0\,\text{kg}$ and the heat of combustion $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ are known to two figures.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Reasonableness.** One kilogram of wood gives $15\,\text{MJ}$, which is about $4\,\text{kWh}$, so six kilograms give about six times as much: $6 \cdot 4\,\text{kWh} = 24\,\text{kWh}$, which matches the result. The energy equals that of a one-kilowatt ($1\,\text{kW}$) heater switched on for $25\,\text{h}$. However, you do not get all of it as heat for the room, because some of the heat leaves with the flue gases (chapter *Efficiency*).

## Common misconception
- **Heat of combustion is not temperature.** Many people think the heat of combustion tells you how hot the fuel burns. It is actually energy per unit of mass.
- **Energy is not created in burning.** The chemical energy of the fuel is transformed into heat and light, but energy does not appear out of nothing.
- **Released energy is not useful energy.** In a boiler, a fireplace or an engine, part of the energy is wasted, and the useful share is calculated with efficiency (chapter *Efficiency*).

Burning also produces carbon dioxide, which is covered in the chapter *Energy production, the environment and climate change*.

## Summary
- The chemical energy of a fuel is released as heat and light when it burns. Energy is not created but transformed.
- The heat of combustion $H$ is the energy released per kilogram of fuel, with the unit $\text{MJ/kg}$.
- The energy released is $E = Hm$ when the mass $m$ is in kilograms.
- The heat of combustion of petrol and diesel is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$, of coal $26$, of air-dried wood $15$ and of peat $10\,\text{MJ/kg}$.
- The $\text{kJ}$ per $100\,\text{g}$ on a food package corresponds to the heat of combustion: the amount of chemical energy in the food per unit of mass.
- Released energy is not the same as useful energy: useful energy is found with efficiency.
