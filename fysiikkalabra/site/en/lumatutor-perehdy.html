Get ready: grow into a LUMA tutor

Ten short topics on how to spark interest, ask, explain and guide. Each has an exercise and an example situation.

How the induction works

You can do the induction on your own or in two or three shared meetings led by the teacher. Shared meetings are best for the exercises that need an audience.

The workload is about $3 \cdot 75\,\text{min}$. For each topic, write one insight and the exercise you did in your portfolio.

Suggested shared meetings

  1. Meeting 1: The tutor's role

    Get to know each other and the tutor's task. Practise starting a group and making sure everyone gets involved.

    • What is a LUMA tutor and what do they do?
    • How do you get children and young people interested in science?
    • How do you guide a group?

  2. Meeting 2: Ask, explain, show

    Everyone gives a two-minute science demonstration and gets feedback. Practise turning a ready answer into a question.

    • How do you ask good questions?
    • How do you explain difficult things clearly?
    • How do you get participants to explore and try things themselves?
    • What makes a good science demonstration?

  3. Meeting 3: For everyone, and safely

    Go through safety and who is responsible for what. Try one workshop from the workshop bank within the group.

    • How do you take different learners and participants into account?
    • How do you act safely and responsibly?
    • What is good science communication?

1

What is a LUMA tutor and what do they do?

A LUMA tutor is an upper secondary student who gets others interested in science, mathematics and technology. They run workshops, help at events and show by their own example that science is interesting and belongs to everyone.

A tutor is not a teacher or a science show performer. Their most important task is to get the participants doing, asking and discovering things themselves. Younger children often find an upper secondary student easier to approach than an adult, and that is the tutor's strength.

  • A tutor guides, not lectures.
  • A tutor does not need to know everything: ”I don't know, let's find out together” is a good answer.
  • A tutor always works together with the teacher and the other tutors.

Exercise: Me as a tutor

Write three sentences in your portfolio: why you want to be a LUMA tutor, what your strength is and what you want to learn. This is the first part of the portfolio.

Example situation

A third-grader asks: ”Are you a teacher?” What do you answer, and how can your answer make the situation more relaxed?

2

How do you get children and young people interested in science?

Interest starts when something surprises and the participant wants to know why. A good start is a phenomenon, not a definition: a bulb that lights with one wire but not another, or a sheet of paper that holds coins once you fold it.

Success keeps interest going. Plan the workshop so that the first success comes within a few minutes and a harder challenge only after that. Your own enthusiasm spreads: if you genuinely wonder, the participants wonder too.

  • Start with a phenomenon or a question, not an explanation.
  • First success quickly, the challenge after it.
  • Praise trying and a good question, not only the right answer.

Exercise: The hook

Invent a start for some phenomenon that lasts at most 30 seconds and raises a question. Try it on a friend: did they ask something?

Example situation

A lower secondary student says right at the start: ”Physics is boring.” How do you go on so that they get a chance to be surprised, without arguing with them?

3

How do you guide a group?

Guiding a group starts before anyone touches the equipment. Introduce yourself, say briefly what you will do and agree on one clear signal for everyone to stop and listen, for example a raised hand.

Give instructions one step at a time and show them. Hand out the equipment only after the instructions, or no one will listen. Go round the groups, look at everyone and give each person a task: builder, measurer, recorder, presenter. The roles rotate.

  • One instruction at a time, show it and say it.
  • Equipment only after the instructions.
  • A role for everyone, and the roles rotate.
  • Agree on a stop signal right at the start.

Exercise: Everyone in

Guide a group of three to build a tower from ten sheets of paper. Rules: everyone touches the structure in turn, and you do not touch it at all. What did you notice?

Example situation

One participant does everything, and the other two watch. What do you say or do to change this without offending anyone?

4

How do you ask good questions?

A question with one right word as its answer stops thinking. An open question starts it: ”What do you think will happen?”, ”What do you notice?”, ”How could you test it?”

When a participant asks you something, do not answer straight away. Turn the question back or suggest trying: ”Good question. What if we try?” Wait calmly for an answer. Silence often means someone is thinking.

  • An open question before the explanation.
  • Wait at least five seconds for an answer.
  • Turn the question into an experiment.
  • All the question cards are in the toolkit.

Exercise: From answer to question

Turn these ready answers into questions that make the participant think for themselves: ”The bulb does not light because the circuit is not closed.” ”Folded paper holds more because it is stiffer.” ”The red film lets only red light through.”

Example situation

A participant asks: ”Why doesn't this work?” Write three questions that help them find the fault themselves.

5

How do you explain difficult things clearly?

A good explanation starts from what the listener already knows. Use everyday words, comparisons and pictures. Say one thing at a time and check that the listener is still with you.

A comparison helps, but every comparison breaks down somewhere. Say so out loud: ”A circuit is a bit like water pipes, but electricity does not pour out of a wire if the wire breaks.” Avoid terms you cannot explain yourself without other terms.

  • Start from what the listener already knows.
  • One thing at a time, then a check.
  • A comparison and a picture, and say where the comparison breaks down.
  • You may use the proper terms once you have explained them.

Exercise: For a primary pupil

Explain a scientific phenomenon to a primary school pupil without difficult concepts, for example why the sky is blue or why ice floats. Write the explanation in at most five sentences and ask a friend to find a word in it that a 9-year-old would not understand.

Example situation

You are explaining a circuit, and a participant says: ”I don't get it.” What do you do next if the same explanation again does not help?

6

How do you get participants to explore and try things themselves?

Exploring often goes like this: predict, try, observe, explain. When a participant first guesses what will happen, they watch the experiment more closely and remember the result better.

Give the participants a task with many solutions and keep your own hands in your pockets. A mistake is part of research: ”What did we learn from this?” is better than ”Wrong.”

  • Predict before trying.
  • Tasks that allow many solutions.
  • The tutor does not do it for the participant.
  • Ask what a mistake taught.

Exercise: Predict first

Choose a simple experiment, for example whether an orange floats with and without its peel. Write a prediction question for it, a one-sentence instruction for trying it, and a question with which the participant explains the result.

Example situation

A group wants you to show them the right solution straight away. How do you keep the exploring with them and still help them forward?

7

What makes a good science demonstration?

A demonstration is a short show in which the audience sees a phenomenon. A good demonstration can be seen from the back row, lasts a few minutes, works reliably and leaves a question the audience wants answered.

Build the demonstration in three parts: a question or prediction, the show and a discussion. Rehearse it many times in advance and think about what you will do if it fails. A failure can also teach something when you say out loud what you think happened.

  • Visible to everyone, lasts a few minutes.
  • A question before the show, a discussion after it.
  • Rehearsed, with a backup plan ready.
  • The planning template is in the toolkit.

Exercise: A two-minute demonstration

Prepare a two-minute science demonstration with everyday equipment and give it to a group or film it for yourself. Time it. Ask for feedback on two things: could the phenomenon be seen, and did a question stay in mind?

Example situation

The demonstration fails, and the audience starts to giggle. What do you say to make the situation part of science?

8

How do you take different learners and participants into account?

A group always has people of different ages, who learn in different ways and speak different languages. Someone is quick, someone needs more time, someone does not see or hear well, and someone is nervous. A good workshop works in many ways: by showing, saying, trying and with a picture.

Plan an easier and a harder version of every workshop. Speak clearly in short sentences, show as you speak, and check that the equipment can also be reached from a wheelchair. Do not assume anything about a participant from their appearance or their name.

  • Show and say, do not only say.
  • An easier and a harder version ready.
  • Clear language: short sentences, one thing at a time.
  • Space and equipment within everyone's reach.

Exercise: Without words

Guide a friend through a simple task, for example folding a paper plane, without speaking at all. What does this tell you about instructions for a participant whose language is different from yours?

Example situation

One participant is done in half the time and starts to disturb the others. What could you give them to do?

9

How do you act safely and responsibly?

Safety is planned in advance. Every workshop card has safety instructions: read them and go through them with the teacher. The teacher is responsible for supervision, but you are responsible for following the instructions and for stopping the activity if something looks dangerous.

Responsibility also means respecting the participants. Do not collect names or other personal data from the participants, do not photograph anyone without permission, and tell the teacher at once if someone gets hurt or a situation worries you.

  • Read the workshop's safety instructions and go through them with the teacher.
  • You may, and must, stop the activity if something is dangerous.
  • No personal data, no photos without permission.
  • Tell the teacher at once about anything that worries you.
  • Use the safety checklist before every workshop.

Exercise: Spot the risks

Choose one workshop from the workshop bank and find three things in it that could go wrong. For each, write how to prevent it and what you will do if it happens anyway.

Example situation

In the middle of a workshop, a participant puts a button cell in their mouth. What do you do at once, and whom do you tell?

10

What is good science communication?

Science communication means telling people who are not experts in a field about research-based knowledge. Good science communication is true, understandable and interesting, and it also tells how the knowledge was gained and what is not yet known.

As a tutor you are a science communicator. Say where the knowledge comes from, separate an observation from an interpretation, do not exaggerate, and say plainly if you do not know. A reliable source is, for example, a research institute, a university or a textbook, not just a social media post.

  • True, understandable and interesting, in that order.
  • Say where the knowledge comes from.
  • Separate observation (what we saw) and interpretation (what it means).
  • ”I don't know” is an honest answer.

Exercise: One picture, one message

Choose one graph or picture from a reliable source and write a title for it and two sentences that tell a lower secondary student what can be seen in it. Give the source.

Example situation

A participant says they saw a video claiming that climate change is a hoax. How do you talk with them respectfully and based on knowledge?

The induction is done when

  • you have gone through all ten topics
  • you have done the exercise for each topic or done it in a shared meeting
  • you have given a two-minute science demonstration
  • your portfolio has your starting point and your main insights from the induction
