Thermal expansion

Why is there a comb-shaped steel joint at the end of a long bridge, which makes the car tyres thump as they cross it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain with the particle model why a solid expands when it is heated.
  • investigate by experiment what the change in the length of a rod depends on.
  • calculate the change in the length and volume of an object when its temperature changes.

Prior knowledge

  • Temperature describes how strongly the particles move in random thermal motion; in a gas it tells you the average kinetic energy of the particleschapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature
  • A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius, $T = \theta + 273.15$chapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature

Prediction 3: why does steel expand?

Four students explain what happens inside steel when it warms up and gets longer. Whose explanation fits the particle model?

  • The micro model in the simulation assumes that the particles are the same size at all temperatures, and yet the lattice expands in the same proportion as the rod: only the average distance between the particles grows, not their size. Temperature describes the motion of the particles, not their size.

  • Heat is not a substance but energy that is transferred: a rod that has warmed up is no heavier, and nothing new enters between the particles. The transferred energy makes the particles vibrate more strongly, and the asymmetry of the vibration increases their average distance.

  • This fits the model. In the micro model the vibration gets stronger as the temperature rises, and the gaps between the particles grow in the same proportion as the whole rod, $\Delta L/L_0 = \alpha \Delta T$. The vibration increases the average distance only because a swing away is longer than a swing closer. The simulation does not show this asymmetry, because it draws the vibration as symmetric; the Theory summary explains it.

  • Stronger vibration is the right start, but if a particle swung as far towards its neighbour as away from it, its average position would not change and the object would not get longer. Expansion requires that the swing away is longer: when particles are close together they repel each other strongly, but when they move apart the attraction weakens gradually.

    Which feature of the micro model best explains why a gas expands the most and a solid the least?

    • Exactly. The particles of a solid vibrate around their equilibrium positions, held in place by the attractive forces of their neighbours, the particles of a liquid stay together but move past each other, and the particles of a gas move almost freely.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Almost all substances expand when they are heated and contract when they are cooled. This phenomenon is called thermal expansion.

    Why does a substance expand?

    The micro model explains thermal expansion. In a solid the particles vibrate around their equilibrium positions. When the temperature rises, the vibration gets stronger.

    It is easier for the particles to move apart than to be squeezed closer: particles pressed close together repel each other strongly, but the attraction between particles moving apart weakens gradually. So a particle swings farther from its neighbour than closer to it, and for more strongly vibrating particles the average distance grows. When all the gaps between the particles grow, the whole object expands.

    Thermal expansion in everyday life

    The change is small but powerful. A steel rod gets longer by only about one hundred-thousandth of its length for each kelvin, but if the ends of the rod are fixed, the expanding steel pushes on them with an enormous force. That is why long structures have expansion joints. A steel bridge deck is shorter in a winter frost than in a summer heatwave, and the comb-shaped joint at the end of the bridge lets the deck move freely. For the same reason electric power lines hang lower in a heatwave than in a frost.

    An object expands in all directions in the same proportion, as when a photograph is enlarged. A hole in a plate also gets bigger when the plate warms up. This is used in a shrink fit: a heated steel ring is slipped onto an axle, and as it cools it is pressed tightly in place.

    Thermal expansion of different substances

    Different substances expand differently. A bimetallic strip, in which two different metals are joined together, bends when it warms up, because one metal lengthens more. Such a strip opens and closes an electric circuit in old-style thermostats.

    Liquids usually expand clearly more than solids, and gases much more still. The particles of a liquid do not stay in place, and the interactions between them are weaker than in a solid, so a rise in temperature moves them farther apart. The particles of a gas are far apart and almost free, so at constant pressure the volume of a gas grows much more. The volume of a gas also depends on pressure, and we return to this in the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law. A liquid thermometer is based on the expansion of a liquid: when the temperature rises, the liquid column rises in a thin tube.

    Water is an exception: between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ it contracts when it warms up, so its density is greatest at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The densest, 4-degree water sinks to the bottom, and colder water stays at the surface and freezes. That is why a lake freezes from the surface, and even in winter there is water at about 4 °C at the bottom, where fish survive.

    Thermal expansion of length and volume

    When the temperature of an object changes by $\Delta T$, its length changes by

    $$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$$

    where $\Delta L$ is the change in length (m), $\alpha$ is the coefficient of linear expansion (1/K), $L_0$ is the original length (m) and $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature (K). The coefficient of linear expansion $\alpha$ tells you what fraction of its length an object lengthens when its temperature rises by $1\,\text{K}$. So the change is proportional to the original length: a rod twice as long has twice as many gaps between particles, so it lengthens twice as much. The number 12 in the table means that $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$. The new length is $L = L_0 + \Delta L$. When the object cools, $\Delta T$ is negative, so $\Delta L$ is also negative, that is, the object gets shorter.

    The change in the volume of liquids (and of gases at constant pressure) is calculated in the same way:

    $$\Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$$

    where $\Delta V$ is the change in volume (m³), $\gamma$ is the coefficient of volume expansion (1/K) and $V_0$ is the original volume (m³). For a solid $\gamma \approx 3\alpha$, because an object expands in all three directions: length, width and height each grow in proportion $\alpha \Delta T$, and when the changes are small, the relative growth in volume is almost the sum of these, $3\alpha \Delta T$. The formulas are good models when the change in temperature is moderate. For solids that means, for example, at most about a hundred kelvins. For liquids, especially water, the coefficient $\gamma$ changes clearly with temperature, so the values in the table at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are used only close to $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that when a substance warms up, its particles themselves grow. In the micro model the particles stay the same size, and only their average distances grow. Another mistake is to think that a hole in a plate gets smaller because the plate supposedly expands from its edges towards the hole. The hole gets bigger in the same proportion as the plate does everywhere else. A third mistake is to assume that all substances always expand when they warm up: water contracts between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and when it freezes it expands. That is why ice floats and lakes freeze from the surface.

    Summary

    • When a substance warms up, the vibration of its particles gets stronger and their average distance grows, so the substance expands.
    • The change in length is $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$ and the change in volume is $\Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$. For a solid $\gamma \approx 3\alpha$.
    • Liquids expand more than solids, and gases much more than liquids.
    • An object expands in all directions in the same proportion, so holes also get bigger.
    • Water is an exception: its density is greatest at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      Problem

      The deck of a steel bridge is $120\,\text{m}$ long. At the location the temperature varies during the year from a frost of $-30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to a heatwave of +$30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the coefficient of linear expansion of steel is 12 · 10⁻⁶ 1/K. By how much does the length of the deck change, that is, how much must the expansion joint give at least?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The length of the deck is $L_0 = 120\,\text{m}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ for steel (given in the problem), the lowest temperature is $-30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the highest $+30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The question is the change $\Delta L$ in the length of the deck over the whole year.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The vibration of the particles of a solid gets stronger when it warms up and their average distance grows, so the deck gets longer. Steel stays solid all year and the deck is free to expand, so the change in length is proportional to the initial length and the change in temperature: $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The quantity asked for is already alone on the left side of the formula, so the formula does not need rearranging:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta L &= \alpha L_0 \Delta T \\ \Delta T &= T_\text{max} - T_\text{min} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, so

      $\Delta T$$= 30\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-30\,{}^\circ\text{C})$
      $= 60\,\text{K}$

      The lengths are in metres, so $\Delta L$ comes out in metres; finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta L &= \alpha L_0 \Delta T \\ &= 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 120\,\text{m} \cdot 60\,\text{K} \\ &= 0.0864\,\text{m} \\ &= 86.4\,\text{mm} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The length of the deck changes by about $86\,\text{mm}$ during the year. The result is given to two significant figures. The limiting data are the coefficient $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = 60\,\text{K}$ (both have two figures); the length $120\,\text{m}$ has at least two figures and does not limit the result.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. A rough estimate:

      $10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 100\,\text{m} \cdot 60\,\text{K}$
      $= 60\,\text{mm}$

      so the result is of the right order of magnitude. The deck gets almost nine centimetres longer by summer, because the temperature rises. That is only $\alpha \Delta T = 0.072\,\%$ of the length of the deck, but without an expansion joint the expanding steel would push on the ends of the bridge with such a large force that the structures would be damaged. That is why there is a comb-shaped joint at the end of the bridge, with teeth that slide between each other.

      Given

      metal rod, $L_0 = 2.00\,\text{m}$ at $T_0 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $\Delta L = 1.84\,\text{mm}$ at $60\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $\Delta L = 3.68\,\text{mm}$ at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

      the coefficient of linear expansion $\alpha$ in the unit $10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$

      Principle

      The change in the length of a solid is proportional to the initial length and the change in temperature, and the constant of proportionality $\alpha$ is a constant characteristic of the material. In the table $\Delta L$ doubles when the change in temperature $\Delta T$ ($40\,\text{K} \to 80\,\text{K}$) doubles, so the proportionality holds and the rod does not change its state of matter.

      Formula

      $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, from which

      $$\alpha = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0 \Delta T}$$

      Conversions

      $\Delta L$$= 1.84\,\text{mm}$
      $= 1.84 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$

      so that the length units cancel.

      $\Delta T$$= 60\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
      $= 40\,\text{K}$

      $$\begin{aligned} \alpha &= \frac{1.84 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}}{2.00\,\text{m} \cdot 40\,\text{K}} \\ &= 2.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \\ &= 23 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \end{aligned}$$

      $\alpha = 23 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$

      Two significant figures.       The other column gives the same:

      Optional 4. Explain and transfer

      Use the micro model to explain why a steel rail gets longer when it warms up, and why the same rise in temperature increases the volume of air at constant pressure proportionally much more than the volume of the rail.

      Model answer

      The particles of the rail vibrate around their equilibrium positions. When the temperature rises, the vibration gets stronger. It is easier for the particles to move apart than to be squeezed closer, so a particle swings farther from its neighbour than closer to it, and the average distance between the particles grows. The particles themselves do not grow. When all the gaps between the particles grow in the same proportion, the whole rail gets longer. In a solid, strong bonds hold the particles tightly in place, so the relative change is small. The particles of a gas are far apart and almost free, so at constant pressure a rise in temperature increases their average distance much more: the coefficient of volume expansion of air is about a hundred times the $\gamma \approx 3\alpha$ of steel.

The points of the graph give the change in temperature and the change in length, and from them $\alpha$, which can be compared with the table.

          Reading the graph

          The line starts at the grid crossing $(20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $0\,\text{mm})$ and ends at the crossing $(70\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $3.0\,\text{mm})$. So the change in temperature is

          $\Delta T$$= 70\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 50\,\text{K}$

          and the change in length is $\Delta L = 3.0\,\text{mm}$.

          Formula

          $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, from which

          $\alpha$$= \dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0 \Delta T}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta L$$= 3.0\,\text{mm}$
          $= 0.0030\,\text{m}$

          so that the units cancel. The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins. The result is given in the unit $10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, so the factor $10^{-6}$ is left in the unit.

          Substitution

          $\alpha$$= \dfrac{0.0030\,\text{m}}{5.00\,\text{m} \cdot 50\,\text{K}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.0030}{250}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$
          $= 0.000012\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$
          $= 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$

          Answer

          $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$

          So the beam is steel. Two significant figures, because the change in length $3.0\,\text{mm}$ was read with two figures. The values are read exactly from the grid, so $11.6\ldots12.4$ is accepted.

          Reasonableness

          Units:

          $\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}$$= \tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$

          Comparison with the table: an aluminium beam would lengthen

          $23 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 5.00\,\text{m} \cdot 50\,\text{K}$
          $= 5.75\,\text{mm}$

          and a copper beam $4.25\,\text{mm}$, so only steel gives the $3.0\,\text{mm}$ that was read. A fraction of a millimetre per metre of length is typical of metals.

        1. In a farm machine workshop a steel gear is fitted on the axle of a tractor with a shrink fit. The diameter of the central hole of the gear is 75.000 mm at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the diameter of the axle is 75.090 mm. For steel $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$.

          To what temperature must the gear be heated at least so that it fits on the axle? Also justify why the hole gets bigger and not smaller when the gear warms up, and why the gear is held tightly in place after it has cooled.

          °C
          Answer

          $T = 120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          steel gear, diameter of the hole $d_0 = 75.000\,\text{mm}$ at the temperature $T_0 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, diameter of the axle $d = 75.090\,\text{mm}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)

          Find

          the lowest temperature $T$ at which the hole is at least as big as the axle

          Principle

          An object expands in all directions in the same proportion, as when a photograph is enlarged, and so does a hole. The diameter of the hole is a dimension of the object itself, so its change follows the same formula $\Delta d = \alpha d_0 \Delta T$ as a change in length. So the hole does not get smaller even though the material around it expands. When it cools, the hole tries to return to the size $75.000\,\text{mm}$, but the axle is in the way, and the gear is pressed tightly in place.

          Formula

          $\Delta d = \alpha d_0 \Delta T$, from which

          $\Delta T$$= \dfrac{\Delta d}{\alpha d_0}$

          and $T = T_0 + \Delta T$. Here $\Delta d = d - d_0$.

          Conversions

          All the dimensions are in millimetres, so $\Delta d$ and $d_0$ cancel in the same unit and no conversion is needed. $\Delta T$ comes out in kelvins and is the same as in degrees Celsius.

          Substitution

          $\Delta d$$= 75.090\,\text{mm} - 75.000\,\text{mm}$
          $= 0.090\,\text{mm}$

          and

          $\Delta T$$= \dfrac{0.090\,\text{mm}}{12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 75.000\,\text{mm}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.090}{9.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\text{K}$
          $= 100\,\text{K}$

          So

          $T$$= 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 100\,\text{K}$
          $= 120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $T = 120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Two significant figures. The difference of the diameters $\Delta d = 0.090\,\text{mm}$ has two figures, because the dimensions are given to the nearest thousandth of a millimetre ($75.000$ and $75.090$), and the coefficient $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ also has two figures; they limit the result $\Delta T = 100\,\text{K}$ and thus the temperature $120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          Reasonableness

          Check:

          $75.000\,\text{mm} \cdot (1 + 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 100\,\text{K})$
          $= 75.090\,\text{mm}$

          The temperature is clearly higher than room temperature but below the melting of steel. In practice the gear is heated a little above $120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ so that a clearance is left between the hole and the axle.

        2. The thermostat of an old-style electric sauna heater has a bimetallic strip in which copper and steel strips, each 50.0 mm long, are joined together (copper $\alpha = 17.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, steel $\alpha = 12.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$). As the sauna warms up, the temperature of the strip rises from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          How much more would the copper strip lengthen than the steel strip if they were separate? Give the answer in micrometres, that is, thousandths of a millimetre. Justify which way the joined strip bends.

          µm
          Answer

          $20\,\mu\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          copper and steel strips, $L_0 = 50.0\,\text{mm}$, $T_0 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha_\text{Cu} = 17.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, $\alpha_\text{Fe} = 12.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)

          Find

          the difference of the lengthenings $\Delta L_\text{Cu} - \Delta L_\text{Fe}$ in micrometres and the direction in which the strip bends

          Principle

          Both strips are solids and lengthen according to the formula $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$. The strips have the same initial length and the same change in temperature, so the difference in length comes only from the difference of the coefficients. Copper lengthens more because its $\alpha$ is greater.

          Formula

          $\Delta L_\text{Cu} - \Delta L_\text{Fe}$$= (\alpha_\text{Cu} - \alpha_\text{Fe}) L_0 \Delta T$

          where $\Delta T = T - T_0$.

          Conversions

          $\Delta T$$= 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 80\,\text{K}$

          the same in kelvins. $L_0$ is in millimetres, so the difference comes out in millimetres, and $1\,\text{mm} = 1000\,\mu\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $\Delta L_\text{Cu}$$= 17.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 80\,\text{K}$
          $= 0.068\,\text{mm}$

          and

          $\Delta L_\text{Fe}$$= 12.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 80\,\text{K}$
          $= 0.048\,\text{mm}$

          The difference is

          $0.068\,\text{mm} - 0.048\,\text{mm}$
          $= 0.020\,\text{mm}$
          $= 20\,\mu\text{m}$

          Answer

          $20\,\mu\text{m}$

          Two significant figures. The limiting datum is the difference of the coefficients $17.0 - 12.0 = 5.0$ (in the unit $10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$), which has two figures because the coefficients are given to the nearest tenth; $\Delta T = 80\,\text{K}$ also has two figures. If the coefficients were given only as whole numbers, the difference $17 - 12 = 5$ would have one figure.

          Reasonableness

          The same result with a single multiplication:

          $(17.0 - 12.0) \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 80\,\text{K}$
          $= 0.020\,\text{mm}$

          The difference is positive because copper lengthens more. The strips are fastened to each other, so the longer copper stays on the outer edge of the curve and the strip bends towards the steel side. The end of the strip moves much more than $20\,\mu\text{m}$ and opens the electric circuit.

        3. A 20.0-litre steel can for a motorboat is filled to the brim at a filling station with petrol at $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and closed. On the jetty the sun heats the can and the petrol to $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. For petrol $\gamma = 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ and for steel $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$. The inner volume of the can grows in the same way as the volume of a steel object of the same size.

          How many cubic centimetres of petrol would try to overflow if the cap is opened? Justify why a can is not filled to the brim even though the can also expands.

          cm³
          Answer

          $\Delta V_\text{over} \approx 460\,\text{cm}^3$

          Show the solution

          Given

          petrol $V_0 = 20.0\,\textsf{l}$, $T_0 = 10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\gamma_\text{petrol} = 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$; steel can $\alpha_\text{steel} = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)

          Find

          the volume of petrol that overflows $\Delta V_\text{over}$ in cubic centimetres and a justification for why the can is not filled to the brim

          Principle

          Both the petrol and the inner space of the can expand, and the inner volume of the can grows like that of a steel object of the same size ($\gamma \approx 3\alpha$). The can was full, so what overflows is the difference between the growth of the petrol and the growth of the inner volume. Both volumes are calculated with the same formula.

          Formula

          $\Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$ for both, $\gamma_\text{steel} \approx 3\alpha_\text{steel}$ and $\Delta V_\text{over} = \Delta V_\text{petrol} - \Delta V_\text{can}$.

          Conversions

          $V_0$$= 20.0\,\textsf{l}$
          $= 20\,000\,\text{cm}^3$

          because $1\,\textsf{l} = 1000\,\text{cm}^3$.

          $\Delta T$$= 35\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 25\,\text{K}$

          the same in kelvins.

          Substitution

          $\Delta V_\text{petrol}$$= 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 20\,000\,\text{cm}^3 \cdot 25\,\text{K}$
          $= 475\,\text{cm}^3$

          and

          $\Delta V_\text{can}$$= 3 \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 20\,000\,\text{cm}^3 \cdot 25\,\text{K}$
          $= 18\,\text{cm}^3$

          So

          $\Delta V_\text{over}$$= 475\,\text{cm}^3 - 18\,\text{cm}^3$
          $= 457\,\text{cm}^3$

          Answer

          $\Delta V_\text{over} \approx 460\,\text{cm}^3$

          Two significant figures. The growth of the petrol $475\,\text{cm}^3$ is limited by the coefficient $\gamma = 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (two figures), so it is known only to the nearest tens of cubic centimetres. In the difference $475 - 18$ the less precise number determines the decimals, so the result is rounded to tens: $460\,\text{cm}^3$. The $\alpha$ of steel and the volume $20.0\,\textsf{l}$ affect only the small part $18\,\text{cm}^3$.

          Reasonableness

          The growth of the petrol is $0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{K} = 2.4\,\%$ and that of the can only $0.09\,\%$, so the difference is almost the whole growth of the petrol, about $0.46\,\textsf{l}$. The expansion of the can is not enough to compensate, because the $\gamma$ of petrol is more than 25 times the $\gamma$ of steel. That is why an air space is left in the can.
