Pressure and hydrostatic pressure

Why is the wall of a dam much thicker at the bottom than at the top, even though the water pressing on it is the same water all the way down?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate pressure from force and area and explain how a force is spread over a surface.
  • calculate the pressure of a liquid at different depths.
  • interpret the constant term and the slope of a graph of pressure against depth.

Prior knowledge

  • Weight is a force, $G = mg$, and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$chapter Force as a measure of interaction and weight $G = mg$
  • Pressure is a state variable that arises in the micro model from particles colliding with the wallchapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature

1Think about it

You dive towards the bottom of a swimming-pool diving pool. Already at a depth of a couple of metres you feel pressure in your ears, and the lower you go, the more clearly you feel it. At the surface your ears feel nothing, even though the atmosphere presses on them too. The same thing loads the wall of a dam and your eardrum: the water above.

Optional Review: the weight of water
A cubic metre of water (density $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$) has a mass of $1000\,\text{kg}$. What is the weight $G = mg$ of this amount of water?

  • The kilogram is the unit of mass. Weight is a force, and the unit of force is the newton: $G = mg = 9810\,\text{N}$.

  • You mixed up mass and weight. Weight is a force, and you get it by multiplying the mass by the acceleration of free fall: $G = mg = 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 9810\,\text{N}$.

  • You divided the mass by the acceleration of free fall. Weight is mass times $g$: every kilogram weighs $9.81\,\text{N}$ at the Earth's surface, so $1000\,\text{kg}$ weighs $9810\,\text{N}$.

  • Correct: $G = mg = 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 9810\,\text{N} \approx 9.8\,\text{kN}$. You need this weight of a column of water in this lesson.

Prediction 3: a narrow tube and a wide pool

A narrow vertical tube and a wide pool are filled with water to the same height, $2.0\,\text{m}$. The pool holds a thousand times as much water as the tube. Three students explain where the pressure at the bottom is greater.Who is right?

  • The water in the tube weighs less, and it is spread over a smaller area. When the water is at the same height, the smaller weight and the smaller area cancel each other exactly. In the theory you can see why the area cancels.

  • The water in the pool does weigh more in total, but the weight is also spread over a bottom that is a thousand times larger. Pressure is force divided by area, $p = F/A$. In the theory the weight of the column of water is divided by the area of its base; see what happens to the area.

Simulation

How to use

Adjust the depth and the density and follow the reading of the gauge and the $(h, p)$ graph. The box Show gauge pressure only removes atmospheric pressure from the graph.

Assumptions of the model

Atmospheric pressure at the surface is a fixed $101\,\text{kPa}$, and the liquid is at rest and equally dense everywhere (its compression is ignored). The readings are values from a model, not measurements from a real lake.

How the calculation works

Pressure grows steadily with depth: $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, so every metre adds $\rho g \cdot 1\,\text{m}$ of pressure. The slope of the line, $\rho g$, depends on the density, but at the surface ($h = 0$) the pressure is always the atmospheric pressure $p_0$.

By how many kilopascals does the pressure in water grow for every metre of depth? Read it from your measurements.

Show the solution

Given

water, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$ constant; measurements at two depths with $\Delta h = 10.0\,\text{m} - 5.0\,\text{m} = 5.0\,\text{m}$ between them: at the depth $5.0\,\text{m}$ the total pressure is $p_1 = 150.1\,\text{kPa}$ and at the depth $10.0\,\text{m}$ the pressure is $p_2 = 199.1\,\text{kPa}$

Find

the increase in pressure per metre of depth, $\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$, in $\text{kPa/m}$

Principle

In a liquid $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, and because only the depth changes ($\rho$ and $p_0$ stay the same), the pressure grows steadily: the increase per metre is always the same number $\rho g$. Atmospheric pressure $p_0$ does not change with depth, so it cancels when we take the difference between the pressures at two depths.

Formula

$p_2 - p_1 = \rho g (h_2 - h_1)$, so

$$\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta h} = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{h_2 - h_1} \;(= \rho g)$$

Conversions

The pressures are already in kilopascals and the depths in metres, so the result comes directly in $\text{kPa/m}$ and no conversions are needed. For comparison,

$\rho g$$= 1000 \cdot 9.81\,\text{Pa/m}$
$= 9.81\,\text{kPa/m}$

Substitution

$\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$$= \dfrac{199.1\,\text{kPa} - 150.1\,\text{kPa}}{10.0\,\text{m} - 5.0\,\text{m}}$
$= \dfrac{49.0\,\text{kPa}}{5.0\,\text{m}}$
$= 9.80\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}}$

Answer

$9.8\,\text{kPa/m}$

(from your readings), two significant figures, because the depth difference $5.0\,\text{m}$ is known to two figures, although the pressure difference $49.0\,\text{kPa}$ has three. The value of the model is $\rho g = 9.81\,\text{kPa/m}$.

Reasonableness

The increase is about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, which fits the fact that $10\,\text{m}$ of water adds about one atmosphere ($101\,\text{kPa}$). Pressure grows in the same proportion as depth only for the gauge pressure: $5\,\text{m}$ gives $49\,\text{kPa}$ and $10\,\text{m}$ gives $98\,\text{kPa}$, which is double. The total pressure does not double ($150 \to 199\,\text{kPa}$), because $101\,\text{kPa}$ is included in both.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    When a force is spread over a surface, its effect depends on how large an area it is spread over. The sharp tip of a drawing pin sinks into a wall with the same force that leaves no mark when applied to the blunt head, and skis carry you on deep snow although boots sink in. The quantity that describes this is pressure: force divided by the area over which the force is spread. The unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), and $1\,\text{Pa} = 1\,\text{N/m}^2$. The pascal is a small unit, so in everyday life we use the kilopascal: $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.

    Where does pressure come from?

    According to the micro model, the pressure of a gas comes from particles colliding with the walls of the container. The pressure of a liquid comes from the weight of the liquid itself: above every layer of water there is more water pressing down on it. The deeper you go, the more water there is above you and the greater the pressure. In a liquid at rest, a horizontal surface of area $A$ below the surface carries the weight of the column of water above it. The height of the column is the depth $h$ and its volume is $V = Ah$. Density $\rho$ is mass per volume ($m = \rho V$), so the mass of the column is $\rho A h$ and its weight is $G = mg = \rho A h g$. Pressure is weight divided by area, so the area cancels and what is left is $\rho g h$. The pressure produced by the weight of a liquid is called hydrostatic pressure. It does not depend on the shape of the container or on the total amount of water, only on the depth and the density of the liquid.

    Pressure is not a force but the ratio of force to area, and in a liquid at rest the pressure acts equally in every direction. If the pressure sideways were smaller than downwards, the water would flow sideways and would not stay at rest. If you make holes at several heights in the side of a bottle, the water squirts out hardest from the lowest hole, because the pressure grows with depth. The force caused by pressure is always perpendicular to the surface: on the vertical wall of a dam the force points horizontally, and on a diver's body it acts from all sides. That is why here we can treat the force as just a magnitude; the direction comes from the surface.

    Atmospheric pressure and gauge pressure

    Atmospheric pressure, the pressure of the atmosphere, already acts on the surface of the water. At sea level it is about $101\,\text{kPa}$. It is the weight of the column of air per unit area. Atmospheric pressure also acts on the surface of the water and is transmitted through the water to depth. Under water the total pressure is therefore the sum of atmospheric pressure and hydrostatic pressure. At a depth of ten metres the hydrostatic pressure is already about the same as one atmosphere.

    This answers the opening question. Atmospheric pressure acts on both sides of the dam wall, so the wall is loaded only by the gauge pressure of the water, that is, the part of the pressure that is greater than atmospheric pressure. The gauge pressure is zero at the top and grows steadily with depth: at a depth of 30 metres it is about $290\,\text{kPa}$, so every square metre of the wall feels a force of about $290\,\text{kN}$. The lower part of the wall must therefore withstand a much greater force than the upper part, and the wall is built thick at the bottom.

    Pressure and hydrostatic pressure

    Pressure is the force perpendicular to a surface divided by the area:

    $$p = \frac{F}{A}$$

    where $p$ is the pressure (Pa), $F$ the force perpendicular to the surface (N) and $A$ the area (m²). One pascal is a force of one newton on an area of one square metre: $1\,\text{Pa} = 1\,\text{N/m}^2$.

    In a liquid at depth $h$ the pressure is

    $$p = p_0 + \rho g h$$

    where $p_0$ is the pressure at the surface of the liquid, usually atmospheric pressure (Pa), $\rho$ the density of the liquid (kg/m³), $g$ = $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $h$ the depth below the surface (m). The term $\rho g h$ is the hydrostatic pressure, that is, the gauge pressure $\Delta p = p - p_0$, which tells how much greater the pressure is than the pressure at the surface. The graph $(h, p)$ is a straight line whose slope is $\rho g$ and whose constant term is $p_0$, as in the linear model $y = kx + b$ of FY1. For water $\rho$ = $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, so the pressure grows by $9.81\,\text{kPa}$ for every metre.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the pressure at the bottom depends on how much water the container holds. In a narrow tube and a wide pool, where the water reaches the same height, the pressure at the bottom is the same, even though the pool holds a thousand times as much water. Pressure depends only on the depth, the density and the pressure at the surface. Another mistake is to confuse pressure and force: the sharp tip of a drawing pin does not produce a greater force than the head, but spreads the same force over a smaller area, so the pressure is greater. A third mistake is to forget atmospheric pressure: $\rho g h$ alone gives only the gauge pressure, and the total pressure under water is always greater than that.

    Summary

    • Pressure is force per unit area: $p = F/A$. The unit is the pascal, $1\,\text{Pa} = 1\,\text{N/m}^2$, and $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.
    • In a liquid the pressure grows with depth: $p = p_0 + \rho g h$. The term $\rho g h$ is the hydrostatic pressure, and it depends only on the depth and the density, not on the shape of the container.
    • The force caused by pressure is perpendicular to the surface and acts in every direction in a liquid.
    • Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about $101\,\text{kPa}$. In water the pressure grows by about one atmosphere for every ten metres.

      Example 1: Pressure on a diver

      A diver is in a lake at a depth of $12.0\,\text{m}$, next to the window of an underwater observation room. Atmospheric pressure at the surface is $101\,\text{kPa}$ and the density of water is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$. What is the pressure around the diver, and how large is the force on a window of area $150\,\text{cm}^2$ when the room contains air at normal pressure?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Depth $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, atmospheric pressure $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, the density of water from a table or from the problem $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and window area $A = 150\,\text{cm}^2$. We want the total pressure $p$ around the diver and the force $F$ on the window.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The water is at rest and its density is constant, so the pressure grows steadily with depth: the total pressure is the atmospheric pressure at the surface plus the hydrostatic pressure of the water $\rho g h$. Only the gauge pressure $\rho g h$ acts on the window, because the atmospheric pressure in the room pushes the window from inside just as much as the atmospheric pressure at the surface pushes from outside. The force is pressure times area, because pressure is force per unit area.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. Both unknowns are already alone on the left-hand side:

      $$\begin{aligned} p &= p_0 + \rho g h \\ F &= \rho g h \cdot A \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Atmospheric pressure in pascals is $101\,\text{kPa} = 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$. The area of the window in square metres is

      $150\,\text{cm}^2$$= 150 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$
      $= 0.015\,\text{m}^2$

      because $1\,\text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$. The other quantities are already in SI units.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. We calculate the hydrostatic pressure, the total pressure and the force one after another:

      $$\begin{aligned} &\rho g h \\ &= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \\ &\quad \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \\ &\quad \cdot 12.0\,\text{m} \\ &= 117\,720\,\text{Pa} \\[6pt] p &= p_0 + \rho g h \\ &= 101\,000\,\text{Pa} \\ &\quad + 117\,720\,\text{Pa} \\ &= 218\,720\,\text{Pa} \\[6pt] F &= \rho g h \cdot A \\ &= 117\,720\,\text{Pa} \\ &\quad \cdot 0.015\,\text{m}^2 \\ &= 1765.8\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The total pressure is $p \approx 219\,\text{kPa}$ and the force on the window is $F \approx 1.8\,\text{kN}$. The pressure is given with three significant figures, because the depth $12.0\,\text{m}$ and the atmospheric pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$ are given with three figures. The force is given with two, because the area $150\,\text{cm}^2$ is given with two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The total pressure is about 2.2 times atmospheric pressure, which fits the fact that 10 metres of water add about one atmosphere and 12 metres add a little more. Estimate:

      $10\,\text{kPa/m} \cdot 12\,\text{m} + 101\,\text{kPa}$
      $\approx 220\,\text{kPa}$

      The force equals the weight of a mass of about $180\,\text{kg}$, although the window is small. The pressure does not crush the diver's body, because in water the same pressure acts on the body from all sides and the body fluids transmit it to the inside as well. Only the pressure in the body's air spaces, such as the ears, has to be equalised.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      The water in a pool is $4.0\,\text{m}$ deep. A student calculates the hydrostatic pressure at a point $1.0\,\text{m}$ above the bottom of the pool: $\rho g h = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} \approx 9.8\,\text{kPa}$. Where is the error?

      • The question asked for the hydrostatic pressure, that is, the gauge pressure $\rho g h$, which does not include atmospheric pressure. In the total pressure it would be included. The error is in where the depth is measured from.

      • Correct. The pressure is determined by the column of water above the point, and its height is $4.0\,\text{m} - 1.0\,\text{m} = 3.0\,\text{m}$. So $\rho g h = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m} \approx 29\,\text{kPa}$.

      • Multiplying by the area would give a force, $F = pA$, not a pressure. The pressure $\rho g h$ does not depend on the area. Check where the depth $h$ is measured from.

      • The form of the calculation is correct, but $h$ is wrong. Depth is measured downwards from the surface of the liquid; a point $1.0\,\text{m}$ above the bottom is at a depth of $3.0\,\text{m}$.

      Optional Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      A mercury barometer has a vertical glass tube that is closed at the top and whose lower end is in a dish of mercury. In the upper part of the tube, above the mercury, there is a vacuum, so the pressure at the top of the column is zero. The atmospheric pressure of $101\,\text{kPa}$ holds the column of mercury up in the tube.

      How tall is the column? The density of mercury is $13\,600\,\text{kg/m}^3$. Give the answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      At the level of the surface in the dish, the pressure inside the tube is equal to the pressure outside, that is, atmospheric pressure. What produces this pressure inside the tube?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      At the top of the column the pressure is zero, so the hydrostatic pressure of the column equals atmospheric pressure: $\rho g h = p_0$, so $h = \dfrac{p_0}{\rho g}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Substitute in pascals:

      $h$$= \dfrac{101\,000\,\text{Pa}}{13\,600\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      density of mercury $\rho = 13\,600\,\text{kg/m}^3$ (given in the problem), atmospheric pressure $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, pressure at the top of the column $0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the height $h$ of the column of mercury in metres

      Principle

      The column is at rest, so at the level of the liquid surface the pressure is the same in the dish and inside the tube. In the dish it is atmospheric pressure $p_0$, and in the tube it is produced by the hydrostatic pressure of the column of mercury, because above the column there is a vacuum ($p = 0$) and nothing else presses the column downwards. Therefore $p_0 = 0 + \rho g h$.

      Formula

      $p_0 = \rho g h$, so

      $$h = \frac{p_0}{\rho g}$$

      Conversions

      $101\,\text{kPa} = 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$, so that the units $\text{Pa} = \text{kg}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^2)$ cancel to metres.

      Substitution

      $h$$= \dfrac{101\,000\,\text{Pa}}{13\,600\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}}$
      $= \dfrac{101\,000\,\text{Pa}}{133\,416\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^2\text{s}^2}}$
      $= 0.75703\,\text{m}$

      Answer

      $0.757\,\text{m}$

      Three significant figures, like the density $13\,600\,\text{kg/m}^3$ and the atmospheric pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$.

      Reasonableness

      A column of water would be $101\,\text{kPa} / 9.81\,\text{kPa/m} \approx 10.3\,\text{m}$ tall, and mercury is $13.6$ times denser, so the column is $10.3\,\text{m} / 13.6 \approx 0.757\,\text{m}$. Checking backwards, $13\,600 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.757\,\text{Pa} \approx 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$. About $76\,\text{cm}$ is a suitable height for a glass tube.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The slope is $\rho g = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$, so $\rho = \dfrac{\Delta p}{g\,\Delta h}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$$= \dfrac{171\,\text{kPa} - 101\,\text{kPa}}{8.0\,\text{m}}$
      $= 8.75\,\text{kPa/m}$
      $= 8750\,\text{Pa/m}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      two points of the line $(h, p)$: $(0\,\text{m},\ 101\,\text{kPa})$ and $(8.0\,\text{m},\ 171\,\text{kPa})$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the density of the liquid $\rho$ in $\text{kg/m}^3$

      Principle

      In a liquid $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, so the $(h, p)$ graph is a straight line whose constant term is the pressure at the surface $p_0$ and whose slope is $\rho g$. The density therefore comes from the slope, when the density of the liquid is constant at all depths. The constant term does not affect the density.

      Reading the graph

      From the point $h = 0$ we read $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$ (atmospheric pressure at the surface) and from the point $h = 8.0\,\text{m}$ the pressure $171\,\text{kPa}$. The slope is the change in pressure divided by the change in depth:

      $\Delta p$$= 171\,\text{kPa} - 101\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 70\,\text{kPa}$

      $\Delta h = 8.0\,\text{m}$.

      Formula

      slope $\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h} = \rho g$, so

      $$\rho = \frac{\Delta p}{g\,\Delta h}$$

      Conversions

      $\Delta p$$= 70\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 70\,000\,\text{Pa}$

      so that the result comes in $\text{kg/m}^3$. At the same time the slope is

      $\dfrac{70\,000\,\text{Pa}}{8.0\,\text{m}}$$= 8750\,\text{Pa/m}$

      Substitution

      $\rho$$= \dfrac{70\,000\,\text{Pa}}{9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 8.0\,\text{m}}$
      $= \dfrac{70\,000\,\text{Pa}}{78.48\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
      $= 891.9\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}$

      Answer

      $890\,\text{kg/m}^3$

      Two significant figures, because the pressure difference $\Delta p = 70\,\text{kPa}$ and the depth difference $\Delta h = 8.0\,\text{m}$ are given to two figures.

      Reasonableness

      The slope for water would be $9.81\,\text{kPa/m}$, so $8.0\,\text{m}$ would give $78.5\,\text{kPa}$. The measured increase of $70\,\text{kPa}$ is slightly smaller, so the liquid is lighter than water, for example oil. Checking backwards, $890 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 8.0\,\text{Pa} \approx 70\,000\,\text{Pa}$.

      Optional 5. Explanation task: Explain and transfer

      A diver is at a depth of $3.0\,\text{m}$ and feels pressure in an ear. When the diver equalises the pressure by letting air from the throat into the middle ear, the feeling goes away. Use pressure to explain why a force acts on the eardrum at first and why it disappears when the pressure is equalised. Use the concepts pressure, gauge pressure and force.

      Model answer

      At the surface there is atmospheric pressure on both sides of the eardrum, so the forces cancel. At a depth of three metres the outside pressure is greater than atmospheric pressure by the gauge pressure $\rho g h = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m} \approx 29\,\text{kPa}$, but the air in the middle ear is still at atmospheric pressure. The pressure difference pushes the membrane inwards, perpendicular to its surface, with a force $F = \Delta p \cdot A$; if the area of the membrane is about $0.6\,\text{cm}^2$ (an approximation), the force is about $1.8\,\text{N}$. When the diver lets more air into the middle ear, its pressure rises to the same size as the outside pressure. Then the pressure on the two sides of the membrane is the same, the forces cancel and the feeling goes away.
