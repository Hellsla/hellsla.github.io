Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments

Why can a paint can be placed on the overhang of a plank lying across trestles, but only up to a certain point before the plank topples?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • write the equilibrium conditions of a body for translation and for rotation.
  • calculate support forces using a free-body diagram and the moment condition.
  • explain how the position of the centre of mass decides whether a supported body stays in place or topples.

Prior knowledge

  • The moment of a force is $M = Fr$, where $r$ is the lever arm of the force, and the sign of the moment tells the direction of rotationchapter Moment: the turning effect of a force
  • Only the forces acting on the body are drawn in a free-body diagram, and for a body at rest the sum of the forces acting on it is zerochapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces

Optional Recall: the moment of a force
A door is pushed at the handle with a force of $20\,\text{N}$ perpendicular to the door. The handle is $0.80\,\text{m}$ from the hinges.What is the moment of the force about the hinges?

  • The moment is the product of the force and the lever arm, not a quotient: $M = Fr$. Farther from the hinges the same force turns the door more, so the moment grows as the distance grows. The correct value is $16\,\text{Nm}$.

  • The moment also depends on how far from the axis of rotation the force acts. The same $20\,\text{N}$ force right next to the hinge hardly turns the door at all. The moment is $M = Fr = 16\,\text{Nm}$ (chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force).

  • Correct. $M = Fr = 20\,\text{N} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m} = 16\,\text{Nm}$, because the force is perpendicular to the door and the lever arm is the distance from the hinges (chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force). Today we calculate moments for a body that does not rotate at all.

How the calculation works

The weight is $G = m_\text{p} g$ and the force of the box $F_\text{b} = m_\text{b} g$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The moment condition about support 1, with anticlockwise positive:

$$\begin{aligned} &N_2 (x_2 - x_1) \\ &\quad - G (2.0\,\text{m} - x_1) \\ &\quad - F_\text{b} (x_\text{b} - x_1) = 0 \end{aligned}$$

from which $N_2$ is solved. Then from the sum of the forces $N_1 = G + F_\text{b} - N_2$. The readout shows the moments about support 1, and their sum is zero. The common centre of mass is $x_\text{cm} = \dfrac{m_\text{p} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} + m_\text{b} x_\text{b}}{m_\text{p} + m_\text{b}}$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Two equilibrium conditions

    In the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force we learned that a force rotates a body the more, the greater the moment of the force $M = Fr$. For a body to stay at rest, it is not enough that the forces cancel each other. A body is in equilibrium when it neither starts to move nor starts to rotate. For translation the condition is familiar from Newton's laws: the sum of the forces is zero. For rotation the condition is new: the sum of the moments of the forces is zero. In both cases in the figure two equal and opposite forces act on the ruler, so the sum of the forces is zero. On the left the forces lie along the same line and do not rotate the ruler. On the right they act along different lines, and their moments rotate the ruler in the same direction: the ruler starts to rotate, although its middle does not start to move.

    Centre of mass

    The Earth's gravitational pull acts on every part of a body. When the moment of the weight is calculated, however, the whole weight can be thought to act at a single point, the centre of mass of the body. The centre of mass of a uniform, symmetrical body, such as a plank of constant thickness, is at its middle. This is why the weight is drawn in a free-body diagram starting from the centre of mass. The common centre of mass of two bodies is between their centres of mass, nearer the heavier body. When a body is supported at one point, it stays in place only if the centre of mass is on the vertical line through the point of support: otherwise the moment of the weight about the point of support rotates the body.

    When a body topples

    A body standing on a surface rests on its whole base. It stays upright when the vertical line through the centre of mass falls within the base. The moment of the weight about the edge of the base then turns the body back onto its base. If the vertical line falls outside the base, the moment of the weight about the lower edge rotates the body over the edge, and the body topples. A low, wide body therefore withstands a greater tilt than a tall, narrow one. The boxes in the figure are on the same inclined plane, and friction prevents them from sliding.

    Equilibrium conditions

    A body is in equilibrium when both the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments of the forces are zero:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Sigma\overline{F} &= \overline{0} \\ \Sigma M &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

    where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the vector sum of the forces acting on the body ($\text{N}$) and $\Sigma M$ the sum of the moments of the forces about some point ($\text{Nm}$). The first is the equilibrium condition for translation and the second the moment condition, that is, the equilibrium condition for rotation. In components the first condition is $\Sigma F_x = 0$ and $\Sigma F_y = 0$. The moments are calculated as in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force: $M = Fr$, where $r$ is the lever arm of the force, and the sign tells the direction of rotation. In this chapter an anticlockwise moment is positive.

    Choosing the reference point

    A body at rest does not rotate about any point, so the moment condition holds about every point. It is worth choosing the point of application of an unknown force as the reference point for the moments: the lever arm of that force is then zero, so the force does not appear in the moment condition. If a force is not perpendicular to a rod-like body (a plank, a rod), it is resolved into components. When the reference point is on the line of the rod, the moment is given by the component perpendicular to the rod multiplied by the distance from the reference point. In the figure the force $\overline{F}$ acting on the end of the rod makes the angle $\alpha$ with the rod: the component $F\cos\alpha$ along the rod passes through the hinge and does not rotate the rod, and the perpendicular component $F\sin\alpha$ gives the moment $M = F\sin\alpha \cdot r$.

    Two supports

    The plank in the figure rests on two supports. Let us take the moments about the centre of mass: the lever arm of the weight $\overline{G}$ is zero, the support force $\overline{N}_1$ rotates the plank clockwise and $\overline{N}_2$ anticlockwise. From the moment condition $N_2 r_2 - N_1 r_1 = 0$ we get

    $$N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$$

    where $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the support forces ($\text{N}$) and $r_1$ and $r_2$ the distances of the supports from the centre of mass ($\text{m}$). The support nearer the centre of mass thus carries the larger share of the weight. From the condition for translation the sum of the support forces is $N_1 + N_2 = G$.

    Common centre of mass

    For two bodies of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, the common centre of mass is at

    $$x_\text{cm} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

    where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the positions of the centres of mass of the bodies ($\text{m}$) on the same axis. The formula follows from the moment condition: if the bodies are supported at $x_\text{cm}$, the moments of the weights about the point of support cancel each other. A loaded plank resting on two supports topples when the common centre of mass of the plank and the load moves outside the interval between the supports.

    Common misconception

    Many think that two supports always carry half of the weight each, or that the support farther away carries more because its "lever" is longer. The moment condition about the centre of mass shows the opposite: $N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$, so the support nearer the centre of mass carries more. Each support carries half of the weight only when the supports are at equal distances from the centre of mass.

    Another mistake is to think that a loaded plank topples as soon as the load is past a support. The plank topples only when the common centre of mass of the plank and the load is past the support: until then the weight of the plank itself between the supports cancels the moment of the load.

    A third mistake is to believe that moments must be calculated only about the centre of mass or a support. The moment condition holds about any point, and the same forces are obtained from different points. A good choice merely shortens the calculation.

    Summary

    • A body is in equilibrium when the sum of the forces is zero, $\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0}$ (for translation), and the sum of the moments of the forces is zero, $\Sigma M = 0$ (for rotation).
    • The moment condition holds about any point. It is worth choosing the point of application of an unknown force as the reference point.
    • The weight acts at the centre of mass, which is in the middle of a uniform, symmetrical body. For a plank on two supports $N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$: the support nearer the centre of mass carries more.
    • A supported body stays upright when the vertical line through the centre of mass falls within the support area; otherwise it topples.

      Worked example

      Problem

      Two carriers carry a uniform horizontal log of length $4.00\,\text{m}$ and mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$. Carrier A holds the front end of the log, and carrier B is $1.00\,\text{m}$ from the back end. With what force does each carrier hold the log? The carriers support the log vertically upwards.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The log: $L = 4.00\,\text{m}$, $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The centre of mass is in the middle, $2.00\,\text{m}$ from A; B is $3.00\,\text{m}$ from A. We are asked for the forces of the carriers, $N_\text{A}$ and $N_\text{B}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The log is at rest, so it is in equilibrium for both translation and rotation. The body is the log. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on it are the weight $\overline{G}$ downwards from the centre of mass and the forces of the carriers $\overline{N}_\text{A}$ and $\overline{N}_\text{B}$ upwards. A is chosen as the reference point, so the lever arm of $N_\text{A}$ is zero, and the anticlockwise moment is positive. The unknown $N_\text{B}$ is obtained from the moment condition and $N_\text{A}$ then from the sum of the forces.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The moment condition about A and the condition $\Sigma F_y = 0$, with upwards positive:

      $$\begin{aligned} &N_\text{B} r_\text{B} - G r_G = 0 \\ &N_\text{B} = \frac{m g r_G}{r_\text{B}} \\[6pt] &N_\text{A} + N_\text{B} - G = 0 \\ &N_\text{A} = m g - N_\text{B} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the lengths in metres. The lever arms of the forces are $r_G = 2.00\,\text{m}$ and $r_\text{B} = 3.00\,\text{m}$, because the forces are vertical and the log horizontal.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} &G = m g \\ &= 60.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ &= 588.6\,\text{N} \\[6pt] &N_\text{B} = \frac{G r_G}{r_\text{B}} \\ &= \frac{588.6\,\text{N} \cdot 2.00\,\text{m}}{3.00\,\text{m}} \\ &= 392.4\,\text{N} \\[6pt] &N_\text{A} = G - N_\text{B} \\ &= 588.6\,\text{N} - 392.4\,\text{N} \\ &= 196.2\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. Carrier A holds the log with a force of $196\,\text{N}$ and carrier B with a force of $392\,\text{N}$. The results are given to three significant figures, like the mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$ and the lengths.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The sum of the forces is $196\,\text{N} + 392\,\text{N} = 588\,\text{N}$, the weight of the log. B is $1.00\,\text{m}$ and A $2.00\,\text{m}$ from the centre of mass, so B carries twice as much as A, as the moment condition about the centre of mass requires: $196\,\text{N} \cdot 2.00\,\text{m} = 392\,\text{N} \cdot 1.00\,\text{m}$.

      Find the error in the free-body diagram

      A uniform $20\,\text{kg}$ plank rests on two supports. Support 1 is $1.5\,\text{m}$ and support 2 $1.0\,\text{m}$ from the middle of the plank. A student has drawn the free-body diagram of the plank.What is wrong in it?

      • The centre of mass of a uniform plank is in its middle, so $\overline{G}$ correctly starts from the middle. The error is in the support forces: support 2, nearer the centre of mass, carries more, $N_2 = 117.7\,\text{N}$ and $N_1 = 78.5\,\text{N}$.

      • The plank does press the supports downwards, but that force acts on the supports, not on the plank. The free-body diagram of the plank shows the forces of the supports on the plank, and they push the plank upwards. The error is in the lengths of the arrows.

      • The sum of the forces is zero, but that is only one of the equilibrium conditions. About the centre of mass, the moment of $\overline{N}_2$, $98.1\,\text{N} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m}$, does not cancel the moment of $\overline{N}_1$, $98.1\,\text{N} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m}$, so a plank like this would start to rotate.

      • Correct. The moment condition about the centre of mass gives $N_1 \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} = N_2 \cdot 1.0\,\text{m}$. When in addition $N_1 + N_2 = 196.2\,\text{N}$, we get $N_1 = 78.5\,\text{N}$ and $N_2 = 117.7\,\text{N}$.         Show the solution

        Given

        board $L = 4.00\,\text{m}$, $m_\text{p} = 30.0\,\text{kg}$ (centre of mass $2.00\,\text{m}$ from the back end); support $1.50\,\text{m}$ from the back end; diver $m_\text{d} = 60.0\,\text{kg}$ at the front end; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        the force of the bolt $F_\text{bolt}$ in newtons

        The board is at rest, so it is in equilibrium for both translation and rotation, and the sum of the moments about any point is zero.         Free-body diagram

        The body is the board. The forces acting on it are the weight of the board $\overline{G}$ downwards from the middle (the Earth), the force of the diver $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ downwards from the front end (the diver is at rest, so $F_\text{d} = m_\text{d} g$), the force of the support $\overline{N}$ upwards and the force of the bolt $\overline{F}_\text{bolt}$ downwards from the back end.         Formula

        $F_\text{bolt} r_\text{bolt} - m_\text{p} g\, r_\text{p} - m_\text{d} g\, r_\text{d}$
        $= 0$

        $F_\text{bolt}$$= \dfrac{(m_\text{p} r_\text{p} + m_\text{d} r_\text{d})\, g}{r_\text{bolt}}$

        $= 0.50\,\text{m}$
        $r_\text{d}$$= 4.00\,\text{m} - 1.50\,\text{m}$

        $= 1471.5\,\text{Nm}$

        $F_\text{bolt}$$= \dfrac{1618.65\,\text{Nm}}{1.50\,\text{m}}$
        $= 1079.1\,\text{N}$

        1. Centre of mass and toppling

        A bookshelf tilts when it is pulled by its upper edge. When does the shelf topple by itself if it is let go?

        • Correct. The moment of the weight about the lower edge then rotates the shelf over the edge. As long as the vertical line falls within the base, the moment of the weight turns the shelf back onto its base.

        • The height of the centre of mass alone does not decide. A high centre of mass makes the angle of toppling smaller, but the shelf topples only when the vertical line through the centre of mass passes the edge of the base; otherwise its weight turns the shelf back onto its base.

        • The position of the top edge does not decide. The upper part of the shelf can be outside the base, but the shelf stays upright until the vertical line through the centre of mass passes the edge of the base.

        Optional 3. Explain: why the nearer support carries more

        A uniform plank rests on two supports, and one support is nearer the middle of the plank than the other. Using the equilibrium conditions, explain why the nearer support carries the larger share of the weight of the plank.

        Model answer

        The plank is at rest, so the sum of the forces is zero, $N_1 + N_2 = G$, and the sum of the moments about any point is zero. Take the moments about the centre of mass: the lever arm of the weight is zero, and the support forces rotate the plank in opposite directions. They cancel each other when $N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$, where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the distances of the supports from the centre of mass.           1. Write the two equilibrium conditions of a body: for translation and for rotation.
            Answer

            For translation the sum of the forces is zero, $\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0}$. For rotation the sum of the moments of the forces is zero, $\Sigma M = 0$, about any point.

          2. The sum of the forces acting on a body is zero, but the sum of the moments is not. What happens to the body?
            Answer

            The body starts to rotate, although its centre of mass does not start to move. For example, a ruler with equal and opposite forces on its ends along different lines rotates on the spot.

            Answer

            In the figure the angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the vertical and the height direction of the shelf, that is, its front edge, so it is the tilt of the shelf from the vertical.             $12.5^\circ$

            Show the solution

            Given

            plank $L = 4.00\,\text{m}$, $m_\text{p} = 20.0\,\text{kg}$, centre of mass $x_\text{p} = 2.00\,\text{m}$; trestles $x_1 = 0.50\,\text{m}$ and $x_2 = 3.00\,\text{m}$ from the left end; painter $m_\text{pt} = 60.0\,\text{kg}$

            Find

            the greatest distance $x$ of the painter from the left end of the plank before toppling

            Principle

            The plank stays on the trestles until the support force $N_1$ of the left trestle drops to zero, because a trestle can only push upwards. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on the plank are the weight $G_\text{p}$ downwards from the middle, the force of the painter $m_\text{pt} g$ downwards and the support forces upwards. The right trestle is chosen as the reference point: at the limit $N_1 = 0$, and the moment of the weight of the plank (anticlockwise) cancels the moment of the painter (clockwise).

            Formula

            $m_\text{p} g\,(x_2 - x_\text{p}) - m_\text{pt} g\,(x - x_2) = 0$, from which

            $x$$= x_2 + \dfrac{m_\text{p}\,(x_2 - x_\text{p})}{m_\text{pt}}$

            Conversions

            None needed: $g$ and the unit of mass cancel, and the lengths are in metres. The lever arm of the weight of the plank from the trestle is

            $x_2 - x_\text{p}$$= 3.00\,\text{m} - 2.00\,\text{m}$
            $= 1.00\,\text{m}$

            Substitution

            $x$$= 3.00\,\text{m} + \dfrac{20.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.00\,\text{m}}{60.0\,\text{kg}}$
            $= 3.00\,\text{m} + 0.333\,\text{m}$
            $= 3.333\,\text{m}$

            Answer

            $3.33\,\text{m}$

            From the left end of the plank, three significant figures like the data.

            Reasonableness

            At the limit the common centre of mass of the plank and the painter is at the trestle: the sum of the products of mass and position is

            $20.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 2.00\,\text{m} + 60.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.333\,\text{m}$
            $= 240\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}$

            and divided by the total mass $80.0\,\text{kg}$ it gives $3.00\,\text{m}$. The painter can walk only $0.33\,\text{m}$ past the trestle, because he is three times as heavy as the plank.

          2. A shop sign hangs from the end of a horizontal uniform rod. The rod has length $1.20\,\text{m}$ and mass $4.00\,\text{kg}$, and its other end is attached to a wall with a hinge. The sign, of mass $8.00\,\text{kg}$, hangs from the outer end of the rod. A cable runs from the outer end of the rod to the wall above the hinge so that the angle between the cable and the rod is $30.0^\circ$.

            How large is the tension force of the cable?

            N
            Answer

            $196\,\text{N}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            rod $L = 1.20\,\text{m}$, $m_\text{r} = 4.00\,\text{kg}$, centre of mass in the middle; sign $m_\text{s} = 8.00\,\text{kg}$ at the outer end; cable attached to the wall above the hinge, angle between the cable and the rod $\alpha = 30.0^\circ$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

            Find

            the tension force of the cable $T$ in newtons

            Principle

            The rod is at rest, so the sum of the moments about the hinge is zero. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on the rod are its weight downwards from the middle, the force of the sign $m_\text{s} g$ downwards from the outer end, the tension force of the cable $\overline{T}$ from the outer end along the cable towards the wall, and the force of the hinge. The hinge is chosen as the reference point, so its unknown force drops out. $\overline{T}$ is resolved into components: the component along the rod passes through the hinge and causes no moment, and the perpendicular component $T \sin\alpha$ rotates the rod upwards.

            Formula

            $T \sin\alpha \cdot L - m_\text{r} g \cdot \tfrac{L}{2} - m_\text{s} g \cdot L$
            $= 0$

            from which

            $T$$= \dfrac{(\tfrac{1}{2} m_\text{r} + m_\text{s})\, g}{\sin\alpha}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the masses are in kilograms, and the length $L$ cancels.

            Substitution

            $T$$= \dfrac{(2.00\,\text{kg} + 8.00\,\text{kg}) \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{\sin 30.0^\circ}$
            $= \dfrac{98.1\,\text{N}}{0.500}$
            $= 196.2\,\text{N}$

            Answer

            $196\,\text{N}$

            Three significant figures like the data.

            Reasonableness

            The vertical component of the cable force, $T \sin 30.0^\circ = 98.1\,\text{N}$, is less than the combined weight of the rod and the sign, $117.7\,\text{N}$; the hinge carries the rest. A shallow cable requires a large tension: as the angle approaches zero, $T$ would grow without limit.

          3. Two people carry a uniform log of length $5.00\,\text{m}$ and mass $90.0\,\text{kg}$ horizontally. The front carrier holds the front end of the log, and can lift with a force of at most $300\,\text{N}$.

            How far from the back end of the log must the rear carrier hold the log so that the force of the front carrier is exactly $300\,\text{N}$?

            m
            Answer

            $1.21\,\text{m}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            log $L = 5.00\,\text{m}$, $m = 90.0\,\text{kg}$, centre of mass in the middle; force of the front carrier $F = 300\,\text{N}$ at the front end; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

            Find

            the distance $a$ of the rear carrier from the back end of the log

            Principle

            The log is at rest, so the sum of the moments about any point is zero. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on the log are the weight $G$ downwards from the middle and the forces of the carriers upwards. The rear carrier is chosen as the reference point, so his unknown force drops out. The centre of mass is between the carriers, so the weight and the force of the front carrier rotate the log in opposite directions.

            Formula

            $F\,(L - a) - m g\,(\tfrac{L}{2} - a) = 0$, from which

            $a$$= \dfrac{m g \cdot \tfrac{L}{2} - F L}{m g - F}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the mass is in kilograms and the length in metres. The weight is

            $G$$= 90.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
            $= 882.9\,\text{N}$

            Substitution

            The numerator is

            $882.9\,\text{N} \cdot 2.50\,\text{m} - 300\,\text{N} \cdot 5.00\,\text{m}$
            $= 707.25\,\text{Nm}$

            and the denominator $882.9\,\text{N} - 300\,\text{N} = 582.9\,\text{N}$. So

            $a$$= \dfrac{707.25\,\text{Nm}}{582.9\,\text{N}}$
            $= 1.213\,\text{m}$

            Answer

            $1.21\,\text{m}$

            From the back end of the log, three significant figures like the data.

            Reasonableness

            The rear carrier carries $882.9\,\text{N} - 300\,\text{N} = 582.9\,\text{N}$, and about the centre of mass the moments cancel: $300\,\text{N} \cdot 2.50\,\text{m} = 750\,\text{Nm}$ and $582.9\,\text{N} \cdot (2.50 - 1.213)\,\text{m} = 750\,\text{Nm}$. The rear carrier is nearer the centre of mass and carries more.
