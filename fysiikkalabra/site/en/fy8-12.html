Ionising radiation and the body

Why does a sheet of paper stop alpha radiation while gamma radiation passes through the whole human body, and why is a substance that emits alpha radiation nevertheless the most dangerous one when it gets inside the body?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • compare the penetrating power of alpha, beta and gamma radiation and explain it with ionisation.
  • calculate the attenuation of gamma radiation with the half-value thickness.
  • explain how a GM tube detects radiation and how ionisation damages cells.

Prior knowledge

  • The energy of a photon is $E = hf$, and the electronvolt is $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon
  • A radioactive nucleus emits alpha, beta or gamma radiationchapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay
  • Every half-life halves the number of undecayed nuclei, $N = N_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$chapter The decay law and half-life

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

The indoor air in Finland contains radon, which decays by emitting alpha radiation. Alpha radiation is already stopped by a sheet of paper, and an X-ray nurse protects herself from radiation with an apron containing lead. Yet it is precisely the radon in the air we breathe that is the most significant source of radiation to which people in Finland are exposed. Radiation cannot be seen, heard or felt, but a Geiger–Müller counter clicks for every particle it detects.

Why is alpha radiation, which is stopped so easily, harmful inside the body, and why do we protect ourselves from gamma radiation precisely with lead?

Simulation

How to use

Measure starts a $10\,\text{s}$ measurement; the counter shows the pulse count $N$ and the elapsed time $t$, and the finished result stays in the readings. Source selects an alpha, beta or gamma source or no source, in which case the counter counts only background radiation. Barrier selects the material of the plates, and the slider Plates their number $n$ from $0$ to $20$: a sheet of paper is $0.1\,\text{mm}$, an aluminium or lead plate $1\,\text{mm}$. When $n = 0$, there is no barrier. In the picture a violet dot is an alpha particle, a blue one a beta particle and an orange wave a gamma photon; particles that stop in the plates disappear in them. Every particle hitting the tube produces an electron avalanche near the wire, which shows as a flash, and the counter increases by one. The grey waves from above are background radiation. If you change the source or the barrier, the measurement is reset. Speed changes only the pace of the passing of time.

Assumptions of the model

The source is $3\,\text{cm}$ from the window of the tube. The model does not take into account the loss of energy of the alpha particle in the air and in the window of the tube, although in reality alpha particles should be measured from closer. The sources are chosen so that without a barrier the counter counts on average $600$ pulses in $10\,\text{s}$ from each; background radiation gives on average $4$ pulses. Each source emits only one type of radiation: real americium-241 emits also weak gamma radiation in addition to alpha radiation. An alpha particle stops already in one sheet of paper. The maximum energy of the beta particles is $2.28\,\text{MeV}$, as in a strontium-90 source, and their range is $1.1\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^2}$, about $4\,\text{mm}$ of aluminium; bremsstrahlung is not in the model. The gamma radiation is the $662\,\text{keV}$ radiation of caesium-137 as a narrow beam. A real GM tube detects only a small fraction of gamma photons, but in the model every particle or photon hitting the tube is registered, and the tube has no dead time. The readings are values of the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

The fraction $T$ that passes through the barrier is calculated by the type of radiation. For alpha, $T = 0$ behind any barrier. For beta, $T = e^{-6.6 x}$, where $x$ is the product of the density and the thickness of the barrier in the unit $\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^2}$, and $T = 0$ when $x \ge 1.1\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^2}$. For gamma, $T = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$, where $d_{1/2}$ is $100\,\text{mm}$ for paper, $34\,\text{mm}$ for aluminium and $5.5\,\text{mm}$ for lead. The measuring time is divided into $6000$ short periods. In each period a pulse comes from the source with the probability $0.1 \cdot T$ and from the background with the probability $\tfrac{4}{6000}$, so the pulse count varies randomly around the mean $600 \cdot T + 4$. The first measurement with the same settings always gives the same result, and a repeated measurement a different one.

As values of the model, behind the sheet of paper the alpha source gave $5$ pulses in $10\,\text{s}$, as many as the background alone, but the beta source $550$ and the gamma source $577$. Behind lead the pulse count of the gamma source was $590$, $332$, $175$, $89$ and $57$ at the thicknesses $0$, $5$, $10$, $15$ and $20\,\text{mm}$.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Ionising radiation removes electrons

    Radiation is ionising when a single particle or photon of it can remove an electron from an atom or molecule. This produces an ion pair: a positive ion and a free electron. For this the ionisation energy of the atom or molecule is needed, for example $12.6\,\text{eV}$ for a water molecule. The energy of a photon is $E = hf$ (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon), so what matters is the frequency of the photon, not the total power of the radiation. The energy of a visible-light photon is only $1.7$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$, so even bright light does not ionise water. The energy of an X-ray or gamma photon is thousands or millions of electronvolts.

    Ionisation transfers the energy of radiation to matter

    An ionising particle does not usually lose its energy all at once, but removes one electron after another along its path. In air one ion pair takes on average an energy of $W \approx 34\,\text{eV}$. This is more than the ionisation energy of the molecules, because part of the energy goes to exciting the molecules and to the kinetic energy of the released electrons. When the whole kinetic energy $E$ of a particle is transferred to matter, the number of ion pairs is

    $$n_\text{i} = \frac{E}{W}$$

    where $n_\text{i}$ is the number of ion pairs, $E$ the energy the radiation transfers to matter and $W$ the average energy needed for one ion pair. For example, the kinetic energy of an alpha particle of americium-241 is $5.49\,\text{MeV}$, so when it stops in air it produces about $5.49 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{eV} : 34\,\text{eV} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{5}$ ion pairs.

    Types of radiation ionise in different ways

    The types of radiation (chapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay) differ in how densely they ionise. An alpha particle is heavy and doubly charged. It strongly attracts the electrons of nearby atoms and removes thousands of them in every millimetre of its path, even in air. It therefore loses its energy quickly and stops after about $4\,\text{cm}$ in air and about $0.04\,\text{mm}$ in tissue, which is the size of a few cells. A beta particle is a light electron that ionises much less often and travels in a winding path for up to metres in air. Gamma and X-ray photons are electrically neutral. They travel straight until a single interaction removes an electron, and only this fast electron then ionises its surroundings. Neutral neutrons ionise in a similar indirect way: they collide with nuclei and set charged particles in motion.

    Penetrating power

    Penetrating power tells how thick a layer of matter the radiation passes through. The more densely radiation ionises, the faster it gives up its energy and the thinner the layer that stops it. So alpha radiation has the smallest and gamma radiation the greatest penetrating power. The last column of the table tells what stops alpha and beta radiation and what attenuates gamma and neutron radiation effectively. X-radiation is photon radiation and behaves like gamma radiation.

    Radiation What it is Charge In air Barrier
    alpha $\alpha$ helium nucleus $+2e$ a few $\text{cm}$ sheet of paper
    beta $\beta^-$ electron $-e$ metres aluminium, plastic
    gamma $\gamma$ photon $0$ no range lead, concrete
    neutron $n$ neutron $0$ no range water, concrete

    Gamma radiation has no range: every layer attenuates the same fraction of it, so even a thick barrier does not stop it completely. Neutrons are slowed most effectively by materials rich in hydrogen, because a neutron loses the most energy when it collides with a proton of almost the same mass.

    Detecting radiation with a Geiger–Müller tube

    Humans cannot sense ionising radiation, so it is detected with an instrument. The most common one is the Geiger–Müller tube, or GM tube. It is a metal cylinder filled with a noble gas, with a thin wire in the middle. There is a voltage of about $500\,\text{V}$ between the wire and the wall, with the wire positive. When an ionising particle passes through the gas, it removes an electron. The electric field accelerates the electron towards the wire so that it removes new electrons, and an electron avalanche forms near the wire. The avalanche shows up as a current pulse, which a counter counts or a loudspeaker clicks. At one end of the tube there is a thin window, through which even alpha particles can enter the gas.

    Every ionising event triggers a similar avalanche, so the pulse is equally large regardless of the type and energy of the radiation. A GM counter therefore counts events but does not tell what radiation it detects. The type of radiation can be deduced with barriers: if the pulse count drops to the background level behind a sheet of paper, the radiation is alpha radiation. The counter always counts also background radiation, which comes from the ground, building materials, radon in the air and space. A GM tube detects only a small fraction of gamma photons, because most of them pass through the thin gas without interacting. The energy of radiation is measured, for example, with scintillation detectors, and the radiation a person receives is monitored with dosimeters.

    Radiation in the body

    The biological effects of radiation begin with physics: ionisation breaks chemical bonds. Radiation can ionise the DNA of a cell directly (direct effect). More often it ionises water molecules, because a cell is mostly water. This produces highly reactive radicals, such as hydroxyl radicals, which damage the DNA (indirect effect). The cell repairs most of the damage. However, damage that is not repaired or is repaired wrongly can kill the cell or change its genome, and a change in the genome can lead to cancer after years.

    The most harmful case for the body is when many ionisations occur within a short distance. The dense track of an alpha particle can break both strands of the DNA close together, and a cell repairs such damage poorly. Outside the body alpha radiation is almost harmless, because it does not pass through the dead outer layer of the skin. When inhaled, swallowed or entering a wound, an alpha emitter gives up all its energy in a few cell layers of living tissue. In Finland the most important such source is indoor radon, whose decay products remain in the lungs. Gamma radiation, on the other hand, passes through the body, so even an external gamma source irradiates the internal organs.

    The effect of radiation on the body is described by the absorbed dose, which tells how much of the energy of radiation has been transferred to a kilogram of tissue. A large dose in a short time kills so many cells that the function of the tissue is disturbed. Small doses increase the risk of cancer. Doses, their units and protection from radiation are covered in the chapter Radiation dose and radiation safety in Finland.

    Attenuation of gamma radiation

    Every layer attenuates the same fraction

    A gamma photon either passes through a layer of matter without interacting or interacts in it, and is then absorbed or scattered out of the beam. In a thin layer every photon has the same probability of interacting, regardless of how many layers it has already passed. Therefore every layer of equal thickness attenuates the same fraction of the radiation arriving at it, in the same way as every half-life halves the number of undecayed nuclei (chapter The decay law and half-life). The half-value thickness $d_{1/2}$, or half-value layer, is the thickness of a layer that lets half of the radiation through. The radiation that has passed through a thickness $d$ is

    $$I = I_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$$

    where $I_0$ is the intensity of the radiation without a barrier, $I$ the intensity behind the barrier, $d$ the thickness of the barrier and $d_{1/2}$ the half-value thickness. Instead of the intensity, the net pulse count of the counter can be substituted into the equation, that is, the pulse count from which the background radiation has been subtracted. The exponent $\tfrac{d}{d_{1/2}}$ tells how many half-value thicknesses the barrier contains, and it need not be an integer. The thicknesses $d$ and $d_{1/2}$ must be in the same unit.

    The half-value thickness depends on the material and the energy

    The denser the material and the more electrons its atoms have, the smaller the half-value thickness. This is why lead is an effective shield: the same attenuation requires a layer of concrete about seven times thicker. High-energy gamma radiation passes through matter better than low-energy radiation. The values in the table apply to the $662\,\text{keV}$ gamma radiation of caesium-137.

    Material $d_{1/2}$
    lead $5.5\,\text{mm}$
    iron $12\,\text{mm}$
    aluminium $34\,\text{mm}$
    concrete $39\,\text{mm}$
    water $81\,\text{mm}$

    Source: calculated from the attenuation coefficients of the NIST XCOM database for a narrow beam, rounded. In a wide beam some of the scattered photons still reach the counter, so a real shield lets through slightly more than the equation gives.

    The equation applies only to photon radiation. Alpha and beta particles lose their energy gradually in every ionisation, so they have a range: after a certain thickness none of them gets through.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that an irradiated object or food itself becomes radioactive. However, the gamma, X-ray and electron radiation used in sterilisation and medicine only ionises atoms or transfers energy to them; its energy is not enough to change nuclei, so the material does not become radioactive. For example, hospital supplies are sterilised with gamma radiation, and afterwards they are as safe to handle as before. Material can be made radioactive mainly by neutron radiation, whose neutrons are bound into nuclei (chapter Fission, fusion and nuclear power). What is harmful is that radioactive material gets onto the skin or into the body, not that an object has been in radiation.

    Another mistake is to think that two half-value thicknesses stop all the gamma radiation. The second layer attenuates half of the radiation that got through the first, so a quarter gets through.

    Summary

    • Radiation is ionising when its particle or photon can remove an electron from an atom. For a photon the energy $E = hf$ decides: visible light does not ionise, X-rays and gamma radiation do.
    • Ionisation transfers the energy of radiation to matter; in air an ion pair takes on average $34\,\text{eV}$, so the number of ion pairs is $n_\text{i} = \tfrac{E}{W}$.
    • Alpha radiation ionises most densely and stops in paper, beta radiation stops in a few millimetres of aluminium, and gamma radiation is attenuated in lead and concrete: $I = I_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$.
    • A GM tube counts ionising events with the help of electron avalanches, but it cannot distinguish the types of radiation. Background radiation is always included in a measurement.
    • Radiation damages cells by ionising DNA directly and through the radicals of water. An alpha emitter is most dangerous inside the body, because it gives up its energy within a few cells.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A caesium-137 source is kept in a lead shield. The half-value thickness of lead for the gamma radiation of caesium-137 is $5.5\,\text{mm}$. How thick a layer of lead attenuates the intensity of the gamma radiation to $5.0\,\%$?

      Step 1/7

      Known and asked. The half-value thickness is $d_{1/2} = 5.5\,\text{mm}$ and the ratio of the intensities is ${I : I_0} = 5.0\,\% = 0.050$. The thickness $d$ of the lead layer is asked.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Every layer of one half-value thickness halves the intensity of the gamma radiation, so the attenuation follows the equation $I = I_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$. The thickness is in the exponent, so it is solved with a logarithm.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. When the equation is divided by $I_0$ and the natural logarithm is taken of both sides, we get $\ln \tfrac{I}{I_0} = -\tfrac{d}{d_{1/2}} \ln 2$, from which

      $$d = d_{1/2} \cdot \frac{\ln (I_0 : I)}{\ln 2}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The percentage is converted into a ratio: $5.0\,\% = 0.050$, so ${I_0 : I} = {1 : 0.050} = 20$. The thickness is obtained in millimetres, because the half-value thickness is in millimetres.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} d &= d_{1/2} \cdot \frac{\ln (I_0 : I)}{\ln 2} \\ &= 5.5\,\text{mm} \cdot \frac{\ln 20}{\ln 2} \\ &= 5.5\,\text{mm} \cdot \frac{2.9957}{0.69315} \\ &= 5.5\,\text{mm} \cdot 4.3219 \\ &= 23.77\,\text{mm} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. About $24\,\text{mm}$ of lead is needed, with two significant figures like the half-value thickness $5.5\,\text{mm}$ and the ratio $5.0\,\%$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Four half-value thicknesses, that is $22\,\text{mm}$, would let through $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{4} = 6.25\,\%$ and five, that is $27.5\,\text{mm}$, about $3.1\,\%$. The target $5.0\,\%$ lies between these, and so does the thickness $24\,\text{mm}$.

      1. The photon energy of green light is about $2.5\,\text{eV}$ and the maximum photon energy of dental X-rays about $60\,\text{keV}$. Which can ionise a water molecule, whose ionisation energy is $12.6\,\text{eV}$, and why does brighter green light not change this?

      2. Medical supplies are sterilised with gamma radiation. Do they become radioactive?
        Answer

        No. Gamma radiation ionises atoms and kills microbes by breaking their molecules, but it does not change nuclei. Radioactivity can arise mainly from neutron radiation. Only the radioactive material itself is dangerous, if it gets onto the skin or into the body.

      1. Concept

      The decay products of radon stay on the surface of the bronchi when breathed in and emit alpha radiation.Why is alpha radiation more dangerous in the lungs than gamma radiation of the same energy, even though a sheet of paper stops it?

      • Alpha radiation ionises atoms but does not make the nuclei of the tissue radioactive. The harm is caused by the alpha particle giving up all its energy within a few cells: the ionisations and the DNA damage are densely packed.

      • In the question the energies are equal, and the energy of a gamma photon can even be greater than that of an alpha particle. What matters is how densely the ionisations occur: an alpha particle gives up its energy within a few cells.

      • Correct. Dense ionisation can break both strands of the DNA close together, and a cell repairs such damage poorly. A gamma photon spreads the same energy as sparse ionisations over a long distance, partly even outside the body.

      2. Read the half-value thickness from a graph

      Plates of an unknown material have been added between a gamma source and a GM tube. The graph shows the net pulse rate, from which the background radiation has been subtracted, as a function of the total thickness $d$ of the plates. Read from the graph the half-value thickness of the material for this radiation. Answer in millimetres.

      mm
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the pulse rate when there are no plates? And half of that?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Find the point where the curve is at half of its initial value and read the thickness from the horizontal axis.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The initial value is $800$ pulses per minute, so find the point where the curve is at $400$ pulses.

      Show the solution

      Given

      the net pulse rate of the graph as a function of the thickness $d$ of the plates, grid spacing $2\,\text{mm}$ and $50$ pulses per minute

      Asked

      the half-value thickness $d_{1/2}$ in millimetres

      Principle

      The net pulse rate is proportional to the intensity of the gamma radiation hitting the GM tube, and every half-value thickness halves it. So the half-value thickness is the thickness at which the pulse rate has dropped to half of the initial value.

      Reading the graph

      When $d = 0$, the curve starts from $800$ pulses per minute. Half of that is $400$ pulses per minute. The horizontal line at $400$ meets the curve at $d = 12\,\text{mm}$. Check: at $200$ pulses the curve is at the thickness $24\,\text{mm}$.

      Formula

      $I = I_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$; when $I = \tfrac{1}{2} I_0$, the exponent is $\tfrac{d}{d_{1/2}} = 1$, so $d_{1/2} = d$

      Conversions

      Not needed: the thickness is read directly in millimetres.

      Substitution

      $\tfrac{1}{2} I_0$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 800\,\tfrac{1}{\text{min}}$
      $= 400\,\tfrac{1}{\text{min}}$

      which is reached at the thickness $d = 12\,\text{mm}$, so $d_{1/2} = 12\,\text{mm}$

      Answer

      $12\,\text{mm}$

      Two significant figures, because the thickness can be read from the graph with an accuracy of about half a millimetre.

      Reasonableness

      At two half-value thicknesses, that is $24\,\text{mm}$, the pulse rate should be $200$ and at four, that is $48\,\text{mm}$, $50$ pulses per minute; the graph agrees with both. The value is the same as the half-value thickness of iron for the gamma radiation of caesium-137.

      Optional 3. Explain: what does a GM counter tell?

      A GM counter clicks near an unknown radiation source.

      Why can the type of radiation, alpha, beta or gamma, not be deduced from the pulse count alone? How can the type of radiation be found out with the same counter?

      Model answer

      In a GM tube every ionising particle hitting the gas removes at least one electron. Near the wire the strong electric field accelerates the electrons so that an electron avalanche forms, and the avalanche is always the same size regardless of which particle started it and how much energy it had. So every event shows up as a similar pulse, and the counter only counts events. The type of radiation can be found out with barriers: if the pulse count drops to the background level behind a sheet of paper, the radiation is alpha radiation. If it passes through paper but stops in a few millimetres of aluminium, it is beta radiation. If it passes through aluminium and is attenuated gradually in lead, it is gamma radiation. The background radiation must be subtracted from every reading.

          Answer

          The total pulse rate is ${1260 : 180}\,\text{s} = 7.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$ and the background ${72 : 180}\,\text{s} = 0.40\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$; the difference is $6.6\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$. The share of the background is small but not insignificant.

The half-value thickness is close to the value for caesium-137, $5.5\,\text{mm}$.

        3. Exercise 12-12

          A WLAN network and a microwave oven use electromagnetic radiation with a frequency of $2.45\,\text{GHz}$. The ionisation energy of a water molecule is $12.6\,\text{eV}$.

          How many times greater is the ionisation energy than the energy of one photon of this radiation? Give the answer in the unit $10^{6}$.

          · 10⁶
          Answer

          $1.24 \cdot 10^{6}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          frequency $f = 2.45\,\text{GHz}$, ionisation energy of a water molecule $E_\text{i} = 12.6\,\text{eV}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Asked

          the ratio $\tfrac{E_\text{i}}{E}$, where $E$ is the energy of one photon, in the unit $10^{6}$, and why the radiation is not ionising

          Principle

          Electromagnetic radiation interacts with matter as photons, and a single photon can ionise a molecule only if its own energy $E = hf$ is at least the ionisation energy. The number of photons, that is the power of the radiation, does not help, because individual photons do not combine their energies into one ionisation.

          Formula

          $E = hf$, so

          $\dfrac{E_\text{i}}{E}$$= \dfrac{E_\text{i}}{hf}$

          Conversions

          $f$$= 2.45\,\text{GHz}$
          $= 2.45 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}$

          the energy of the photon is converted into electronvolts: $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.45 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $= 1.6234 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{J}$
          $= \dfrac{1.6234 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}}$
          $= 1.0134 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{eV}$
          $\dfrac{E_\text{i}}{E}$$= \dfrac{12.6\,\text{eV}}{1.0134 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{eV}}$
          $= 1.2434 \cdot 10^{6}$

          Answer

          $1.24 \cdot 10^{6}$

          Times, three significant figures like the frequency and the ionisation energy. The energy of one microwave photon is over a million times too small to ionise a water molecule, so even powerful microwave radiation does not ionise: it only heats matter, when the energy of the photons is transferred to the motion of the molecules.

          Reasonableness

          The energy of a visible-light photon is about $2\,\text{eV}$ and its frequency about $5 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$; the frequency of microwaves is about $2 \cdot 10^{5}$ times smaller, so the energy of the photon is about $2\,\text{eV} : (2 \cdot 10^{5}) = 10^{-5}\,\text{eV}$, as calculated.
