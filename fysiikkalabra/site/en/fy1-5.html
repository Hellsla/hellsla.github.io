Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs

Why can the position of the Voyager 2 probe be calculated in advance for any day, even though it is more than 20 billion kilometres from the Sun?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the distance, time or speed in uniform motion with the formula $s = s_0 + vt$.
  • read the speed and the initial position from a $(t, s)$ graph.
  • explain when the distance is directly proportional to time and when the position is.

Prior knowledge

  • A straight line is fitted to measurement points, and its slope tells you how much $y$ changes when $x$ increases by onechapter Graphing and fitting a straight line
  • In the equation of a straight line, the constant term is where the line crosses the $y$-axischapter Graphing and fitting a straight line

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 3.

1Think about it

The Voyager 2 probe left the Earth in 1977. In November 2018 it crossed the heliopause, the boundary beyond which the solar wind no longer dominates space. According to the measurements, the probe was then $119.0\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun (Burlaga et al. 2019), and it was moving away at a speed of $15.341\,\text{km/s}$ (NASA/JPL). The astronomical unit AU is roughly the distance between the Earth and the Sun. You cannot hold a ruler next to the probe, but you can still give its distance for any day.

What information do you need to calculate the distance of the probe from the Sun, say a year from now, and what do you assume about its speed?

Optional Recap: the slope of a line
The measurement points (time, position) lie on a straight line in the $(t, s)$ coordinate system. What does the slope of the line tell you?

  • That is where the line crosses the $s$-axis, the constant term, not the slope (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line). The slope tells you how steep the line is: by how many units $s$ changes when $t$ increases by one.

  • The length of the line does not matter, because the line continues in both directions. The slope is the ratio of two changes, $\Delta s : \Delta t$ (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line).

  • Correct. The slope is $\Delta s : \Delta t$ (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line). In motion it is the speed, and you apply it today.

Assumptions of the model

The positions on the track and the values in the graph are values calculated by the model, not measurements. The trolley moves in the positive direction only, so the velocity is positive.

How the calculation works

The position is $s = s_0 + vt$ and the distance travelled is $\Delta s = s - s_0 = vt$. The slope triangle in the graph goes from the moment $t = 2.0\,\text{s}$ to the moment $t = 6.0\,\text{s}$, so $\Delta t = 4.0\,\text{s}$, and the slope $\Delta s : \Delta t$ is the speed $v$.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Uniform motion is motion along a straight line at constant speed: in equal intervals of time, the object travels equal distances. Speed tells you how far the object travels in one second, so its unit is metres per second (m/s). If a trolley moves two metres every second, its speed is $2\,\text{m/s}$.

    Direct proportionality

    Two quantities are directly proportional if their ratio is always the same. When uniform motion starts from the zero point, the position $s$, which here equals the distance travelled, and the time $t$ are directly proportional: double the time gives double the distance, and the ratio $s : t$ is the speed $v$. In a $(t, s)$ graph this shows as a straight line through the origin.

    Linear model

    Uniform motion is a model, not reality. A cyclist pedals unevenly, and Voyager 2 slows down slightly because of the Sun's gravity. The model is still good when the speed changes only a little during the time being considered. Its graph is a straight line, and a straight line is described by a linear model: the position changes by the same amount every second. Values calculated with the model are model values, which are compared with measurements.

    Uniform motion

    Suppose that at the start of the observation ($t = 0$) an object is at position $s_0$ and moves along a straight line at constant speed $v$. Its position at time $t$ is

    $$s = s_0 + v t$$

    where $s$ is the position (m) measured from the chosen zero point, $s_0$ the initial position (m), $v$ the speed (m/s) and $t$ the time from the start (s). The distance travelled by the object is $\Delta s = s - s_0 = vt$. In the examples of this chapter the object moves in the positive direction, so the velocity is positive.

    Speed from a graph

    The $(t, s)$ graph is a straight line, and the slope of the line is the speed:

    average speed
    $$v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$$

    where $\Delta s$ is the change in position (m) and $\Delta t$ is its time interval (s). The line crosses the $s$-axis at the initial position $s_0$. If $s_0 = 0$, the position is directly proportional to time: $s = vt$. If $s_0 \neq 0$, the line is as steep as the line through the origin at the same speed, but shifted up or down. Then only the distance $\Delta s$, not the position $s$, is directly proportional to time.

    Units

    Always convert to compatible units (usually SI units) before substituting: substitute the value of the unit in its place. For example $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$, so $72\,\text{km/h} = \tfrac{72\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20\,\text{m/s}$. In the same way $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$. Distances on the scale of the Solar System are often given in astronomical units: $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$ is roughly the mean distance between the Earth and the Sun.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that in uniform motion the position doubles when the time doubles. This happens only if the motion starts from the zero point. When a trolley starts at $s_0 = 6\,\text{m}$ and moves at $2.0\,\text{m/s}$, it is at $10\,\text{m}$ at $2.0\,\text{s}$ and at $14\,\text{m}$ at $4.0\,\text{s}$, not at $20\,\text{m}$. The distance, however, does double: $4.0\,\text{m}$ becomes $8.0\,\text{m}$. Another mistake is to calculate the speed as the quotient of position and time $s : t$ instead of the quotient of the change in position and the time interval: the speed is $\Delta s : \Delta t$, not $s : t$, when $s_0 \neq 0$.

    Summary

    • In uniform motion the speed is constant, and the object travels equal distances in equal intervals of time: $s = s_0 + vt$.
    • The distance travelled $\Delta s = vt$ is directly proportional to time. The position $s$ is proportional to time only when $s_0 = 0$.
    • The $(t, s)$ graph is a straight line. Its slope $\Delta s : \Delta t$ is the speed, and the intercept on the $s$-axis is the initial position $s_0$.
    • Convert units before calculating by substitution, for example $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.
    • Uniform motion is a linear model. The values it gives are model values, which can be compared with measurements, such as the distance of Voyager 2.

      Example 1: Voyager 2's distance

      Voyager 2 crossed the heliopause on 5 November 2018 at a distance of $s_0 = 119.0\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun (Burlaga et al. 2019, Nature Astronomy). The speed of the probe relative to the Sun at the crossing was $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$ (NASA/JPL). Assume the speed is constant. According to the model, how far from the Sun is the probe on 5 November 2026?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $s_0 = 119.0\,\text{AU}$ at time $t = 0$ (5 November 2018), $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$ and $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$. The time interval from 5 November 2018 to 5 November 2026 is 8 years, including the leap days of 2020 and 2024: $8 \cdot 365 + 2 = 2922$ days. The quantity to find is the distance $s$ from the Sun.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. When the speed is assumed constant, the probe moves uniformly and its position follows the linear model. The result is a model value, not a measurement: the Sun's gravity slows the probe slightly, by only about $0.1\,\text{km/s}$ over 2018–2026, which is negligible here.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. $$s = s_0 + v t$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The time is already in days, $t = 2922\,\text{days}$, so the speed is converted to AU/day. In place of the units we substitute $1\,\text{km} = \tfrac{1}{149\,597\,870.7}\,\text{AU}$ and $1\,\text{s} = \tfrac{1}{86\,400}\,\text{day}$:

      $v$$= 15.341\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}$
      $= 15.341 \cdot \tfrac{1}{149\,597\,870.7}\,\text{AU} : \left(\tfrac{1}{86\,400}\,\text{day}\right)$
      $= 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} s &= s_0 + v t \\ &= 119.0\,\text{AU} + 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}} \cdot 2922\,\text{days} \\ &= 119.0\,\text{AU} + 25.89\,\text{AU} \\ &= 144.89\,\text{AU} \end{aligned}$$$$\begin{aligned} s &= s_0 + v t \\ &= 119.0\,\text{AU} \\ &\quad + 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}} \cdot 2922\,\text{days} \\ &= 119.0\,\text{AU} + 25.89\,\text{AU} \\ &= 144.89\,\text{AU} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. According to the model, the probe is $s \approx 144.9\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun, about $2.17 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{km}$. The initial distance is given to the nearest tenth, so the result is rounded to the nearest tenth.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. A speed of $15\,\text{km/s}$ covers in a year about

      $15\,\text{km/s} \cdot 3.16 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$
      $\approx 4.7 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{km}$
      $\approx 3.2\,\text{AU}$

      so in 8 years the probe advances about $26\,\text{AU}$ and $119.0\,\text{AU} + 26\,\text{AU} \approx 145\,\text{AU}$. For 15 February 2026 the same model gives a distance from the Sun of $142.6\,\text{AU}$. The NASA/JPL Voyager mission status page gave the probe's distance from the Earth in February 2026 as $143.05\,\text{AU}$. The Earth orbits the Sun, so the probe's distance from the Earth differs from its distance from the Sun by up to about $1\,\text{AU}$ over the year. The difference of $0.5\,\text{AU}$ is smaller than this: the model and the observation agree to within about $1\,\text{AU}$, and this comparison cannot test the model more precisely. That is less than one percent of the distance, so the constant-speed model suits predictions over a few years.

      Example 2: Find the error

      A student solves a problem: a trolley starts at $s_0 = 5.0\,\text{m}$ and moves at $3.0\,\text{m/s}$.Where is it at $t = 6.0\,\text{s}$? The student calculates: “At $2.0\,\text{s}$ the position is $5.0\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.0\,\text{s} = 11\,\text{m}$. The time $6.0\,\text{s}$ is three times as much, so the position is $3 \cdot 11\,\text{m} = 33\,\text{m}$.” Where is the error?

      • Correct. Only the distance $\Delta s = vt$ triples ($6.0\,\text{m}$ becomes $18\,\text{m}$), and the initial position is added once: $5.0\,\text{m} + 18\,\text{m} = 23\,\text{m}$. The error could have been noticed by reasoning: the trolley cannot be at $33\,\text{m}$, because it starts at $5.0\,\text{m}$ and in $6.0\,\text{s}$ it travels only $18\,\text{m}$.

      • No conversion is needed: the speed is already in m/s and the time in seconds, so the units are compatible. The error is in the reasoning, where the position is multiplied by three.

      • The speed does not change, because the motion is uniform: it is $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ all the time. The time triples, and so the distance triples, but the initial position does not.

      • When the initial position is not zero, the distance is proportional to time, but the position is not. Check by substituting $t = 6.0\,\text{s}$ directly: $5.0\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{s} = 23\,\text{m}$, not $33\,\text{m}$.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      At time $0\,\text{s}$ a cyclist is already $120\,\text{m}$ from the starting line and rides away from it uniformly at $21.6\,\text{km/h}$.

      How far from the starting line is the cyclist $45\,\text{s}$ later? Give the answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the initial position, and what is the speed? Are the units compatible?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Use the formula $s = s_0 + vt$. First convert the speed to m/s by substituting $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $v$$= 21.6\,\text{km/h}$
      $= \dfrac{21.6\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
      $= 6.0\,\text{m/s}$

      and $s = 120\,\text{m} + 6.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 45\,\text{s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      initial position $s_0 = 120\,\text{m}$, speed $v = 21.6\,\text{km/h}$, time $t = 45\,\text{s}$

      Find

      position $s$ from the starting line in metres

      Principle

      The speed of the cyclist is constant, so the motion is uniform, and the position follows the linear model. The initial position is not zero, so it must be added to the distance travelled.

      Formula

      $s = s_0 + vt$

      Conversions

      Substitute $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$:

      $v$$= 21.6\,\text{km/h}$
      $= \dfrac{21.6\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
      $= 6.0\,\text{m/s}$

      The time and the initial position are already in SI units.

      Substitution

      $s$$= 120\,\text{m} + 6.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 45\,\text{s}$
      $= 120\,\text{m} + 270\,\text{m}$
      $= 390\,\text{m}$

      Answer

      $390\,\text{m}$

      Two significant figures as in the time $45\,\text{s}$.

      Reasonableness

      The speed $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ suits cycling, and $6 \cdot 45 = 270$ matches the distance travelled. The initial position $120\,\text{m}$ is added to this, and the position is greater than the initial position, as it should be when the cyclist moves away.

        A cyclist rides uniformly at $5.5\,\text{m/s}$. How far does the cyclist travel in $20\,\text{s}$?

      2. Exercise 5-2

        A runner covers $360\,\text{m}$ in $45\,\text{s}$ at a steady pace. What is the runner's speed?

        m/s
        Answer

        $8.0\,\text{m/s}$

        Given

        distance $\Delta s = 360\,\text{m}$, time $t = 45\,\text{s}$; the motion is uniform

        speed $v$ in m/s

        In uniform motion, the speed is the distance travelled divided by the time it took.

        Conversions

        None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.

      3. Exercise 5-3Core

        The graph shows the position of a walker as a function of time. The walker keeps moving uniformly in the same direction also beyond the graph.

        How many seconds pass from the start until the walker has travelled $150\,\text{m}$?

        s
        Answer

        $60\,\text{s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        a graph in which the position $s$ of a walker as a function of time $t$ is a straight line from the origin; the motion continues uniformly beyond the graph; distance $s = 150\,\text{m}$

        Find

        time $t$ in seconds when the distance is $150\,\text{m}$

        Principle

        A straight line from the origin means uniform motion, and the slope of the line is the speed. When the speed is known, the time beyond the graph is obtained by dividing the distance by the speed.

        Reading the graph

        The line passes through the grid crossing where $t_1 = 0\,\text{s}$ and $s_1 = 0\,\text{m}$ (the origin), and through the crossing where $t_2 = 8\,\text{s}$ and $s_2 = 20\,\text{m}$. Other crossings are for example $(4\,\text{s}$; $10\,\text{m})$ and $(10\,\text{s}$; $25\,\text{m})$. The reading is exact from the grid, so an answer that deviates by at most $3\,\%$ is accepted.

        Formula

        $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$ and $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$

        Conversions

        None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.

        Substitution

        $v$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{8\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
        $= 2.5\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
        $t$$= \dfrac{150\,\text{m}}{2.5\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}$
        $= 60\,\text{s}$

        Answer

        $60\,\text{s}$

        $s_0 = 119.0\,\text{AU}$ at time $t = 0$ (5 November 2018), $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$, $s = 150\,\text{AU}$, $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$, $1\,\text{day} = 86\,400\,\text{s}$

        Find

        time $t$ in days when the probe is at a distance of $150\,\text{AU}$

        Principle

        The speed is assumed constant, so the uniform motion model $s = s_0 + vt$ applies. The result is a model value, because the Sun's gravity slows the probe slightly and the real time is therefore a little longer.

        Formula

        $s = s_0 + vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s - s_0}{v}$

        Conversions

        The speed in astronomical units per day by substituting $1\,\text{km} = \tfrac{1}{149\,597\,870.7}\,\text{AU}$ and $1\,\text{s} = \tfrac{1}{86\,400}\,\text{day}$:

        $v$$= 15.341\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}$
        $= \dfrac{15.341 \cdot \tfrac{1}{149\,597\,870.7}\,\text{AU}}{\tfrac{1}{86\,400}\,\text{day}}$
        $= 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}}$

        Substitution

        $t$$= \dfrac{150\,\text{AU} - 119.0\,\text{AU}}{0.0088602\,\text{AU/day}}$
        $= \dfrac{31.0\,\text{AU}}{0.0088602\,\text{AU/day}}$
        $= 3498.8\,\text{days}$

        Answer

        $3.50 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{days}$

        About $3500\,\text{days}$, three significant figures: $150\,\text{AU}$ is an exact limit, and the difference $31.0\,\text{AU}$ is accurate to three figures.

        Reasonableness

        The rate is about $3.2\,\text{AU}$ per year, so $31.0\,\text{AU}$ takes about $31.0 : 3.2 \approx 9.7$ years. The result $3500\,\text{days} \approx 9.6$ years fits this and falls in June 2028. The model and the observation agree to within about $1\,\text{AU}$ (see the worked example), and $1\,\text{AU}$ corresponds to about $110\,\text{days}$ in the date, so the date is a rough one.

      2. Exercise 5-18

        Assume that Voyager 2 is at a distance of $150\,\text{AU}$ from the Earth. A radio signal travels in vacuum uniformly at $299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}$.

        How many hours pass from the moment a command leaves the Earth to the moment the reply of the probe reaches the Earth? The motion of the probe and its processing time for the reply are ignored. Use the value $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$. Justify why the motion can be ignored.

        h
        Answer

        $41.6\,\text{h}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        distance $d = 150\,\text{AU}$, speed of the signal $c = 299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$

        Find

        round-trip travel time $t$ in hours

        Principle

        A radio signal travels in vacuum at constant speed, so the distance it travels is $s = ct$. The command and the reply each travel the distance $d$, so the total distance is $2d$. During this time the probe moves about $15\,\text{km/s} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{km} \approx 0.015\,\text{AU}$, which is only $0.01\,\%$ of the distance $150\,\text{AU}$. In the same time the Earth moves about $30\,\text{km/s} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} \approx 0.03\,\text{AU}$, only about $0.02\,\%$, so neither motion needs to be taken into account.

        Formula

        $2d = ct$, so $t = \dfrac{2d}{c}$

        Conversions

        $d$$= 150 \cdot 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$
        $= 2.2440 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{km}$
        $= 2.2440 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}$

        Finally $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

        Substitution

        $t$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 2.2440 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}}{299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}}$
        $= 1.497 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s}$
        $= \dfrac{1.497 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s}}{3600\,\text{s/h}}$
        $= 41.58\,\text{h}$

        Answer

        $41.6\,\text{h}$

        The given values are exact (the assumed distance $150\,\text{AU}$, and the speed of light and $1\,\text{AU}$ by definition), so the precision is chosen: three significant figures are enough.

        Reasonableness

        Light travels one AU in about $499\,\text{s} \approx 8.3\,\text{min}$, so $150\,\text{AU}$ takes about

        $150 \cdot 8.3\,\text{min}$$\approx 1250\,\text{min}$
        $\approx 20.8\,\text{h}$

        and there and back $41.6\,\text{h}$. A delay of more than a day is reasonable, because the probe is tens of billions of kilometres away.

      3. Exercise 5-19

        Two friends set off from the school door along the same straight street in the same direction. The first walks uniformly at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$. The second leaves the door $40\,\text{s}$ later on a bicycle and rides uniformly at $4.5\,\text{m/s}$.

        How far from the door does the cyclist catch up with the walker?

        m
        Answer

        $90\,\text{m}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        speed of the walker $v_1 = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$, speed of the cyclist $v_2 = 4.5\,\text{m/s}$, delay of the cyclist $t_0 = 40\,\text{s}$; both start from the door, so $s_0 = 0$

        Find

        distance from the door $s$ at the point where the cyclist catches up with the walker

        Principle

        Both move uniformly. They are at the same position when their distances from the door are equal. The time $t$ is measured from the start of the walker, so the cyclist has been moving for the time $t - t_0$.

        Formula

        $s_1 = v_1 t$ and $s_2 = v_2 (t - t_0)$. From the condition $s_1 = s_2$ we get $v_1 t = v_2 (t - t_0)$, so $t = \dfrac{v_2 t_0}{v_2 - v_1}$ and $s = v_1 t$.

        Conversions

        None needed: the speeds are in m/s and the delay in seconds.

        Substitution

        $t$$= \dfrac{4.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 40\,\text{s}}{4.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}$
        $= 60\,\text{s}$
        $s$$= 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
        $= 90\,\text{m}$

        Answer

        $90\,\text{m}$

        Two significant figures as in the speeds.

        Reasonableness

        The cyclist rides for $60\,\text{s} - 40\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{s}$, and $4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 90\,\text{m}$, the same result. Another check: the walker's lead after $40\,\text{s}$ is $1.5 \cdot 40 = 60\,\text{m}$, and the cyclist closes it at the speed difference $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ in $60 : 3.0 = 20\,\text{s}$.

      4. Exercise 5-20

        The position $s$ of a cyclist from the start of the track was measured by video analysis as a function of time $t$. Draw a graph of the points.

        The cyclist's speed changes partway, so the motion cannot be modelled with a single straight line. Find in the table the moment when the speed changes, and model the motion as two uniform parts.

        Use the model to calculate the cyclist's average speed in the interval $5.0\text{–}35\,\text{s}$, where the average speed is the change in position divided by the length of the interval. The moment $35\,\text{s}$ is not in the table. Why is the average speed not the mean of the speeds of the parts?

        Position of the cyclist as a function of time
        $t\,(\text{s})$$s\,(\text{m})$
        0.00.0
        5.015.0
        10.030.0
        15.035.0
        20.040.0
        30.050.0
        40.060.0
        m/s
        Answer

        $1.3\,\text{m/s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        measurement points of the table $(t$; $s)$: $(0\,\text{s}$; $0\,\text{m})$, $(5.0\,\text{s}$; $15\,\text{m})$, $(10\,\text{s}$; $30\,\text{m})$, $(15\,\text{s}$; $35\,\text{m})$, $(20\,\text{s}$; $40\,\text{m})$, $(30\,\text{s}$; $50\,\text{m})$ and $(40\,\text{s}$; $60\,\text{m})$

        Find

        average speed $v_\text{avg}$ in the interval $5.0\text{–}35\,\text{s}$ and the reason why it is not the mean of the speeds of the parts

        Principle

        In the graph, the points lie on two straight parts with different slopes, so a single line does not fit the whole motion. The motion is modelled piecewise: each part has its own uniform speed, and the position at the moment $35\,\text{s}$ is calculated from the model of the latter part. The average speed is the change in position divided by the length of the interval.

        Reading the graph

        The points in the interval $0\text{–}10\,\text{s}$ are on the same steep line. From the moment $10\,\text{s}$ on, the line is gentler, and the points $(10\,\text{s}$; $30\,\text{m})$, $(20\,\text{s}$; $40\,\text{m})$ and $(40\,\text{s}$; $60\,\text{m})$ are on the same gentle line. So the speed changes at the moment $t = 10\,\text{s}$.

        Formula

        speed of a part $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$, position on the gentle part $s = s_{10} + v_2 (t - 10\,\text{s})$ and average speed

        $v_\text{avg}$$= \dfrac{s(35\,\text{s}) - s(5.0\,\text{s})}{35\,\text{s} - 5.0\,\text{s}}$

        Conversions

        None needed: the positions are in metres and the times in seconds.

        Substitution

        Part 1:

        $v_1$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{10\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
        $= 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

        Part 2:

        $v_2$$= \dfrac{60\,\text{m} - 30\,\text{m}}{40\,\text{s} - 10\,\text{s}}$
        $= 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

        Position at $35\,\text{s}$:

        $s$$= 30\,\text{m} + 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 25\,\text{s}$
        $= 55\,\text{m}$

        Average speed:

        $v_\text{avg}$$= \dfrac{55\,\text{m} - 15\,\text{m}}{35\,\text{s} - 5.0\,\text{s}}$
        $= \dfrac{40\,\text{m}}{30\,\text{s}}$
        $= 1.33\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

        Answer

        $1.3\,\text{m/s}$

        Two significant figures as in the data. The mean of the speeds of the parts would be $(3.0 + 1.0)\,\text{m/s} : 2 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, but that would be wrong, because the parts last for different times: the speed $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ is in effect for only $5.0\,\text{s}$ and the speed $1.0\,\text{m/s}$ for as long as $25\,\text{s}$.

        Reasonableness

        The partial distances are $3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 15\,\text{m}$ and $1.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 25\,\text{s} = 25\,\text{m}$, together $40\,\text{m}$, the same as above. The average speed $1.3\,\text{m/s}$ is closer to the slower speed $1.0\,\text{m/s}$ than to the faster speed $3.0\,\text{m/s}$, as it should be, because most of the time is spent riding slowly.

      5. Exercise 5-21

        A fast bullet is filmed with a camera that takes a picture every $0.010\,\text{s}$. The position $s$ of the bullet from the starting point, measured from the pictures, is in the table. The speed of the bullet is constant during this time. Calculate the speed of the bullet.

        A $(t, s)$ graph is drawn in which $1.0\,\text{s}$ on the horizontal axis is $10\,\text{cm}$ and $1.0\,\text{m}$ on the vertical axis is $1.0\,\text{cm}$.

        How many centimetres does the line rise during one imaging interval, and how long is the imaging interval on the horizontal axis? Use these to justify why the line looks almost vertical but cannot be exactly vertical.

        Position of the bullet as a function of time
        $t\,(\text{s})$$s\,(\text{m})$
        0.0002.0
        0.0105.0
        0.0208.0
        0.03011.0
        m/s
        Answer

        $300\,\text{m/s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        imaging interval $\Delta t = 0.010\,\text{s}$, positions of the bullet $s = 2.0\,\text{m}$, $5.0\,\text{m}$, $8.0\,\text{m}$ and $11.0\,\text{m}$ at the moments $0.000\,\text{s}$, $0.010\,\text{s}$, $0.020\,\text{s}$ and $0.030\,\text{s}$; scale of the drawing: $1.0\,\text{s}$ is $10\,\text{cm}$ and $1.0\,\text{m}$ is $1.0\,\text{cm}$

        speed $v$ of the bullet in m/s, and the rise of the graph and the length of the imaging interval in centimetres

        The position grows by the same amount in every imaging interval ($3.0\,\text{m}$), so the motion is uniform and the speed is the slope of the graph, $\Delta s : \Delta t$. An object has only one position at every moment, so every value of time corresponds to one value of position. A vertical line would have $\Delta t = 0$ and $\Delta s \neq 0$, and then the quotient would not be any number.

        Reading the graph

        The points of the table lie on the same straight line, and between two consecutive points the position changes by

        $\Delta s$$= 5.0\,\text{m} - 2.0\,\text{m}$
        $= 3.0\,\text{m}$

        in the time $\Delta t = 0.010\,\text{s}$.

        Formula

        $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$; in the drawing, the length is the value times the scale

        Conversions

        Not needed for calculating the speed. In the drawing,

        $\Delta s$$= 3.0 \cdot 1.0\,\text{cm}$
        $= 3.0\,\text{cm}$
        $\Delta t$$= 0.010 \cdot 10\,\text{cm}$
        $= 0.10\,\text{cm}$

        Substitution

        $v$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{m}}{0.010\,\text{s}}$
        $= 300\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

        In one imaging interval the line rises $3.0\,\text{cm}$ and advances horizontally only $0.10\,\text{cm}$, that is, $1.0\,\text{mm}$.

        Answer

        $300\,\text{m/s}$

        Two significant figures as in the difference of positions $3.0\,\text{m}$. The line rises $3.0\,\text{cm}$ and advances $0.10\,\text{cm}$, so it rises about $30$ times as much as it advances horizontally and looks almost vertical. It is still not vertical: the imaging interval always takes a time $\Delta t > 0$, and the bullet is at exactly one position at every moment. A vertical line would require the bullet to be at two positions at the same moment, that is, the speed would be infinite.

        Reasonableness

        The speed $300\,\text{m/s}$ is of the same order of magnitude as the speed of sound $343\,\text{m/s}$ and suits a fast bullet. Check: $300\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.010\,\text{s} = 3.0\,\text{m}$, the same as in the table. A stopped object would be drawn as a horizontal line, because $\Delta s = 0$.

      2. Concept task

      An object moves uniformly, and its $(t, s)$ graph is a straight line that does not pass through the origin. Which statement is correct?

      • The mark of uniform motion is a straight line, not that the line passes through the origin. A line that does not pass through the origin describes uniform motion that starts from $s_0$: $s = s_0 + vt$.

      • Correct. The line is equally steep everywhere, so $\Delta s = vt$ and the ratio $\Delta s : t = v$ is constant. When the initial position $s_0$ is subtracted from the position $s$, the remaining distance is proportional to time.

      • The graph of a directly proportional quantity passes through the origin, because the ratio $s : t$ is constant. Here the line starts from $s_0 \neq 0$, so the ratio $s : t$ changes with time.

      4. Calculation task: Reading a graph

      The line of a $(t, s)$ graph of uniform motion passes through the points $(1.0\,\text{s}$; $7.0\,\text{m})$ and $(5.0\,\text{s}$; $19\,\text{m})$.

      What is the initial position $s_0$ of the motion? Give the answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      The initial position is the position at time $t = 0$, that is, the point where the line crosses the $s$-axis. What is the slope of the line?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The slope is $v = \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$. You get the initial position by going back in time from the point $(t_1, s_1)$ to the start: $s_0 = s_1 - v t_1$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $v$$= \dfrac{19\,\text{m} - 7.0\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s} - 1.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= 3.0\,\text{m/s}$

      and $s_0 = 7.0\,\text{m} - 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      points $(t_1, s_1) = (1.0\,\text{s}$; $7.0\,\text{m})$ and $(t_2, s_2) = (5.0\,\text{s}$; $19\,\text{m})$ on the same line

      Find

      initial position $s_0$, that is, the position at time $t = 0$, in metres

      Principle

      The motion is uniform, so the line of the $(t, s)$ graph follows the equation $s = s_0 + vt$. The slope of the line is the speed, and the point where the line crosses the $s$-axis is the initial position.

      Reading the graph

      The two given points are enough. The change in position between them is divided by the time interval, which gives the slope. Then we go back from the first point to the moment $t = 0$: in one second the position decreases by $v \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$.

      Formula

      $v = \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$ and $s_1 = s_0 + v t_1$, so $s_0 = s_1 - v t_1$

      Conversions

      None needed: the positions are in metres and the times in seconds.

      Substitution

      $v$$= \dfrac{19\,\text{m} - 7.0\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s} - 1.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= 3.0\,\text{m/s}$
      $s_0$$= 7.0\,\text{m} - 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{m}$

      Answer

      $4.0\,\text{m}$

      Two significant figures as in the positions $7.0\,\text{m}$ and $19\,\text{m}$.

      Reasonableness

      Check with the second point:

      $s$$= 4.0\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 19\,\text{m}$

      which is the given value. The initial position is smaller than $7.0\,\text{m}$, as it should be, because the object moves forwards.
