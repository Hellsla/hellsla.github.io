## fy3-16-L1-01
**Given:** stones $m = 20\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; given in the problem $c = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the stones, in megajoules
**Principle:** The stones warm up but stay solid, so their state does not change and no latent heat is needed. Then the quantity of heat absorbed is proportional to the mass and to the change in temperature, and the constant of proportionality is the specific heat capacity $c$.
**Formula:** $Q = c\,m\,\Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins: $\Delta T = 300\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 280\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 20\,\text{kg} \cdot 280\,\text{K} = 4\,480\,000\,\text{J} = 4.48\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $4.5\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures, because the mass $20\,\text{kg}$ is known to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $800 \cdot 20 \cdot 300\,\text{J} \approx 4.8\,\text{MJ}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. $Q$ is positive because the stones warm up. Heating the same mass of water by the same amount would take about five times the energy, because the $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ of water is about five times larger.

## fy3-16-L1-02
**Given:** water $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, already at the boiling point $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** quantity of heat $Q$ needed for vaporisation, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The water is already at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and is vaporising, so its temperature does not change: all the energy goes into the change of state and none into raising the temperature. So we use the specific latent heat of vaporisation $r$, not $c\,\Delta T$.
**Formula:** $Q = r\,m$
**Conversions:** Not needed: $r$ is in kilojoules per kilogram and the mass is in kilograms, so the product comes directly in kilojoules.
**Substitution:** $Q = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.20\,\text{kg} = 452\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $450\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.20\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Vaporising a kilogram takes over two megajoules, so a fifth of a kilogram takes about $0.2 \cdot 2300\,\text{kJ} \approx 460\,\text{kJ}$. Heating the same amount of water from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ would take only $4190 \cdot 0.20 \cdot 80\,\text{J} \approx 67\,\text{kJ}$, so vaporisation takes about seven times as much.

**Substitution:** $P = 1.6\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 28\,\text{K} = 26.88\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $27\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the values $1.6$ and $0.60$ and the difference $28\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $1.6 \cdot 0.6 \approx 1\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{K}}$, and $28\,\text{K}$ gives about $28\,\text{W}$. The power is positive because heat comes in from outside. **Conversions:** The change in temperature is already in kelvins. Units: $\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{K} = \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = \text{W}$, and finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = 3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot 8.0\,\text{K} = 14\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = 14\,000\,\text{W} = 14\,\text{kW}$
**Answer:** $14\,\text{kW}$, two significant figures like the values $0.50\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$ and $8.0\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** Every second, $14\,\text{kJ}$ is transferred to the liquid. Estimate: $3500 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 8 = 14\,000$. The power carried by the coolant of a passenger car is typically tens of kilowatts, so the value is of the right order of magnitude.

**Answer:** $650\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.25\,\text{kg}$.

The warming of the steam and the heating of the stove are not taken into account.

**Answer:** $26\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the masses $0.20\,\text{kg}$ and $25\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Vaporisation takes $452\,\text{kJ}$, which is $87\,\%$ of the energy, and warming alone would lower the temperature of the stones by only about $3.4\,\text{K}$. ## fy3-16-L3-02

**Find:** final temperature of the mixture $T$ in degrees Celsius
**Principle:** Energy is conserved, because the container is insulated: the energy given out by the water melts the ice and warms the melted water to the final temperature. This works only if all the ice melts. First check: as it cools to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the water can give out at most $c\,m_\text{v}\,T_\text{v} = 251\,400\,\text{J}$, and melting the ice needs only $s\,m_\text{ice} = 167\,000\,\text{J}$. Since $251\,400\,\text{J} > 167\,000\,\text{J}$, all the ice melts and the final temperature is above $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Formula:** $c\,m_\text{v}(T_\text{v} - T) = s\,m_\text{ice} + c\,m_\text{ice}\,T$, so $T = \dfrac{c\,m_\text{v}\,T_\text{v} - s\,m_\text{ice}}{c\,(m_\text{v} + m_\text{ice})}$
**Conversions:** The ice is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the change in temperature of the melted water is $T - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = T$ and the water cools by $T_\text{v} - T$; the differences are the same in kelvins, and $T$ comes directly in degrees Celsius. $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$.

$$T = \dfrac{84\,400\,\text{J}}{10\,475\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}} = 8.057\,{}^\circ\text{C}$$

**Answer:** $8.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures. The initial values $0.500\,\text{kg}$, $2.00\,\text{kg}$ and $30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ have three figures, but the subtraction in the numerator $251\,400 - 167\,000 = 84\,400\,\text{J}$ loses one figure, so two figures are left in the result.
**Reasonableness:** The result is between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and closer to the temperature of the ice, because melting also cools the water. Energy balance: the water gives out $4190 \cdot 2.00 \cdot (30.0 - 8.057)\,\text{J} = 183\,880\,\text{J}$, and the ice takes in $167\,000\,\text{J} + 4190 \cdot 0.500 \cdot 8.057\,\text{J} = 183\,880\,\text{J}$. ## fy3-16-L1-06
**Find:** why a metal handle feels colder than a wooden handle even though both are at room temperature.
**Principle:** Objects that have been in a room for a long time are in thermal equilibrium with their surroundings, so the handles are at the same temperature. What decides the feel is how fast energy moves from the warm hand into the handle: because of the temperature difference there is a net transfer of energy from the hand to the handle, and the rate of the transfer depends on the thermal conductivity of the material.
**Model answer:** Metal conducts heat well, so a lot of energy moves quickly from the skin of the hand into the metal and the temperature of the skin drops quickly. Wood conducts heat poorly, so energy moves more slowly and the surface of the skin stays warmer. So what we sense is the temperature of the skin and the rate at which energy is transferred, not the temperature of the handle. Cold does not flow from the handle into the hand, and the internal energy of the handle does not decide the matter: both are at the temperature of the room.
