Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker

Why does a loudspeaker cone vibrate and the blades of a desk fan turn, when nothing visible pushes either of them?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field and deduce its direction.
  • explain why parallel current-carrying wires attract or repel each other, and calculate the force between them.
  • explain how a direct-current motor and a loudspeaker work.

Prior knowledge

  • A magnetic field exerts on a moving charged particle a force perpendicular to the velocity and the fieldchapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields
  • The magnetic field of a straight current-carrying wire circles the wire, and its magnitude is $B = \mu_0 I : (2\pi r)$chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil

1Think about it

When a loudspeaker plays a bass note, you can see its cone moving back and forth. The blades of a desk fan start to turn as soon as the plug is pushed into the wall. Nobody touches either of them, and neither has any burning fuel. Both, however, contain a magnet and a wire carrying an electric current.

How can a magnet make a wire move, if the wire is copper, which a magnet does not attract?

Assumptions of the model

The field is uniform between the poles and zero outside them. Only the horizontal lower part of the wire is in the field; the forces on the vertical lead wires are horizontal and do not change the reading of the balance. The wire is rigidly fixed to the stand, so it does not move. The magnitudes of the fields of the magnets are not shown: they are determined from the measurements. The readings are values calculated by the model to a precision of $0.01\,\text{g}$, not measurements, and they contain no measurement error.

How the calculation works

The field exerts on the wire a vertical force $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. By Newton's third law the wire exerts on the magnet an equal and opposite force, so the balance shows the reading

$$m = m_0 + \frac{F}{g}$$

where $m_0 = 245.30\,\text{g}$ is the reading without a current and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. From the change in the reading $\Delta m = m - m_0$ we obtain the force $F = \Delta m \cdot g$ and from it the magnetic flux density $B = F : (I l)$, when the wire is perpendicular to the field.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A magnetic field pushes a current-carrying wire

    In the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields, a magnetic field exerted on a moving charged particle a force perpendicular to both the velocity of the particle and the field. An electric current in a wire is a motion of charges: the conduction electrons of the metal move along the wire. The magnetic field exerts a force on every moving electron, and the electrons pass it on to the metal of the wire, so the force acts on the whole wire. When a light copper wire is hung between the poles of a horseshoe magnet and a current is switched on, the wire swings sideways. Copper is not ferromagnetic, and the magnet does not attract it: the force appears only when there is a current in the wire.

    The direction of the force

    The force is always perpendicular to both the direction of the current and the magnetic field. Its direction is given by the right-hand rule: when the thumb of an outstretched right hand points in the direction of the current and the other fingers in the direction of the magnetic field, the force points out of the palm. It is the same rule as for a positively charged particle, because the direction of the current is the direction in which positive charge moves. When the direction of the current or of the field is reversed, the direction of the force reverses. If the wire is parallel to the field, there is no force at all.

    The electric motor

    In a direct-current motor, a current loop that can turn about an axle sits between the poles of a magnet. In the two sides of the loop that cross the field, the current flows in opposite directions, so the forces on them are in opposite directions. The forces are not on the same line, so they have a turning effect: they turn the loop. When the loop has turned to be perpendicular to the field, the forces point away from the axle and no longer turn it, and the loop keeps moving because of its inertia. Exactly then the commutator, a split ring with the carbon brushes that touch it, reverses the direction of the current in the loop. In this way the forces turn the loop in the same direction every half turn. A real motor has many coils and an iron core, so it turns smoothly and strongly.

    The loudspeaker

    The magnet of a loudspeaker is built so that in the ring-shaped gap the field points radially outwards. In the gap is a light voice coil, and every part of it is perpendicular to the field, so an equal force acts on every part in the direction of the axis of the coil. A radial field is essential: in a uniform field the forces on opposite sides of a closed coil would be in opposite directions and would cancel, so nothing would push the coil. The coil is attached to the cone. The alternating current from the amplifier changes its direction in step with the sound signal, so the force pushes the cone alternately forwards and backwards. The cone vibrates at the frequency of the signal, compresses and rarefies the air and produces a sound wave. The larger the current, the larger the force and the louder the sound. Most of the electrical energy delivered to the loudspeaker is, however, transformed into internal energy of the coil and the magnet.

    Force on a current-carrying wire

    When a current $I$ flows in a straight wire and the wire is in a uniform magnetic field, the field exerts on the wire a force of magnitude

    $$F = I l B \sin\alpha$$

    where $F$ is the force (N), $I$ the current (A), $l$ the length of the part of the wire that is in the field (m), $B$ the magnetic flux density (T) and $\alpha$ the angle between the wire and the field. Only the component of the field perpendicular to the wire, $B \sin\alpha$, produces a force. The force is greatest when the wire is perpendicular to the field ($\sin 90^\circ = 1$) and zero when the wire is parallel to the field ($\sin 0^\circ = 0$). At an angle of $30^\circ$ the force is half of its greatest value, because $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$. The formula gives the unit of the tesla: $1\,\text{T} = 1\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$.

    The force between two current-carrying wires

    There is a force between two parallel current-carrying wires, because each is in the magnetic field of the other. In the chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil, the field of wire $1$ circles around it, and at a distance $r$ its magnitude is $B_1$. At wire $2$ the field is perpendicular to wire $2$, so a force $F$ acts on the part of wire $2$ whose length is $l$:

    $$\begin{aligned} &B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r} \\[6pt] &F = I_2 l B_1 \\ &\quad = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2 l}{2\pi r} \end{aligned}$$

    where $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the currents of the wires (A), $r$ is the distance between the wires (m), $l$ the length of the part that runs parallel (m) and $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$ the permeability of free space. By Newton's third law, an equal and opposite force acts on wire $1$. The right-hand rule shows that currents in the same direction attract each other and currents in opposite directions repel each other. Until 2019 the ampere was defined with this force: the current is $1\,\text{A}$ when the force between wires $1\,\text{m}$ apart is $2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{N}$ for every metre of wire.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a magnet attracts a wire and that the wire moves along the field lines towards the other pole. The force is, however, perpendicular to the field, and a copper or aluminium wire experiences a force only when it carries a current. When the direction of the current is reversed, the wire moves in the opposite direction, although the magnet is the same. Another common mistake is to conclude from the charges that currents in the same direction repel each other. The force between wires is magnetic, and currents in the same direction attract each other. A third mistake is to use the cosine in the formula: the angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the wire and the field, and the force is zero when the wire is parallel to the field.

    Summary

    • A magnetic field exerts on a current-carrying wire a force $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the wire and the field. The force is greatest when the wire is perpendicular to the field and zero when the wire is parallel to the field.
    • The force is perpendicular to both the current and the field; its direction is given by the right-hand rule, and it reverses when the direction of the current or of the field reverses.
    • Currents in the same direction attract each other, and currents in opposite directions repel each other. The force is $F = I_2 l B_1$, where $B_1 = \mu_0 I_1 : (2\pi r)$.
    • In a direct-current motor the forces turn a current loop, and the commutator reverses the current every half turn.
    • In a loudspeaker an alternating current in a radial field pushes the voice coil and the cone back and forth in step with the signal.

      Example 1: A loudspeaker's voice coil

      A loudspeaker voice coil has $60$ turns of thin wire, and the radius of the coil is $1.3\,\text{cm}$. The coil is in the gap of a magnet, where the magnetic flux density of the radial field is $0.95\,\text{T}$, and the field is perpendicular to the wire everywhere. How large is the force on the coil when the current is at its peak value of $1.5\,\text{A}$?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Number of turns $N = 60$, radius of the coil $r = 1.3\,\text{cm}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.95\,\text{T}$, current $I = 1.5\,\text{A}$ and angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 90^\circ$. We are asked for the force $F$ on the coil.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The field is radial, so every part of the wire of the coil is perpendicular to the field, and the force on every part points in the same direction, along the axis of the coil. The forces therefore add up, and in the formula $F = I l B \sin\alpha$ the length $l$ is the whole length of the wire of the coil: $N$ turns, each with the length of the circumference of a circle, $2\pi r$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} l &= N \cdot 2\pi r \\ F &= I l B \sin 90^\circ = I l B \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The radius in metres:

      $r$$= 1.3\,\text{cm}$
      $= 1.3 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.013\,\text{m}$

      The other quantities are in SI units.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the length of the wire and then the force:

      $$\begin{aligned} l &= N \cdot 2\pi r \\ &= 60 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 0.013\,\text{m} \\ &= 4.9009\,\text{m} \\[6pt] F &= I l B \\ &= 1.5\,\text{A} \cdot 4.9009\,\text{m} \cdot 0.95\,\text{T} \\ &= 6.984\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The force on the coil is $F \approx 7.0\,\text{N}$ along the axis of the coil. The result is given to two significant figures, like the radius $1.3\,\text{cm}$ and the current $1.5\,\text{A}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The units agree:

      $\text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{T}$$= \text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$
      $= \text{N}$

      If the mass of the cone and the coil is about $10\,\text{g}$, their weight is only about $0.1\,\text{N}$, so the force is about $70$ times the weight. This is plausible, because the cone has to accelerate back and forth hundreds of times a second.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      In a uniform magnetic field $B = 0.30\,\text{T}$ there is $20\,\text{cm}$ of straight wire carrying a current of $5.0\,\text{A}$. The angle between the wire and the field is $30^\circ$.A student calculates

      $$\begin{aligned} F &= I l B \cos 30^\circ \\ &= 5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \\ &\quad \cdot 0.866 \\ &\approx 0.26\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • The angle affects the magnitude of the force: a wire parallel to the field experiences no force at all. The force $I l B = 0.30\,\text{N}$ is obtained only when the wire is perpendicular to the field. Here the angle is $30^\circ$, so the force is $0.30\,\text{N} \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 0.15\,\text{N}$.

      • Correct. The angle is between the wire and the field, and the component of the field perpendicular to the wire is $B \sin\alpha$. Therefore $F = 5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot 0.5 = 0.15\,\text{N}$. Check with a limiting case: for a wire parallel to the field ($\alpha = 0^\circ$) the force must be zero, but the cosine would give the greatest force.

      • The length has been correctly converted to metres: $20\,\text{cm} = 20 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.20\,\text{m}$. Since $\text{T} = \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$, the result is in newtons only if the length is in metres. The error is in the function of the angle.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation: jump leads

      A car with a flat battery is started with the help of the battery of another car, using jump leads. When the starter motor turns the engine, a current of $150\,\text{A}$ flows in one lead and an equal current back in the other. The leads run parallel for $2.0\,\text{m}$, with the distance between their centres $1.5\,\text{cm}$.

      How large is the force between the leads? Answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      In what field is the second lead? What is the magnetic field of the first lead at the second lead, and in which direction does it point relative to the lead?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The field of the first lead at the second is $B_1 = \mu_0 I_1 : (2\pi r)$, and it is perpendicular to the second lead, so $F = I_2 l B_1$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $B_1$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 150\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.015\,\text{m}}$
      $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$

      and $F = 150\,\text{A} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} \cdot B_1$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      currents of the leads $I_1 = I_2 = 150\,\text{A}$ in opposite directions, length of the parallel part $l = 2.0\,\text{m}$, distance $r = 1.5\,\text{cm}$, $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

      Find

      force $F$ between the leads in newtons

      Principle

      Each lead is in the magnetic field of the other. The field of the first lead circles around it, so at the second lead it is perpendicular to the lead and equally strong along the whole parallel stretch. A force $F = I_2 l B_1$ therefore acts on the second lead. The currents are in opposite directions, so the leads repel each other.

      Formula

      $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ and $F = I_2 l B_1$

      Conversions

      $r$$= 1.5\,\text{cm}$
      $= 1.5 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.015\,\text{m}$

      Substitution

      $B_1$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 150\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.015\,\text{m}}$
      $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
      $F$$= 150\,\text{A} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
      $= 0.60\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $0.60\,\text{N}$

      Two significant figures like the distance $1.5\,\text{cm}$ and the length $2.0\,\text{m}$. The leads repel each other.

      Reasonableness

      The field at the second lead is $B_1 = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$, about $40$ times the Earth's field but small compared with a magnet. The force corresponds to the weight of an object of about $60\,\text{g}$, so the leads may jerk slightly when the motor starts, but they do not jump.

      1. Exercise 4-17

        A horizontal copper rod of mass $12\,\text{g}$ and length $20\,\text{cm}$ hangs from two light conducting wires. The rod is entirely in a uniform horizontal magnetic field with a magnetic flux density of $0.25\,\text{T}$, and perpendicular to the field.

        A current is led to the rod through the wires.

        How large must the current be for the wires to go slack, that is, for the magnetic force to support the whole weight of the rod?

        A
        Answer

        $2.4\,\text{A}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        mass of the rod $m = 12\,\text{g}$, length in the field $l = 20\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.25\,\text{T}$, rod perpendicular to the horizontal field; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Find

        current $I$ at which the wires go slack

        Principle

        The wires are slack when their tension is zero. Then only the weight, downwards, and the magnetic force act on the rod. The rod is at rest, so by Newton's first law the forces must cancel: the magnetic force must point upwards and equal the weight. The field is horizontal and the rod is perpendicular to it, so the force $I l B$ is vertical; the direction of the current is chosen so that the force points upwards.

        Formula

        $I l B = m g$, so $I = \dfrac{m g}{l B}$

        Conversions

        $m$$= 12\,\text{g}$
        $= 12 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
        $= 0.012\,\text{kg}$
        $l$$= 20\,\text{cm}$
        $= 20 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
        $= 0.20\,\text{m}$

        Substitution

        $I$$= \dfrac{0.012\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.25\,\text{T}}$
        $= \dfrac{0.11772\,\text{N}}{0.050\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}}$
        $= 2.354\,\text{A}$

        Answer

        $2.4\,\text{A}$

        Two significant figures like the data.

        Reasonableness

        A current of a few amperes is enough to support a light rod, which suits a laboratory experiment. If the direction of the current were reversed, the force would point downwards and the wires would tighten: the tension would then be twice the weight.

        A
        Answer

        Given

        length of the upper wire $l = 0.50\,\text{m}$, mass $m = 3.0\,\text{g}$, distance between the wires $r = 1.0\,\text{cm}$, the same current $I$ in both; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

        Principle

        The upper wire floats when the forces on it cancel each other: the weight points down, so the force of the field of the lower wire must point up, that is, the wires must repel each other.         Conversions

        $m = 3.0\,\text{g} = 0.0030\,\text{kg}$ and $r = 1.0\,\text{cm} = 0.010\,\text{m}$

      Optional 4. Explanation task: The loudspeaker

      Explain why the cone of a loudspeaker vibrates at the same frequency as the current from the amplifier. Also explain why the field of the loudspeaker magnet is made radial in the gap.

      Model answer

      The voice coil is in the gap of the magnet, and the field exerts on the wire of the coil a force $F = I l B$ along the axis of the coil. The direction of the force depends on the direction of the current. The alternating current from the amplifier changes direction in step with the signal, so the force pushes the coil, and the cone attached to it, alternately forwards and backwards at the same frequency. The cone compresses and rarefies the air, and a sound wave of the same frequency is produced. In a uniform field the forces on opposite sides of a closed coil would be in opposite directions and would cancel, so the coil would not move. In a radial field every part of the coil is perpendicular to the field, so the greatest possible force acts on every part and all the forces point in the same direction, along the axis. Then the coil moves straight back and forth and does not tilt.
