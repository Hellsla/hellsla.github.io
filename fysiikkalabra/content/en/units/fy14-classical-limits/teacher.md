## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–8 min, Think about it.** Read the hook aloud (Kelvin's "two clouds", 1900). Pairs name three hot, glowing things and their colours. Quick review of the electromagnetic spectrum: the ordering task.
- **8–13 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, committed with a short reason. Do not comment on them yet.
- **13–28 min, Investigate.** Pairs read the black-body curves, find the pattern in the peak table ($\lambda_\text{max} T$ is constant) and predict the blue star's peak. Then the written description of the graph with the word bank.
- **28–40 min, Theory summary.** Key idea, the ultraviolet catastrophe and the formula section together. The reflection: back to the predictions.
- **40–60 min, Apply.** The worked example (filament lamp) step by step; the thermal-camera task. Then the timeline: pairs explore it, order the six steps, and do the one-minute biography talk and the short written text.
- **60–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the explanation task, the summary and the French corner in pairs.

## Plan for fast pairs and for running short of time
- **Fast pairs:** the optional error-spotting task (degrees Celsius in Wien's law); then estimate the peak wavelength of the Sun and of the cosmic background radiation ($2.7\ \text{K}$, peak about $1\ \text{mm}$, microwaves).
- **Short of time:** keep the predictions, the curves, the peak table and the catastrophe. Shorten the timeline to the ordering task in class and give the biography text as homework; the French corner can also be homework.

## Organisation and preparation
- Pairs share one device for the timeline; each student answers the predictions and checks on their own device or on paper.
- If possible, show a filament lamp on a dimmer (dark red at low voltage, yellow-white at full voltage), or look at it through a diffraction grating: the spectrum is continuous and its blue end becomes brighter as the voltage rises. A thermal camera, if the school has one, makes the infrared of skin visible.
- This unit prepares the Fotoni lab visit, where one station measures the spectrum of a filament (thermal radiation and black-body radiation). The next unit, *Light as particles: the photoelectric effect*, starts from Planck's packets $E = hf$.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed with the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade, use the same criteria: the graph description shows both effects of temperature with numbers; the final explanation names what classical physics did well, the catastrophe, the measured curve and Planck's packets, and takes a position.
- Language: correct use of *peak, wavelength, shift, black body, thermal radiation*; comparative forms (*higher, shorter, more … than*); sentence starters used to argue.

## Common difficulties
- "Red hot is hottest": everyday colour meanings (red = danger) conflict with physics. Ask: "Which glows first when a stove ring warms up?"
- Celsius in Wien's law (the error-spotting task).
- Students think Planck discovered photons. Make the difference clear: Planck quantised the exchange of energy in 1900, Einstein proposed light quanta in 1905.
- The ultraviolet catastrophe is a *prediction of a model*, not something that happened. Stress that it shows the model is wrong.
- The curves in the figure and the peak table are model values from Planck's law, which agrees with the measurements; they are not raw data.

## Extension ideas
- Kelvin and the quote "There is nothing new to be discovered in physics now", which is often attributed to him without evidence. Ask students to search for the quote and evaluate the sources: who says Kelvin said it, and what is their evidence?
- The Stefan–Boltzmann law: the total power grows as $T^4$. How much more does a body at $6000\ \text{K}$ radiate than one at $3000\ \text{K}$? ($2^4 = 16$ times.)
- Planck's law itself: plot it in a spreadsheet for two temperatures and find the peaks.
```latex
B_\lambda = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_\text{B} T)} - 1}
```
