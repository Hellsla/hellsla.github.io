## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** Read the hook; one minute of pair talk on the question. The review question recalls why a ring has no open ends.
- **5–10 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, each with a reason. No answers yet: the feedback opens with the theory sections.
- **10–32 min, Investigate.** Pairs explore the two 3D machines: all layers, all five step cards and the short checks inside the component. Then the pair discussion with the word bank and sentence starters, and the table of long plasmas.
- **32–47 min, Theory summary.** Three short parts: the twist and the formula on the board, then the two designs (pairs can split *The tokamak* and *The stellarator* and tell each other), then pulsed and steady operation. The vocabulary match; back to the predictions.
- **47–62 min, Apply.** The worked example (ITER's toroidal field) on the board, step by step; the inner-edge field task individually; the misconception text.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the argument "Which design?" with its sentence starters, the summary and the French corner.

## Organisation and preparation
- One device per student or per pair. The 3D component works best on a laptop or tablet; on a phone, turn it sideways.
- Hands help: let students show *toroidal* (a finger round a ring, the long way) and *poloidal* (round the cross-section) with a ring-shaped object such as a bagel, a rubber ring or a roll of tape. A twisted paper strip glued into a ring shows a helical line.
- Remind students of the transformer from the electromagnetism course: only a *changing* primary current induces a current.
- Records change. The unit gives the state on 1 November 2026 (WEST 1337 s in February 2025); check the CEA, IPP and ITER news pages before the lesson.

## Plan for fast pairs and for running out of time
- **Fast pairs:** the optional error-spotting task; then calculate the field at the outer edge of ITER's plasma ($R = 8.2\ \text{m}$, $4.0\ \text{T}$) and explain why the inner and outer fields differ by a factor of two.
- **Running out of time:** skip the table of long plasmas (the numbers return in the theory), and set the French corner and check 3 as homework. The argument "Which design?" should not be dropped: it answers the chapter's big question.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade: the discussion names the source of the twist in both designs and one advantage and one drawback of each; the argument chooses a design, gives an advantage and a drawback, and separates a measured result (with its year) from a plan.
- Calculation: $B = \mu_0 N I / (2\pi R)$ with $N = 18 \cdot 134$, the current in amperes and the correct radius; the answer has a unit and sensible significant figures.
- Language: *toroidal*, *poloidal*, *coil*, *plasma current*, *pulse*, *steady* used correctly; argument structure (I think …, One advantage …, On the other hand …).

## Common difficulties
- *Toroidal* and *poloidal* are easily mixed up. Toroidal = round the whole ring; poloidal = round the tube.
- "Closed field lines are enough." The ring removes the ends, but the field is stronger on the inside, so the particles drift across the field lines. The twist cancels the effect of the drift.
- "The plasma current is only for heating." Its main job in a tokamak is the poloidal field.
- "Tokamaks can only make short pulses." Inductive drive alone is limited, but non-inductive drive is not.
- In the formula, $N$ is the total number of turns of all coils, and $R$ is measured from the central axis, not from the centre of the plasma tube.

## Extension ideas
- Compare Wendelstein 7-X's 8-minute plasma of 2023 (about $1.3\ \text{GJ}$ of energy) with WEST's 22 minutes: what was the average heating power of each? Why is energy, not only time, a fair measure?
- Look up the spherical tokamak (unit *Pinches, lasers and other paths to fusion*): is it a tokamak or something new?
- Find out what a *heliotron* or a *quasi-symmetric* stellarator is, and which private companies are building stellarators (claims, not results).
