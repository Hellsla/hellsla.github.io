The generator and alternating current

Why does the rotating rotor of a wind turbine produce in the electricity grid a voltage that reverses its direction a hundred times a second?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why a sinusoidal alternating voltage arises in a coil that rotates at a steady rate.
  • calculate the peak value of a generator's voltage, $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$, and the period.
  • calculate the rms values of an alternating voltage and current and use them to find the average power.

Prior knowledge

  • An induced voltage $e = -N\,\Delta\Phi : \Delta t$ arises in a coil only when the magnetic flux through it, $\Phi = BA$, changeschapter Electromagnetic induction
  • A voltage $e = vBl$ arises in a conductor moving in a fieldchapter Electromagnetic induction
  • An induced current opposes the change that produces itchapter Lenz's law and eddy currents

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

On the coast of Ostrobothnia the rotor of a wind turbine turns steadily, about once every five seconds. Hundreds of kilometres away, at home, the voltage of the wall socket reverses its direction a hundred times a second, and all the power plants of the grid are synchronised to the same frequency, $50\,\text{Hz}$. A wind turbine, a hydropower plant and a nuclear power plant produce electricity on the same principle: something rotates in a magnetic field.

How does steady rotation produce a voltage that reverses its direction?

Optional Recall: when does a voltage arise?
A coil is at rest in front of a strong magnet, and a large magnetic flux passes through it without changing.How large is the induced voltage in the coil?

  • By the law of induction $e = -N\,\Delta\Phi : \Delta t$ the voltage depends on how fast the flux changes. A flux that does not change, however large, produces no voltage (chapter Electromagnetic induction).

  • Correct. The induced voltage depends on the rate of change of the flux, not on the size of the flux (chapter Electromagnetic induction). Today the change in flux is produced by rotating the coil.

  • The number of turns tells how many turns the voltage is produced in, but if the flux does not change, the voltage of each turn is zero: $e = -N\,\Delta\Phi : \Delta t = 0$ (chapter Electromagnetic induction).

How the calculation works

The angle $\theta$ is the angle between the normal of the plane of the coil and the field, and at the moment $t = 0$ it is zero.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    A rotating coil in a magnetic field

    In the chapter Electromagnetic induction a voltage was induced in a coil when the magnetic flux through it changed. A generator produces the change in flux by rotation: a coil is turned between the poles of a magnet. When the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field, the greatest number of field lines pass through the coil and the flux is at its greatest, $\Phi = BA$. After a quarter of a turn the plane of the coil is parallel to the field, and not a single field line passes through it: the flux is zero. After the next quarter the field passes through the coil perpendicularly again, but from the other side of the coil, so the flux is $-BA$. In a rotating coil the flux thus varies smoothly and continuously between $BA$ and $-BA$.

    Slip rings and brushes

    The ends of the wire of the coil are connected to two slip rings on the axis. Carbon brushes touch the rings, and the voltage is led through the brushes to an external circuit, such as the lamp in the picture. Unlike the commutator of a direct-current motor in the chapter Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker, slip rings do not reverse the direction of the current, so the external circuit receives the voltage induced in the coil as it is.

    Why the voltage is sinusoidal

    By the law of induction the voltage does not depend on how large the flux is, but on how fast it changes. In the graph of $\Phi(t)$ the rate of change is the steepness of the graph. When the flux is at its greatest, the sides of the coil move along the field lines, the flux hardly changes for a moment and the voltage is zero. When the plane of the coil is parallel to the field, the flux is zero, but the sides move perpendicular to the field lines and the flux changes fastest: the voltage is at its greatest. The flux varies like a cosine, and its steepness varies like a sine. In every half revolution the direction of the change in flux reverses, so the sign of the voltage also reverses. The result is an alternating voltage, and in a closed circuit an alternating current flows, whose direction reverses twice during every revolution.

    The generator of a power plant and the wind turbine

    In the large generators of power plants an electromagnet rotates and the coils stay in place around it. The flux through a stationary coil changes in the same way as in a rotating coil, so a similar alternating voltage arises, and a large current is obtained from the coils without sliding contacts. In a wind turbine the wind turns the rotor, and the shaft of the rotor turns the generator. The rotor turns slowly, about $10$–$15$ revolutions per minute, so the generator has either a gearbox that raises the rotation rate or a large number of magnetic poles. The wind speed varies, so a frequency converter changes the voltage of the generator to the grid frequency, $50\,\text{Hz}$. At the end of 2024 Finland had $1835$ wind turbines with a combined rated power of $8358\,\text{MW}$. In 2024 they produced $19.8\,\text{TWh}$ of electricity, about $24\,\%$ of Finland's electricity consumption (Suomen Uusiutuvat ry, wind power statistics 2024).

    The voltage of a generator

    When a coil with $N$ turns and area $A$ rotates steadily in a uniform magnetic field $B$ at a rotation frequency $f$, and at the moment $t = 0$ the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field, the flux through the coil and the voltage induced in it are

    $$\begin{aligned} \Phi &= BA\cos(2\pi f t) \\[4pt] e &= e_0 \sin(2\pi f t) \\[4pt] e_0 &= NBA \cdot 2\pi f \end{aligned}$$

    where $\Phi$ is the magnetic flux through one turn (Wb), $e$ the instantaneous value of the voltage (V), $e_0$ the peak value of the voltage (V), $N$ the number of turns, $B$ the magnetic flux density (T), $A$ the area of the coil (m²) and $f$ the rotation frequency (Hz). The frequency of the voltage is the same as the rotation frequency, and the period is $T = 1 : f$.

    Where the peak value comes from

    The peak value is obtained from the voltage of a moving conductor, $e = vBl$ (chapter Electromagnetic induction). The two sides of a rectangular coil, of length $l$, circle the axis at a radius $b : 2$, where $b$ is the width of the coil. The speed of the sides is $v = 2\pi f \cdot b : 2 = \pi f b$. Only the component of the velocity perpendicular to the field, $v \sin\theta$, contributes to the voltage, where $\theta$ is the angle between the velocity and the field. A voltage $B l v \sin\theta$ is induced in each side, and the voltages of the sides add up in a turn. In one turn the voltage is therefore $2 B l \cdot \pi f b \sin\theta = BA \cdot 2\pi f \sin\theta$, because $lb = A$, and in $N$ turns it is $N$ times as large. When $\theta = 90^\circ$, the plane of the coil is parallel to the field and the voltage is at its peak value $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. Doubling the rotation frequency doubles both the frequency and the peak value of the voltage, because the sides move twice as fast.

    The rms value

    The average of an alternating voltage over one period is zero, but a lamp or a resistor still heats up, because the power $p = ui$ is positive for both directions of the current. The power in a resistor varies between zero and the peak value $P_0 = U_0 I_0$, and its average is half of the peak value. The rms value (effective value) is the direct voltage or direct current that would deliver the same average power to a resistor:

    $$\begin{aligned} U &= \frac{U_0}{\sqrt{2}} \\[4pt] I &= \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} \end{aligned}$$

    where $U_0$ and $I_0$ are the peak values of the voltage and the current and $U$ and $I$ the rms values. The average power is then $P = UI$, as with direct current. The $230\,\text{V}$ of a wall socket is an rms value: the peak value of the voltage is $230\,\text{V} \cdot \sqrt{2} \approx 325\,\text{V}$, and the frequency is $50\,\text{Hz}$, so the voltage reverses its direction a hundred times a second. Fingrid normally keeps the frequency of the national grid between $49.9$ and $50.1\,\text{Hz}$. Meters show the rms values of an alternating voltage and current.

    Common misconception

    Many think that the voltage is greatest when the most field lines pass through the coil, that is, when the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field. The voltage is then, however, zero, because the flux is not changing at that moment. The voltage is greatest when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field and the flux is zero, because then the flux changes fastest. Another common mistake is to take the $230\,\text{V}$ of the wall socket as the greatest value of the voltage. It is an rms value, and the voltage swings between $-325\,\text{V}$ and $325\,\text{V}$. A third mistake is to conclude that alternating current does not transfer energy because its average is zero. The power is positive for both directions of the current.

    Summary

    • In a generator a coil rotates in a magnetic field (or a magnet rotates inside a coil), and the flux through the coil, $\Phi = BA\cos(2\pi f t)$, varies continuously.
    • The induced voltage depends on the rate of change of the flux: it is zero when the flux is at its greatest, and greatest when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field and the flux is zero.
    • A sinusoidal alternating voltage $e = e_0 \sin(2\pi f t)$ arises, with peak value $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$ and the same frequency as the rotation frequency.
    • The rms value of a sinusoidal alternating voltage is $U = U_0 : \sqrt{2}$ and $I = I_0 : \sqrt{2}$, and the average power is $P = UI$. The peak value of the mains voltage $230\,\text{V}$ is about $325\,\text{V}$ and its frequency is $50\,\text{Hz}$.
    • The rotor of a wind turbine turns the generator, and a frequency converter matches the voltage to the frequency of the grid.

      Worked example

      Problem

      The coil of a generator used for teaching has $250$ turns, and the sides of the rectangular coil are $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and $5.0\,\text{cm}$ long. The coil rotates in a uniform magnetic field whose magnetic flux density is $0.18\,\text{T}$, and it makes $12$ revolutions per second. How large is the rms value of the alternating voltage induced in the coil?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The number of turns is $N = 250$, the sides of the coil are $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and $5.0\,\text{cm}$, the magnetic flux density is $B = 0.18\,\text{T}$ and the rotation frequency is $f = 12\,\text{Hz}$. We are asked for the rms value $U$ of the voltage.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The coil rotates steadily in a uniform field, so a sinusoidal alternating voltage is induced in it, with peak value $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. The rms value of a sinusoidal voltage is the peak value divided by $\sqrt{2}$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} e_0 &= NBA \cdot 2\pi f \\ U &= \frac{e_0}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{NBA \cdot 2\pi f}{\sqrt{2}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The area in square metres:

      $A$$= 4.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.0020\,\text{m}^2$

      The rotation frequency is $12$ revolutions per second, that is $f = 12\,\text{Hz}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the peak value and then the rms value:

      $$\begin{aligned} e_0 &= NBA \cdot 2\pi f \\ &= 250 \cdot 0.18\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0020\,\text{m}^2 \\ &\quad \cdot 2\pi \cdot 12\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \\ &= 6.786\,\text{V} \\[6pt] U &= \frac{6.786\,\text{V}}{\sqrt{2}} \\ &= 4.798\,\text{V} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The rms value of the voltage is $U \approx 4.8\,\text{V}$ and the peak value is about $6.8\,\text{V}$. The result is given to two significant figures, like the dimensions of the coil and the magnetic flux density.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The units fit:

      $\text{T} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$$= \tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}}$
      $= \text{V}$

      With round numbers

      $250 \cdot 0.2\,\text{T} \cdot 0.002\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 6 \cdot 10\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
      $\approx 6\,\text{V}$

      which fits the peak value. The rms value is smaller than the peak value, as it should be. A voltage of a few volts is of the same order of magnitude as the voltage of a bicycle dynamo.

      1. Concept: rms value

      What does the rms value of an alternating voltage mean?

      • The average of a sinusoidal voltage over a period is zero, because the positive and negative half-periods cancel each other. The rms value is defined through power: it is the direct voltage that would deliver the same average power to a resistor.

      • Correct. The power in a resistor varies, and its average is half of the peak value. This is why the rms value is $U = U_0 : \sqrt{2}$, and the average power is $P = UI$ as with direct current.

      • The greatest voltage is the peak value $U_0$. The rms value is smaller, $U = U_0 : \sqrt{2}$: it is the direct voltage that would deliver the same average power to a resistor.

      Optional 3. Explain: why is the voltage sinusoidal?

      Explain why a sinusoidal alternating voltage arises in a coil that rotates steadily. Say in which position of the coil the voltage is zero and in which it is greatest, and why the sign of the voltage changes.

      Model answer

      When the coil rotates, the flux through it varies like a cosine: $\Phi = BA\cos(2\pi f t)$. By the law of induction the voltage is proportional to the rate of change of the flux, that is, to the steepness of the $\Phi(t)$ graph. When the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field, the flux is at its greatest but hardly changes for a moment, because the sides move along the field lines: the voltage is zero. When the plane of the coil is parallel to the field, the flux is zero, but the sides move perpendicular to the field lines and the flux changes fastest: the voltage is greatest. The steepness of a cosine curve varies like a sine, so the voltage is $e = e_0 \sin(2\pi f t)$. Every half revolution the direction of the change in flux reverses (the flux starts to increase instead of decrease), so the sign of the voltage and the direction of the current change.

        Level 1 · basic practice 5 exercises

        One formula or one step of reasoning. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          The coil of a school generator model has $300$ turns, and its area is $0.0030\,\text{m}^2$. The coil rotates in a uniform magnetic field of $0.15\,\text{T}$ at a rotation frequency of $10\,\text{Hz}$.

          How large is the peak value of the voltage induced in the coil?

          V
          Answer

          $8.5\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 300$, area $A = 0.0030\,\text{m}^2$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.15\,\text{T}$, rotation frequency $f = 10\,\text{Hz}$

          Find

          the peak value $e_0$ of the voltage in volts

          Principle

          The coil rotates steadily in a uniform field, so the flux through it varies sinusoidally and a sinusoidal alternating voltage is induced in the coil. Its peak value is $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$: the voltage is greatest when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field and the flux changes fastest.

          Formula

          $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the quantities are in SI units, and $\text{T} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{\text{s}} = \text{V}$.

          Substitution

          $e_0$$= 300 \cdot 0.15\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0030\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 10\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $= 8.482\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $8.5\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the initial values.

          Reasonableness

          With round numbers $300 \cdot 0.15\,\text{T} \cdot 0.003\,\text{m}^2 \approx 0.14\,\text{Wb}$, and multiplying this by $2\pi \cdot 10 \approx 60$ gives about $8\,\text{V}$. A voltage of a few volts suits a teaching generator turned by hand.

        2. Core

          The electrical system of a passenger aircraft has an alternating voltage with an rms value of $115\,\text{V}$ and a frequency of $400\,\text{Hz}$.

          How large is the peak value of the voltage?

          V
          Answer

          $163\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          rms value of the voltage $U = 115\,\text{V}$, frequency $400\,\text{Hz}$

          Find

          the peak value $U_0$ of the voltage in volts

          Principle

          An alternating voltage is given as an rms value. The rms value of a sinusoidal voltage is the peak value divided by $\sqrt{2}$, so the peak value is $\sqrt{2}$ times the rms value. The frequency does not affect this ratio.

          Formula

          $U = \dfrac{U_0}{\sqrt{2}}$, so $U_0 = \sqrt{2} \cdot U$

          Conversions

          Not needed.

          Substitution

          $U_0$$= \sqrt{2} \cdot 115\,\text{V}$
          $= 162.63\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $163\,\text{V}$

          Three significant figures like the voltage $115\,\text{V}$.

          Reasonableness

          The peak value is about $1.4$ times the rms value: $1.4 \cdot 115\,\text{V} \approx 161\,\text{V}$. In the same way the peak value of the household $230\,\text{V}$ is about $325\,\text{V}$.

        3. The sinusoidal alternating current in the element of a kettle has a peak value of $8.5\,\text{A}$. How large is the rms value of the current?

          A
          Answer

          $6.0\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          peak value of the current $I_0 = 8.5\,\text{A}$, the current is sinusoidal

          Find

          the rms value $I$ of the current in amperes

          Principle

          The rms value is the direct current that would deliver the same average power to a resistor. For a sinusoidal current the power in a resistor is on average half of the peak power, so the rms value is the peak value divided by $\sqrt{2}$.

          Formula

          $I = \dfrac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed.

          Substitution

          $I$$= \dfrac{8.5\,\text{A}}{\sqrt{2}}$
          $= 6.010\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $6.0\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the peak value $8.5\,\text{A}$.

          Reasonableness

          The rms value is about $0.7$ times the peak value: $0.7 \cdot 8.5\,\text{A} \approx 6\,\text{A}$. At the mains voltage $230\,\text{V}$ this corresponds to a power of about $1.4\,\text{kW}$, which suits a kettle.

        4. The shaft of a hydropower plant's generator makes $1500$ revolutions per minute. How large is the rotation frequency of the shaft in hertz?

          Hz
          Answer

          $25\,\text{Hz}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the shaft makes $1500$ revolutions per minute

          Find

          the rotation frequency $f$ in hertz

          Principle

          The rotation frequency is the number of revolutions per second. When the revolutions are known per minute, they are divided by the number of seconds in a minute.

          Formula

          $f = \dfrac{n}{t}$, where $n$ is the number of revolutions in the time $t$

          Conversions

          $t = 1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$

          Substitution

          $f$$= \dfrac{1500}{60\,\text{s}}$
          $= 25\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $= 25\,\text{Hz}$

          Answer

          $25\,\text{Hz}$

          ; the reading $1500$ revolutions per minute is exact, so the result is exactly $25\,\text{Hz}$.

          Reasonableness

          The grid frequency $50\,\text{Hz}$ would correspond to $3000$ revolutions per minute with one pair of magnetic poles. Half of that gives half the frequency, so such a generator has two pairs of poles so that the frequency of the voltage is $50\,\text{Hz}$.

        5. An electric heater is connected to a mains voltage with an rms value of $230\,\text{V}$, and the rms value of its current is $4.35\,\text{A}$.

          How large is the average power of the heater? Give the answer in kilowatts.

          kW
          Answer

          $1.00\,\text{kW}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          rms value of the voltage $U = 230\,\text{V}$, rms value of the current $I = 4.35\,\text{A}$

          Find

          the average power $P$ in kilowatts

          Principle

          The rms value is defined so that with alternating current the average power is calculated from the rms values with the same formula as with direct current. A heater is a resistor, so the voltage and the current are in step.

          Formula

          $P = UI$

          Conversions

          The result is converted to kilowatts: $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.

          Substitution

          $P$$= 230\,\text{V} \cdot 4.35\,\text{A}$
          $= 1000.5\,\text{W}$
          $= 1000.5 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kW}$
          $= 1.0005\,\text{kW}$

          Answer

          $1.00\,\text{kW}$

          Three significant figures like the current $4.35\,\text{A}$ and the voltage $230\,\text{V}$.

          Reasonableness

          The power of an ordinary electric heater is $0.5$–$2\,\text{kW}$. A power calculated with the peak values would be $2.00\,\text{kW}$, twice as large, but that is only the instantaneous peak value, not the average.

        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          The rectangular coil of a generator has $150$ turns, and the lengths of its sides are $6.0\,\text{cm}$ and $8.0\,\text{cm}$. The coil rotates in a uniform magnetic field of $0.25\,\text{T}$ and makes $1200$ revolutions per minute.

          How large is the peak value of the voltage induced in the coil?

          V
          Answer

          $23\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 150$, sides of the coil $6.0\,\text{cm}$ and $8.0\,\text{cm}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.25\,\text{T}$, rotation rate $1200$ revolutions per minute

          Find

          the peak value $e_0$ of the voltage in volts

          Principle

          The coil rotates steadily in a uniform field, so a sinusoidal alternating voltage is induced in it, with peak value $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. First the area of the coil and the rotation frequency are calculated in SI units.

          Formula

          $A = ab$ and $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$

          Conversions

          $A$$= 6.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} \cdot 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.0048\,\text{m}^2$
          $f$$= \dfrac{1200}{60\,\text{s}}$
          $= 20\,\text{Hz}$

          Substitution

          $e_0$$= 150 \cdot 0.25\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0048\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $= 22.62\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $23\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the dimensions of the coil and the magnetic flux density.

          Reasonableness

          With round numbers $150 \cdot 0.25\,\text{T} \cdot 0.005\,\text{m}^2 \approx 0.19\,\text{Wb}$ and $2\pi \cdot 20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \approx 125\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, so $e_0 \approx 23\,\text{V}$. If the rotation rate $1200$ had been used as hertz, the result would have been $60$ times as large.

        2. Core

          The graph shows the voltage of a small generator as a function of time. The coil of the generator has $200$ turns, its area is $30\,\text{cm}^2$, and it rotates in a uniform magnetic field. Read the peak value and the period of the voltage from the graph.

          How large is the magnetic flux density?

          T
          Answer

          $0.13\,\text{T}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 200$, area $A = 30\,\text{cm}^2$, peak value and period read from the graph

          Find

          the magnetic flux density $B$ in teslas

          Principle

          The coil rotates steadily in a uniform field, so its voltage is sinusoidal and the peak value is $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. The graph gives the peak value and the period, and the period gives the rotation frequency $f = 1 : T$.

          Reading the graph

          The peaks of the curve are on the grid line $12\,\text{V}$ (at the times $10\,\text{ms}$ and $50\,\text{ms}$), so $e_0 = 12\,\text{V}$. The curve is zero and rising at the times $0\,\text{ms}$, $40\,\text{ms}$ and $80\,\text{ms}$, so one period lasts $T = 40\,\text{ms}$.

          Formula

          $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ and $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$, so

          $B$$= \dfrac{e_0}{NA \cdot 2\pi f}$
          $= \dfrac{e_0 T}{NA \cdot 2\pi}$

          Conversions

          $T$$= 40\,\text{ms}$
          $= 40 \cdot 0.001\,\text{s}$
          $= 0.040\,\text{s}$
          $A$$= 30\,\text{cm}^2$
          $= 30 \cdot 0.0001\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 0.0030\,\text{m}^2$

          Substitution

          $f$$= \dfrac{1}{0.040\,\text{s}}$
          $= 25\,\text{Hz}$
          $B$$= \dfrac{12\,\text{V}}{200 \cdot 0.0030\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 25\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}}$
          $= \dfrac{12\,\text{V}}{94.25\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}}}$
          $= 0.1273\,\text{T}$

          Answer

          $0.13\,\text{T}$

          Two significant figures, because the values $12\,\text{V}$ and $40\,\text{ms}$ read from the graph are known to two figures.

          Reasonableness

          The field of a permanent magnet is typically a few tenths of a tesla, so $0.13\,\text{T}$ is believable. Check:

          $200 \cdot 0.13\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0030\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 25\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $\approx 12.3\,\text{V}$

          which matches the peak of the graph.

        3. A hiker charges a battery with a hand-cranked generator whose voltage must have an rms value of $12\,\text{V}$. The coil of the generator has $200$ turns, its area is $0.0050\,\text{m}^2$, and the magnetic field is $0.30\,\text{T}$.

          At what rotation frequency must the coil be turned?

          Hz
          Answer

          $9.0\,\text{Hz}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          rms value of the voltage $U = 12\,\text{V}$, number of turns $N = 200$, area $A = 0.0050\,\text{m}^2$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.30\,\text{T}$

          Find

          the rotation frequency $f$ in hertz

          Principle

          The voltage of a rotating coil is sinusoidal, and its peak value is $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. The given $12\,\text{V}$ is an rms value, so the peak value is $\sqrt{2}$ times as large. The rotation frequency is solved from the formula for the peak value.

          Formula

          $e_0 = \sqrt{2} \cdot U$ and $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$, so

          $f$$= \dfrac{\sqrt{2} \cdot U}{NBA \cdot 2\pi}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the quantities are in SI units.

          Substitution

          $e_0$$= \sqrt{2} \cdot 12\,\text{V}$
          $= 16.97\,\text{V}$
          $f$$= \dfrac{16.97\,\text{V}}{200 \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0050\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 2\pi}$
          $= \dfrac{16.97\,\text{V}}{1.885\,\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}$
          $= 9.003\,\text{Hz}$

          Answer

          $9.0\,\text{Hz}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage $12\,\text{V}$ and the field $0.30\,\text{T}$.

          Reasonableness

          Nine revolutions per second is brisk but possible with a crank that has gearing. If the rms value had been substituted in place of the peak value, the frequency would have come out too small, $6.4\,\text{Hz}$, and the rms value of the voltage would have been below $12\,\text{V}$.

        4. In 2024 Finland's wind turbines produced $19.8\,\text{TWh}$ of electricity, and at the end of the year their combined rated power was $8358\,\text{MW}$ (Suomen Uusiutuvat ry, wind power statistics 2024). The year 2024 had $366$ days.

          What percentage of the rated power at the end of the year was the average power of the wind turbines?

          %
          Answer

          $27\,\%$

          Show the solution

          Given

          electrical energy produced $E = 19.8\,\text{TWh}$ in 2024, length of the year $366$ days, rated power at the end of the year $P_\text{n} = 8358\,\text{MW}$ (Suomen Uusiutuvat ry)

          Find

          the share of the average power in the rated power as a percentage

          Principle

          The average power is the energy produced during the year divided by the length of the year. Wind turbines produce their rated power only in a strong wind, so the average power is clearly smaller than the rated power. The share is obtained by dividing the average power by the rated power.

          Formula

          $P = \dfrac{E}{t}$ and share $= \dfrac{P}{P_\text{n}} \cdot 100\,\%$

          Conversions

          $t$$= 366 \cdot 24\,\text{h}$
          $= 8784\,\text{h}$
          $E$$= 19.8\,\text{TWh}$
          $= 19.8 \cdot 10^{12}\,\text{Wh}$
          $P_\text{n}$$= 8358\,\text{MW}$
          $= 8358 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}$

          Substitution

          $P$$= \dfrac{19.8 \cdot 10^{12}\,\text{Wh}}{8784\,\text{h}}$
          $= 2.254 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{W}$

          and share $= \dfrac{2.254 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{W}}{8358 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}} \cdot 100\,\% = 26.97\,\%$

          Answer

          $27\,\%$

          Two significant figures, because the production $19.8\,\text{TWh}$ is given to three figures and the result is an estimate.

          Reasonableness

          The average power of wind turbines is typically a quarter to a third of the rated power, so $27\,\%$ is believable. The true share is slightly larger, because the rated power grew during the year: at the beginning of the year there were fewer turbines than at the end.

        Level 3 · challenging 3 exercises

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. The coil of a generator rotates steadily, and the peak value of its voltage is $20.0\,\text{V}$. At the moment $t = 0$ the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field.

          How large is the voltage of the coil when the coil has turned $30^\circ$ from this position?

          V
          Answer

          $10.0\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          peak value of the voltage $e_0 = 20.0\,\text{V}$, at the moment $t = 0$ the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field, the coil turns $30^\circ$ from this position

          Find

          the instantaneous value $e$ of the voltage in volts

          Principle

          When the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field, the flux is at its greatest and does not change for a moment, so the voltage is zero. From then on the voltage grows like a sine, $e = e_0 \sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle through which the coil has turned from this position. The voltage is at its peak value at the angle $90^\circ$, when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field.

          Formula

          $e = e_0 \sin\theta$

          Conversions

          Not needed.

          Substitution

          $e$$= 20.0\,\text{V} \cdot \sin 30^\circ$
          $= 20.0\,\text{V} \cdot 0.5$
          $= 10.0\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $10.0\,\text{V}$

          Three significant figures like the peak value $20.0\,\text{V}$.

          Reasonableness

          The angle $30^\circ$ is a third of the way from the position of zero voltage to the position of peak voltage, but the voltage is already half the peak value, because the sine curve rises most steeply at the start. A $17.3\,\text{V}$ calculated with the cosine would be wrong: it would give the greatest voltage in the position where the flux does not change.

        2. A generator whose voltage has a peak value of $40\,\text{V}$ is connected to a $40\,\Omega$ resistor. The resistance of the generator itself is so small that it can be ignored.

          How large is the average power of the resistor?

          W
          Answer

          $20\,\text{W}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          peak value of the generator's voltage $U_0 = 40\,\text{V}$, resistance $R = 40\,\Omega$, resistance of the generator negligible

          Find

          the average power $P$ of the resistor in watts

          Principle

          The resistance of the generator is negligible, so the whole voltage is across the resistor. The average power is calculated with rms values as with direct current: $P = UI$, and by Ohm's law $I = U : R$, so $P = U^2 : R$. A power calculated with the peak value would be only the instantaneous peak power.

          Formula

          $U$$= \dfrac{U_0}{\sqrt{2}}$
          $P$$= UI$
          $= \dfrac{U^2}{R}$
          $= \dfrac{U_0^2}{2R}$

          Conversions

          Not needed.

          Substitution

          $U$$= \dfrac{40\,\text{V}}{\sqrt{2}}$
          $= 28.28\,\text{V}$
          $P$$= \dfrac{(28.28\,\text{V})^2}{40\,\Omega}$
          $= \dfrac{800\,\text{V}^2}{40\,\Omega}$
          $= 20\,\text{W}$

          Answer

          $20\,\text{W}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage $40\,\text{V}$ and the resistance $40\,\Omega$.

          Reasonableness

          The instantaneous peak power is

          $U_0^2 : R$$= 1600\,\text{V}^2 : 40\,\Omega$
          $= 40\,\text{W}$

          and the average is half of that, because the power varies between zero and the peak value. Units:

          $\tfrac{\text{V}^2}{\Omega}$$= \text{V} \cdot \text{A}$
          $= \text{W}$

        3. In a direct-drive wind turbine a permanent-magnet generator rotates at the same rate as the rotor. The rms value of the generator's voltage is $690\,\text{V}$ when the rotor turns at $15$ revolutions per minute. The wind weakens, and the rotor now turns at only $9.0$ revolutions per minute. The magnets and coils of the generator are the same, and the frequency converter is not taken into account.

          How large is the rms value of the generator's voltage now?

          V
          Answer

          $410\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          rms voltage $U_1 = 690\,\text{V}$ at a rotation rate of $15$ revolutions per minute, new rotation rate $9.0$ revolutions per minute, magnets and coils the same

          Find

          the new rms voltage $U_2$ in volts

          Principle

          The peak voltage of a generator is $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. When $N$, $B$ and $A$ stay the same, the peak voltage, and with it the rms value $U = e_0 : \sqrt{2}$, are directly proportional to the rotation frequency. In a direct-drive turbine the generator rotates at the same rate as the rotor, so the frequency is proportional to the revolutions per minute of the rotor.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{U_2}{U_1} = \dfrac{f_2}{f_1}$, so $U_2 = U_1 \cdot \dfrac{f_2}{f_1}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: in the ratio the revolutions per minute cancel, so they need not be converted to hertz.

          Substitution

          $U_2$$= 690\,\text{V} \cdot \dfrac{9.0}{15}$
          $= 690\,\text{V} \cdot 0.60$
          $= 414\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          about $410\,\text{V}$, two significant figures like the rotation rates $15$ and $9.0$ revolutions per minute.

          Reasonableness

          The rotation rate fell by $40\,\%$, so the voltage also falls by $40\,\%$. The frequency of the voltage falls as well, which is why a wind turbine needs a frequency converter that matches both the voltage and the frequency to the grid.
