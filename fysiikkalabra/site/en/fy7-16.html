Applied chapter: wireless charging, the induction hob and the fibre link

Why does a phone charge without a cable, a pan get hot without a flame and an internet connection travel as light in a glass fibre?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how a wireless charger, an induction hob and a fibre link make use of induction and total internal reflection.
  • assess why wireless charging weakens as the distance grows and why an induction hob needs a steel pan.
  • calculate the attenuation of a fibre in decibels and the power that reaches the receiver.

Prior knowledge

  • A changing magnetic flux induces a voltage $e = -N \cdot \Delta\Phi : \Delta t$ in a coilchapter Electromagnetic induction
  • A changing magnetic field produces eddy currents in a conductor, and they change electrical energy into internal energychapter Lenz's law and eddy currents
  • In an optical fibre light travels in the core by total internal reflection at the speed $v = c : n$chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre

1Think about it

On a kitchen worktop a phone charges on a round pad without a cable, water boils in a steel pan on an induction hob although nothing glows, and the internet connection to the router on the wall travels as light in a thin glass fibre. If the phone is lifted a few centimetres off the pad, charging stops. If an aluminium pan is put in place of the steel pan, the hob does not heat it. In the fibre, light travels tens of kilometres before it has to be amplified.

What physical phenomenon is behind each device, and what limits how it works?

Optional Recall: the transformer
The primary coil of a transformer is connected to a voltage source. When does a voltage appear in the secondary coil?

  • The flux produced by a direct current is constant, and a constant flux does not induce a voltage. A voltage appears only when the flux changes, for example with an alternating current or at the moment a current is switched on.

  • The coils are not connected to each other by wires. Energy is transferred by a changing magnetic flux: the change of flux induces a voltage in the secondary coil.

  • Correct. A voltage is induced in the secondary coil only when the flux through it changes (chapter The transformer and the transmission of electrical energy). An alternating current changes the flux all the time. Today the same phenomenon works without an iron core.

Prediction 3: why the pan gets hot

On the glass top of an induction hob the water in a steel pan boils, but the glass next to the pan is only lukewarm.Which explanation is physically correct?

  • If the heat came from the coil through the glass, the glass would be the hottest part and would also get hot next to the pan.   • A microwave oven heats water with electromagnetic radiation, but an induction hob works differently. An empty steel pan gets hot quickly on the hob, so the heat is produced in the base of the pan: the changing field of the coil induces eddy currents in it.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Three devices, two phenomena

    Three everyday devices in the kitchen work by phenomena from this course. A wireless charger and an induction hob are based on electromagnetic induction: a changing magnetic flux produces a voltage in a wire loop or eddy currents in a metal (chapters Electromagnetic induction and Lenz's law and eddy currents). A fibre link is based on total internal reflection: light stays in the core of the fibre because it meets the boundary between the core and the cladding at an angle of incidence larger than the critical angle (chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre). In this chapter the ideas are combined: in which device energy is transferred, where it is lost and what limits how the device works.

    Wireless charging: a transformer without an iron core

    The pad of a wireless charger contains a flat transmitter coil, and under the back cover of the phone there is a similar receiver coil. An alternating current flows in the transmitter coil, at a frequency of about $100$–$200\,\text{kHz}$ in Qi-standard chargers. The current produces a changing magnetic field, and part of its flux passes through the receiver coil. The changing flux induces an alternating voltage in the receiver coil according to the law of induction: the instantaneous value of the voltage depends on the rate of change of the flux at each moment. The electronics of the phone rectifies the voltage and uses it to charge the battery. The device is like a transformer (chapter The transformer and the transmission of electrical energy), but the coils are not joined by an iron core: between them there is only air and plastic.

    Why distance decides

    In a transformer the iron core guides almost all of the flux through the secondary coil. Without a core, the field lines of the transmitter coil loop around the wires of the coil and turn back close to the coil. When the phone lies right on the pad, a large part of the flux passes through the receiver. When the phone is lifted, fewer and fewer field lines reach the receiver, and the flux and the voltage fall steeply. In the figure the green lines are field lines of the transmitter coil, the green triangle shows the direction of the field at the centre of the coil, and the lines that pass through the receiver coil are drawn darker. This is why the gap between the charger and the phone is only a few millimetres. A thin ferrite sheet is placed behind the coils. Ferrite is magnetised like a ferromagnetic substance but conducts electricity poorly, so large eddy currents are not produced in it. The sheet gathers the field between the coils and keeps it from heating the metal parts of the phone.

    Losses in wireless charging

    A sufficient voltage is obtained in the receiver even if only part of the flux reaches it, but then a larger current has to flow in the transmitter coil. In the resistance of the coils the current changes electrical energy into internal energy at the power $P = RI^2$, so the charger and the phone get warm.

    In one device comparison made in 2020 (Ravenscraft, OneZero, 2020), charging a phone from empty to full took $14.26\,\text{Wh}$ from the wall socket with a cable and on average $21.01\,\text{Wh}$ with a charging pad with the phone in place, that is about $47\,\%$ more energy. When the coils were slightly offset from each other, charging took $25.62\,\text{Wh}$. The difference depends on the charger, the phone, the charging method and the alignment of the coils, however, so the figures do not apply to all wireless chargers. If there is a coin or a key between the charger and the phone, eddy currents are produced in it and it gets hot. This is why the charger detects a foreign metal object and stops charging.

    Induction hob: the base of the pan as the secondary

    Under the glass top of an induction hob there is a flat copper coil in which an alternating current flows at a frequency of tens of kilohertz. The changing magnetic field of the coil passes through the glass into the base of the pan. The flux through the base changes all the time, so eddy currents are induced in the base. According to Lenz's law they circulate so that their own field opposes the change of flux. In the resistance of the base the electrical energy of the eddy currents changes into internal energy of the base, and the hot base heats the food.

    The working of an induction hob thus resembles a transformer in which the metal base of the pan acts as the secondary. The base does not, however, form a single turn of an ordinary coil: the changing magnetic field produces many paths of eddy currents in it. Glass does not conduct electricity, so no currents are produced in it. The glass gets warm only from the hot pan.

    Why the pan must be steel or cast iron

    Ordinary household induction hobs are designed for pans whose base contains a ferromagnetic substance, such as steel or cast iron. The principle of induction heating does not physically require ferromagnetism, however: other conductors also heat up if the device is designed for them. A ferromagnetic base strengthens the field of the coil and gathers the flux into itself (chapter Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field). In addition, steel conducts electricity worse than aluminium, and the eddy currents flow in it only in a thin surface layer with a large resistance, so with the same current in the coil the internal energy of the base grows quickly.

    Aluminium and copper are not ferromagnetic, and they conduct electricity extremely well. In them the eddy currents spread over a thicker region, the resistance stays small and there is little heating. An ordinary induction hob therefore usually does not heat an aluminium pan: it detects an unsuitable pan and does not start. Many pans have a steel plate under the base for just this reason.

    The fibre link: light travels in glass

    In a fibre link the transmitter changes the information into pulses of infrared light, which travel in the core of a fibre as thick as a hair by total internal reflection. The refractive index of the core is slightly larger than that of the cladding, and light travelling almost along the axis meets the wall of the core at an angle of incidence larger than the critical angle. In glass with a refractive index of $1.47$ light travels at the speed $v = c : n$, so every kilometre delays the signal by about $4.9\,\mu\text{s}$. Total internal reflection does not waste light, but the glass itself attenuates it: some of the light is absorbed and some is scattered sideways by small variations in the density of the glass. The attenuation of silica glass is smallest at a wavelength of about $1550\,\text{nm}$, so long links use it.

    Attenuation limits the length of a link

    According to the manufacturer, the attenuation of a common telecommunications fibre is at most $0.18\,\text{dB/km}$ at a wavelength of $1550\,\text{nm}$ and $0.32\,\text{dB/km}$ at a wavelength of $1310\,\text{nm}$ (Corning, product information sheet of the SMF-28 Ultra fibre, 2016). The decibel is familiar from the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale (FY5): every $10\,\text{dB}$ means that the power falls to a tenth, and about $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the power. At a wavelength of $1550\,\text{nm}$ the power therefore halves over about $17\,\text{km}$, and after $100\,\text{km}$ less than $2\,\%$ of it is left. The receiver detects pulses even in very weak light, but on long links the light has to be amplified with an optical amplifier about every $50$–$100\,\text{km}$.

    Attenuation in decibels

    When the power of light is $P_0$ at the start of a fibre and $P$ at the end, the attenuation of the fibre in decibels is

    $$\begin{aligned} A &= 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg \frac{P_0}{P} \\[6pt] A &= aL \end{aligned}$$

    where $A$ is the attenuation ($\text{dB}$), $\lg$ the base-ten logarithm, $a$ the attenuation coefficient of the fibre ($\text{dB/km}$) and $L$ the length of the fibre ($\text{km}$). The formula $A = aL$ holds when the fibre is uniform along its whole length. The attenuations of successive sections in decibels are added, and the ratios of their powers are multiplied: two successive $10\,\text{dB}$ sections attenuate by $20\,\text{dB}$, and the power falls to a tenth of a tenth, that is a hundredth. Splices and sharp bends add to the attenuation, and they are added in the same way. The graph shows that the power falls in the same proportion on every kilometre: the curves are not straight but flatten out.

    Voltage of a wireless charger

    When the flux through one turn of the receiver coil varies sinusoidally with the peak value $\Phi_0$ and the frequency $f$, an alternating voltage is produced in the coil, and its peak value is found in the same way as in a generator (chapter The generator and alternating current):

    $$e_0 = N \cdot 2\pi f \cdot \Phi_0$$

    where $e_0$ is the peak value of the voltage ($\text{V}$), $N$ the number of turns of the receiver coil, $f$ the frequency ($\text{Hz}$) and $\Phi_0$ the peak value of the flux through one turn ($\text{Wb}$). A high frequency makes the change of flux fast, so even a small flux is enough. As the distance grows, $\Phi_0$ and with it $e_0$ fall steeply.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a wireless charger sends energy far into the room like radio waves, and that a phone could be charged anywhere near the charger. The magnetic field of the charger, however, weakens steeply already over a few centimetres, and energy is transferred only when the receiver coil is in the field right next to the transmitter. Another common mistake is to think that an induction hob heats the glass top, which then warms the pan. The heat is produced in the base of the pan by eddy currents, and the glass warms up only from the hot pan. The third mistake concerns decibels: doubling the attenuation in decibels does not halve the power but raises the ratio of the powers to the second power. $20\,\text{dB}$ leaves $1\,\%$ of the power, not $5\,\%$.

    Summary

    • A wireless charger is a transformer without an iron core: the flux of the alternating current in the transmitter coil induces a voltage $e_0 = N \cdot 2\pi f \cdot \Phi_0$ in the receiver coil. The flux and the voltage fall steeply when the distance between the coils grows or when they move sideways.
    • In wireless charging part of the energy changes into internal energy of the coils: in one device comparison, charging took about $47\,\%$ more energy than with a cable, but the difference depends on the devices and the alignment of the coils.
    • In an induction hob the changing field of the coil produces eddy currents in the base of the pan, and they heat the base. A ferromagnetic steel base gets hot well, aluminium and copper poorly, and glass, which does not conduct electricity, not at all.
    • In an optical fibre light stays in the core by total internal reflection, but the glass attenuates it: $A = aL$ and $A = 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg (P_0 : P)$. Every $10\,\text{dB}$ reduces the power to a tenth.

      Principle. The fibre is uniform along its whole length, so the attenuation is the attenuation coefficient times the length. The ratio of the powers is solved from the definition of attenuation, and from it the power that arrives.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} A &= aL \\[6pt] \lg \frac{P_0}{P} &= \frac{A}{10\,\text{dB}} \\[6pt] P &= P_0 \cdot 10^{-\frac{A}{10\,\text{dB}}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the kilometres cancel in the product $aL$, and the power comes out in the same unit as $P_0$. Finally $1\,\text{mW} = 1000\,\mu\text{W}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} A &= aL \\ &= 0.20\,\tfrac{\text{dB}}{\text{km}} \cdot 80\,\text{km} \\ &= 16\,\text{dB} \\[6pt] P &= P_0 \cdot 10^{-\frac{A}{10\,\text{dB}}} \\ &= 2.0\,\text{mW} \cdot 10^{-\frac{16\,\text{dB}}{10\,\text{dB}}} \\ &= 2.0\,\text{mW} \cdot 10^{-1.6} \\ &= 0.0502\,\text{mW} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The power that reaches the receiver is

      $P$$\approx 0.050\,\text{mW}$
      $= 50\,\mu\text{W}$

      to two significant figures like the transmitted power $2.0\,\text{mW}$ and the attenuation coefficient.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Ten decibels reduce the power to a tenth and twenty to a hundredth, so after $16\,\text{dB}$ the power lies between those, about $2.5\,\%$. Of the power $2.0\,\text{mW}$, $2.5\,\%$ is $0.050\,\text{mW}$, as in the result.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      A student writes: "An attenuation of $10\,\text{dB}$ reduces the power to a tenth.When the attenuation doubles to $20\,\text{dB}$, a twentieth of the power is left, that is $5\,\%$." Where is the error?

      • Correct. Decibels are added, but the ratios of powers are multiplied: two successive $10\,\text{dB}$ parts first reduce the power to a tenth and then to a tenth of that. $P : P_0 = 10^{-2} = 0.01$.

      • The attenuation in decibels is proportional to the length of the fibre, not to the power that is lost. Every $10\,\text{dB}$ divides the remaining power by ten, so after $20\,\text{dB}$ $1\,\%$ is left, not $5\,\%$.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation: how strong a field in the phone

      The receiver coil of a phone has $12$ turns, and the area of one turn is $11\,\text{cm}^2$. The frequency of the charger is $140\,\text{kHz}$, and the phone needs a voltage with a peak value of $9.0\,\text{V}$. Assume that at the coil the magnetic field is uniform and perpendicular to the plane of the coil and that the magnetic flux density varies sinusoidally.

      How large must the peak value $B_0$ of the magnetic flux density be? Give the answer in millitesla.

      mT
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which formula gives the peak value of an alternating voltage when the flux varies sinusoidally? And what is the flux in a uniform field?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $e_0 = N \cdot 2\pi f \cdot \Phi_0$ and $\Phi_0 = B_0 A$, so $B_0 = e_0 : (N A \cdot 2\pi f)$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Convert $11\,\text{cm}^2 = 11 \cdot (0.01\,\text{m})^2$ and $140\,\text{kHz} = 140 \cdot 1000\,\text{Hz}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      number of turns $N = 12$, area of one turn $A = 11\,\text{cm}^2$, frequency $f = 140\,\text{kHz}$, peak voltage $e_0 = 9.0\,\text{V}$

      Find

      the peak value $B_0$ of the magnetic flux density in millitesla

      Principle

      The flux through the receiver coil varies sinusoidally, so a sinusoidal voltage is induced in the coil, and its peak value is found as in a generator (chapter The generator and alternating current). The field is assumed to be uniform and perpendicular to the plane of the coil, so the flux through one turn is $\Phi_0 = B_0 A$ (chapter Electromagnetic induction).

      Formula

      $e_0$$= N \cdot 2\pi f \cdot \Phi_0$
      $= N \cdot 2\pi f \cdot B_0 A$

      so $B_0 = \dfrac{e_0}{N A \cdot 2\pi f}$

      Conversions

      $A$$= 11\,\text{cm}^2$
      $= 11 \cdot (0.01\,\text{m})^2$
      $= 1.1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2$
      $f$$= 140\,\text{kHz}$
      $= 140 \cdot 1000\,\text{Hz}$
      $= 1.40 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{Hz}$

      Finally $1\,\text{T} = 1000\,\text{mT}$.

      Substitution

      $B_0$$= 9.0\,\text{V} : (12 \cdot 1.1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 1.40 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{Hz})$
      $= 7.751 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T}$
      $= 0.7751\,\text{mT}$

      Answer

      $0.78\,\text{mT}$

      Two significant figures like the voltage $9.0\,\text{V}$ and the area $11\,\text{cm}^2$.

      Reasonableness

      Units:

      $\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{Hz}}$$= \tfrac{\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{m}^2}$
      $= \text{T}$

      The field is about $15$ times as strong as the Earth's magnetic field, $52\,\mu\text{T}$, but much weaker than that of a fridge magnet. A small field is enough because the high frequency makes the flux change quickly.

        $\dfrac{f_\text{C}^2}{R_\text{C}}$$= \dfrac{f^2}{R_\text{A}}$

        so

        $f_\text{C}$$= f \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{R_\text{C}}{R_\text{A}}}$

        Conversions

        $f = 3.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{Hz}$, $B_0 = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$, $r = 0.090\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.

        Substitution

        a)

        $\Phi_0$$= 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} \cdot \pi \cdot (0.090\,\text{m})^2$
        $= 7.63 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{Wb}$
        $e_0$$= 2\pi \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{Hz} \cdot 7.63 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{Wb}$
        $= 14.39\,\text{V}$

        The powers $P = \dfrac{(14.39\,\text{V})^2}{2R}$ are $2.59\,\text{kW}$ for pan A, $2.07\,\text{kW}$ for B and $1.29\,\text{kW}$ for C.
        b)

        $f_\text{C}$$= 30\,\text{kHz} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{0.080\,\Omega}{0.040\,\Omega}}$
        $= 30\,\text{kHz} \cdot \sqrt{2}$
        $= 42.43\,\text{kHz}$

        Answer

        $42\,\text{kHz}$

        Two significant figures like the initial data. The resistance of pan C is double, so its power is half; raising the frequency increases the rate of change of the flux and the voltage, and the power grows as the square of the frequency. c) The magnetic field of the eddy current itself was ignored: according to Lenz’s law it opposes the change of flux, so the real current and power are smaller than the model gives. In a base that conducts very well (aluminium, copper) the field of the eddy current itself cancels a large part of the flux, so the model does not apply to them; the ferromagnetism of steel, on the other hand, increases the heating.

        Reasonableness

        The ratio of the powers $2.59\,\text{kW} : 1.29\,\text{kW} = 2.0$ corresponds to the ratio of the resistances $0.080 : 0.040 = 2.0$. Raising the frequency by the factor $\sqrt{2} = 1.414$ increases the power by the factor $1.414^2 = 2.0$, so the power of pan C at the frequency $42.4\,\text{kHz}$ is $1.29\,\text{kW} \cdot 2.0 = 2.59\,\text{kW}$.

      1. A coin is left between the phone and the wireless charger. Why does the coin get hot?
        Answer

        The changing magnetic field of the transmitter coil of the charger passes through the coin, and the flux through the coin changes all the time. Eddy currents are induced in the coin, and in the resistance of the coin their electrical energy changes into internal energy of the coin. This is why the charger detects a foreign metal object and stops charging.

        Every $10\,\text{dB}$ reduces the power to a tenth, and a tenth of a tenth is a hundredth.

      2. Concept task: The pan on an induction hob

      Why does an ordinary induction hob heat a steel pan well but an aluminium pan poorly, although both conduct electricity?

      • Eddy currents are induced in aluminium, as in eddy-current brakes. In a good conductor the resistance is small, however, so with the same current in the coil the currents change little electrical energy into internal energy. In steel, ferromagnetism and a larger resistance make the heating efficient.

      • Correct. In steel the path of the eddy currents is thin and has a large resistance, so with the same current in the coil a lot of electrical energy is changed into internal energy. Aluminium is not ferromagnetic and conducts extremely well, so the currents spread out and the base warms up only a little.

      • The heat does not come from the glass: glass does not conduct electricity, and no currents are produced in it. The steel pan gets hot because the changing flux induces eddy currents in its base, and the ferromagnetism and large resistance of steel make them effective.

      3. Calculation task: Calculate: the fibre to a house

      The fibre link from the exchange to a house is $30\,\text{km}$ long, and the attenuation coefficient of the fibre is $0.20\,\text{dB/km}$.

      What percentage of the transmitted power reaches the house? Ignore the attenuation of the splices.

      %
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      First calculate the attenuation of the whole fibre in decibels.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $A = aL$, and the ratio of the powers is $P : P_0 = 10^{-A/(10\,\text{dB})}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $A = 6.0\,\text{dB}$, so $P : P_0 = 10^{-0.60}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      length of the fibre $L = 30\,\text{km}$, attenuation coefficient $a = 0.20\,\text{dB/km}$

      Find

      the share $P : P_0$ of the power that reaches the house, as a percentage

      Principle

      The fibre is uniform along its whole length, so the attenuation is $A = aL$. The ratio of the powers is solved from the definition of attenuation $A = 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg (P_0 : P)$.

      Formula

      $A$$= aL$
      $\lg \dfrac{P_0}{P}$$= \dfrac{A}{10\,\text{dB}}$

      so

      $\dfrac{P}{P_0}$$= 10^{-\frac{aL}{10\,\text{dB}}}$

      Conversions

      Not needed: the kilometres cancel in the product $aL$. Finally the ratio is multiplied by one hundred to give a percentage.

      Substitution

      $A$$= 0.20\,\tfrac{\text{dB}}{\text{km}} \cdot 30\,\text{km}$
      $= 6.0\,\text{dB}$

      so

      $\dfrac{P}{P_0}$$= 10^{-0.60}$
      $= 0.2512$
      $= 25.12\,\%$

      Answer

      about $25\,\%$, two significant figures like the attenuation coefficient $0.20\,\text{dB/km}$.

      Reasonableness

      About $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the power, so $6\,\text{dB}$ halves it twice, and about a quarter, that is $25\,\%$, is left, as in the result.

      Optional 4. Explanation task: The wireless charger

      Explain how a wireless charger transfers energy to a phone although there is no wire between them. Also explain why charging weakens when the phone is lifted off the pad.

      Model answer

      An alternating current flows in the transmitter coil of the charger and produces a changing magnetic field. Part of the flux of the field passes through the receiver coil of the phone. Because the flux changes all the time, an alternating voltage is induced in the receiver coil according to the law of induction, and the phone uses it to charge its battery. The device is like a transformer without an iron core. The field lines of the transmitter coil loop around its wires and turn back close to the coil. When the phone is lifted, a smaller and smaller part of the flux passes through the receiver, so the voltage falls steeply. Then part of the energy changes only into internal energy of the coils, and charging weakens or stops.
