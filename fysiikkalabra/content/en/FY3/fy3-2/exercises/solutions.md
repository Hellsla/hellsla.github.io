## fy3-2-L1-01
**Given:** $F = 80\,\text{N}$ (in the direction of motion), $s = 25\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the force in kilojoules
**Principle:** The pushing force is in the direction of motion and constant, so it does work equal to the product of force and displacement. If the force were at an angle or changed along the way, another method would be needed.
**Formula:** $W = F s$, so the work is already solved for: $W = F s$
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the distance in metres, so the product is directly in joules ($1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$). Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 80\,\text{N} \cdot 25\,\text{m} = 2000\,\text{J} = 2.000\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the given values $80\,\text{N}$ and $25\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Rough estimate: $80\,\text{N} \approx 8\,\text{kg}$ in weight, and $8\,\text{kg}\cdot 10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot 25\,\text{m} = 2000\,\text{J}$. The same work would lift a $20\,\text{kg}$ object about $10\,\text{m}$ ($20\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot 10\,\text{m} \approx 2.0\,\text{kJ}$). $W$ is positive because the force is in the direction of motion.

## fy3-2-L1-02
**Given:** weight $G = 150\,\text{N}$, lifting height $h = 1.2\,\text{m}$, slow lift
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the lifting force in joules
**Principle:** In a slow lift the lifting force is equal to the weight and in the direction of motion, so the force is constant over the whole distance. The work is then the product of force and lifting height.
**Formula:** $W = F h$ and $F = G$, so $W = G h$
**Conversions:** The weight is in newtons and the height in metres, so no conversions are needed: $\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = \text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 150\,\text{N} \cdot 1.2\,\text{m} = 180\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $180\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the height $1.2\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Rough estimate: $150\,\text{N} \cdot 1\,\text{m} = 150\,\text{J}$, and the distance is a little over a metre. A weight of $150\,\text{N}$ corresponds to a mass of about $\tfrac{150\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 15\,\text{kg}$, so the work corresponds to lifting a $15\,\text{kg}$ bookcase a little over a metre. $W$ is positive because the lifting force and the motion are both upwards.

## fy3-2-L1-03
**Given:** work $W = 36\,\text{kJ}$, time $t = 12\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the power $P$ of the motor in kilowatts
**Principle:** Power tells you how fast work is done, that is, work per unit time. The motor does the work steadily in the given time, so the average power is the total work divided by the time.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$, so the power is already solved for: $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$
**Conversions:** $36\,\text{kJ} = 36 \cdot 1000\,\text{J} = 36\,000\,\text{J}$, so that the result comes out in watts ($1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s}$). Finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = \dfrac{36\,000\,\text{J}}{12\,\text{s}} = 3000\,\text{W} = 3.000\,\text{kW}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{kW}$, two significant figures like the time $12\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The units kilojoule and second give kilowatts directly: $\tfrac{36\,\text{kJ}}{12\,\text{s}} = 3\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{s}} = 3\,\text{kW}$. With this power you could lift a load of about $300\,\text{kg}$ at a steady one metre per second ($300\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot 1\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \approx 2.9\,\text{kW}$), which suits the motor of a lift.

## fy3-2-L1-04
**Given:** read from the graph, force $F_1 = 25\,\text{N}$ over $0\ldots 6\,\text{m}$ and $F_2 = 10\,\text{N}$ over $6\ldots 10\,\text{m}$, the force is in the direction of motion
**Find:** the total work $W$ done by the force in joules
**Principle:** The work is the area between the $(s,F)$ graph and the $s$-axis. The force is not constant over the whole distance, so the formula $W = F s$ does not apply directly to the whole distance. The area splits into two rectangles in which the force is constant.
**Reading the graph:** The first horizontal segment is on the grid line $F_1 = 25\,\text{N}$ and extends from $s = 0$ to $s = 6\,\text{m}$, where it drops vertically. The second segment is on the line $F_2 = 10\,\text{N}$ and extends from $s = 6\,\text{m}$ to $s = 10\,\text{m}$. The width of the second rectangle is $s_2 = 10\,\text{m} - 6\,\text{m} = 4\,\text{m}$.
**Formula:** $W = W_1 + W_2$, so $W = F_1 s_1 + F_2 s_2$
**Conversions:** The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed: $\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = \text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 25\,\text{N} \cdot 6\,\text{m} + 10\,\text{N} \cdot 4\,\text{m} = 150\,\text{J} + 40\,\text{J} = 190\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $190\,\text{J}$, two significant figures, because the values of the graph were read from the grid lines as whole numbers.
**Reasonableness:** The average force over the whole $10\,\text{m}$ is $\dfrac{190\,\text{J}}{10\,\text{m}} = 19\,\text{N}$, which lies between the smallest ($10\,\text{N}$) and the largest ($25\,\text{N}$) value of the force. If you calculated $25\,\text{N} \cdot 10\,\text{m} = 250\,\text{J}$ for the whole distance, you would get too large a result, because the force drops partway through.

## fy3-2-L1-05
**Given:** force $F = 20\,\text{N}$ (in the direction of motion), speed $v = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$, steady motion
**Find:** the power $P$ of the pulling force in watts
**Principle:** Power is work per unit time. The motion is steady and the force is constant and in the direction of motion, so the distance travelled in one second is $v$ and the work done in one second is $F v$.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$ and $W = F s$, $s = v t$, so $P = \dfrac{F v t}{t} = F v$
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the speed in metres per second, so the product comes directly in watts: $1\,\text{N}\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 1\,\text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = 20\,\text{N} \cdot 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 60\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $60\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the speed $3.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check through time: in one second the sled travels $3.0\,\text{m}$, so the work is $20\,\text{N} \cdot 3.0\,\text{m} = 60\,\text{J}$ and the power is $\tfrac{60\,\text{J}}{1\,\text{s}} = 60\,\text{W}$. A power of a few tens of watts is typical for the pulling power at a brisk walking pace.

## fy3-2-L2-01
**Given:** work $W = 45\,\text{kJ}$, force $F = 1.5\,\text{kN}$ (in the direction of motion)
**Find:** the displacement $s$ of the load in metres
**Principle:** The force is in the direction of motion and constant, so the work is the product of force and distance. From this the distance can be solved when the work and the force are known.
**Formula:** $W = F s$, so $s = \dfrac{W}{F}$
**Conversions:** $45\,\text{kJ} = 45\,000\,\text{J}$ and $1.5\,\text{kN} = 1500\,\text{N}$, so that the units are SI units and $\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{N}} = \text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $s = \dfrac{45\,000\,\text{J}}{1500\,\text{N}} = 30\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $30\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the force $1.5\,\text{kN}$.
**Reasonableness:** Units: $\dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{N}} = \dfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{N}} = \text{m}$. Reverse check: $1500\,\text{N} \cdot 30\,\text{m} = 45\,000\,\text{J}$, as in the given work. A displacement of thirty metres is believable for a tractor pulling a load.

## fy3-2-L2-02
**Given:** power $P = 1.5\,\text{kW}$, time $t = 4.0\,\text{min}$
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the pump in kilojoules
**Principle:** Power is work per unit time. The power of the pump stays constant, so the work is power times time.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$, so $W = P t$
**Conversions:** $1.5\,\text{kW} = 1500\,\text{W}$ and $4.0\,\text{min} = 4.0 \cdot 60\,\text{s} = 240\,\text{s}$, so that the work comes out in joules. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 1500\,\text{W} \cdot 240\,\text{s} = 360\,000\,\text{J} = 360\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $360\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the power $1.5\,\text{kW}$ and the time $4.0\,\text{min}$.
**Reasonableness:** Another route: $1.5\,\text{kW} \cdot \tfrac{4}{60}\,\text{h} = 0.10\,\text{kWh}$, and $1\,\text{kWh} = 3600\,\text{kJ}$, so $0.10 \cdot 3600\,\text{kJ} = 360\,\text{kJ}$. This work could lift $1000\,\text{kg}$ of water to a height of about $37\,\text{m}$ ($\tfrac{360\,000\,\text{J}}{1000\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 37\,\text{m}$).

## fy3-2-L2-03
**Given:** mass $m = 850\,\text{kg}$, lifting height $h = 30\,\text{m}$, slow lift, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the lifting force in kilojoules
**Principle:** In a slow lift the lifting force is equal to the weight and is constant and in the direction of motion. The work is therefore the product of weight and lifting height, and the weight is obtained from the mass.
**Formula:** $W = F h$, $F = G = m g$, so $W = m g h$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and the height in metres, so no conversions are needed before the calculation. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 850\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 30\,\text{m} = 250\,155\,\text{J} = 250.155\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $250\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the height $30\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the element is $850\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \approx 8.3\,\text{kN}$. Rough estimate: $8\,\text{kN} \cdot 30\,\text{m} = 240\,\text{kJ}$, close to the result. A height of thirty metres corresponds to a ten-storey building, and a work of a quarter of a megajoule is believable for a crane.

## fy3-2-L2-04
**Given:** force $F_1 = 10\,\text{N}$ constant over the distance $s_1 = 2.0\,\text{m}$ ($0\text{–}2.0\,\text{m}$); then the force increases steadily from $F_1 = 10\,\text{N}$ to $F_2 = 30\,\text{N}$ over the distance $s_2 = 4.0\,\text{m}$ ($2.0\text{–}6.0\,\text{m}$)
**Find:** the total work $W$ in joules
**Principle:** The force changes, so the formula $W = F s$ does not work for the whole distance. The work is the area under the $(s,F)$ graph, and it can be calculated in parts: the constant-force part is a rectangle and the steadily increasing part is a trapezium.
**Reading the graph:** The graph has a horizontal segment at the height $10\,\text{N}$ from $0\ldots 2.0\,\text{m}$ and, continuing from it, a straight segment from the height $10\,\text{N}$ to the height $30\,\text{N}$ from $2.0\ldots 6.0\,\text{m}$. The rectangle has height $10\,\text{N}$ and width $2.0\,\text{m}$. The parallel sides of the trapezium are $10\,\text{N}$ and $30\,\text{N}$, and its height (the width along the $s$-axis) is $4.0\,\text{m}$.
**Formula:** $W = A_\text{rectangle} + A_\text{trapezium}$, so $W = F_1 s_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(F_1 + F_2)\, s_2$
**Conversions:** The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $W = 10\,\text{N} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (10\,\text{N} + 30\,\text{N}) \cdot 4.0\,\text{m} = 20\,\text{J} + 80\,\text{J} = 100\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $100\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the given values (for example $2.0\,\text{m}$).
**Reasonableness:** The average force over the whole $6.0\,\text{m}$ is $\dfrac{100\,\text{J}}{6.0\,\text{m}} \approx 17\,\text{N}$, which lies between the smallest ($10\,\text{N}$) and greatest ($30\,\text{N}$) values of the force. Rough bounds: at least $10\,\text{N} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} = 60\,\text{J}$ and at most $30\,\text{N} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} = 180\,\text{J}$, and the result lies between them.

## fy3-2-L3-01
**Given:** peak force $F = 40\,\text{N}$; the force increases from zero to $F$ over the distance $s_1 = 0.50\,\text{m}$, stays constant over the distance $s_2 = 1.0\,\text{m}$ and decreases to zero over the distance $s_3 = 0.50\,\text{m}$; total distance $s = 2.0\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the average magnitude of the force with respect to distance, $F_\text{avg}$, in newtons
**Principle:** The force changes, so the work is the area under the $(s,F)$ graph. The average force is the constant force that would do the same work over the same distance, so $W = F_\text{avg}\, s$.
**Reading the graph:** The graph is a trapezium: a rising triangle ($0\ldots 0.50\,\text{m}$, height $40\,\text{N}$), a horizontal part as a rectangle ($0.50\ldots 1.5\,\text{m}$, height $40\,\text{N}$) and a falling triangle ($1.5\ldots 2.0\,\text{m}$, height $40\,\text{N}$). The work is the sum of these three areas.
**Formula:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2} F s_1 + F s_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} F s_3$ and $W = F_\text{avg}\, s$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{W}{s}$
**Conversions:** The forces are in newtons and the distances in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 40\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} + 40\,\text{N} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 40\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} = 10\,\text{J} + 40\,\text{J} + 10\,\text{J} = 60\,\text{J}$; $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{60\,\text{J}}{2.0\,\text{m}} = 30\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $30\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the distance $2.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The average is smaller than the peak value $40\,\text{N}$, because the force is small at the beginning and at the end, but greater than half of the peak, because the force is at its peak for half of the distance. The trapezium formula gives the same work: $\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0\,\text{m} + 1.0\,\text{m}) \cdot 40\,\text{N} = 60\,\text{J}$. The average of a force increasing steadily from zero would be $20\,\text{N}$.

## fy3-2-L3-02
**Given:** mass $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$, falling height $h = 1.5\,\text{m}$, number of drops $n = 20$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the increase in the internal energy of the water $\Delta U$ in joules
**Principle:** Energy is conserved. As it falls, the weight loses an amount of energy equal to the lifting work $m g h$, and by assumption all of this energy is transferred through the paddle wheel to the water as internal energy. Each drop transfers the same energy, so the drops are added together.
**Formula:** $W_1 = m g h$ and $\Delta U = n W_1$, so $\Delta U = n m g h$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and the height in metres, so no conversions are needed. The number of drops $n = 20$ is just a number.
**Substitution:** $W_1 = 2.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} = 29.43\,\text{J}$; $\Delta U = 20 \cdot 29.43\,\text{J} = 588.6\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $590\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and the height $1.5\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Rough estimate: the weight is about $20\,\text{N}$, so one drop gives about $20\,\text{N} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} = 30\,\text{J}$ and 20 drops about $600\,\text{J}$. The result is the same as lifting an object of about $60\,\text{kg}$ by a metre, a small amount of energy, so the temperature of the water changed only a little and the measurement needed accuracy. That is why Joule repeated the drops many times.

## fy3-2-L3-03
**Given:** power of the panel $P = 180\,\text{W}$, mass of water $m = 1000\,\text{kg}$, lifting height $h = 50\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, all the energy goes into lifting
**Find:** the time $t$ in minutes
**Principle:** By assumption the energy produced by the panel is equal to the work needed to lift the water, and there are no losses. The power is constant, so the energy produced is power times time. The work of lifting is $m g h$, because the water is lifted slowly.
**Formula:** $W = m g h$ and $W = P t$, so $t = \dfrac{m g h}{P}$
**Conversions:** The mass, height and power are in SI units, so the time comes out first in seconds. Finally $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 50\,\text{m} = 490\,500\,\text{J}$; $t = \dfrac{490\,500\,\text{J}}{180\,\text{W}} = 2725\,\text{s} = \dfrac{2725}{60}\,\text{min} = 45.42\,\text{min}$
**Answer:** $45\,\text{min}$, two significant figures like the height $50\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** In one hour the panel produces $180\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 648\,\text{kJ}$, which is more than the required $490\,\text{kJ}$, so the time is less than an hour. The ratio $\tfrac{490.5}{648} \approx 0.76$ hours is about $45\,\text{min}$, as in the result. This matches everyday experience of solar electricity.
