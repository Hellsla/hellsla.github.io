Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell

Why does an LED light up only when it is connected one way round, and why does it always need a resistor, although a light bulb can be connected straight to a battery?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain the forward and reverse direction of a diode and the threshold voltage using the characteristic curve.
  • calculate the series resistor, the current and the power of an LED in a series circuit.
  • interpret the $(U, I)$ graph of a solar cell and calculate the power and the efficiency of the cell.

Prior knowledge

  • The voltage of a resistor is $U = RI$, and the characteristic curve of a resistor that obeys Ohm's law is a straight linechapter Resistance and Ohm's law
  • In a series connection the same current flows through every component, and the voltage of the source is shared among themchapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in an electric circuit
  • The power of a component is $P = UI$chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor

The same kit contains a small solar panel marked $6\,\text{V}$ and $200\,\text{mA}$, but the panel does not deliver $1.2\,\text{W}$ at any load.

Optional Review: Ohm's law
The voltage of a resistor is $3.0\,\text{V}$, and a current of $15\,\text{mA}$ flows through it. What is the resistance of the resistor?

  • You divided the voltage by the current in milliamperes. The current is converted to amperes: $15\,\text{mA} = 0.015\,\text{A}$, and $R = 3.0\,\text{V} : 0.015\,\text{A} = 200\,\Omega$.

  • Correct. By Ohm's law $R = \tfrac{U}{I} = 3.0\,\text{V} : 0.015\,\text{A} = 200\,\Omega$ (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law). Today a resistor limits the current of an LED.

  • You multiplied the voltage and the current. Resistance is the voltage divided by the current, $R = \tfrac{U}{I}$: with the same voltage a greater resistance gives a smaller current. The correct value is $200\,\Omega$.

Assumptions of the model

In the forward direction the current of a diode grows exponentially with the voltage: the current is $10\,\text{mA}$ when the voltage of a silicon diode is $0.70\,\text{V}$, of a red LED $1.90\,\text{V}$ and of a blue LED $2.90\,\text{V}$. In the reverse direction the current is zero: the small leakage current is ignored, and the breakdown in the reverse direction is not modelled, although a real LED often withstands only about $5\,\text{V}$ in the reverse direction. The greatest recommended current of the LEDs is $20\,\text{mA}$. The source and the wires are ideal. The solar panel has ten silicon cells in series, and its area is $60\,\text{cm}^2$. When $G = 1000\,\text{W/m}^2$, the short-circuit current is $200\,\text{mA}$ and the open-circuit voltage $6.0\,\text{V}$. The short-circuit current is directly proportional to the light intensity, and the temperature of the cells stays the same. The numbers are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

In the LED circuit the source, the resistor and the diode are in series, so by Kirchhoff's voltage law $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$ and by Ohm's law $U_R = RI$. The current of the diode is

$$I = I_\text{s} \left(e^{U_\text{D}/m} - 1\right)$$

where $m$ is a constant of the component ($0.040$–$0.060\,\text{V}$) and $I_\text{s}$ a very small current chosen so that the current is $10\,\text{mA}$ at the threshold voltage of the component. The operating point is the voltage of the diode for which $U_\text{D} + RI = U$; the program finds it by the bisection method. The power of the diode is $P_\text{D} = U_\text{D} I$. The current of the solar panel is $I = I_\text{L} - I_0 \left(e^{U/m} - 1\right)$, where $I_\text{L}$ is the current produced by the light, directly proportional to the light intensity, and $m = 0.40\,\text{V}$. With a load $R$ the operating point is the voltage for which this current is $\tfrac{U}{R}$. The power is $P = UI$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Semiconductors lie between conductors and insulators

    Metals conduct electricity well, and insulators, such as plastic and glass, hardly at all. A semiconductor, for example silicon, lies between them. Its ability to conduct electricity can be changed by adding very small amounts of other substances to it, and it also depends on temperature and light. Semiconductors are used to make electronic components: diodes, LEDs, solar cells, and the transistors of phones and computers. In this chapter semiconductors are studied as components: what they do in an electric circuit and how their voltage and current depend on each other.

    A diode lets current through in one direction only

    A diode has two different layers of semiconductor, and the boundary between them lets current through in one direction only. The symbol of a diode is a triangle and a bar: the triangle points in the direction in which the current can flow. The terminal on the side of the triangle is the anode and the terminal on the side of the bar is the cathode, which is usually marked on the component with a ring.

    When the anode is connected to the side of the positive terminal of the source, the diode is in the forward direction, and current flows. When the diode is turned round, it is in the reverse direction: practically no current flows, and the whole voltage of the source is across the diode, as long as the voltage stays below the breakdown voltage of the diode. This is why an LED lights up only when it is connected the right way round. The metal wire of a light bulb conducts in both directions, so a bulb lights up either way round. The resistance of the wire of a bulb grows as it heats up, so the bulb limits its own current.

    The threshold voltage and the characteristic curve

    A diode does not obey Ohm's law. Even in the forward direction the current is almost zero until the voltage of the diode reaches the threshold voltage. After that a small increase in voltage increases the current a lot. The threshold voltage of a silicon diode is about $0.6$–$0.7\,\text{V}$.

    The characteristic curve of a diode is usually drawn with the voltage $U_\text{D}$ on the horizontal axis and the current $I$ on the vertical axis, the other way round than in the chapter Resistance and Ohm's law. From such a $(U, I)$ graph you can see directly at which voltage the current starts to flow. A negative voltage on the graph means the reverse direction, and there the current is zero.

    An LED changes electrical energy into light

    An LED, a light-emitting diode, is a diode that emits light when current flows through it in the forward direction. The colour of the light depends on the semiconductor material of the LED, and the colour shows also in the threshold voltage. When the current is a few milliamperes, the voltage of a red LED is about $1.8$–$2.0\,\text{V}$, of a green one about $2.2\,\text{V}$ and of a blue or white one about $2.9$–$3.3\,\text{V}$. An LED changes a greater part of the electrical energy into light than a light bulb, in which most of the electrical energy becomes internal energy of the wire and is transferred to the surroundings as heat.

    Why an LED needs a series resistor

    When an LED is lit, its voltage changes only a little although the current changes a lot. If an LED were connected straight to a $4.5\,\text{V}$ battery, almost the whole $4.5\,\text{V}$ would be across the LED, which is clearly above its threshold voltage. Only the internal resistance of the battery and the wires would limit the current, so the current would grow so large that the LED would overheat and break. This is why a series resistor is connected in series with an LED. The part of the voltage of the source that is not across the LED is left across the resistor, and the resistor determines the current. The resistance of the wire of a light bulb grows as the wire heats up, so a bulb limits its own current.

    A solar cell is a direct-voltage source

    A solar cell is a large semiconductor component that produces electric current in light. The light brings energy to the cell, and the cell separates charges so that a voltage appears between its terminals. The cell has a positive and a negative terminal, and like a battery it gives direct current: in the external circuit the current always flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. One silicon cell gives only about $0.5$–$0.6\,\text{V}$, so a solar panel has many cells in series. A cell does not store energy: in the dark it gives no current, so a solar lamp also has a battery.

    The $(U, I)$ graph of a cell is not a falling straight line like that of a battery (chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance). When the terminals are connected with a conductor, the short-circuit current $I_\text{sc}$ flows, and it is directly proportional to the light intensity. When no current is drawn, there is an open-circuit voltage $U_\text{oc}$ between the terminals, and it changes only a little with the light. Between these the current stays almost constant at first and then falls steeply. The small panel in the figure has ten cells in series.

    Series resistor and power of a solar cell

    Sizing the series resistor

    When the source, the series resistor and the LED are in series, the same current $I$ flows through all of them. By Kirchhoff's voltage law the voltage of the source is shared between the resistor and the LED, $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$ (chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in an electric circuit). The resistor obeys Ohm's law, $U_R = RI$, so the resistance of the series resistor is

    $$R = \frac{U - U_\text{D}}{I}$$

    where $R$ is the resistance of the series resistor ($\Omega$), $U$ the voltage of the source ($\text{V}$), $U_\text{D}$ the voltage of the LED or the diode ($\text{V}$) and $I$ the current ($\text{A}$). For the voltage of the LED the value given by the manufacturer is used, because it changes only a little with the current. When the resistor is known, the current is solved from the same equation: $I = \tfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{R}$.

    The power of a component is its voltage times its current (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor): the power of the LED is $P_\text{D} = U_\text{D} I$ and the power of the resistor $P_R = U_R I$. In the resistor electrical energy becomes internal energy of the resistor, so the resistor is chosen so that it withstands this power. In the reverse direction $I = 0$, so $U_R = RI = 0$ and the voltage of the diode is the whole voltage $U$ of the source.

    The power of a solar cell

    The electric power given by a solar cell or panel is

    $$P = U I$$

    where $P$ is the power ($\text{W}$), $U$ the voltage between the terminals of the cell ($\text{V}$) and $I$ the current ($\text{A}$). In a short circuit $U = 0$ and at open circuit $I = 0$, so in both the power is zero. Between them is the maximum power point, the knee of the curve, where the power is greatest. On a $(U, I)$ graph the power is the area of the rectangle between the origin and a point of the curve.

    The greatest power of the panel in the figure is about $0.92\,\text{W}$, when the voltage is about $5.0\,\text{V}$ and the current $184\,\text{mA}$. The load resistance is then $5.0\,\text{V} : 0.184\,\text{A} \approx 27\,\Omega$. The maximum power point changes with the light intensity, so the control electronics of solar power plants and chargers keep seeking it.

    Efficiency

    The efficiency of a cell is the electric power it gives divided by the power of the light arriving at the cell (chapter Efficiency). The power of the light is the light intensity times the area of the cell, and the electric power $P$ is the power of the cell at the maximum power point, so

    $$\eta = \frac{P}{G A}$$

    where $\eta$ is the efficiency, $P$ the electric power of the cell ($\text{W}$), $G$ the light intensity, that is, the radiant power per area ($\text{W/m}^2$), and $A$ the area of the cell ($\text{m}^2$). On a clear summer day, when the sun shines perpendicularly onto the cell, $G$ is about $1000\,\text{W/m}^2$. The area of the panel in the figure is $60\,\text{cm}^2 = 0.0060\,\text{m}^2$, so the light arriving at it is $1000\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot 0.0060\,\text{m}^2 = 6.0\,\text{W}$, and the efficiency is $0.92\,\text{W} : 6.0\,\text{W} \approx 15\,\%$. The efficiency of ordinary silicon cells is about $15$–$22\,\%$.

    Common misconception

    Many calculate the series resistor by dividing the whole voltage of the source by the current, $R = \tfrac{U}{I}$. But across the resistor there is only the part of the voltage that is not across the LED, $U - U_\text{D}$. A resistor that is too large gives too small a current, and the LED shines dimly.

    Another mistake is to think that a diode is a resistor with a constant resistance. The ratio of the voltage to the current of a diode, $U_\text{D} : I$, changes strongly with the current: below the threshold voltage hardly any current flows, and above it a small increase in voltage increases the current a lot. A third mistake is to calculate the power of a solar cell as the product of the short-circuit current and the open-circuit voltage. They do not occur at the same time: in a short circuit the voltage is zero and at open circuit the current is zero.

    Summary

    • Semiconductors, such as silicon, conduct electricity worse than metals and better than insulators. Diodes, LEDs and solar cells are made of them.
    • A diode lets current through in one direction only. In the forward direction the anode is on the side of the positive terminal of the source and current flows; in the reverse direction no current flows and the whole voltage of the source is across the diode.
    • Current starts to flow when the voltage of the diode reaches the threshold voltage: about $0.6$–$0.7\,\text{V}$ for a silicon diode and about $1.8$–$3.3\,\text{V}$ for LEDs, depending on the colour. A diode does not obey Ohm's law.
    • A series resistor is connected in series with an LED to limit the current: $R = \tfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{I}$.
    • A solar cell is a direct-voltage source. The short-circuit current is directly proportional to the light intensity, and the open-circuit voltage changes only a little. The power $P = UI$ is greatest at the maximum power point, and the efficiency is $\eta = \tfrac{P}{GA}$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      The red LED of a bicycle's rear light is run on a $6.0\,\text{V}$ battery. The voltage of the LED is $2.0\,\text{V}$ when its current is $20\,\text{mA}$. What series resistor is needed, and how large is the power that becomes internal energy in the resistor?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The voltage of the source $U = 6.0\,\text{V}$, the voltage of the LED $U_\text{D} = 2.0\,\text{V}$ and the current $I = 20\,\text{mA}$. We are asked for the resistance $R$ of the series resistor and the power $P_R$ of the resistor.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The battery, the resistor and the LED are in series, so the same current flows through all of them. The voltage of the battery is shared between the resistor and the LED, $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$, and the voltage of the resistor is $U_R = RI$. The power of the resistor is its voltage times the current.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} R &= \frac{U - U_\text{D}}{I} \\ P_R &= (U - U_\text{D}) I \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Conversions. The current in amperes:

      $I$$= 20\,\text{mA}$
      $= 20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{A}$
      $= 0.020\,\text{A}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} R &= \frac{U - U_\text{D}}{I} \\ \phantom{R} &= \frac{6.0\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V}}{0.020\,\text{A}} \\ \phantom{R} &= 200\,\Omega \\ P_R &= 4.0\,\text{V} \cdot 0.020\,\text{A} \\ \phantom{P_R} &= 0.080\,\text{W} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The resistance of the series resistor is $200\,\Omega$, and the power that becomes internal energy in the resistor is $0.080\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the voltages and the current.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The power of the LED is $2.0\,\text{V} \cdot 0.020\,\text{A} = 0.040\,\text{W}$, and the total $0.080\,\text{W} + 0.040\,\text{W} = 0.12\,\text{W}$ is the same as the power of the battery $6.0\,\text{V} \cdot 0.020\,\text{A}$. If a shop has only $220\,\Omega$ resistors, the current is $4.0\,\text{V} : 220\,\Omega \approx 18\,\text{mA}$, slightly smaller, so the LED is not overloaded.

      Find the error

      A red LED is used with a $9.0\,\text{V}$ battery. The voltage of the LED is $2.0\,\text{V}$, and the desired current is $20\,\text{mA}$.A student calculates the series resistor like this:

      $$R = \frac{9.0\,\text{V}}{0.020\,\text{A}} = 450\,\Omega$$

      Where is the error?

      • In Ohm's law the current is substituted in amperes, which gives the resistance in ohms; $0.020\,\text{A}$ is correct. The error is that the whole voltage of the battery is not across the resistor, but $9.0\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V} = 7.0\,\text{V}$.

      • The voltage of the LED does not add to the voltage of the resistor but subtracts from it: by Kirchhoff's voltage law $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$, so $U_R = 9.0\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V} = 7.0\,\text{V}$ and $R = 350\,\Omega$.

      • Correct. The voltage of the battery is shared between the LED and the resistor, so $R = \tfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{I} = 7.0\,\text{V} : 0.020\,\text{A} = 350\,\Omega$. With the student's $450\,\Omega$ resistor the current would be only $7.0\,\text{V} : 450\,\Omega \approx 16\,\text{mA}$, and the LED would shine dimmer.

      Apply to a new situation

      A solar panel of a cabin gives its greatest power when its voltage is $18.0\,\text{V}$ and its current $5.50\,\text{A}$. The panel is connected straight to a $12\,\text{V}$ battery without control electronics, and the battery holds the voltage of the panel at $12.8\,\text{V}$. The current of the panel is then $5.80\,\text{A}$.

      How much smaller is the power of the panel when connected to the battery than at the maximum power point?

      W
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      The power of the panel is its voltage times its current. Calculate the power separately at both operating points.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      At the maximum power point $P_1 = 18.0\,\text{V} \cdot 5.50\,\text{A}$ and connected to the battery $P_2 = 12.8\,\text{V} \cdot 5.80\,\text{A}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $P_1 = 99.0\,\text{W}$ and $P_2 = 74.24\,\text{W}$. What is asked is their difference.

      Show the solution

      Given

      at the maximum power point $U_1 = 18.0\,\text{V}$ and $I_1 = 5.50\,\text{A}$, connected to the battery $U_2 = 12.8\,\text{V}$ and $I_2 = 5.80\,\text{A}$

      Find

      the decrease in power $\Delta P$ in watts

      Principle

      A solar panel is a direct-voltage source, and its power is the voltage times the current. The battery determines the voltage of the panel, so the panel works at a different point of its $(U, I)$ curve than the maximum power point. The current there is slightly greater, but the voltage is much smaller.

      Formula

      $P_1 = U_1 I_1$ and $P_2 = U_2 I_2$, from which $\Delta P = U_1 I_1 - U_2 I_2$

      Conversions

      None needed: the voltages are in volts and the currents in amperes, and $\text{V} \cdot \text{A} = \text{W}$.

      Substitution

      $\Delta P$$= 18.0\,\text{V} \cdot 5.50\,\text{A} - 12.8\,\text{V} \cdot 5.80\,\text{A}$
      $= 99.0\,\text{W} - 74.24\,\text{W}$
      $= 24.76\,\text{W}$

      Answer

      $24.8\,\text{W}$

      Three significant figures, because the given voltages and currents have three significant figures and the precision of the difference is a tenth of a watt.

      Reasonableness

      Connected to the battery the panel gives about $74\,\text{W} : 99\,\text{W} \approx 75\,\%$ of its greatest power, so a quarter is lost. The current rose only about $5\,\%$, but the voltage fell about $30\,\%$. This is why solar chargers have a controller that seeks the maximum power point.

      1. Concept

      A silicon diode and a bulb are connected in series to a battery. The cathode of the diode, the terminal on the side of the bar, is on the side of the positive terminal of the battery.Which of the following is true?

      • Correct. In the forward direction the anode is on the side of the positive terminal. Now the diode is the other way round, so no current flows, the voltage of the bulb is zero and the whole voltage of the battery is across the diode.

      • A diode does not reverse the direction of the current but blocks it. When the cathode is on the side of the positive terminal, the diode is in the reverse direction, and no current flows in either direction.

      • This would happen in the forward direction: the diode would take about $0.7\,\text{V}$ and the bulb would get the rest. In the reverse direction no current flows at all, so the bulb does not light.

      • The diode is in the forward direction when its anode, the terminal on the side of the triangle, is on the side of the positive terminal. Now the cathode is on the side of the positive terminal, so the diode is in the reverse direction and no current flows.

      2. Graph

      The graph shows the $(U, I)$ curve of a solar panel when the light stays the same. At which voltage is the power given by the panel greatest?

      • Correct. The power is the area of the rectangle $U \cdot I$. At about $5\,\text{V}$ the current is still almost $200\,\text{mA}$, so the product $UI$ is greatest, about $0.9\,\text{W}$. This is the knee of the curve, the maximum power point.

      Optional 3. Explain the series resistor

      A friend connects a red LED straight to a $4.5\,\text{V}$ battery, because “even a light bulb does not need a resistor”. The LED flashes and goes out for good. Explain what happened and why a light bulb withstands the same connection.

      Model answer

      The voltage of the LED in the forward direction is about $2\,\text{V}$, and above that a small increase in voltage increases the current very much. Without a series resistor almost the whole voltage of the battery was across the LED, and only the internal resistance of the battery and the wires limited the current. If their resistance is about $1\,\Omega$, the current is of the order of $(4.5\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V}) : 1\,\Omega \approx 2.5\,\text{A}$, over a hundred times the recommended $20\,\text{mA}$ of the LED. The LED overheated and broke. The filament of a light bulb obeys Ohm's law approximately, and its resistance grows as the filament heats up, so the bulb limits its own current. With an LED a series resistor is therefore needed, which takes the extra voltage and determines the current.

        Exit ticket

        Finally, two quick questions. Answer first by yourself and open the answer only after that.

        1. What does it mean that a diode is connected in the reverse direction, and what is the voltage across the diode then?
          Answer

          In the reverse direction the cathode of the diode is on the side of the positive terminal of the source, and practically no current flows. Since $I = 0$, the voltage of a resistor in series is $RI = 0$, and the whole voltage of the source is across the diode.

        2. The short-circuit current of a solar panel is $5.0\,\text{A}$ and the open-circuit voltage $21\,\text{V}$. Why can the power of the panel not be $5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 21\,\text{V} = 105\,\text{W}$?
          Answer

          The greatest current and the greatest voltage do not occur at the same time: in a short circuit the voltage is zero and at open circuit the current is zero, so the power is zero in both. The greatest power is obtained at the maximum power point, where both the voltage and the current are slightly smaller than their greatest values, so the power stays below $105\,\text{W}$.

        1. Exercise 14-1Core

          The voltage of a red LED is $2.0\,\text{V}$, and a current of $15\,\text{mA}$ flows through it. What is the power of the LED? Give the answer in milliwatts.

          mW
          Answer

          $30\,\text{mW}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          voltage of the LED $U_\text{D} = 2.0\,\text{V}$, current $I = 15\,\text{mA}$

          Find

          the power $P_\text{D}$ of the LED in milliwatts

          Principle

          The electric power of a component is its voltage times the current flowing through it. This holds also for an LED, although it does not obey Ohm's law, because power is always the energy that the charge gives up in the component per unit time.

          Formula

          $P_\text{D} = U_\text{D} I$

          Conversions

          $I$$= 15\,\text{mA}$
          $= 15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{A}$
          $= 0.015\,\text{A}$

          Substitution

          $P_\text{D}$$= 2.0\,\text{V} \cdot 0.015\,\text{A}$
          $= 0.030\,\text{W}$
          $= 30\,\text{mW}$

          Answer

          $30\,\text{mW}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage and the current.

          Reasonableness

          The unit is consistent: $\text{V} \cdot \text{mA} = \text{mW}$, and $2 \cdot 15 = 30$. The power of an indicator LED is tens of milliwatts, much smaller than that of a light bulb, as we know from experience.

        2. Exercise 14-2Core

          A white LED is connected to a $5.0\,\text{V}$ USB power supply through a series resistor. The voltage of the LED is $3.1\,\text{V}$, and the desired current is $20\,\text{mA}$.

          What series resistor is needed? Give the answer in ohms.

          Ω
          Answer

          $95\,\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          voltage of the source $U = 5.0\,\text{V}$, voltage of the LED $U_\text{D} = 3.1\,\text{V}$, current $I = 20\,\text{mA}$

          Find

          the resistance $R$ of the series resistor in ohms

          Principle

          The source, the resistor and the LED are in series, so the same current flows through all of them. By Kirchhoff's voltage law the voltage of the source is shared: $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$. The resistor obeys Ohm's law, $U_R = RI$.

          Formula

          $U = RI + U_\text{D}$, from which $R = \dfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{I}$

          Conversions

          $I$$= 20\,\text{mA}$
          $= 20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{A}$
          $= 0.020\,\text{A}$

          Substitution

          $R$$= \dfrac{5.0\,\text{V} - 3.1\,\text{V}}{0.020\,\text{A}}$
          $= \dfrac{1.9\,\text{V}}{0.020\,\text{A}}$
          $= 95\,\Omega$

          Answer

          $95\,\Omega$

          Two significant figures like the difference of the voltages $1.9\,\text{V}$ and the current.

          Reasonableness

          Check: $95\,\Omega \cdot 0.020\,\text{A} = 1.9\,\text{V}$, and $1.9\,\text{V} + 3.1\,\text{V} = 5.0\,\text{V}$. The voltage of a white LED is large, so only a little voltage is left for the resistor and the resistor is small.

        1. Exercise 14-6Core

          A student connects a red LED and a $470\,\Omega$ series resistor in series to a $9.0\,\text{V}$ battery, but the LED is accidentally in the reverse direction. Assume that no current flows through the LED in the reverse direction.

          What is the voltage across the LED? Give the answer in volts.

          V
          Answer

          $9.0\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          voltage of the battery $U = 9.0\,\text{V}$, series resistor $R = 470\,\Omega$, LED in the reverse direction; assumption: no current flows in the reverse direction

          Find

          the voltage $U_\text{D}$ of the LED in volts

          Principle

          In the reverse direction practically no current flows through the diode. Since the LED and the resistor are in series, the current of the resistor is also zero, so by Ohm's law the voltage of the resistor is zero. By Kirchhoff's voltage law the whole voltage of the battery is then across the LED.

          Formula

          $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$ and $U_R = RI$, so with the current $I = 0$ we get $U_\text{D} = U - RI = U$

          Conversions

          None needed: the current is zero, so the value of the resistor does not need converting.

          Substitution

          $U_\text{D}$$= 9.0\,\text{V} - 470\,\Omega \cdot 0\,\text{A}$
          $= 9.0\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $9.0\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage of the battery.

          Reasonableness

          The series resistor does not protect an LED in the reverse direction, because there is no voltage across the resistor. An ordinary LED often withstands only about $5\,\text{V}$ in the reverse direction, so $9.0\,\text{V}$ can break it; this is why the polarity is checked before connecting.

          All the components are in series, so the same current flows through them. The voltage of the battery is shared between the resistor and both LEDs: $U = U_R + 2U_\text{D}$. The voltage of the resistor is $U_R = RI$.

          $U = RI + 2U_\text{D}$, from which $R = \dfrac{U - 2U_\text{D}}{I}$

        3. Exercise 14-8

          The area of a solar panel on the roof of a detached house is $1.6\,\text{m}^2$. When the light intensity is $800\,\text{W/m}^2$, the panel gives an electric power of $260\,\text{W}$.

          What is the efficiency of the panel? Give the answer as a percentage.

          %
          Answer

          $20\,\%$

          Show the solution

          Given

          area $A = 1.6\,\text{m}^2$, light intensity $G = 800\,\text{W/m}^2$, electric power $P = 260\,\text{W}$

          Find

          the efficiency $\eta$ as a percentage

          Principle

          Efficiency is the useful power divided by the power taken in. The panel receives the power of the light, which is the light intensity times the area, and the useful power is the electric power given by the panel.

          Formula

          $\eta = \dfrac{P}{GA}$

          Conversions

          Substitution

          $\eta$$= \dfrac{260\,\text{W}}{800\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot 1.6\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{260\,\text{W}}{1280\,\text{W}}$
          $= 0.203$
          $= 20.3\,\%$

          Answer

          $20\,\%$

          Two significant figures like the area $1.6\,\text{m}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          About $1300\,\text{W}$ of light arrives at the panel, and about a fifth of it becomes electricity. The efficiency is in the range of ordinary silicon cells, $15$–$22\,\%$.

        1. Exercise 14-10

          A flashlight has a red LED with a voltage of $2.0\,\text{V}$ and a $120\,\Omega$ series resistor. The voltage of new batteries is $4.5\,\text{V}$, and in use it falls to $3.6\,\text{V}$. Assume that the voltage of the LED stays the same.

          By what percentage does the current of the LED decrease when the voltage of the batteries falls like this? Give the answer as a percentage.

          %
          Answer

          $36\,\%$

          Show the solution

          Given

          voltage of the LED $U_\text{D} = 2.0\,\text{V}$, series resistor $R = 120\,\Omega$, voltage of the batteries first $U_1 = 4.5\,\text{V}$ and finally $U_2 = 3.6\,\text{V}$

          Find

          the decrease of the current as a percentage of the original current

          Principle

          By assumption the voltage of the LED stays the same, so the whole change in voltage falls on the resistor. The current is obtained from Kirchhoff's voltage law and Ohm's law.           Formula

          $I = \dfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{R}$, so $I_1 = \dfrac{U_1 - U_\text{D}}{R}$, $I_2 = \dfrac{U_2 - U_\text{D}}{R}$ and the relative change is $\dfrac{I_1 - I_2}{I_1}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the currents are divided by each other, so the unit cancels. The ratio is changed to a percentage by multiplying by a hundred.

          Substitution

          $I_1$$= \dfrac{4.5\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V}}{120\,\Omega}$
          $= 0.02083\,\text{A}$
          $I_2$$= \dfrac{3.6\,\text{V} - 2.0\,\text{V}}{120\,\Omega}$
          $= 0.01333\,\text{A}$
          $\dfrac{I_1 - I_2}{I_1}$$= \dfrac{0.00750\,\text{A}}{0.02083\,\text{A}}$
          $= 0.36$

          Answer

          $36\,\%$

          Two significant figures like the voltages.

          Reasonableness

          The voltage of the batteries fell by only $0.9\,\text{V} : 4.5\,\text{V} = 20\,\%$, but the voltage of the resistor fell from $2.5\,\text{V}$ to $1.6\,\text{V}$, that is, by $36\,\%$. This is why an LED flashlight clearly dims when the batteries start to run down.

        3. Exercise 14-12

          An LED strip has many identical groups in parallel, and each group has three white LEDs in series and a series resistor. The strip is connected to a voltage of $12\,\text{V}$. The voltage of each LED is $3.0\,\text{V}$, and the current of each group is $20\,\text{mA}$.

          %
          Answer

          Given

          Find

          Formula

          $U_R = U - 3U_\text{D}$ and the share is $\dfrac{U_R I}{UI}$, so the share is $\dfrac{U - 3U_\text{D}}{U}$

          Conversions

          $= 0.25$
          $= 25\,\%$

          $25\,\%$

          Two significant figures like the voltage of the LED $3.0\,\text{V}$.
