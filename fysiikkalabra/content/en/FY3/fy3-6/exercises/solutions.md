## fy3-6-L1-01
**Given:** water, $m = 1.8\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 12\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 96\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the water, in megajoules
**Principle:** The water warms and its phase does not change, so the heat transferred to it is proportional to the mass and to the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $Q = c m \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, so $\Delta T = 96\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 12\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 84\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1.8\,\text{kg} \cdot 84\,\text{K} = 633\,528\,\text{J} = 0.633\,528\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $0.63\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures as in the mass $1.8\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Heating one kilogram by one kelvin takes about $4\,\text{kJ}$, so heating almost two kilograms by about $80\,\text{K}$ takes about $4\,\text{kJ} \cdot 2 \cdot 80 \approx 0.6\,\text{MJ}$. $Q$ is positive because the water warms.

**Principle:** When the phase does not change, the change in temperature is $\Delta T = \dfrac{Q}{c m}$. With the same $Q$ and $m$, $\Delta T$ depends only on the specific heat capacity $c$: the greater $c$ is, the smaller $\Delta T$ is.
**Model answer:** The specific heat capacity of water, $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, is greater than that of sand, $800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, so the same quantity of heat raises the temperature of the water less than that of the sand. The explanation is not density, because the masses are the same and density does not appear in the formula. Reflection does not explain the difference either, because in the task both receive the same quantity of heat. The substance does matter, since $c$ is part of the formula $Q = c m \Delta T$.

**Find:** the equilibrium temperature $T$
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: when no heat is transferred to the mug or the air, the heat given out by the hot water is equal to the heat received by the cold water. At the end both have the same temperature $T$.
**Formula:** $c m_1 (T_1 - T) = c m_2 (T - T_2)$. The same $c$ cancels, and $T$ can be solved: $T = \dfrac{m_1 T_1 + m_2 T_2}{m_1 + m_2}$
**Conversions:** The grams do not need to be converted to kilograms, because the mass is in both the numerator and the denominator and the unit cancels. The temperatures can be substituted in degrees Celsius: the equilibrium equation contains only temperature differences, and in the solved formula the sum of the weights is in the denominator, so shifting the scale shifts the result by the same amount.
**Substitution:** $T = \dfrac{160\,\text{g} \cdot 95\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 40\,\text{g} \cdot 8.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{160\,\text{g} + 40\,\text{g}} = \dfrac{15\,200 + 320}{200}\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 77.6\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $78\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures as in the temperature $95\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the mass $40\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** The result is between the initial temperatures and close to $95\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, because there is four times as much hot water. The energy balance holds: $160\,\text{g} \cdot 17.4\,\text{K} = 40\,\text{g} \cdot 69.6\,\text{K}$.
