Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect

Why does the siren of a passing ambulance sound as if it drops in pitch just as it goes by, even though the siren itself keeps sounding exactly the same?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • describe pitch and timbre using frequency and the harmonic series, and classify infrasound and ultrasound.
  • make an FFT graph of a sound and read the fundamental frequency and the overtones from it.
  • explain beats and the Doppler effect and calculate the frequency heard.

Prior knowledge

  • Sound is a longitudinal wave, and the speed of a wave is $v = f\lambda$chapter How waves form and travel
  • When waves meet, their displacements add upchapter When waves combine: superposition and interference
  • The natural frequencies of a string and of a tube open at both ends are multiples of the fundamental frequencychapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies

1Think about it

You are standing by the road when an ambulance drives past with its siren sounding. As the car approaches, the siren sounds bright and high. Just as the car passes you, the sound drops clearly lower and stays low as the car moves away. To the driver of the ambulance, the siren sounds the same all the time.

How can the motion of the car change the pitch you hear, even though the siren itself does not change?

Optional Review: natural frequencies of a string
The fundamental frequency of a guitar string is $110\,\text{Hz}$. At what other frequencies can a standing wave form on the string?

  • The fundamental frequency is the lowest natural frequency of the string: the longest wave that fits on the string is $\lambda_1 = 2L$. The other natural frequencies are higher than it, whole-number multiples of the fundamental frequency.

  • Correct. The natural frequencies of a string fixed at both ends are whole-number multiples of the fundamental frequency, $f_n = n f_1$ (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies). Today we will see that the string sounds at all of these at the same time and that they decide the timbre of the sound.

  • Plucking harder changes the amplitude, not which standing waves fit on the string. The fixed ends are nodes, so a standing wave forms only at the natural frequencies $f_n = n f_1$.

  • Only the odd multiples belong to a tube closed at one end, which has a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end. Both ends of a string are nodes, so any whole number of half wavelengths fits on it and $f_n = n f_1$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$

How to use

Choose the Situation. When the source moves, the loudspeaker travels to the right towards listener A and away from listener B. When the listeners move, the loudspeaker is at rest and both listeners travel to the left: A towards the loudspeaker, B away from it. Adjust the frequency $f_0$ and the speed of the source. The grey circles are wavefronts, that is, compressions, and the orange ring at a listener's ear glows whenever a wavefront arrives. The readings show the frequency and the wavelength each listener hears in their direction, and the frequency bar below shows $f_\text{B}$, $f_0$ and $f_\text{A}$. The picture starts from the beginning, before the source and the listener meet. Speed changes only the pace of the animation.

Assumptions of the model

The speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$ relative to still air, and there is no wind. The source and the listeners move along the same straight line at a steady speed that is less than the speed of sound. The picture is slowed down by a factor of about $400$, and in it one metre is $100$ pixels. Sound does not weaken on the way, and the model has no reflections. The frequencies are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

Wavefront $k$ leaves at the time $t_k = \tfrac{k}{f_0}$ from the point where the source is at that moment and travels at speed $v$. For two successive wavefronts the simulation solves the moment when the wavefront reaches a moving or a stationary listener. The frequency heard is one divided by the difference of these arrival times. The Doppler formula is not used; the result comes from the geometry of the wavefronts. The wavelength is the distance of two successive wavefronts at the same moment in the direction of the listener.

      Two tuning forks sound at the same time, and a microphone records their combined sound for one second. The beat frequency is how many times per second the sound grows louder.       Hz
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      A loud moment is where the filled area is at its widest. At which moments are there such points?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The time between two successive loud moments is the period of the beats $T_\text{h}$, and $f_\text{h} = \tfrac{1}{T_\text{h}}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      from the graph, the loud moments (filled area at its widest) are $t = 0$, $0.25$, $0.50$, $0.75$ and $1.00\,\text{s}$

      Find

      the beat frequency $f_\text{h}$ in hertz

      Principle

      Two sounds of almost the same frequency reinforce each other when they are in phase and weaken each other when they are in opposite phase. The time between the loud moments is the period of the beats $T_\text{h}$, and the number of loud moments per second is $f_\text{h}$.

      Reading the graph

      The filled area is at its widest at the moments $0\,\text{s}$, $0.25\,\text{s}$, $0.50\,\text{s}$, $0.75\,\text{s}$ and $1.00\,\text{s}$. So there are four intervals in a second, and the time between two successive loud moments is $T_\text{h} = 0.25\,\text{s}$.

      Formula

      $f_\text{h} = \dfrac{1}{T_\text{h}}$

      Conversions

      The time is already in seconds, so the frequency comes out in hertz ($\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} = \text{Hz}$).

      Substitution

      $f_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{1}{0.25\,\text{s}}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{Hz}$

      Answer

      $4.0\,\text{Hz}$

      Two significant figures like the time $0.25\,\text{s}$ read from the graph.

      Reasonableness

      The graph has four narrowing points per second and four intervals between loud moments, which corresponds to four beats per second. If one of the tuning forks is $440\,\text{Hz}$, the other is, by $f_\text{h} = |f_1 - f_2|$, either $436\,\text{Hz}$ or $444\,\text{Hz}$; the difference is less than $10\,\text{Hz}$, so the beats are clearly audible.

      4Theory summary

      Key idea

      Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave

      The cone of a loudspeaker or the string of a guitar vibrates and pushes the air particles alternately forward and backward. The particles do not travel along with the sound. They vibrate in place along the direction in which the wave travels, so sound in air is a longitudinal wave (chapter How waves form and travel). Where the particles bunch together, a compression forms, where the pressure is slightly higher than the normal air pressure $p_0$. Between the compressions lie rarefactions, where the pressure is lower. The pressure variation is small: in ordinary speech it is about $0.02\,\text{Pa}$, while the air pressure is about $101\,\text{kPa}$. Sound needs a medium such as a gas, a liquid or a solid. In a vacuum, sound does not travel.

      The distance between two successive compressions is the wavelength $\lambda$. As for all waves, the speed of sound satisfies $v = f\lambda$, where $f$ is the frequency (chapter How waves form and travel). When sound passes from one substance to another, its frequency stays the same, because the particles at the boundary vibrate in step with the incoming wave. The speed and the wavelength, however, change.

      The speed of sound

      The speed of sound depends on the medium: the less a substance can be compressed and the lower its density, the faster sound travels in it. Water is much denser than air, but it is so hard to compress that sound travels in water more than four times as fast as in air. In a gas the speed increases as the temperature rises, because the particles move faster and pass the pressure variation on faster. In air the speed increases by about $0.6\,\text{m/s}$ for every degree Celsius. In this chapter we use the value $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$ for air, which corresponds to a temperature of about $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, as in the earlier chapters.

      Medium $v$ (m/s)
      air, $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ 331
      air, $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ 340
      air, $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ 343
      water, $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ 1480
      steel about 5900

      Determining the speed of sound

      The speed of sound is determined by measuring how long sound takes to travel a known distance. In the echo method, two wooden blocks are clapped together at a distance $d$ from a wall, and the time $t$ after which the echo returns is measured. The sound travels to the wall and back, a distance $2d$, so $v = \tfrac{2d}{t}$. A more accurate value comes from two microphones a distance $d$ apart on the same straight line as the sound source. The measuring program records the signals of both microphones, and the difference $\Delta t$ in the arrival times of the sound is read from the graph, giving $v = \tfrac{d}{\Delta t}$. A third way is a resonance tube, where the wavelength is obtained from the length of a standing wave (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies).

      Pitch and the range of hearing

      Pitch is determined by frequency: the higher the frequency, the higher the sound. When the frequency doubles, the sound rises by an octave. For example, the tuning note A is $440\,\text{Hz}$ and the A an octave higher is $880\,\text{Hz}$. A young person hears frequencies from about $20\,\text{Hz}$ to about $20\,\text{kHz}$, and the highest audible frequency falls with age. The wavelengths of audible sounds in air range from about $17\,\text{m}$ to $1.7\,\text{cm}$. Sound lower than the range of hearing is infrasound and higher is ultrasound. They are longitudinal waves just like audible sound, but the human ear does not sense them.

      Infrasound is produced, for example, by earthquakes, thunder and volcanic eruptions, and elephants communicate partly with infrasound. Infrasound is weakly absorbed in air, so it can travel hundreds of kilometres. Bats and dolphins use ultrasound for echolocation: the short wavelength is reflected even by small targets such as insects. In technology, ultrasound is used, for example, in cleaning equipment, in looking for flaws in materials and in the echo sounders of boats. Medical ultrasound imaging comes later in this course, and the loudness of sound and decibels are covered in the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale.

      Timbre and the harmonic series

      A flute, a violin and a singer can produce the same note, and yet they sound different. The difference is in the timbre of the sound. The string or the air column of an instrument vibrates at several of its natural frequencies at once (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies). The lowest of them is the fundamental frequency $f_1$, and the higher ones are overtones. In a string and in a tube open at both ends the overtones are whole-number multiples of the fundamental frequency, and these frequencies form the harmonic series

      $$f_n = n f_1, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

      where $f_n$ is the $n$th frequency of the series (Hz) and $f_1$ is the fundamental frequency (Hz). We hear the pitch from the fundamental frequency. The timbre depends on how strong the overtones are compared with the fundamental frequency. A tuning fork produces an almost pure sine wave that contains only the fundamental frequency.

      The FFT spectrum

      A microphone measures the variation of air pressure as a function of time. From this series of measurements the measuring program calculates the spectrum of the sound, that is, the FFT graph (Fast Fourier Transform). The spectrum shows which frequencies are in the sound and how strong they are. Each sinusoidal component of the sound appears as a peak at its own frequency, in the same way as in the chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference a complicated wave was built as a sum of sine waves. In the spectrum of an instrument the peaks are at the frequencies of the harmonic series, so the spacing of neighbouring peaks is the fundamental frequency. The fundamental frequency is usually also the frequency of the lowest peak, but the highest peak is not always at the fundamental frequency.

      Beats and the Doppler effect

      Beats

      When two sounds whose frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$ differ only slightly sound at the same time, their pressure variations add up. At times the waves are in phase and reinforce each other, at times in opposite phase and weaken each other. We hear one sound whose pitch corresponds to the average of the frequencies and whose loudness alternately grows and fades. This is called beats. In one second one wave completes $|f_1 - f_2|$ more vibrations than the other, and each time the waves return to the same phase. So the number of loud moments per second is

      $$f_\text{h} = |f_1 - f_2|$$

      where $f_\text{h}$ is the beat frequency (Hz) and $f_1$ and $f_2$ are the frequencies of the sounds (Hz). The time between two loud moments, the period of the beats, is $T_\text{h} = \tfrac{1}{f_\text{h}}$. The subscript h, used in the figures as well, comes from the Finnish edition of this chapter: for the beat frequency and the period of the beats it stands for huojunta (beats), and for the speed of the listener below it stands for havaitsija (observer).

      Beats are used for tuning instruments. When a string is tightened towards the frequency of the reference note, the beats slow down and stop when the frequencies are equal. Beats stand out clearly when the frequency difference is at most about $10\,\text{Hz}$. With a larger difference the ear hears a rough sound or two separate sounds.

      The Doppler effect when the source moves

      When a sound source moves, each wavefront starts from the point where the source is at that moment and spreads through the air at speed $v$ in every direction. The period of the source is $T_0 = \tfrac{1}{f_0}$. During that time the previous wavefront travels a distance $v T_0$, but the source travels after it a distance $v_\text{s} T_0$. In front of the source the spacing of the wavefronts, that is, the wavelength, is therefore $(v - v_\text{s}) T_0$, and behind the source it is $(v + v_\text{s}) T_0$. A listener at rest hears the frequency $f = \tfrac{v}{\lambda}$, so

      $$f = f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v \mp v_\text{s}}$$

      where $f$ is the frequency heard (Hz), $f_0$ is the frequency of the source (Hz), $v$ is the speed of sound (m/s) and $v_\text{s}$ is the speed of the source (m/s). The minus sign is chosen when the source approaches the listener and the plus sign when the source moves away.

      The Doppler effect when the listener moves

      When the source is at rest and the listener moves, the wavelength does not change. The listener, however, meets wavefronts more often when moving towards them, because the wavefronts approach at speed $v + v_\text{h}$. When moving away, the wavefronts catch up with the listener at speed $v - v_\text{h}$. The frequency heard is the rate at which the wavefronts are met:

      $$f = f_0 \cdot \frac{v \pm v_\text{h}}{v}$$

      where $v_\text{h}$ is the speed of the listener, or observer (m/s), and the other quantities are as above. The plus sign is chosen when the listener approaches the source and the minus sign when the listener moves away.

      Signs and assumptions

      The sign can be checked with a rule: approaching raises the frequency heard and moving away lowers it. So choose the sign that gives $f > f_0$ when approaching and $f < f_0$ when moving away. The speeds are measured relative to the air, which is at rest, that is, there is no wind. The formulas apply to motion along the straight line between the source and the listener, and they hold when only one of them moves and the speed of the source is less than the speed of sound. When the speed is much smaller than the speed of sound, both formulas give almost the same change, about $f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v_\text{s}}{v}$ or $f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v_\text{h}}{v}$. The Doppler effect is used to measure speeds: an ultrasound device measures the flow speed of blood.

      Common misconception

      Many people think that the sound of an approaching siren keeps rising as the car gets closer. The frequency heard, however, depends on the speed, not on the distance. When an ambulance drives at a steady speed straight towards the listener, the frequency heard is the same throughout the approach and higher than $f_0$, and at the moment of passing it drops. As the car approaches, the loudness grows, not the pitch. If the listener stands at the side of the road, the change happens gradually during the passing, because only the part of the velocity that points towards the listener or away from them affects the frequency heard.

      Another mistake is to think that the motion of the source changes the speed of sound or the frequency of the source itself. Sound travels relative to the air at speed $v$ whether the source moves or not, and the siren vibrates at the frequency $f_0$ all the time. A moving source changes the wavelength, and a moving listener changes how often they meet wavefronts. A third mistake is to read the pitch from a spectrum as the frequency of the highest peak: the fundamental frequency is the lowest frequency of the harmonic series and the spacing of the peaks, even if some overtone is stronger than it.

      Summary

      • Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave in which compressions and rarefactions travel through a medium. The speed of sound depends on the medium and, in a gas, on the temperature; in air it is about $340\,\text{m/s}$. The speed is determined by measuring the travel time of sound over a known distance.
      • Pitch is determined by frequency. The human range of hearing is about $20\,\text{Hz}$–$20\,\text{kHz}$; lower sound is infrasound and higher is ultrasound.
      • Timbre depends on the strengths of the overtones. The partials of an instrument form the harmonic series $f_n = n f_1$, and the FFT spectrum shows them as peaks.
      • When two sounds of almost the same frequency sound together, beats are heard with the frequency $f_\text{h} = |f_1 - f_2|$.
      • The Doppler effect: approaching raises and moving away lowers the frequency heard. For a moving source $f = f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v}{v \mp v_\text{s}}$ and for a moving listener $f = f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v \pm v_\text{h}}{v}$, the upper sign for approaching.

        Problem

        A train runs along a straight track at a steady speed of $108\,\text{km/h}$ and sounds a horn whose frequency is $311\,\text{Hz}$. A passenger stands on the platform right next to the track, so the train comes straight towards them and then moves straight away from them. What frequency does the passenger hear as the train approaches and what frequency as it moves away, and by how much does the frequency drop at the moment the train passes? The air is calm, and the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.

        Step 1/7

        Given and asked. The frequency of the horn is $f_0 = 311\,\text{Hz}$, the speed of the train is $v_\text{s} = 108\,\text{km/h}$ and the speed of sound is $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$. We are asked for the frequency heard as the train approaches ($f_1$) and as it moves away ($f_2$), and for the drop in frequency $\Delta f = f_1 - f_2$.

        Step 2/7

        Principle. The sound source moves and the listener is at rest, so we use the Doppler formula for a moving source. In front of the approaching train the wavefronts bunch together, so the sign is chosen so that $f_1 > f_0$. Behind the receding train the wavefronts spread out, and $f_2 < f_0$.

        Step 3/7

        Formula solved.

        $$\begin{aligned} f_1 &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v - v_\text{s}} \\ f_2 &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v + v_\text{s}} \\ \Delta f &= f_1 - f_2 \end{aligned}$$

        Step 4/7

        Unit conversions. The speed of the train is converted to m/s so that it can be subtracted from the speed of sound:

        $108\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$$= 108 \cdot \tfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
        $= 30.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

        Step 5/7

        Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} f_1 &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v - v_\text{s}} \\ \quad &= 311\,\text{Hz} \cdot \frac{340\,\text{m/s}}{(340 - 30.0)\,\text{m/s}} \\ \quad &= 341.10\,\text{Hz} \\[6pt] f_2 &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v + v_\text{s}} \\ \quad &= 311\,\text{Hz} \cdot \frac{340\,\text{m/s}}{(340 + 30.0)\,\text{m/s}} \\ \quad &= 285.78\,\text{Hz} \\[6pt] \Delta f &= f_1 - f_2 \\ \quad &= 341.10\,\text{Hz} - 285.78\,\text{Hz} \\ \quad &= 55.32\,\text{Hz} \end{aligned}$$

        Step 6/7

        Answer. The passenger hears the approaching train at $341\,\text{Hz}$ and the receding train at $286\,\text{Hz}$, to three significant figures like the given data. At the moment of passing, the frequency drops by about $55\,\text{Hz}$: the difference is accurate to about a hertz, because each frequency is known to about half a hertz.

        Step 7/7

        Reasonableness. At a low speed the change on each side is about

        $f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v_\text{s}}{v}$$= 311\,\text{Hz} \cdot \tfrac{30.0\,\text{m/s}}{340\,\text{m/s}}$
        $\approx 27\,\text{Hz}$

        so the whole drop is about $55\,\text{Hz}$, as calculated. The rise on approach, $30\,\text{Hz}$, is slightly larger than the fall on moving away, $25\,\text{Hz}$, because in front we divide by the smaller number $v - v_\text{s}$. The speed of the train is less than a tenth of the speed of sound, so the change on each side is only about $9\,\%$ of the frequency $f_0$.

        Optional Find the error
        A moped approaches a listener at $20.0\,\text{m/s}$, and the frequency of its horn is $620\,\text{Hz}$.A student calculates the frequency heard like this:

        $$\begin{aligned} f &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v + v_\text{s}} \\ &= 620\,\text{Hz} \cdot \frac{340\,\text{m/s}}{360\,\text{m/s}} \\ &\approx 586\,\text{Hz} \end{aligned}$$

        Where is the error?

        • Correct. Approaching raises the frequency heard, but the student's result $586\,\text{Hz}$ is lower than $620\,\text{Hz}$. When the source approaches, the wavefronts bunch together in front of it and the wavelength is $(v - v_\text{s}) T_0$, so $f = 620\,\text{Hz} \cdot \tfrac{340}{320} \approx 659\,\text{Hz}$.

        • The choice of the formula depends on which one moves relative to the air. Here the source moves, so the moving-source formula is the right one. The error is in the sign: the result $586\,\text{Hz}$ is lower than $f_0$ even though the moped approaches.

        • Sound always travels relative to the air at $340\,\text{m/s}$, whether the source moves or not. The motion of the moped changes the wavelength in front of it, not the speed of sound. The error is in the sign of the denominator.

        • The wavefronts stretch behind the source. In front of an approaching source they bunch together, the wavelength shortens and the frequency heard grows. The result $586\,\text{Hz} < 620\,\text{Hz}$ should have raised suspicion.

        A cyclist rides towards a church bell at rest that sounds at a steady frequency. The cyclist hears the sound of the bell higher than a person standing in the churchyard.Which explanation is correct?

Pitch depends on the frequency, that is, on how often the wavefronts reach the ear, not on the speed of sound as such.

        2. Measuring the speed of sound

        Two microphones are placed $1.20\,\text{m}$ apart on the same straight line as a loudspeaker. The loudspeaker emits a short click, and from the signals recorded by the measuring program it is read that the click arrives at the farther microphone $3.50\,\text{ms}$ later than at the nearer one.

        What is the speed of sound according to the measurement? Answer in m/s.

        m/s
        Hint 1: a guiding question

        What distance does the sound travel in the time in which the click goes from the nearer microphone to the farther one?

        Hint 2: what is the connection

        Sound travels the distance $d$ between the microphones in the time $\Delta t$, so $v = \tfrac{d}{\Delta t}$.

        Hint 3: an intermediate step

        Convert the time to seconds: $3.50\,\text{ms} = 3.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$.

        Show the solution

        Given

        distance of the microphones $d = 1.20\,\text{m}$, difference in arrival times $\Delta t = 3.50\,\text{ms}$

        Find

        the speed of sound $v$ in m/s

        Principle

        The microphones are on the same straight line as the loudspeaker, so the sound travels to the farther microphone the same distance as to the nearer one and in addition the distance $d$ between the microphones. The time difference $\Delta t$ is therefore the time in which sound travels the distance $d$ at a steady speed.

        Formula

        $d = v \Delta t$, so $v = \dfrac{d}{\Delta t}$

        Conversions

        $3.50\,\text{ms} = 3.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$

        Substitution

        $v$$= \dfrac{1.20\,\text{m}}{3.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}}$
        $= 342.86\,\text{m/s}$

        Answer

        $343\,\text{m/s}$

        Three significant figures like the distance and the time difference.

        Reasonableness

        The result matches the table value $343\,\text{m/s}$ for air at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, that is, room temperature. If the time difference were misread by $0.05\,\text{ms}$, the result would change by about $5\,\text{m/s}$, so reading the time difference accurately decides the accuracy of the measurement.

        Optional 3. Explain timbre

        A violin and a flute play the same note with the fundamental frequency $440\,\text{Hz}$, and yet they sound different. Explain what is the same and what is different in the sounds, and how the difference would show in their FFT graphs. Use the concepts fundamental frequency, overtone and harmonic series.

        Model answer

        Both sounds have the fundamental frequency $440\,\text{Hz}$, so the ear hears the same pitch. However, both instruments also vibrate at their overtones at the same time. These are the multiples of the fundamental frequency, $880\,\text{Hz}$, $1320\,\text{Hz}$ and so on, and they form the harmonic series $f_n = n f_1$. Timbre depends on how strong the overtones are compared with the fundamental frequency, and it is different for different instruments. In the FFT graphs both have peaks at the same frequencies, but the heights of the peaks are different: in the sound of the violin there are strong overtones far up the series, in the sound of the flute the fundamental frequency dominates and the overtones are weaker.

          1. Core

            A student stands $86.0\,\text{m}$ from the wall of a school and claps two wooden blocks together. The timing app of a phone measures that the echo returns $0.505\,\text{s}$ after the clap.

            What is the speed of sound according to the measurement?

            m/s
            Answer

            $341\,\text{m/s}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            distance to the wall $d = 86.0\,\text{m}$, return time of the echo $t = 0.505\,\text{s}$

            Find

            the speed of sound $v$ in $\text{m/s}$

            Principle

            The sound travels to the wall and is reflected back, so during the return time it covers a distance $2d$. In air sound travels at a steady speed, so the speed is the distance travelled divided by the time.

            Formula

            $2d = v t$, so $v = \dfrac{2d}{t}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.

            Substitution

            $v$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 86.0\,\text{m}}{0.505\,\text{s}}$
            $= \dfrac{172.0\,\text{m}}{0.505\,\text{s}}$
            $= 340.59\,\text{m/s}$

            Answer

            $341\,\text{m/s}$

            Three significant figures like the distance and the time.

            Reasonableness

            The result is close to the table value $340\,\text{m/s}$ for air at about $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. If the distance had not been multiplied by two, the speed would have come out as about $170\,\text{m/s}$, which is clearly too small. With timing started by hand, the reaction time can still change the result by several per cent.

          2. Core

            A bat emits ultrasound for echolocation with a frequency of $45.0\,\text{kHz}$.

            What is the wavelength of the sound in air when the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$? Give the answer in millimetres.

            mm
            Answer

            $7.56\,\text{mm}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            frequency of the ultrasound $f = 45.0\,\text{kHz}$, speed of sound in air $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$

            Find

            the wavelength $\lambda$ in millimetres

            Principle

            Ultrasound is a longitudinal wave just like audible sound, so the basic wave equation $v = f\lambda$ holds for it.

            Formula

            $v = f\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$

            Conversions

            $f$$= 45.0\,\text{kHz}$
            $= 45.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{Hz}$
            $= 45\,000\,\text{Hz}$

            finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.

            Substitution

            $\lambda$$= \dfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{45\,000\,\text{Hz}}$
            $= 0.0075556\,\text{m}$
            $= 7.5556\,\text{mm}$

            Answer

            $7.56\,\text{mm}$

            Three significant figures like the frequency $45.0\,\text{kHz}$ and the speed $340\,\text{m/s}$.

            Reasonableness

            At the upper limit of hearing, $20\,\text{kHz}$, the wavelength is $1.7\,\text{cm}$, and at more than twice the frequency the wavelength is less than half of that. A wavelength of a few millimetres is the size of an insect, so a bat can detect insects by echolocation.

          3. The fundamental frequency of the D string of a cello is $147\,\text{Hz}$. The string vibrates at the frequencies of the harmonic series at the same time.

            What is the frequency of the fourth member of the series, that is, the third overtone?

            Hz
            Answer

            $588\,\text{Hz}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            fundamental frequency $f_1 = 147\,\text{Hz}$

            Find

            the frequency $f_4$ of the fourth member of the harmonic series

            Principle

            A string fixed at both ends vibrates at all its natural frequencies at once, which are whole-number multiples of the fundamental frequency and form the harmonic series. The first member of the series is the fundamental frequency, so the fourth member is the third overtone.

            Formula

            $f_n = n f_1$, so $f_4 = 4 f_1$

            Conversions

            None needed: the frequency is in hertz.

            Substitution

            $= 588\,\text{Hz}$

            $588\,\text{Hz}$

            Three significant figures like the fundamental frequency.

            The members of the series are $147\,\text{Hz}$, $294\,\text{Hz}$, $441\,\text{Hz}$ and $588\,\text{Hz}$, so the fourth member is two octaves above the fundamental: two doublings give four times the frequency.

          4. An ice-cream van drives straight away from a listener at $15.0\,\text{m/s}$ and plays a melody, one note of which is $415\,\text{Hz}$.

            What frequency of this note does a listener at rest hear? The air is calm, and the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.

            Hz
            Answer

            $397\,\text{Hz}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            frequency of the note $f_0 = 415\,\text{Hz}$, speed of the car $v_\text{s} = 15.0\,\text{m/s}$ away from the listener, speed of sound $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$

            Find

            the frequency heard $f$

            Principle

            The sound source moves and the listener is at rest, so we use the Doppler formula for a moving source. The source moves away, so the wavefronts spread out behind it and the frequency heard is lower than $f_0$: the denominator gets a plus sign.

            Formula

            $f$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v}{v + v_\text{s}}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the frequency in hertz.

            Substitution

            $f$$= 415\,\text{Hz} \cdot \dfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{340\,\text{m/s} + 15.0\,\text{m/s}}$
            $= 415\,\text{Hz} \cdot \dfrac{340}{355}$
            $= 397.46\,\text{Hz}$

            Answer

            $397\,\text{Hz}$

            Three significant figures like the given data.

            Reasonableness

            The frequency drops because the source moves away. The speed of the car is about $4.4\,\%$ of the speed of sound, and the frequency drops by about $\tfrac{415 - 397}{415} \approx 4.2\,\%$, so the change is of the same order as the ratio of the speeds.

          5. Classify the sounds as infrasound, audible sound or ultrasound: a) the lowest part of a thunder rumble, $12\,\text{Hz}$, b) a reference tuning note, $440\,\text{Hz}$, c) a dog whistle, $35\,\text{kHz}$, d) the sound of ultrasound imaging, $2.5\,\text{MHz}$. Justify using the limits.

            Answer

            a) infrasound; b) audible sound; c) ultrasound; d) ultrasound

            Show the solution

            Find

            the class of each sound: infrasound, audible sound or ultrasound

            Principle

            A human hears frequencies from about $20\,\text{Hz}$ to about $20\,\text{kHz}$. Sound below $20\,\text{Hz}$ is infrasound and sound above $20\,\text{kHz}$ is ultrasound. All of them are longitudinal pressure waves of the same kind; the class depends only on the frequency.

            Model answer

            a) $12\,\text{Hz} < 20\,\text{Hz}$, so the lowest part of the thunder is infrasound. b) $440\,\text{Hz}$ lies between the limits $20\,\text{Hz}$ and $20\,\text{kHz}$, so the tuning note is audible sound. c) $35\,\text{kHz} > 20\,\text{kHz}$, so the sound of the dog whistle is ultrasound: a dog hears it, but a human does not. d) $2.5\,\text{MHz} = 2\,500\,000\,\text{Hz}$ is many orders of magnitude above the range of hearing, so the sound of ultrasound imaging is ultrasound.

          1. Core

            A piano tuner strikes a tuning fork with a frequency of $262\,\text{Hz}$ and at the same time plays a piano string that sounds slightly lower than the tuning fork. He counts $15$ loud moments, that is, beats, in $5.0\,\text{s}$.

            What is the frequency of the string?

            Hz
            Answer

            $259\,\text{Hz}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            frequency of the tuning fork $f_1 = 262\,\text{Hz}$, $N = 15$ loud moments in a time $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$, the string is lower than the tuning fork

            Find

            the frequency of the string $f_2$

            Principle

            Two sounds of almost the same frequency produce beats, and the number of loud moments per second equals the difference of the frequencies. The beat frequency is obtained by counting the loud moments per unit time. Because the string is lower, its frequency is smaller than that of the tuning fork by the beat frequency.

            Formula

            $f_\text{h} = \dfrac{N}{t}$ and $f_\text{h} = |f_1 - f_2| = f_1 - f_2$, so $f_2 = f_1 - \dfrac{N}{t}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the time is in seconds, so $\tfrac{N}{t}$ comes out in hertz.

            Substitution

            $f_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{15}{5.0\,\text{s}}$
            $= 3.0\,\text{Hz}$
            $f_2$$= 262\,\text{Hz} - 3.0\,\text{Hz}$
            $= 259\,\text{Hz}$

            Answer

            $259\,\text{Hz}$

            ; the difference $3.0\,\text{Hz}$ is known to a tenth of a hertz, so the result is given in whole hertz like the frequency of the tuning fork.

            Reasonableness

            Three loud moments per second are easy to count by ear, and the frequency difference is less than $10\,\text{Hz}$, so the beats stand out clearly. When the tuner tightens the string, the frequency rises towards $262\,\text{Hz}$ and the beats slow down.

          2. Core

            A loudspeaker emits sound in air with a wavelength of $0.850\,\text{m}$. The sound passes from the surface of a lake into the water.

            What is the wavelength of the sound in water? The speed of sound in air is $340\,\text{m/s}$ and in water $1480\,\text{m/s}$.

            m
            Answer

            $3.70\,\text{m}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            wavelength in air $\lambda_\text{a} = 0.850\,\text{m}$, speed of sound in air $v_\text{a} = 340\,\text{m/s}$ and in water $v_\text{w} = 1480\,\text{m/s}$

            Find

            the wavelength in water $\lambda_\text{w}$

            Principle

            When sound passes from air into water, the frequency stays the same, because the particles at the boundary vibrate in step with the incoming wave. The speed changes, so the wavelength changes. In both substances $v = f\lambda$ holds.

            Formula

            $f$$= \dfrac{v_\text{a}}{\lambda_\text{a}}$
            $\lambda_\text{w}$$= \dfrac{v_\text{w}}{f}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the wavelength in metres.

            Substitution

            $f$$= \dfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{0.850\,\text{m}}$
            $= 400\,\text{Hz}$
            $\lambda_\text{w}$$= \dfrac{1480\,\text{m/s}}{400\,\text{Hz}}$
            $= 3.70\,\text{m}$

            Answer

            $3.70\,\text{m}$

            Three significant figures like the given data.

            Reasonableness

            Sound travels $\tfrac{1480}{340} \approx 4.4$ times as fast in water as in air, and at the same frequency the wavelength grows in the same ratio: $0.850\,\text{m} \cdot 4.35 \approx 3.7\,\text{m}$.

          3. A driver drives at $90.0\,\text{km/h}$ straight towards a junction where a stationary warning siren sounds at $650\,\text{Hz}$.

            What frequency does the driver hear? The air is calm, and the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.

            Hz
            Answer

            $698\,\text{Hz}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            frequency of the siren $f_0 = 650\,\text{Hz}$, speed of the driver $v_\text{h} = 90.0\,\text{km/h}$ towards the siren, speed of sound $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$

            Find

            the frequency heard by the driver $f$

            Principle

            The source is at rest and the listener moves, so the wavelength does not change, but the driver meets the wavefronts more often, because they approach at speed $v + v_\text{h}$. We use the Doppler formula for a moving listener with the plus sign for approaching.

            Formula

            $f$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v + v_\text{h}}{v}$

            Conversions

            $v_\text{h}$$= 90.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$
            $= 90.0 \cdot \tfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
            $= 25.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

            Substitution

            $f$$= 650\,\text{Hz} \cdot \dfrac{340\,\text{m/s} + 25.0\,\text{m/s}}{340\,\text{m/s}}$
            $= 650\,\text{Hz} \cdot \dfrac{365}{340}$
            $= 697.79\,\text{Hz}$

            Answer

            $698\,\text{Hz}$

            Three significant figures like the given data.

            Reasonableness

            The frequency rises because the driver approaches. The rise is

            $f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v_\text{h}}{v}$$= 650\,\text{Hz} \cdot \tfrac{25.0}{340}$
            $\approx 48\,\text{Hz}$

            which is about $7\,\%$, like the ratio of the speeds $\tfrac{25}{340}$.

          4. The engine of a racing car sounds at $425\,\text{Hz}$ in the pit at rest. When the car drives at the same engine speed straight away from a spectator standing on the extension of the straight, the spectator hears the frequency $398\,\text{Hz}$.

            How fast is the car moving? The air is calm, and the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.

            m/s
            Answer

            $23\,\text{m/s}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            frequency of the engine at rest $f_0 = 425\,\text{Hz}$, frequency heard as the car moves away $f = 398\,\text{Hz}$, speed of sound $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$

            Find

            the speed of the car $v_\text{s}$

            Principle

            The car, that is, the sound source, moves straight away from the spectator, and the spectator is at rest, so the Doppler formula for a moving source applies with the plus sign for moving away. The engine speed is the same, so the engine's own frequency is $f_0$. The speed of the source is solved from the formula.

            Formula

            $f$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v}{v + v_\text{s}}$

            so

            $v + v_\text{s}$$= \dfrac{f_0 v}{f}$
            $v_\text{s}$$= v \left(\dfrac{f_0}{f} - 1\right)$

            Conversions

            None needed: the frequencies are in hertz, and the unit cancels in their ratio.

            Substitution

            $v_\text{s}$$= 340\,\text{m/s} \cdot \left(\dfrac{425\,\text{Hz}}{398\,\text{Hz}} - 1\right)$
            $= 340\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.067839$
            $= 23.065\,\text{m/s}$

            Answer

            $23\,\text{m/s}$

            Two significant figures, because the result depends on the frequency difference $27\,\text{Hz}$, which is known to two figures.

            Reasonableness

            $23\,\text{m/s}$ is about $83\,\text{km/h}$, which is a believable speed on a racetrack. Check: $425\,\text{Hz} \cdot \tfrac{340}{340 + 23.07} \approx 398\,\text{Hz}$, as measured.

          1. A listener stands right next to a straight road and measures with a phone app the frequency of the siren of a passing ambulance: $476\,\text{Hz}$ as the car approaches and $404\,\text{Hz}$ as it moves away. The speed of the ambulance is constant, but the siren's own frequency is not known.

            How fast is the ambulance moving? The air is calm, and the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.

            m/s
            Answer

            $28\,\text{m/s}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            frequency heard when approaching $f_1 = 476\,\text{Hz}$ and when moving away $f_2 = 404\,\text{Hz}$, speed of sound $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$; the frequency of the siren $f_0$ is unknown

            Find

            the speed of the ambulance $v_\text{s}$

            Principle

            The listener is at rest and the source moves straight towards them and straight away from them at the same speed, so the moving-source formula holds in both phases, with the minus sign when approaching and the plus sign when moving away. The unknown $f_0$ cancels when the frequencies are divided by each other.

            Formula

            $f_1$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v}{v - v_\text{s}}$
            $f_2$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v}{v + v_\text{s}}$

            so

            $\dfrac{f_1}{f_2}$$= \dfrac{v + v_\text{s}}{v - v_\text{s}}$

            Cross-multiplying, $f_1 v - f_1 v_\text{s} = f_2 v + f_2 v_\text{s}$, so

            $v_\text{s} (f_1 + f_2)$$= v (f_1 - f_2)$
            $v_\text{s}$$= v \cdot \dfrac{f_1 - f_2}{f_1 + f_2}$

            Conversions

            None needed: the unit of the frequencies cancels, and the speed comes out in $\text{m/s}$.

            Substitution

            $v_\text{s}$$= 340\,\text{m/s} \cdot \dfrac{476\,\text{Hz} - 404\,\text{Hz}}{476\,\text{Hz} + 404\,\text{Hz}}$
            $= 340\,\text{m/s} \cdot \dfrac{72}{880}$
            $= 27.818\,\text{m/s}$

            Answer

            $28\,\text{m/s}$

            Two significant figures, because the frequency difference $72\,\text{Hz}$ is known to two figures.

            Reasonableness

            $28\,\text{m/s}$ is about $100\,\text{km/h}$, which suits an emergency drive. The siren's own frequency is

            $f_0$$= f_1 \cdot \tfrac{v - v_\text{s}}{v}$
            $\approx 437\,\text{Hz}$

            and it lies between the frequencies heard, as it should. Also

            $f_2$$= 437\,\text{Hz} \cdot \tfrac{340}{367.8}$
            $\approx 404\,\text{Hz}$

            agrees.

          2. A guitarist plays the A string at the same time as a $440\,\text{Hz}$ reference note and hears a beat frequency of $4\,\text{Hz}$, that is, four loud moments every second. When she tightens the string slightly, the beat frequency grows to $6\,\text{Hz}$.

            What was the frequency of the string before tightening? Justify why the answer is unique.

            Hz
            Answer

            $444\,\text{Hz}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            reference note $f_0 = 440\,\text{Hz}$, beat frequency before tightening $f_\text{h} = 4\,\text{Hz}$ and after tightening $6\,\text{Hz}$

            Find

            the frequency of the string $f$ before tightening

            Principle

            The beat frequency is the absolute value of the frequency difference, so from the beats alone the frequency of the string could be either $440\,\text{Hz} - 4\,\text{Hz}$ or $440\,\text{Hz} + 4\,\text{Hz}$. Tightening increases the speed of the wave in the string and therefore raises the frequency of the string. Because the beats sped up, that is, the difference grew, the rising frequency moved away from the reference note: the string was already higher than the reference note. If the string had been $436\,\text{Hz}$, tightening would have taken it towards $440\,\text{Hz}$ and the beats would have slowed down.

            Formula

            $f_\text{h} = |f - f_0|$, and when $f > f_0$, $f = f_0 + f_\text{h}$

            Conversions

            None needed: all the frequencies are in hertz.

            Substitution

            $f$$= 440\,\text{Hz} + 4\,\text{Hz}$
            $= 444\,\text{Hz}$

            Answer

            $444\,\text{Hz}$

            In whole hertz like the reference note and the beat frequency.

            Reasonableness

            After tightening, the frequency of the string is $440\,\text{Hz} + 6\,\text{Hz} = 446\,\text{Hz}$, so it rose by $2\,\text{Hz}$, which suits a small tightening. To tune the string, it must therefore be loosened until the beats stop.

          3. A student measures the FFT spectrum of a low note of a clarinet.

            The spectrum has clear peaks at the frequencies $147\,\text{Hz}$, $441\,\text{Hz}$, $735\,\text{Hz}$ and $1029\,\text{Hz}$, and almost nothing in between. a) What is the fundamental frequency of the note? b) Which members of the harmonic series are missing from the spectrum? c) What does this tell about the standing wave of the air column of the clarinet?

            Answer

            Fundamental frequency $147\,\text{Hz}$; the even multiples ($294\,\text{Hz}$, $588\,\text{Hz}$, $882\,\text{Hz}$) are missing, so the air column vibrates like a tube closed at one end.

            Show the solution

            Find

            the fundamental frequency of the note, the members of the harmonic series missing from the spectrum, and what they mean for the air column of the clarinet

            Principle

            The peaks in the spectrum of an instrument are at the natural frequencies of the air column, and the fundamental frequency is the lowest natural frequency. In a tube open at both ends the natural frequencies are all multiples $n f_1$ of the fundamental frequency, but in a tube closed at one end only the odd multiples, because the closed end is always a node and the open end an antinode (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies).

            Model answer

            The lowest peak is $147\,\text{Hz}$, and the others are its multiples: $441\,\text{Hz} = 3 \cdot 147\,\text{Hz}$, $735\,\text{Hz} = 5 \cdot 147\,\text{Hz}$ and $1029\,\text{Hz} = 7 \cdot 147\,\text{Hz}$. So the fundamental frequency is $147\,\text{Hz}$. The even members of the harmonic series, $294\,\text{Hz}$, $588\,\text{Hz}$ and $882\,\text{Hz}$, are almost entirely missing. Only the odd multiples are natural frequencies of a tube closed at one end, so the air column of the clarinet vibrates like a tube with one end closed: the mouthpiece end, where the reed is, acts as the closed end with a node of the air motion, and the bell end as the open end with an antinode. The missing overtones give the clarinet its dark timbre.
