Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments

Why can a paint can be placed on the overhang of a plank lying across trestles, but only up to a certain point before the plank topples?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • write the equilibrium conditions of a body for translation and for rotation.
  • calculate support forces using a free-body diagram and the moment condition.
  • explain how the position of the centre of mass decides whether a supported body stays in place or topples.

Prior knowledge

  • The moment of a force is $M = Fr$, where $r$ is the lever arm of the force, and the sign of the moment tells the direction of rotationchapter Moment: the turning effect of a force
  • Only the forces acting on the body are drawn in a free-body diagram, and for a body at rest the sum of the forces acting on it is zerochapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

A painter has laid a long plank across two trestles so that one end of the plank reaches well beyond a trestle. He lifts a heavy paint can onto the plank and pushes it towards the end of the overhang. At last the other end of the plank lifts off its trestle and the plank topples.

What decides how far along the overhang the can can be moved, and how large a share of the load does each trestle carry?

Optional Recall: the moment of a force
A door is pushed at the handle with a force of $20\,\text{N}$ perpendicular to the door. The handle is $0.80\,\text{m}$ from the hinges.What is the moment of the force about the hinges?

  • The moment is the product of the force and the lever arm, not a quotient: $M = Fr$. Farther from the hinges the same force turns the door more, so the moment grows as the distance grows. The correct value is $16\,\text{Nm}$.

  • The moment also depends on how far from the axis of rotation the force acts. The same $20\,\text{N}$ force right next to the hinge hardly turns the door at all. The moment is $M = Fr = 16\,\text{Nm}$ (chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force).

  • Correct. $M = Fr = 20\,\text{N} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m} = 16\,\text{Nm}$, because the force is perpendicular to the door and the lever arm is the distance from the hinges (chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force). Today we calculate moments for a body that does not rotate at all.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Two equilibrium conditions

    In the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force we learned that a force rotates a body the more, the greater the moment of the force $M = Fr$. For a body to stay at rest, it is not enough that the forces cancel each other. A body is in equilibrium when it neither starts to move nor starts to rotate. For translation the condition is familiar from Newton's laws: the sum of the forces is zero. For rotation the condition is new: the sum of the moments of the forces is zero. In both cases in the figure two equal and opposite forces act on the ruler, so the sum of the forces is zero. On the left the forces lie along the same line and do not rotate the ruler. On the right they act along different lines, and their moments rotate the ruler in the same direction: the ruler starts to rotate, although its middle does not start to move.

    Centre of mass

    The Earth's gravitational pull acts on every part of a body. When the moment of the weight is calculated, however, the whole weight can be thought to act at a single point, the centre of mass of the body. The centre of mass of a uniform, symmetrical body, such as a plank of constant thickness, is at its middle. This is why the weight is drawn in a free-body diagram starting from the centre of mass. The common centre of mass of two bodies is between their centres of mass, nearer the heavier body. When a body is supported at one point, it stays in place only if the centre of mass is on the vertical line through the point of support: otherwise the moment of the weight about the point of support rotates the body.

    When a body topples

    The support area of a body at rest is the region bounded by the points where the body touches the ground: for a box its base, for a chair the area between its legs. Toppling is judged with the vertical line through the centre of mass, the vertical projection of the centre of mass. When the line falls within the support area, the moment of the weight about the edge of the support area turns the body back onto the ground, and the body stays upright. If the line falls outside the support area, the moment of the weight about the lower edge rotates the body over the edge, and the body topples. A low, wide body therefore withstands a greater tilt than a tall, narrow one. Toppling is rotation about an edge, whereas sliding is the motion of the whole body along the surface, and friction prevents sliding. The boxes in the figure are on the same inclined plane, and friction prevents them from sliding, so they can only stay put or topple.

    Equilibrium conditions

    A body is in equilibrium when both the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments of the forces are zero:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Sigma\overline{F} &= \overline{0} \\ \Sigma M &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

    where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the vector sum of the forces acting on the body ($\text{N}$) and $\Sigma M$ the sum of the moments of the forces about some point ($\text{Nm}$). The first is the equilibrium condition for translation and the second the moment condition, that is, the equilibrium condition for rotation. In components the first condition is $\Sigma F_x = 0$ and $\Sigma F_y = 0$. The moments are calculated as in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force: $M = Fr$, where $r$ is the lever arm of the force, and the sign tells the direction of rotation. In this chapter an anticlockwise moment is positive.

    Choosing the reference point

    A body at rest does not rotate about any point, so the moment condition holds about every point. It is worth choosing the point of application of an unknown force as the reference point for the moments: the lever arm of that force is then zero, so the force does not appear in the moment condition. If a force is not perpendicular to a rod-like body (a plank, a rod), it is resolved into components. When the reference point is on the line of the rod, the moment is given by the component perpendicular to the rod multiplied by the distance from the reference point. In the figure the force $\overline{F}$ acting on the end of the rod makes the angle $\alpha$ with the rod: the component $F\cos\alpha$ along the rod passes through the hinge and does not rotate the rod, and the perpendicular component $F\sin\alpha$ gives the moment $M = F\sin\alpha \cdot r$.

    Two supports

    The plank in the figure rests on two supports. Let us take the moments about the centre of mass: the lever arm of the weight $\overline{G}$ is zero, the support force $\overline{N}_1$ rotates the plank clockwise and $\overline{N}_2$ anticlockwise. From the moment condition $N_2 r_2 - N_1 r_1 = 0$ we get

    $$N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$$

    where $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the support forces ($\text{N}$) and $r_1$ and $r_2$ the distances of the supports from the centre of mass ($\text{m}$). The support nearer the centre of mass thus carries the larger share of the weight. From the condition for translation the sum of the support forces is $N_1 + N_2 = G$.

    Common centre of mass

    For two bodies of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, the common centre of mass is at

    $$x_\text{cm} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

    where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the positions of the centres of mass of the bodies ($\text{m}$) on the same axis. The formula follows from the moment condition: if the bodies are supported at $x_\text{cm}$, the moments of the weights about the point of support cancel each other. A loaded plank resting on two supports topples when the common centre of mass of the plank and the load moves outside the interval between the supports.

    Common misconception

    Many think that two supports always carry half of the weight each, or that the support farther away carries more because its "lever" is longer. The moment condition about the centre of mass shows the opposite: $N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$, so the support nearer the centre of mass carries more. Each support carries half of the weight only when the supports are at equal distances from the centre of mass.

    Another mistake is to think that a loaded plank topples as soon as the load is past a support. The plank topples only when the common centre of mass of the plank and the load is past the support: until then the weight of the plank itself between the supports cancels the moment of the load.

    A third mistake is to believe that moments must be calculated only about the centre of mass or a support. The moment condition holds about any point, and the same forces are obtained from different points. A good choice merely shortens the calculation.

    Summary

    • A body is in equilibrium when the sum of the forces is zero, $\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0}$ (for translation), and the sum of the moments of the forces is zero, $\Sigma M = 0$ (for rotation).
    • The moment condition holds about any point. It is worth choosing the point of application of an unknown force as the reference point.
    • The weight acts at the centre of mass, which is in the middle of a uniform, symmetrical body. For a plank on two supports $N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$: the support nearer the centre of mass carries more.
    • A supported body stays upright when the vertical line through the centre of mass falls within the support area; otherwise it topples.

      Example 1: Two people carrying a log

      Two carriers carry a uniform horizontal log of length $4.00\,\text{m}$ and mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$. Carrier A holds the front end of the log, and carrier B is $1.00\,\text{m}$ from the back end. With what force does each carrier hold the log? The carriers support the log vertically upwards.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The log: $L = 4.00\,\text{m}$, $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The centre of mass is in the middle, $2.00\,\text{m}$ from A; B is $3.00\,\text{m}$ from A. We are asked for the forces of the carriers, $N_\text{A}$ and $N_\text{B}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The log is at rest, so it is in equilibrium for both translation and rotation. The body is the log. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on it are the weight $\overline{G}$ downwards from the centre of mass and the forces of the carriers $\overline{N}_\text{A}$ and $\overline{N}_\text{B}$ upwards. A is chosen as the reference point, so the lever arm of $N_\text{A}$ is zero, and the anticlockwise moment is positive. The unknown $N_\text{B}$ is obtained from the moment condition and $N_\text{A}$ then from the sum of the forces.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The moment condition about A and the condition $\Sigma F_y = 0$, with upwards positive:

      $$\begin{aligned} &N_\text{B} r_\text{B} - G r_G = 0 \\ &N_\text{B} = \frac{m g r_G}{r_\text{B}} \\[6pt] &N_\text{A} + N_\text{B} - G = 0 \\ &N_\text{A} = m g - N_\text{B} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the lengths in metres. The lever arms of the forces are $r_G = 2.00\,\text{m}$ and $r_\text{B} = 3.00\,\text{m}$, because the forces are vertical and the log horizontal.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} &G = m g \\ &= 60.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ &= 588.6\,\text{N} \\[6pt] &N_\text{B} = \frac{G r_G}{r_\text{B}} \\ &= \frac{588.6\,\text{N} \cdot 2.00\,\text{m}}{3.00\,\text{m}} \\ &= 392.4\,\text{N} \\[6pt] &N_\text{A} = G - N_\text{B} \\ &= 588.6\,\text{N} - 392.4\,\text{N} \\ &= 196.2\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. Carrier A holds the log with a force of $196\,\text{N}$ and carrier B with a force of $392\,\text{N}$. The results are given to three significant figures, like the mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$ and the lengths.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The sum of the forces is $196\,\text{N} + 392\,\text{N} = 588\,\text{N}$, the weight of the log. B is $1.00\,\text{m}$ and A $2.00\,\text{m}$ from the centre of mass, so B carries twice as much as A, as the moment condition about the centre of mass requires: $196\,\text{N} \cdot 2.00\,\text{m} = 392\,\text{N} \cdot 1.00\,\text{m}$.

      Example 2: Find the error in the free-body diagram

      A uniform $20\,\text{kg}$ plank rests on two supports. Support 1 is $1.5\,\text{m}$ and support 2 $1.0\,\text{m}$ from the middle of the plank. A student has drawn the free-body diagram of the plank.What is wrong in it?

      • The centre of mass of a uniform plank is in its middle, so $\overline{G}$ correctly starts from the middle. The error is in the support forces: support 2, nearer the centre of mass, carries more, $N_2 = 117.7\,\text{N}$ and $N_1 = 78.5\,\text{N}$.

      • The plank does press the supports downwards, but that force acts on the supports, not on the plank. The free-body diagram of the plank shows the forces of the supports on the plank, and they push the plank upwards. The error is in the lengths of the arrows.

      • The sum of the forces is zero, but that is only one of the equilibrium conditions. About the centre of mass, the moment of $\overline{N}_2$, $98.1\,\text{N} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m}$, does not cancel the moment of $\overline{N}_1$, $98.1\,\text{N} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m}$, so a plank like this would start to rotate.

      • Correct. The moment condition about the centre of mass gives $N_1 \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} = N_2 \cdot 1.0\,\text{m}$. When in addition $N_1 + N_2 = 196.2\,\text{N}$, we get $N_1 = 78.5\,\text{N}$ and $N_2 = 117.7\,\text{N}$. Equally long arrows satisfy only the condition on the sum of the forces, not the moment condition.

          the force $F_\text{n}$ with which the bar pulls the nail, in newtons

          The bar stays at rest, so the sum of the moments about the pivot is zero.           What happens to the rod?

          Choose an option.

          • Correct: equilibrium requires two conditions, the sum of the forces is zero and the sum of the moments is zero. Here the first condition is met, but the forces act on different lines. About the midpoint of the rod both moments have the same direction, so their sum is not zero and the rod rotates.

          • The forces are equal and opposite, so the sum of the forces is zero and the motion of the centre of mass of the rod does not change. The point of application of a force does not decide the translation but the rotation.

          • A zero sum of forces only guarantees that the centre of mass of the rod does not start to move. Rotation is prevented only by the moment condition, and it is violated here: the forces are not on the same line.

          • Equal forces do not yet mean equal opposite moments. About the midpoint of the rod each force turns the rod in the same direction, so the moments add up and do not cancel.

          Answer

          The rod starts to rotate, because the moments of the forces turn it in the same direction.

          Show the solution

          Find

          what happens to a light rod when equal and opposite forces act at different ends of the rod.

          Principle

          A body is in equilibrium only if both the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments of the forces are zero. A zero sum of forces tells that the centre of mass does not start to move. It tells nothing about rotation. Here the forces $10\,\text{N}$ up and $10\,\text{N}$ down cancel each other, so the sum of the forces is zero. However, the lines of the forces are at different places, and about the midpoint of the rod both forces turn the rod in the same direction. The sum of the moments is therefore $2 \cdot 10\,\text{N} \cdot r$, where $r$ is half the length of the rod, and it is not zero.

          Model answer

          The rod starts to rotate, because the moments of the forces turn it in the same direction. Equal and opposite forces on different lines cause pure rotation without the centre of mass starting to move. The centre of mass of the rod stays in place, because the sum of the forces is zero, but the moment condition is not met. The claim that the rod would stay at rest because the sum of the forces is zero is wrong: equilibrium requires both conditions. The claim that equal forces would produce moments that cancel each other is also wrong, because both moments have the same direction. The rod does not start to move upwards either, because the sum of the forces is zero.

          A uniform bookshelf has depth $0.400\,\text{m}$ and height $1.80\,\text{m}$, and its centre of mass is in the middle. The shelf is tilted forwards so that it rests only on its front edge.

          What is the angle of tilt from the vertical when the shelf is at the limit of toppling, that is, when it would topple by itself if the angle grew any more? Answer in degrees.

          °
          Answer

          $12.5^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          depth of the shelf $d = 0.400\,\text{m}$, height $h = 1.80\,\text{m}$, centre of mass in the middle

          Find

          the angle of tilt $\alpha$ from the vertical at the limit of toppling, in degrees

          Principle

          In the figure the angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the vertical and the height direction of the shelf, that is, its front edge, so it is the tilt of the shelf from the vertical. A shelf resting on its front edge stays in place when the vertical line through the centre of mass falls behind the edge: the moment of the weight about the edge then turns the shelf back. The limit is when the centre of mass is exactly above the edge, and the moment of the weight about the edge is zero. In the free-body diagram the only forces acting on the shelf are the weight downwards from the centre of mass and the support force of the floor upwards at the edge.

          Formula

          At the limit the segment from the centre of mass to the front edge $P$ is vertical. The centre of mass is in the middle of the shelf, so it is half the height, $\tfrac{h}{2}$, from the middle of the base along the height direction of the shelf, and the middle of the base is half the depth, $\tfrac{d}{2}$, from the edge $P$. This gives a right-angled triangle whose legs are $\tfrac{d}{2}$ and $\tfrac{h}{2}$ and whose hypotenuse is the vertical segment. At the centre of mass the angle between the vertical and the leg $\tfrac{h}{2}$ is $\alpha$, because the leg $\tfrac{h}{2}$ is along the front edge of the shelf, and the leg opposite the angle is $\tfrac{d}{2}$. So

          $\tan\alpha$$= \dfrac{d/2}{h/2}$
          $= \dfrac{d}{h}$

          from which $\alpha = \arctan\dfrac{d}{h}$

          None needed: both lengths are in metres, and the unit cancels.

          Substitution

          $\alpha$$= \arctan\dfrac{0.400\,\text{m}}{1.80\,\text{m}}$
          $= \arctan 0.2222$
          $= 12.53^\circ$

          Answer

          $12.5^\circ$

          Three significant figures like the dimensions $0.400\,\text{m}$ and $1.80\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The vertical component of the cable force, $T \sin 30.0^\circ = 98.1\,\text{N}$, is less than the combined weight of the rod and the sign, $117.7\,\text{N}$; the hinge carries the rest. A shallow cable requires a large tension: as the angle approaches zero, $T$ would grow without limit.

        3. Exercise 2-19

          Two people carry a uniform log of length $5.00\,\text{m}$ and mass $90.0\,\text{kg}$ horizontally. The front carrier holds the front end of the log, and can lift with a force of at most $300\,\text{N}$.

          How far from the back end of the log must the rear carrier hold the log so that the force of the front carrier is exactly $300\,\text{N}$?

          m
          Answer

          $1.21\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          log $L = 5.00\,\text{m}$, $m = 90.0\,\text{kg}$, centre of mass in the middle; force of the front carrier $F = 300\,\text{N}$ at the front end; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the distance $a$ of the rear carrier from the back end of the log

          Principle

          The log is at rest, so the sum of the moments about any point is zero. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on the log are the weight $G$ downwards from the middle and the forces of the carriers upwards. The rear carrier is chosen as the reference point, so his unknown force drops out. The centre of mass is between the carriers, so the weight and the force of the front carrier rotate the log in opposite directions.

          Formula

          $F\,(L - a) - m g\,(\tfrac{L}{2} - a) = 0$, from which

          $a$$= \dfrac{m g \cdot \tfrac{L}{2} - F L}{m g - F}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the mass is in kilograms and the length in metres. The weight is

          $G$$= 90.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 882.9\,\text{N}$

          Substitution

          The numerator is

          $882.9\,\text{N} \cdot 2.50\,\text{m} - 300\,\text{N} \cdot 5.00\,\text{m}$
          $= 707.25\,\text{Nm}$

          and the denominator $882.9\,\text{N} - 300\,\text{N} = 582.9\,\text{N}$. So

          $a$$= \dfrac{707.25\,\text{Nm}}{582.9\,\text{N}}$
          $= 1.213\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $1.21\,\text{m}$

          From the back end of the log, three significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          The rear carrier carries $882.9\,\text{N} - 300\,\text{N} = 582.9\,\text{N}$, and about the centre of mass the moments cancel: $300\,\text{N} \cdot 2.50\,\text{m} = 750\,\text{Nm}$ and $582.9\,\text{N} \cdot (2.50 - 1.213)\,\text{m} = 750\,\text{Nm}$. The rear carrier is nearer the centre of mass and carries more.

        4. Exercise 2-20

          A uniform ladder ($5.00\,\text{m}$, $12.0\,\text{kg}$) leans with its upper end against a smooth vertical wall and with its lower end against a horizontal floor. The ladder makes an angle of $65.0\,{}^\circ$ with the floor. The wall is smooth, so the force of the wall is horizontal. The coefficient of static friction between the floor and the ladder is $\mu = 0.40$.

          A person of mass $75.0\,\text{kg}$ climbs the ladder. The weight of the person acts along the ladder at the point where the person is.

          a) Draw the free-body diagram of the ladder when the person is at the middle of the ladder. Name the forces.

          b) How far $s$ along the ladder from its lower end can the person climb before the ladder starts to slip?

          c) Justify in words why the danger of slipping grows as the person climbs higher.

          The answer box takes the result of part b).

          m
          Answer

          $4.6\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          ladder $L = 5.00\,\text{m}$, $m_\text{t} = 12.0\,\text{kg}$, centre of mass in the middle; angle with the floor $\alpha = 65.0^\circ$; person $m_\text{h} = 75.0\,\text{kg}$; coefficient of static friction $\mu = 0.40$; the wall is smooth; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          b) the distance $s$ from the lower end of the ladder along the ladder when the ladder is at the limit of slipping; a) the free-body diagram and c) the justification

          Principle

          The ladder is at rest, so both the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments are zero. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on the ladder are the weight of the ladder $G_\text{t}$ from the middle downwards, the weight of the person $G_\text{h}$ from the point $s$ downwards, the normal force $N$ of the floor from the lower end upwards, the static friction $f$ of the floor from the lower end horizontally towards the wall and the normal force $N_\text{s}$ of the wall from the upper end horizontally away from the wall. The wall is smooth, so the force of the wall is perpendicular to the wall and there is no friction at the wall. The lower end of the ladder is chosen as the point of moments, so $N$ and $f$ drop out. At the limit of slipping the friction is at its largest, $f = \mu N$. From the distance $s$ along the ladder, the lever arm of the weight is horizontally $s\cos\alpha$ and the lever arm of the force of the wall vertically $L\sin\alpha$.

          Formula

          Sum of the forces: $N = G_\text{t} + G_\text{h}$ and $N_\text{s} = f = \mu N$. Moment condition about the lower end: $M_\text{s} = M_\text{t} + M_\text{h}$, where $M_\text{s} = N_\text{s} L \sin\alpha$, $M_\text{t} = G_\text{t}\,\tfrac{L}{2}\cos\alpha$ and $M_\text{h} = G_\text{h}\, s \cos\alpha$. So

          $s$$= \dfrac{M_\text{s} - M_\text{t}}{G_\text{h}\cos\alpha}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the masses are in kilograms and the lengths in metres. The weights are

          $G_\text{t}$$= 12.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 117.7\,\text{N}$
          $G_\text{h}$$= 75.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 735.8\,\text{N}$

          and $\sin 65.0^\circ = 0.9063$ and $\cos 65.0^\circ = 0.4226$.

          Substitution

          In the vertical direction

          $N$$= 117.7\,\text{N} + 735.8\,\text{N}$
          $= 853.5\,\text{N}$

          At the limit

          $N_\text{s}$$= f$
          $= \mu N$
          $= 0.40 \cdot 853.5\,\text{N}$
          $= 341.4\,\text{N}$

          The moments are

          $N_\text{s} L \sin\alpha$$= 341.4\,\text{N} \cdot 5.00\,\text{m} \cdot 0.9063$
          $= 1547\,\text{Nm}$
          $G_\text{t}\,\tfrac{L}{2}\cos\alpha$$= 117.7\,\text{N} \cdot 2.50\,\text{m} \cdot 0.4226$
          $= 124.4\,\text{Nm}$

          The denominator is

          $G_\text{h}\cos\alpha$$= 735.8\,\text{N} \cdot 0.4226$
          $= 310.9\,\text{N}$

          So

          $s$$= \dfrac{1547\,\text{Nm} - 124.4\,\text{Nm}}{310.9\,\text{N}}$
          $= 4.575\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          b) $4.6\,\text{m}$ from the lower end of the ladder, two significant figures like the coefficient of friction $0.40$. c) The normal force $N$ of the floor is the same all the time, because it carries the combined weight of the ladder and the person, so the largest possible friction $\mu N$ does not change. However, the moment of the weight of the person about the lower end grows with the distance $s$, and to balance it the wall must push the ladder harder and harder. At the same time the friction must grow equally large, $f = N_\text{s}$. When the friction needed exceeds $\mu N$, the ladder slips.

          Reasonableness

          The result is within the length of the ladder, $4.6\,\text{m} < 5.00\,\text{m}$, so the upper end is in the danger zone for slipping: the last $0.4\,\text{m}$ or so are unsafe. A check about a second point of moments, the upper end of the ladder: the moment of the normal force of the floor is $N L \cos\alpha = 1803.5\,\text{Nm}$. The moment of the friction is $1547.0\,\text{Nm}$, that of the weight of the ladder $124.4\,\text{Nm}$ and that of the weight of the person $132.1\,\text{Nm}$, and together these are $1803.5\,\text{Nm}$.

The table gives the horizontal distance $x_\text{h}$ of the centre of mass of the person from the rear legs as a function of the lean angle $\theta$ of the upper body.           Given

          distance between the legs $d = 0.40\,\text{m}$; mass of the chair $m_\text{t} = 8.00\,\text{kg}$ and centre of mass at $0.20\,\text{m}$ from the rear legs; person $m_\text{h} = 72.0\,\text{kg}$; points of the table $(\theta$; $x_\text{h})$: $(0^\circ$; $20.0\,\text{cm})$, $(10^\circ$; $25.1\,\text{cm})$, $(20^\circ$; $29.4\,\text{cm})$, $(30^\circ$; $34.2\,\text{cm})$, $(40^\circ$; $38.1\,\text{cm})$, $(50^\circ$; $41.3\,\text{cm})$ and $(60^\circ$; $44.4\,\text{cm})$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          a) the largest distance $x_\text{h}$ of the centre of mass of the person from the rear legs; b) the lean angle $\theta$ at which the chair starts to topple; c) the justification

          Principle

          The feet of the person do not reach the floor, so the whole weight of the person rests on the chair. The chair topples onto its front legs when the common centre of mass of the chair and the person moves to the front legs: then the support force of the rear legs is zero and the moment of the weights about the front legs is zero. In the free-body diagram the forces acting on the chair and the person are the weights $m_\text{t} g$ and $m_\text{h} g$ downwards and the support forces of the floor from the legs upwards. The common centre of mass is calculated from the masses and positions as for two bodies, and the limit is when it is at the front legs, $0.40\,\text{m}$ from the rear legs. This gives the limiting value of the centre of mass of the person, and the angle is read from the table at the point where $x_\text{h}$ reaches it.

          Formula

          $x_\text{cm}$$= \dfrac{m_\text{t} x_\text{t} + m_\text{h} x_\text{h}}{m_\text{t} + m_\text{h}}$
          $= d$

          from which

          $x_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{(m_\text{t} + m_\text{h})\, d - m_\text{t} x_\text{t}}{m_\text{h}}$

          Linear interpolation between two table points $(\theta_1$; $x_1)$ and $(\theta_2$; $x_2)$:

          $\theta$$= \theta_1 + (\theta_2 - \theta_1)\,\dfrac{x_\text{h} - x_1}{x_2 - x_1}$

          Conversions

          Not needed in the calculation of part a), but the distances in the table are in centimetres: $0.4222\,\text{m} = 42.22\,\text{cm}$.

          Substitution

          In part a) the numerator is $80.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.40\,\text{m} = 32.0\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}$ minus the term of the chair $8.00\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.20\,\text{m} = 1.6\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}$, that is $30.4\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}$. So

          $x_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{30.4\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{72.0\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 0.4222\,\text{m}$
          $= 42.2\,\text{cm}$

          In the table the limiting value is between the points $(50^\circ$; $41.3\,\text{cm})$ and $(60^\circ$; $44.4\,\text{cm})$, so in part b)

          $\theta$$= 50^\circ + 10^\circ \cdot \dfrac{42.22 - 41.3}{44.4 - 41.3}$
          $= 50^\circ + 2.97^\circ$
          $= 52.97^\circ$

          Answer

          b) $53^\circ$, two significant figures, because the angle is interpolated between table points. c) When the person reaches forward, the centre of mass of the person moves forward, and with it the common centre of mass of the chair and the person. As long as the common centre of mass is between the legs, the rear legs carry part of the weight and the chair stays in place. When the common centre of mass passes the front legs, the moment of the weights about the front legs turns the chair forward and no force can turn it back, because the support force of the rear legs is already zero. The chair topples. A longer reach would be allowed, for example, by moving the front legs further forward, so that the limiting value $x_\text{h}$ grows: if the distance between the legs were $0.50\,\text{m}$ and the centre of mass of the chair $0.25\,\text{m}$ away, the numerator would be $80.0 \cdot 0.50 - 8.00 \cdot 0.25 = 38.0$ and the limit $x_\text{h} = 38.0 / 72.0\,\text{m} = 0.528\,\text{m}$.

          In the initial situation $\theta = 0^\circ$ the centre of mass of the person is at $20.0\,\text{cm}$, that is halfway between the legs, so the chair stands firmly. At the limit the sum of the products of masses and positions is $8.00 \cdot 20.0 + 72.0 \cdot 42.22 = 3200$, and divided by the total mass $80.0$ the common centre of mass is $40.0\,\text{cm}$, that is at the front legs, and the front legs then carry the whole weight $80.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 785\,\text{N}$.

        1. Write the two equilibrium conditions of a body: for translation and for rotation.
          Answer

          For translation the sum of the forces is zero, $\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0}$. For rotation the sum of the moments of the forces is zero, $\Sigma M = 0$, about any point.

        2. The sum of the forces acting on a body is zero, but the sum of the moments is not. What happens to the body?
          Answer

          The body starts to rotate, although its centre of mass does not start to move. For example, a ruler with equal and opposite forces on its ends along different lines rotates on the spot.

        2. Concept task: Centre of mass and toppling

        A bookshelf tilts when it is pulled by its upper edge. When does the shelf topple by itself if it is let go?

        • Correct. The moment of the weight about the lower edge then rotates the shelf over the edge. As long as the vertical line falls within the base, the moment of the weight turns the shelf back onto its base.

        • The height of the centre of mass alone does not decide. A high centre of mass makes the angle of toppling smaller, but the shelf topples only when the vertical line through the centre of mass passes the edge of the base; otherwise its weight turns the shelf back onto its base.

        • The position of the top edge does not decide. The upper part of the shelf can be outside the base, but the shelf stays upright until the vertical line through the centre of mass passes the edge of the base.

        3. Calculation task: A seesaw

        A seesaw is supported at its middle, so its own weight does not rotate it. A child of mass $24.0\,\text{kg}$ sits at one end of the board, $1.50\,\text{m}$ from the pivot. An adult keeps the board horizontal by pressing the other side down with a hand, vertically, $1.20\,\text{m}$ from the pivot.

        With what force does the adult press? Answer in newtons.

        N
        Hint 1: a guiding question

        Choose the pivot of the board as the reference point. Which forces then drop out of the moment condition?

        Hint 2: what is the connection

        The force of the child rotates the board in one direction and the force of the adult in the other: $F \cdot 1.20\,\text{m} = m g \cdot 1.50\,\text{m}$.

        Show the solution

        Given

        child $m = 24.0\,\text{kg}$, lever arm $r_1 = 1.50\,\text{m}$; lever arm of the force of the adult $r_2 = 1.20\,\text{m}$; board supported at the middle; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Find

        the pressing force of the adult $F$ in newtons

        Principle

        The board stays horizontal at rest, so the sum of the moments about the pivot is zero. The pivot is a good reference point, because the lever arm of the support force and of the weight of the board is then zero.

        Free-body diagram

        The body is the board. The forces acting on it are the force of the child $\overline{F}_\text{c}$ downwards at one end (the child sits at rest, so the magnitude of the force is $F_\text{c} = mg$), the force of the adult $\overline{F}$ downwards on the other side, the weight of the board $\overline{G}$ downwards from the middle and the force of the support $\overline{N}$ upwards. $\overline{G}$ and $\overline{N}$ act at the pivot. The force of the child rotates the board in one direction and the force of the adult in the other; the direction of rotation of the force of the adult is chosen as positive.

        Formula

        $F r_2 - m g r_1 = 0$, from which $F = \dfrac{m g r_1}{r_2}$

        Conversions

        None needed: the mass is in kilograms and the lever arms in metres.

        Substitution

        $F$$= \dfrac{24.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.50\,\text{m}}{1.20\,\text{m}}$
        $= \dfrac{353.16\,\text{Nm}}{1.20\,\text{m}}$
        $= 294.3\,\text{N}$

        Answer

        $294\,\text{N}$

        Three significant figures like the mass and the lever arms.

        Reasonableness

        The weight of the child is $235\,\text{N}$. The adult presses nearer the pivot, so the force is greater, $1.50 : 1.20 = 1.25$ times as large: $1.25 \cdot 235\,\text{N} \approx 294\,\text{N}$.

        Optional 4. Explanation task: Why the nearer support carries more

        A uniform plank rests on two supports, and one support is nearer the middle of the plank than the other. Using the equilibrium conditions, explain why the nearer support carries the larger share of the weight of the plank.

        Model answer

        The plank is at rest, so the sum of the forces is zero, $N_1 + N_2 = G$, and the sum of the moments about any point is zero. Take the moments about the centre of mass: the lever arm of the weight is zero, and the support forces rotate the plank in opposite directions. They cancel each other when $N_1 r_1 = N_2 r_2$, where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the distances of the supports from the centre of mass. When $r_2 < r_1$, we must have $N_2 > N_1$: the force of the nearer support has the shorter lever arm, so it must be greater for its moment to be equal to that of the farther support.
