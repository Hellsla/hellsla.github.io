## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** The hook and the review question on the D–T Q-value. Ask: "If the Sun is 'too cold' for fusion, how does it shine?"
- **5–10 min, Predict.** The three predictions individually, each with a short written reason.
- **10–30 min, Investigate.** In pairs: read the 1934 source (one reads each point aloud, the other says what it shows) and answer its question; step through the D–T reaction diagram (seven steps, including tunnelling) twice; write the description of the process with the word bank.
- **30–44 min, Theory summary.** The proton–proton chain, the key idea (Coulomb barrier and tunnelling) and the formula for sharing the energy; the vocabulary match; back to the predictions.
- **44–62 min, Apply.** The worked example (the energy share, a step-by-step repeat of the diagram) briefly on the board; read *Fusion in the laboratory* together (the table and three short paragraphs); the pair comparison of the Sun and a reactor in three or four sentences.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks and the transfer task on the neutrons, the summary and the French corner.

## Organisation and preparation
- This is a full lesson. The energy-scale chart with the coal-equivalent task, the order task on the proton–proton chain and the biography of Mark Oliphant are optional, for fast pairs or homework.
- The reaction diagram is the investigation: ask pairs to say each step aloud before they press *Next step*. With reduced motion turned on, the steps change without animation.
- Fast pairs: the optional tasks (the energy scales and the coal equivalent first); then ask them to estimate how much faster a nucleus with $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ moves than one with $3.5\ \text{MeV}$ (the ratio of the speeds is the inverse of the mass ratio, about $4$).
- Running out of time: skip the worked example (the diagram already did the calculation), read *Fusion in the laboratory* at home and keep the comparison task as homework. Keep the checks in class.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade: the process description is in time order and names the Coulomb barrier, the strong force, $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ and the share; the comparison links the higher temperature to the low density and short confinement and says that only the temperature is higher; the transfer task explains why the neutron leaves the plasma and what the wall must do.
- Calculation: the energy share from momentum conservation with the mass ratio, the sum checked against $Q$.
- Language: describing a process in order (first, then, when, finally), comparing with *whereas* or *while*, and at least three key words.

## Common difficulties
- "The nuclei must climb over the barrier." Stress that almost all fusions in the Sun and in reactors happen by tunnelling below the top; temperature raises the chance by giving more nuclei a higher energy. D–T fuses readily at collision energies near $0.1\ \text{MeV}$, a fifth of the $0.5\ \text{MeV}$ barrier top.
- Sharing the energy equally, or giving more to the heavier product. Ask: "Which ball moves faster after a small and a large ball push apart from rest?"
- "Hotter than the Sun" as "more extreme in every way". The table and the third check separate temperature from density and pressure.
- The reaction diagram's arrows compare speeds within one step only; the line under the figure gives the real speeds (the products are about $13$ and $52$ times faster than a $10\ \text{keV}$ deuteron). Point this out when the products appear. The diagram shows a short-lived helium-5 nucleus between fusion and break-up. This is the usual picture of the reaction; students do not need to know more about it.
- The value $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ is rounded from $14.06\ \text{MeV}$ with $Q = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$; with all digits the share is $14.05\ \text{MeV}$ and $3.54\ \text{MeV}$. Both round to the textbook values.

## Extension ideas
- Calculate the speed of the $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ neutron with $E_\text{k} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ (about $5.2 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$, $17\ \%$ of the speed of light) and discuss whether the classical formula is still good enough.
- Find out why lithium is needed in a D–T reactor (tritium breeding) and look at the reaction *Tritium breeding* ($\text{n} + {}^{6}\text{Li}$) in the Binding Energy Explorer of the unit *Mass defect and binding energy*.
- Compare the D–T reaction with D–D and with proton–boron fusion: which needs the highest temperature, and why?
