How waves form and travel

Why does a bell hanging on a post ring when the other end of a long rope is flicked once, although nobody touches the bell?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how wave motion arises and what a wave carries with it.
  • recognise a transverse and a longitudinal wave and tell a snapshot of a wave from a graph of one point.
  • calculate the speed, frequency or wavelength of a wave with the fundamental equation of wave motion.

Prior knowledge

  • The period $T$ is the time of one full vibration, and the frequency $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$ tells the number of vibrations per secondchapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude
  • The amplitude $A$ is the greatest displacement from the equilibrium positionchapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude
  • A body in simple harmonic motion moves back and forth on both sides of its equilibrium positionchapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

A rope ten metres long has been stretched across a yard, with one end tied to a post. A small bell hangs on the post at the height of the rope. When the free end of the rope is flicked once quickly up and down, a hump appears in the rope and travels towards the post. After a moment the bell rings, although nobody touched it.

Optional Revision: period and frequency
A weight hanging from a spring makes $12$ full vibrations in $6.0\,\text{s}$. What is the frequency of the vibration?

  • This is the time divided by the number of vibrations, $\tfrac{6.0\,\text{s}}{12} = 0.50\,\text{s}$, that is, the period $T$ and not the frequency. The frequency is its reciprocal: $f = \tfrac{1}{T} = 2.0\,\text{Hz}$.

  • Correct. The frequency is the number of vibrations per second: $f = \tfrac{12}{6.0\,\text{s}} = 2.0\,\text{Hz}$, and the period is $T = \tfrac{1}{f} = 0.50\,\text{s}$ (chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude). Today the same frequency passes from the vibrating body to the wave.

  • $12$ is the number of vibrations in six seconds, not in one second. In one second there is a sixth of that number of vibrations, $2.0$.

  • The number of vibrations and the time are not multiplied together. The frequency tells how many vibrations occur in one second, so the number is divided by the time: $\tfrac{12}{6.0\,\text{s}} = 2.0\,\text{Hz}$.

How the calculation works

The displacement of the point $x$ at the moment $t$ is the displacement of the source at the moment $t - \tfrac{x}{v}$:

$$y = A \sin\left(2\pi f \left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)\right)$$

when $t - \tfrac{x}{v} \ge 0$, otherwise $y = 0$. In a pulse the source moves for only half a period $\tfrac{T}{2}$, so the pulse is a single crest whose length is $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$. In the readings $T = \tfrac{1}{f}$, $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$ and the distance travelled by the wave is $x = vt$. The greatest displacement of the point $P$ transversely and longitudinally is the greatest $|y|$ that has occurred since the start.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A wave is a travelling disturbance

    When the end of a rope is flicked upwards, the hand pulls the nearest point of the rope along with it. The taut rope in turn pulls the next point, and that pulls the next one, so every point starts to move a little later than the one before. In this way the displacement from the equilibrium position, the disturbance, travels along the rope away from the hand. Mechanical wave motion is the propagation of a disturbance in a medium. The particles of the medium vibrate only around their equilibrium positions and do not travel with the wave. In a gas the molecules also move randomly because of thermal motion, but the wave does not carry them along. So in the ideal wave model a wave carries energy away from the source, but not matter. This is why the bell at the other end of the rope can ring although no point of the rope travels all the way from the hand to the bell. Real waves can also carry a little matter: in a water surface wave, for example, the water particles move round almost closed paths, but a floating object drifts slowly in the direction of the wave.

    A mechanical wave needs a medium, such as a rope, a spring, water or air. One flick makes a pulse, a single hump that travels along the rope. When the hand is moved steadily up and down, a periodic wave forms, in which crests and troughs repeat. Every point of the rope then vibrates with the same frequency $f$ as the hand. Period $T$, frequency $f$ and amplitude $A$ mean the same as in the chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude. When the wave reaches the end of the rope, it is reflected back; reflection is studied in the chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap.

    Transverse and longitudinal waves

    Waves are divided into two kinds according to the direction in which the particles of the medium vibrate. In a transverse wave the particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel of the wave: the points of the rope move up and down while the wave travels horizontally. In a longitudinal wave the particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel. When the end of a long coil spring is pushed back and forth, compressions form in the spring, where the coils are close together, and rarefactions, where they are far apart. The compressions and rarefactions travel along, but each coil only swings back and forth around its own place. Sound in air, for example, is a longitudinal wave.

    The fundamental equation of wave motion

    The wavelength $\lambda$ is the distance between two successive crests or two successive compressions. It is the shortest distance between two points that move in exactly the same way. When the source makes one full vibration, that is, during one period $T$, the wave travels exactly one wavelength. The four moments in the figure show how each quarter of a period takes the wave a quarter of a wavelength further.

    So the speed of the wave is the wavelength divided by the period. Since the frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$, we get the fundamental equation of wave motion

    $$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda$$

    where $v$ is the speed of propagation of the wave ($\text{m/s}$), $\lambda$ the wavelength ($\text{m}$), $T$ the period ($\text{s}$) and $f$ the frequency ($\text{Hz}$). The units agree, because $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{s}^{-1}$, so $1\,\text{Hz} \cdot 1\,\text{m} = 1\,\text{m/s}$. The speed of a pulse can also be measured directly: a pulse travels at a steady speed in the same medium, so $v = \tfrac{s}{t}$, where $s$ is the distance and $t$ the time it takes.

    The speed depends on the medium, the frequency on the source

    The speed of a wave is determined by the properties of the medium. In a taut rope a wave travels faster than in a slack one, and sound travels in air at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at about $343\,\text{m/s}$ but in water at about $1500\,\text{m/s}$. The frequency is determined by the source: it is the frequency of the vibration of the hand, the end of the spring or the loudspeaker. In an ideal rope the speed of the wave is determined by the tension and the mass per unit length of the rope, so it does not depend on the frequency or on a small amplitude; likewise in air the speed of sound practically does not depend on the frequency. This is not a general law for all waves: the speed of water surface waves, for example, can depend on the wavelength, and such a wave is called dispersive. The very long wave of a tsunami, again, travels the faster the deeper the sea is. When the same rope is shaken more rapidly, the speed stays the same and the wavelength gets shorter: doubling the frequency halves the wavelength. When a wave passes from one medium to another, the frequency stays the same, but the speed and the wavelength change.

    A snapshot and a graph of one point

    A wave can be described by two graphs that look like similar sine waves. A snapshot, the $(x, y)$ graph, shows the shape of the whole rope at one moment: the horizontal axis shows the position $x$, and the distance between two crests is the wavelength $\lambda$. A graph of one point, the $(t, y)$ graph, shows the displacement $y$ of one point of the rope as a function of time: the horizontal axis shows the time $t$, and the distance between two peaks is the period $T$. The amplitude $A$ can be read from either graph. So always read first which quantity is on the horizontal axis.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a wave travels faster the more rapidly the hand is shaken. In the same rope, however, the speed stays the same. When the frequency increases from $1.0\,\text{Hz}$ to $2.0\,\text{Hz}$, the wavelength shortens from $2.0\,\text{m}$ to $1.0\,\text{m}$, and the product $f\lambda$ is still $2.0\,\text{m/s}$. The speed can be changed only by changing the rope or its tension, for example by tightening the rope.

    Another common mistake is to read the wavelength from the graph of one point, the $(t, y)$ graph. There the distance between peaks is the period $T$ in seconds, not the wavelength. A third mistake is to think that the wave carries the matter of the rope along: the points of the rope only vibrate in place, and it is energy that moves forward.

    Summary

    • Mechanical wave motion is the propagation of a disturbance in a medium. The particles of the medium vibrate around their equilibrium positions, and the ideal wave carries energy but not matter.
    • In a transverse wave the particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel (rope), in a longitudinal wave parallel to it (coil spring, sound in air).
    • One flick makes a pulse, steady shaking makes a periodic wave. The wavelength $\lambda$ is the distance between two successive crests.
    • The fundamental equation of wave motion is $v = \tfrac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda$. The speed depends on the medium, the frequency on the source.
    • The wavelength is read from a snapshot, the $(x, y)$ graph, and the period from the graph of one point, the $(t, y)$ graph.

      Example 1: A pulse on a rope

      A long rope is stretched taut across a yard. When the end of the rope is flicked once, the pulse travels a distance of $6.0\,\text{m}$ in $2.5\,\text{s}$. Then the same rope is shaken steadily at a frequency of $3.0\,\text{Hz}$, and the tension of the rope does not change. What is the wavelength of the wave that forms?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The distance travelled by the pulse is $s = 6.0\,\text{m}$ and the time taken is $t = 2.5\,\text{s}$; the frequency of the periodic wave is $f = 3.0\,\text{Hz}$. We are asked for the wavelength $\lambda$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The speed of a wave depends on the properties of the medium, here the tension of the rope and its mass per unit length, not on whether a pulse or a periodic wave is made in the rope. So the periodic wave travels at the same speed $v$ as the pulse, and the pulse travels steadily, so $v = \tfrac{s}{t}$. The wavelength then follows from the fundamental equation $v = f\lambda$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} v &= \frac{s}{t} \quad\text{and}\quad v = f\lambda \\ \lambda &= \frac{v}{f} = \frac{s}{f\,t} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds, and $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{s}^{-1}$, so $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{Hz}} = \text{m}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate the speed and then the wavelength:

      $$\begin{aligned} v &= \frac{s}{t} \\ &= \frac{6.0\,\text{m}}{2.5\,\text{s}} = 2.4\,\text{m/s} \\[6pt] \lambda &= \frac{v}{f} \\ &= \frac{2.4\,\text{m/s}}{3.0\,\text{Hz}} = 0.80\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The wavelength is $0.80\,\text{m}$. The answer is given with two significant figures like the initial values $6.0\,\text{m}$, $2.5\,\text{s}$ and $3.0\,\text{Hz}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The period is $T = \tfrac{1}{f} = 0.333\,\text{s}$, and in that time the wave travels $2.4\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.333\,\text{s} = 0.80\,\text{m}$, exactly one wavelength, as it should.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      The end of a rope is shaken steadily. A sensor measures the displacement of one point of the rope, and a student gets from it a graph whose horizontal axis shows the time $t$ in seconds. The distance between successive peaks of the graph is $0.50$. The student concludes: “The wavelength is $0.50\,\text{m}$.Since the frequency is $2.0\,\text{Hz}$, the speed is $v = f\lambda = 2.0\,\text{Hz} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} = 1.0\,\text{m/s}$.” Where is the error?

      • The $0.50$ read from the graph is in seconds, that is, the period, and the frequency is its reciprocal: $f = \tfrac{1}{0.50\,\text{s}} = 2.0\,\text{Hz}$, as the student wrote. The error is that the same number was also interpreted as the wavelength.

      • Correct. On the horizontal axis of the $(t, y)$ graph of one point there is time, so the distance between peaks is the period $T = 0.50\,\text{s}$. It gives the same frequency $f = \tfrac{1}{T} = 2.0\,\text{Hz}$, but the wavelength must be read from a snapshot of the rope or measured on the rope.

      • The equation $v = f\lambda$ is correct: during one period the wave travels one wavelength, so $v = \tfrac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda$. The units show the same: $\text{Hz} \cdot \text{m} = \text{m/s}$. The error is in reading the graph.

      • The distance between peaks is the wavelength only in a snapshot, that is, the $(x, y)$ graph, whose horizontal axis shows position. On the horizontal axis of this graph there is time, so the distance between peaks is the period.

      Optional Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      A long coil spring has been stretched on a table. Its end is pushed back and forth along the spring steadily $2.5$ times a second, and compressions form in the spring with $0.60\,\text{m}$ between their centres.

      How long does it take for one compression to travel to the other end of the spring, $6.0\,\text{m}$ away? Answer in seconds.

      s
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which number is the frequency and which the wavelength? Which quantity is needed to get a time from a distance?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The speed of the wave follows from the fundamental equation $v = f\lambda$, and the compression travels at a steady speed, so $t = \tfrac{s}{v}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $v$$= 2.5\,\text{Hz} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}$
      $= 1.5\,\text{m/s}$

      so $t = \tfrac{6.0\,\text{m}}{1.5\,\text{m/s}}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      frequency $f = 2.5\,\text{Hz}$ (pushes per second), distance between compressions, that is, the wavelength $\lambda = 0.60\,\text{m}$, distance $s = 6.0\,\text{m}$

      Find

      the time $t$ in which a compression travels the distance $s$, in seconds

      Principle

      Compressions are parts of a longitudinal wave, and they travel at the speed of the wave. The distance between two successive compressions is the wavelength, and the frequency is that of the source, that is, the pushes of the hand, so the speed follows from the fundamental equation. In the same spring the speed is constant, so the time is the distance divided by the speed.

      Formula

      $v = f\lambda$ and $s = vt$, so

      $$t = \frac{s}{v} = \frac{s}{f\lambda}$$

      Conversions

      Not needed: $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{s}^{-1}$, so $f\lambda$ is in m/s and the distance is in metres.

      Substitution

      $t$$= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{m}}{2.5\,\text{s}^{-1} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}}$
      $= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{m}}{1.5\,\text{m/s}}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{s}$

      Answer

      $4.0\,\text{s}$

      Two significant figures like the initial values $2.5$, $0.60\,\text{m}$ and $6.0\,\text{m}$.

      Reasonableness

      The spring has room for $6.0\,\text{m} : 0.60\,\text{m} = 10$ wavelengths, and the wave travels one wavelength during each period

      $T$$= \tfrac{1}{2.5\,\text{Hz}}$
      $= 0.40\,\text{s}$

      : $10 \cdot 0.40\,\text{s} = 4.0\,\text{s}$. The speed $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ is a walking pace, which suits a slack table spring.

        The quotients $d/\Delta t$ of the rows are about $333\,\text{m/s}$, $345\,\text{m/s}$, $341\,\text{m/s}$ and $339\,\text{m/s}$, and the slope is among them. The result $3.4 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m/s}$ is close to the speed of sound in room air, $343\,\text{m/s}$.

        Two photographs are taken of a rope at an interval of $0.15\,\text{s}$.

        In the first picture a crest is at $x = 0.60\,\text{m}$ and in the second at $x = 1.05\,\text{m}$, and between the pictures the crest has not had time to travel one wavelength. In both pictures the distance between successive crests is $1.20\,\text{m}$.

        At what frequency is the end of the rope shaken? Also justify why the displacement of the crest is not a displacement of the matter of the rope.

        Hz
        Answer

        $2.5\,\text{Hz}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        a crest at $x_1 = 0.60\,\text{m}$ and $\Delta t = 0.15\,\text{s}$ later at $x_2 = 1.05\,\text{m}$; the crest has not travelled one wavelength; distance between crests $\lambda = 1.20\,\text{m}$

        Find

        the frequency of the shaking $f$

        Principle

        A crest is a part of the wave, so it travels at the speed of the wave, $v = \tfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$. Since the crest has travelled less than a wavelength, the photographs show the same crest and not already the next one. The displacement of the crest is not a displacement of the matter of the rope: the points of the rope only move up and down, and the crest is always at the point that is at its highest position at that moment. The frequency follows from the fundamental equation.

        Formula

        $v = \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{\Delta t}$ and $v = f\lambda$, so

        $f$$= \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{\lambda\,\Delta t}$

        Conversions

        Not needed: all lengths are in metres and the time in seconds.

        Substitution

        $v$$= \dfrac{1.05\,\text{m} - 0.60\,\text{m}}{0.15\,\text{s}}$
        $= \dfrac{0.45\,\text{m}}{0.15\,\text{s}}$
        $= 3.0\,\text{m/s}$
        $f$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{m/s}}{1.20\,\text{m}}$
        $= 2.5\,\text{Hz}$

        Answer

        $2.5\,\text{Hz}$

        Two significant figures, because the time $0.15\,\text{s}$ and the displacement $0.45\,\text{m}$ are known to two figures.

        Reasonableness

        The period is

        $T$$= \tfrac{1}{2.5\,\text{Hz}}$
        $= 0.40\,\text{s}$

        so the interval between the photographs, $0.15\,\text{s}$, is less than a period, and the crest travelled $0.45\,\text{m}$, less than the wavelength $1.20\,\text{m}$, as assumed.

      2. Exercise 10-18

        One end of a long railway rail is struck with a hammer.

        A listener at the other end of the rail hears two bangs: first the sound that came along the rail and $0.50\,\text{s}$ later the sound that came through the air. Sound travels in steel at a speed of $5100\,\text{m/s}$ and in air at $340\,\text{m/s}$.

        How long is the rail? Justify why the same blow of the hammer produces two sounds that arrive at different times.

        m
        Answer

        $1.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        time difference $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{s}$, speed of sound in steel $v_\text{s} = 5100\,\text{m/s}$ and in air $v_\text{a} = 340\,\text{m/s}$

        Find

        the length of the rail $d$

        Principle

        The blow of the hammer produces sound both in the rail and in the air. The speed of a wave depends on the medium, so the two sounds travel the same distance $d$ at different speeds and arrive at different times: the sound that travelled in the steel first. Each travels at a steady speed, so the travel time is $\tfrac{d}{v}$, and the time difference is the difference of the travel times.

        Formula

        $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{d}{v_\text{a}} - \dfrac{d}{v_\text{s}}$

        so

        $d$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\dfrac{1}{v_\text{a}} - \dfrac{1}{v_\text{s}}}$

        Conversions

        Not needed: the speeds are in metres per second and the time in seconds.

        Substitution

        $d$$= \dfrac{0.50\,\text{s}}{\dfrac{1}{340\,\text{m/s}} - \dfrac{1}{5100\,\text{m/s}}}$
        $= \dfrac{0.50\,\text{s}}{0.002\,745\,\text{s/m}}$
        $= 182.1\,\text{m}$

        Answer

        $1.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m}$

        That is, about $180\,\text{m}$, two significant figures, because the time difference $0.50\,\text{s}$ is known to two figures.

        Justification

        The same blow produces sound both in the rail and in the air, and sound travels much faster in steel than in air. Both sounds travel the same distance, but the one that travelled in the steel arrives first, so the listener hears two bangs.

        Reasonableness

        In air the distance takes $\tfrac{182\,\text{m}}{340\,\text{m/s}} = 0.536\,\text{s}$ and in steel $\tfrac{182\,\text{m}}{5100\,\text{m/s}} = 0.036\,\text{s}$; the difference is $0.50\,\text{s}$. The sound in the steel is so fast that the length is only slightly more than the sound in air would travel in half a second ($170\,\text{m}$).

      3. Exercise 10-19

        A wave goes round a stadium stand: every spectator stands up $0.050\,\text{s}$ later than the one sitting next to them and then sits down again.

        The distance between adjacent seats is $0.55\,\text{m}$, and the length of the row going round the stand is $440\,\text{m}$.

        How long does it take for the wave to go once round the stand? Justify why the wave travels although no spectator changes places.

        s
        Answer

        $40\,\text{s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        delay relative to the neighbour $\Delta t = 0.050\,\text{s}$, distance between seats $\Delta x = 0.55\,\text{m}$, length of the row $s = 440\,\text{m}$

        Find

        the time $t$ in which the wave goes once round the stand

        Principle

        The wave in a stadium stand is a model of wave motion: every spectator repeats the movement of the neighbour after a small delay, so the standing up, the disturbance, travels along the row although the spectators stay in their places and only stand up and sit down. The disturbance travels one seat spacing in the time $\Delta t$, so its speed is constant and the time of one round is the distance divided by the speed.

        Formula

        $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ and $s = vt$, so

        $t$$= \dfrac{s}{v}$
        $= \dfrac{s\,\Delta t}{\Delta x}$

        Conversions

        Not needed: the lengths are in metres and the time in seconds.

        Substitution

        $v$$= \dfrac{0.55\,\text{m}}{0.050\,\text{s}}$
        $= 11\,\text{m/s}$
        $t$$= \dfrac{440\,\text{m}}{11\,\text{m/s}}$
        $= 40\,\text{s}$

        Answer

        $40\,\text{s}$

        Two significant figures like the delay $0.050\,\text{s}$ and the seat spacing $0.55\,\text{m}$.

        Reasonableness

        The row has $440\,\text{m} : 0.55\,\text{m} = 800$ spectators, and each adds a delay of $0.050\,\text{s}$: $800 \cdot 0.050\,\text{s} = 40\,\text{s}$. About $11\,\text{m/s}$ is close to the speed of the wave observed in stadiums, and an individual spectator moves only about a metre up and down.

      4. Exercise 10-20

        Example data: a $12.0\,\text{m}$ long rope is tightened with different forces $F$, and a pulse made at the end of the rope travels the whole length of the rope in time $t$. The measurement results are in the table.

        Calculate the speed of the pulse for each tension. Investigate whether the speed depends on the tension directly proportionally or in some other way.

        The rope is shaken at a frequency of $5.0\,\text{Hz}$ when the tension is $30\,\text{N}$. What is the wavelength of the wave that forms? Justify why the wavelength would change if the rope were replaced with a thicker one but the frequency were kept the same.

        Give the answer as the wavelength.

        Travel time of the pulse as a function of tension (example data)
        $F\,(\text{N})$$t\,(\text{s})$
        5.01.15
        10.00.800
        20.00.570
        40.00.410
        m
        Answer

        $5.1\,\text{m}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        length of the rope $L = 12.0\,\text{m}$, points of the tension $F$ and the travel time of the pulse $t$: $(5.0\,\text{N}$; $1.15\,\text{s})$, $(10.0\,\text{N}$; $0.800\,\text{s})$, $(20.0\,\text{N}$; $0.570\,\text{s})$ and $(40.0\,\text{N}$; $0.410\,\text{s})$, frequency $f = 5.0\,\text{Hz}$ and tension $F = 30\,\text{N}$ when shaking

        Find

        the wavelength $\lambda$ at a tension of $30\,\text{N}$ and a justification why the wavelength would change in a thicker rope

        Principle

        The pulse travels in the rope at a steady speed, so $v = L/t$. Once the speeds are calculated, we investigate how they depend on the tension: if $v$ were directly proportional to the tension, doubling the tension would double the speed. If instead the ratio $C = v^2/F$ is constant, the speed depends on the tension in another way. The model gives the speed at a tension of $30\,\text{N}$, and the wavelength comes from the fundamental equation. The speed is determined by the rope and the tension, the frequency by the source.

        Formula

        $v = \dfrac{L}{t}$, $\quad v^2 = CF$ and $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$

        Conversions

        Not needed: the length is in metres and the time in seconds.

        Substitution

        The speeds are $v = \tfrac{12.0\,\text{m}}{t}$:

        $$\begin{aligned} v_1 &= \frac{12.0\,\text{m}}{1.15\,\text{s}} = 10.4\,\text{m/s} \\ v_2 &= \frac{12.0\,\text{m}}{0.800\,\text{s}} = 15.0\,\text{m/s} \\ v_3 &= \frac{12.0\,\text{m}}{0.570\,\text{s}} = 21.1\,\text{m/s} \\ v_4 &= \frac{12.0\,\text{m}}{0.410\,\text{s}} = 29.3\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned}$$

        When the tension doubles, the speed grows by the factors $\tfrac{15.0}{10.4} = 1.44$, $\tfrac{21.1}{15.0} = 1.41$ and $\tfrac{29.3}{21.1} = 1.39$, that is, about $\sqrt{2} = 1.41$. So the speed is not directly proportional to the tension, but $v^2$ is. We calculate the ratios $C = v^2/F$:

        $$\begin{aligned} C_1 &= \frac{(10.4)^2}{5.0} = 21.8 \\ C_2 &= \frac{(15.0)^2}{10.0} = 22.5 \\ C_3 &= \frac{(21.1)^2}{20.0} = 22.2 \\ C_4 &= \frac{(29.3)^2}{40.0} = 21.4 \end{aligned}$$

        The ratios are approximately equal. Their sum is $87.9$, so the mean is

        $C$$= \tfrac{87.9}{4}$
        $= 22.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2\,\text{N}}$

        At a tension of $30\,\text{N}$ the speed and the wavelength are

        $$\begin{aligned} v &= \sqrt{CF} \\ &= \sqrt{22.0 \cdot 30} = 25.7\,\text{m/s} \\[6pt] \lambda &= \frac{v}{f} \\ &= \frac{25.7\,\text{m/s}}{5.0\,\text{Hz}} = 5.1\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

        Answer

        $5.1\,\text{m}$

        Two significant figures like the frequency $5.0\,\text{Hz}$ and the tension $30\,\text{N}$. On the basis of the data the speed grows as the tension grows but not directly proportionally: $v^2$ is proportional to the tension. Replacing the rope with a thicker one changes the medium: usually the same tension makes a heavier rope move more slowly, so the speed decreases. The frequency stays the same, because it is determined by the shaking hand, so the wavelength $\lambda = v/f$ becomes shorter.

        Reasonableness

        The tension $30\,\text{N}$ is between the values $20\,\text{N}$ and $40\,\text{N}$ of the data, so the speed must be between their speeds $21.1\,\text{m/s}$ and $29.3\,\text{m/s}$, and $25.7\,\text{m/s}$ is. The wavelength must be between $\tfrac{21.1}{5.0} = 4.2\,\text{m}$ and $\tfrac{29.3}{5.0} = 5.9\,\text{m}$, and $5.1\,\text{m}$ is.

      2. Concept task: What stays the same?

      A heavy rope has been knotted to the end of a light rope. A wave travelling in the light rope arrives at the knot and continues into the heavy rope, where waves travel more slowly.Which quantity stays the same when the wave passes from one rope to the other?

      • Correct. The knot is the source of the heavy rope: it vibrates with the frequency of the wave of the light rope, and the heavy rope vibrates in step with it.       4. Concept task: Sound as a wave

      A loudspeaker plays a steady sound, and the sound travels in air from the loudspeaker towards the listener.How does an air molecule between the loudspeaker and the listener move because of the sound?

      • About $343\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of propagation of the sound, the disturbance, in air, not the journey of a molecule. Sound does not carry air to the listener: it adds a small back-and-forth vibration to the random thermal motion of the molecules, and it is energy that moves forward.

      • A mechanical wave needs a medium that vibrates. The molecules of air move back and forth in the direction of travel of the sound; they just do not travel with the wave.

      • Correct. Sound in air is a longitudinal wave: the cone of the loudspeaker pushes and pulls the air, and the molecules vibrate in the direction of travel, so travelling compressions and rarefactions form. The molecule does not travel from the loudspeaker to the listener.

      • This is the description of a transverse wave, as in a rope. The cone of the loudspeaker moves towards the listener and back, so the air vibrates in the same direction in which the sound travels: sound in air is a longitudinal wave.
