Newton's laws and the free-body diagram

Why can a car set a trailer moving, even though the trailer pulls the car backwards with a force as large as the force with which the car pulls the trailer forwards?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • draw a free-body diagram and write the equations of motion for a new situation without help
  • solve friction, slope and connected-body tasks with Newton's laws
  • evaluate free-body diagrams, solutions and friction measurements with justification

Prior knowledge

  • Force and counterforce act on different objectschapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws
  • The net force is $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$ and points in the direction of the accelerationchapter Newton's second law
  • A free-body diagram is drawn in eight steps, and components replace the force in the calculationchapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces
  • Kinetic friction is $F_\mu = \mu N$, and on a slope $N = mg\cos\alpha$chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane
  • Each connected body gets its own equation of motionchapter Equation of motion for connected bodies
  • At terminal velocity air resistance is as large as the weightchapter Air resistance and falling through air

1Think about it

On the way to the summer cottage a car pulls a trailer loaded with firewood. When they set off from the traffic lights, the car and the trailer accelerate together. By Newton's third law the trailer pulls the car backwards with a force as large as the force with which the car pulls the trailer forwards. In the matriculation exam a situation like this often begins with the words "Draw a free-body diagram".

How can the car and the trailer set off if the forces they exert on each other are equal and opposite? Which forces decide the motion of the trailer?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Diagnostic 1: a sliding puck

An ice-hockey puck has left the stick and slides straight forwards on the ice. Air resistance is ignored. Which forces act on the puck while it slides?

  • There is no "force of motion", because it has no cause. The puck slows down, so the net force points backwards: horizontally there is only friction. Review in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws.

  • Correct. Nothing pushes the puck forwards any more. The motion continues without a force, and a small friction slows it down gradually. Review if needed in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws.

  • The stick acts on the puck only during contact. Nothing acts forwards on the sliding puck: a force is needed to change motion, not to keep it going. Review in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws.

Diagnostic 2: a collision

A lorry and a small car collide. Which one does the larger force act on in the collision?

  • A moving object has no force that it carries with it. In the collision the vehicles act on each other, and by the third law the forces are equal. Review in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws.

  • Correct. Force and counterforce are equal and opposite, and they act on different vehicles. The velocity of the car, which has the smaller mass, changes more. Review if needed in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws.

  • The velocity of the car changes more because its mass is smaller, not because the force is larger. Force and counterforce are equal. Review in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws.

Diagnostic 3: the lift stops

A lift moves upwards and slows down to stop at the top floor. In which direction does the net force on the lift car point?

  • The net force points in the direction of the acceleration, not of the velocity. While rising and slowing down, the acceleration points downwards. Review in the chapter Newton's second law.

  • The net force is zero only when the velocity does not change. The velocity of the decelerating lift changes, so the net force is not zero. Review in the chapter Newton's second law.

  • Correct. The speed decreases, so the acceleration and the net force point downwards, although the car moves upwards. The force of the cable is smaller than the weight. Review if needed in the chapter Newton's second law.

Diagnostic 4: an oblique pull

A child pulls a sledge by a rope that rises obliquely upwards, and the sledge slides on horizontal snow.How do the normal force $N$ of the snow and the weight $G$ of the sledge compare?

  • Vertically the sum of the forces is zero, but there are three vertical forces: the weight, the normal force and the vertical component of the rope's force. So $N = G - T\sin\alpha$. Review in the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces.

  • The rope pulls the sledge obliquely upwards, so it lessens how hard the sledge presses against the snow. With an oblique push downwards the normal force would be larger than the weight. Review in the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces.

  • Correct. Vertically $N + T\sin\alpha - G = 0$, so $N = G - T\sin\alpha$. Review if needed in the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces.

Diagnostic 5: a hanging mass and a cart

A cart is on a horizontal table, and a string tied to it runs over a pulley at the edge of the table to a hanging mass $m_2$. There is no friction. When the cart is released, the hanging mass descends with acceleration.How large is the tension $T$ in the string?

  • The cart is pulled by the string, not by the weight of the hanging mass. If $T$ were $m_2 g$, the net force on the hanging mass would be zero and it would not accelerate. Review in the chapter Equation of motion for connected bodies.

  • Correct. The equation of motion of the hanging mass is $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$, and a downward acceleration requires $T < m_2 g$. Review if needed in the chapter Equation of motion for connected bodies.

  • The string moves the cart, but the hanging mass accelerates downwards, so the net force on it points downwards: $T$ is smaller than its weight. Review in the chapter Equation of motion for connected bodies.

Diagnostic 6: static friction

A cupboard is pushed horizontally with a force of $40\,\text{N}$, but it does not move. The largest possible value of static friction is $\mu_0 N = 90\,\text{N}$.How large is the friction force on the cupboard?

  • Without friction the push would accelerate the cupboard. Static friction acts precisely because the surfaces tend to slide. Review in the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane.

  • $\mu_0 N$ is the upper limit of static friction, not its value. The cupboard is at rest, so the friction is as large as the push. Review in the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane.

  • Correct. Static friction adapts to the situation: the cupboard stays in place, so the friction cancels the push. Review if needed in the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane.

  • The difference only tells how much the push could still be increased before sliding. At rest the friction is as large as the push. Review in the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane.

mass $m = 100.0\,\text{kg}$; from the graph the velocity of the heavier skydiver is about $61\,\text{m/s}$ at $25\,\text{s}$ and the curve is horizontal; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

the air resistance $F_\text{v}$ in newtons

Principle

The acceleration is the steepness of the $(t, v)$ curve. When the curve is horizontal, the velocity does not change, so the acceleration and, by Newton's second law, the net force are zero. Only the weight downwards and the air resistance upwards act on the skydiver, so they are equal (chapter Air resistance and falling through air).

Reading the graph

The $100\,\text{kg}$ curve has levelled off at about $61\,\text{m/s}$ already before $25\,\text{s}$, so it is horizontal there: the skydiver falls at terminal velocity.

Free-body diagram

The skydiver as a rectangle, positive direction downwards. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ equally long upwards. The downward velocity is marked separately, the acceleration is zero.

Formula

$mg - F_\text{v} = ma = 0$, so $F_\text{v} = mg$

Conversions

None needed, the quantities are in base units.

Substitution

$F_\text{v}$$= 100.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 981\,\text{N}$

Answer

$981\,\text{N}$

Three significant figures like the acceleration of free fall $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Reasonableness

The velocity does not affect the result directly: at terminal velocity the air resistance is always as large as the weight. The air resistance on the lighter skydiver at terminal velocity is $588.6\,\text{N}$, and that is why that skydiver's terminal velocity is smaller, about $48\,\text{m/s}$. At the start of the jump, at $t = 0$, the air resistance is zero and the curves are as steep as the dashed line, $a = g$.

4Theory

Key idea

Every task in this lesson is solved in the same way: first the free-body diagram, then the equation of motion. Choose the object, list its interactions and draw one force for each. In the free-body diagram the object is a rectangle, and each force starts from its point of application. Then choose the axes and write Newton's second law along each axis. In the exam, points for the free-body diagram are lost for a missing force, an extra force or a force drawn in the wrong direction.

The opening question is settled by the choice of object. The car and the trailer act on each other with equal forces, but the forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other. The motion of the trailer is decided only by the forces acting on the trailer: while accelerating, the drawbar pulls it forwards more than the rolling resistance holds it back.

Newton's laws and forces

Newton's laws

$$\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0} \ \Leftrightarrow\ \overline{v} = \text{constant}$$
Newton's second law (fundamental law of dynamics)
$$\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$$
$$\overline{F}_{AB} = -\overline{F}_{BA}$$

where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the sum of the forces acting on the object ($\text{N}$), $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$), $\overline{a}$ the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$) and $\overline{F}_{AB}$ the force with which A acts on B. In a calculation the second law is written in components, $\Sigma F_x = ma_x$ and $\Sigma F_y = ma_y$, and the components of an oblique force are $F\cos\alpha$ and $F\sin\alpha$. The net force points in the direction of the acceleration, not always in the direction of the velocity.

Weight, friction and the slope

weight of a body at the Earth's surface
$$\overline{G} = m\overline{g}$$
kinetic friction
$$F_\mu = \mu N$$
limiting static friction
$$F_{\mu_0} = \mu_0 N$$

where $\overline{g}$ is the acceleration due to gravity, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the magnitude of the weight is $G = mg$, $N$ is the normal force ($\text{N}$), $\mu$ the coefficient of kinetic friction and $\mu_0$ the coefficient of static friction. Static friction is only as large as the situation requires, at most the limiting friction $F_{\mu_0}$, and it opposes the sliding of the surfaces relative to each other, not always the motion. The formulas are experimental models for dry surfaces. In free-body diagrams $\overline{F}_\mu$ stands for either kind of friction, static or kinetic.

On a slope with the angle of inclination $\alpha$, the components of the weight are $G_x = mg\sin\alpha$ down the slope and $G_y = mg\cos\alpha$ towards the slope. When only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface, $N = mg\cos\alpha$. The components are not new forces: they are drawn with dashed lines and replace the weight in the calculation.

Connected bodies and air resistance

For connected bodies, each body gets its own free-body diagram and its own equation of motion. A light, inextensible string and a light, frictionless pulley give the bodies accelerations of the same magnitude and tension forces of the same magnitude. Air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ points against the velocity and grows as the speed grows. At terminal velocity the acceleration is zero, so $F_\text{v} = G$.

Common mistakes

  • A force in the direction of motion. Nothing pushes a sliding object forwards. Every force has a cause, another object.
  • Force and counterforce in the same diagram. They act on different objects and do not cancel each other.
  • $N = mg$ always. On a slope, with an oblique pull and in an accelerating lift, the normal force is different from the weight.
  • $F_\mu = \mu_0 N$ at rest. At rest friction is only as large as the situation requires; $\mu_0 N$ is its upper limit.
  • Connected bodies: $T = m_2 g$. When the hanging body accelerates downwards, the tension in the string is smaller than its weight.
  • Components in addition to the forces. If both the weight and its components are in the calculation, the weight is counted twice.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: a sledge on a slope

A sledge ($25\,\text{kg}$ with the child) slides down a slope, the angle of inclination of the slope is $20^\circ$ and the coefficient of kinetic friction $0.10$.A student calculated the friction: "$F_\mu = \mu m g = 0.10 \cdot 25\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 25\,\text{N}$." What is wrong in the solution?

  • The mass and $g$ are the same, but the normal force is not as large as the weight: on a slope only the perpendicular component of the weight presses the sledge against the snow.

  • Friction is along the slope, but its magnitude depends on the normal force, which is perpendicular to the slope: $N = mg\cos\alpha$. The component $mg\sin\alpha$ takes the sledge down the slope.

  • Correct. Only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface, so $N = mg\cos 20^\circ$ and $F_\mu = 0.10 \cdot 25\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot \cos 20^\circ \approx 23\,\text{N}$.

  • The sledge slides, so kinetic friction $\mu N$ acts on it. The coefficient of static friction only gives the upper limit of static friction for an object that does not slide.

Find the mistake: a box on a belt

A conveyor belt starts moving to the right and accelerates. A cardboard box accelerates with the belt without sliding. Air resistance is ignored. A student drew the free-body diagram of the box.

What is wrong in the student's free-body diagram?

  • The acceleration may be marked next to the object in its own colour, as long as it is not a force. This has been done correctly in the diagram; the mistake is in the direction of one force.

  • Correct. Friction is the only horizontal force, and the box accelerates to the right, so static friction must point to the right. The belt tends to slide forwards from under the box, and static friction prevents it.

  • The belt acts on the box with only two forces: the normal force and friction. "The force of the belt moving it forwards" is exactly the friction, and in the diagram it points the wrong way.

  • The box accelerates horizontally, so vertically the acceleration is zero and the normal force is as large as the weight. This is correct in the diagram.

Which graph: a coffee filter

A light coffee filter is dropped from rest, and it soon reaches its terminal velocity.How does the acceleration of the filter change as a function of time while the filter falls?

  • Even a light filter has a weight, and at the start it is the only force. So the filter accelerates at first, until the air resistance grows as large as the weight.

  • Air resistance grows gradually with the speed, so the net force and the acceleration decrease gradually and do not stop suddenly.

  • This describes the growth of the speed wrongly: at the start the speed is zero but the acceleration is at its largest, $g$, because there is no air resistance yet. The acceleration decreases and does not grow.

  • Correct. At the start there is no air resistance, so $a = g$. As the speed grows, the air resistance grows and the net force decreases, until at terminal velocity $a = 0$.

Justified claim: pulley

Bodies with masses $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and $3.0\,\text{kg}$ hang from the ends of a string that runs over a light, frictionless pulley. The bodies are released from rest.Which claim about the tension $T$ in the string is correct?

  • The sum of the weights would be the force on the pulley's mounting at rest, not the tension in the string. The string pulls each body with the force $T$, which lies between the weights.

  • The heavier body accelerates downwards, so the net force on it points downwards and $T$ is smaller than its weight.

  • Correct. The lighter body accelerates upwards, so $T > m_1 g$, and the heavier one downwards, so $T < m_2 g$. From the equations $T = \tfrac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g \approx 23.5\,\text{N}$.

  • The difference of the weights, $9.8\,\text{N}$, is the net force on the whole system, which accelerates both bodies. The lighter body would not rise if $T$ were smaller than its weight.

A crane lowers a load

A construction crane lowers a $650\,\text{kg}$ concrete element vertically downwards. Before it reaches the ground the crane brakes: the element still moves downwards, but its speed decreases steadily, and the deceleration is $0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$.

How large is the tension in the cable during the braking? Answer in kilonewtons.

kN
Hint 1: a guiding question

In which direction does the acceleration point when a load moving downwards slows down?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The acceleration points upwards, so the net force points upwards: $T - mg = ma$, with upwards positive.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$T$$= m(g + a)$
$= 650\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81 + 0.60)\,\text{m/s}^2$
Show the solution

Given

mass $m = 650\,\text{kg}$, deceleration $0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$ (the load moves downwards and slows down), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the tension $T$ in the cable in kilonewtons

Principle

The element is chosen as the object. The Earth (weight) and the cable (tension) act on it. The velocity points downwards and decreases, so the acceleration points upwards, and by Newton's second law the net force also points upwards (chapter Newton's second law).

Free-body diagram

The element as a rectangle, positive direction upwards. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards and the tension of the cable $\overline{T}$ from the top surface upwards, longer than the weight. The downward velocity and the upward acceleration are marked separately.

Formula

$T - mg = ma$, so $T = m(g + a)$

Conversions

The acceleration points upwards, so $a = +0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 0.001\,\text{kN}$.

Substitution

$T$$= 650\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 0.60\,\text{m/s}^2)$
$= 650\,\text{kg} \cdot 10.41\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 6766.5\,\text{N}$
$= 6.7665\,\text{kN}$

Answer

$6.8\,\text{kN}$

Two significant figures like the deceleration $0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Reasonableness

The weight is $650\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 6.38\,\text{kN}$. During braking the cable must both hold up the weight and slow the load down, so $T$ is about $6\,\%$ larger than the weight, as it should be when $a$ is about $6\,\%$ of $g$.

Pulling a sledge at an angle

A sledge with a mass of $18\,\text{kg}$ including its load is pulled on horizontal snow at a constant velocity. The pull of the rope is $60\,\text{N}$, and the rope is at an angle of $25^\circ$ to the horizontal. Determine the coefficient of kinetic friction between the sledge and the snow.

Hint 1: a guiding question

Constant velocity: the sum of the forces is zero along both axes. Which four forces act on the sledge?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Horizontally $T\cos\alpha = F_\mu$, vertically $N + T\sin\alpha = mg$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$N$$= mg - T\sin\alpha$
$\mu$$= \dfrac{T\cos\alpha}{mg - T\sin\alpha}$
Show the solution

Given

mass $m = 18\,\text{kg}$, pull $T = 60\,\text{N}$, angle $\alpha = 25^\circ$, velocity constant, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu$

Principle

The velocity is constant, so by Newton's first law the sum of the forces is zero along both axes. The force of the rope is oblique, so it is split into components. The sledge slides, so the friction is kinetic friction $\mu N$ (chapters Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces and Weight, friction and the inclined plane).

Free-body diagram

The sledge as a rectangle, the $x$ axis to the right and the $y$ axis upwards. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface upwards, the tension of the rope $\overline{T}$ from the front end obliquely upwards and kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the rear corner of the bottom surface backwards.

Formula

$x$: $T\cos\alpha - F_\mu = 0$ and $y$: $N + T\sin\alpha - mg = 0$. With $F_\mu = \mu N$ we get

$\mu$$= \dfrac{T\cos\alpha}{mg - T\sin\alpha}$

Conversions

None needed, the quantities are in base units.

Substitution

$T\cos\alpha$$= 60\,\text{N} \cdot \cos 25^\circ$
$= 54.38\,\text{N}$
$N$$= 18\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 60\,\text{N} \cdot \sin 25^\circ$
$= 176.58\,\text{N} - 25.36\,\text{N}$
$= 151.22\,\text{N}$

so

$\mu$$= \dfrac{54.38\,\text{N}}{151.22\,\text{N}}$
$= 0.3596$

Answer

$\mu \approx 0.36$

Two significant figures like the mass $18\,\text{kg}$ and the pull $60\,\text{N}$. A coefficient of friction has no unit.

Reasonableness

The coefficient is smaller than $1$ and larger than the few hundredths of a waxed ski, which suits an ordinary plastic sledge in soft snow. If the weight were used as the normal force, the coefficient would be about $0.31$, so the effect of the oblique pull is about $15\,\%$.

A steep sledging hill

A sledge slides down a steep hill. The angle of inclination of the hill is $28^\circ$, and the coefficient of kinetic friction between the sledge and the snow is $0.15$. Air resistance is ignored.

How large is the acceleration of the sledge?

m/s²
Hint 1: a guiding question

Choose the $x$ axis down the slope and the $y$ axis perpendicular to the slope. What are the components of the weight?

Hint 2: what is the connection

$y$: $N = mg\cos\alpha$. $x$: $mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = ma$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The mass cancels: $a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha)$.

Show the solution

Given

angle of inclination $\alpha = 28^\circ$, coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu = 0.15$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is left out

Find

the acceleration $a$ of the sledge

Principle

The Earth (weight) and the snow (normal force and kinetic friction) act on the sledge. The sledge slides down, so friction points up the slope. Newton's second law is written for an axis along the slope and an axis perpendicular to it; perpendicular to the slope the acceleration is zero (chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane).

Free-body diagram

The sledge as a rectangle parallel to the slope, the $x$ axis down the slope. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre straight down, the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface perpendicular to the slope, outwards, and kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the upper corner of the bottom surface up the slope. The components of the weight with dashed lines.

Formula

$y$: $N - mg\cos\alpha = 0$ and $x$: $mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = ma$, so $a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha)$

Conversions

None needed. The mass is not needed, because it cancels.

Substitution

$a$$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (\sin 28^\circ - 0.15 \cdot \cos 28^\circ)$
$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (0.4695 - 0.1324)$
$= 3.306\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a \approx 3.3\,\text{m/s}^2$

Two significant figures like the coefficient of friction $0.15$.

Reasonableness

Without friction the acceleration would be $g\sin 28^\circ \approx 4.6\,\text{m/s}^2$, and friction reduces it by about a quarter. The result is smaller than $g$, as it should be on a slope. In the free-body diagram the weight is $100$ units, so the lengths of the arrows are to scale.

A volleyball hit upwards

A volleyball ($270\,\text{g}$) is hit straight upwards. At one moment during the rise the air resistance on the ball is $0.60\,\text{N}$.

How large is the acceleration of the ball at that moment?

m/s²
Hint 1: a guiding question

In which direction does the air resistance point when the ball rises?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The weight and the air resistance both point downwards. Choose downwards as the positive direction: $mg + F_\text{v} = ma$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$a = g + \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{m}$, with the mass in kilograms.

Show the solution

Given

mass $m = 270\,\text{g}$, air resistance $F_\text{v} = 0.60\,\text{N}$, the ball rises, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the acceleration $a$ of the ball

Principle

After the hit only the Earth (weight) and the air (air resistance) act on the ball. Air resistance points against the velocity, so for a rising ball it points downwards together with the weight. The net force and the acceleration point downwards (chapter Air resistance and falling through air).

Free-body diagram

The ball as a rectangle, positive direction downwards. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ downwards. The upward velocity and the downward acceleration are marked separately.

Formula

$mg + F_\text{v} = ma$, so $a = g + \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{m}$

Conversions

$270\,\text{g}$$= 270 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
$= 0.270\,\text{kg}$

Substitution

$a$$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + \dfrac{0.60\,\text{N}}{0.270\,\text{kg}}$
$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 2.222\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 12.03\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a \approx 12\,\text{m/s}^2$

Downwards, two significant figures like the air resistance $0.60\,\text{N}$.

Reasonableness

The acceleration is larger than $g$, because both forces slow the rise: the ball stops sooner than without air resistance. The air resistance is about a fifth of the weight $2.65\,\text{N}$, which suits a light ball hit hard.

The boat is chosen as the object and its interactions are found: the Earth (weight), the slide strips of the ramp (normal force and friction) and the cable (tension). The boat slides up the ramp, so kinetic friction points down the ramp. The velocity is constant, so the sum of the forces is zero.

Given

Principle

The velocity is constant, so by Newton's first law the sum of the forces is zero both along the ramp and perpendicular to it. The boat slides, so the friction is kinetic friction $\mu N$, and perpendicular to the ramp only the component of the weight and the normal force act (chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane).

Free-body diagram

c)

Given

$m = 280\,\text{kg}$, $\alpha = 14^\circ$, coefficient of static friction $\mu_0 = 0.35$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

whether the boat stays at rest, and the smallest angle of inclination $\alpha_0$ at which the boat starts to slide by itself

Principle

Once the cable is slack, the forces on the boat are the weight, the normal force and static friction, which points up the ramp. Static friction is only as large as the situation requires, at most $\mu_0 N$. The boat stays in place if the static friction needed, $mg\sin\alpha$, does not exceed this upper limit (chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane).

Free-body diagram

The boat as a rectangle parallel to the ramp: the weight from the centre downwards, the normal force from the bottom perpendicular to the ramp, outwards, and static friction from the upper corner of the bottom up the ramp, as large as the component of the weight along the ramp.

Formula

The boat stays when $mg\sin\alpha \le \mu_0 mg\cos\alpha$, that is $\tan\alpha \le \mu_0$. At the limiting angle $\tan\alpha_0 = \mu_0$, so $\alpha_0 = \arctan\mu_0$

Conversions

None needed. The mass cancels.

Substitution

The static friction needed is

$mg\sin\alpha$$= 2746.8\,\text{N} \cdot \sin 14^\circ$
$= 664.5\,\text{N}$

and the upper limit

$\mu_0 mg\cos\alpha$$= 0.35 \cdot 2746.8\,\text{N} \cdot \cos 14^\circ$
$= 932.8\,\text{N}$

the limiting angle $\alpha_0 = \arctan 0.35 = 19.29^\circ$

Answer

The boat stays in place, because $664.5\,\text{N} < 932.8\,\text{N}$ (equally, $\tan 14^\circ \approx 0.25 < 0.35$). It would start to slide by itself if the ramp were at an angle of at least about $19^\circ$; two significant figures like the coefficient of friction $0.35$.

Reasonableness

The limiting angle does not depend on the mass, because both the component pulling down the ramp and the upper limit of friction are proportional to the mass. A wet, slippery or icy strip can lower the coefficient of static friction, and then the boat can start moving on a gentler ramp too: the boat should not be left held by friction alone.

Common mistakes

  • A "force of motion" up the ramp is drawn in the free-body diagram in addition to the force of the cable, or the components of the weight are drawn as forces of their own in addition to the weight.
  • The normal force is taken as $N = mg$, although on the ramp $N = mg\cos\alpha$.
  • Friction is drawn up the ramp, although the boat slides up and kinetic friction opposes the sliding.
  • In part c static friction is taken directly as $\mu_0 N$, although that is only the upper limit; or the condition for sliding is written with the sine, $\sin\alpha_0 = \mu_0$.

Also accepted

  • In part c the comparison $\tan 14^\circ \approx 0.25 < \mu_0 = 0.35$ without numerical values of the forces.

Principle

The horizontal forces on the cart are the pull of the string $F$ forwards and friction $F_\mu$ backwards. $= \dfrac{1}{m}$

so $m = \dfrac{1}{k}$; and $F_\mu = F_0 = F_1 - \dfrac{a_1}{k}$

Model answer

a)

Find

which force accelerates a walker forwards and why setting off is hard on glare ice

Principle

The state of motion of an object is changed only by the external forces acting on it (Newton's second law). Force and counterforce act on different objects (third law). Static friction is at most $\mu_0 N$.

Model answer

When Ella takes a step, her foot pushes the ground backwards. By the third law the ground pushes the foot forwards with a force of the same magnitude. This force is the static friction of the ground: the foot tends to slide backwards relative to the ground, and friction prevents it. It is the only horizontal external force, so it is exactly this force that accelerates Ella forwards. Ella's muscles exert internal forces, and they alone cannot change the state of motion of the whole body. On glare ice the coefficient of static friction is small, so the upper limit of friction $\mu_0 N$ is small. If the foot pushes backwards harder than friction can resist, the foot slides. That is why the largest possible acceleration stays small, and on ice people walk with short steps, placing the foot almost vertically.

b)

Find

Principle

The motion of the car is changed only by the external forces acting on the car. The engine is part of the car, so its forces on the wheels and the body are internal forces that cancel each other (third law).

Model answer

The claim is only partly right. The engine turns the front wheels, but it cannot by itself push the whole car, because it is inside the car. The turning tyre tends to slide backwards relative to the road surface, and the static friction of the road opposes this sliding by pointing forwards. This force of the road on the driving wheels is the external force that accelerates the car. That is why a car cannot set off on glare ice even if the engine runs at full power: the wheels just spin, because the friction is not enough.

  1. A book is at rest on a table. Which force is the counterforce of the book's weight according to Newton's third law? Is it the normal force of the table?
    Answer

    The counterforce of the weight is the force with which the book pulls the Earth upwards: the weight is an interaction between the Earth and the book, so the counterforce acts on the Earth. The normal force of the table is not the counterforce, although it is equal and opposite: it acts on the same object, the book, and is equal only because the book is at rest (first law). The counterforce of the normal force is the force of the book on the table, downwards.

3. Calculation task

Box A ($30\,\text{kg}$) and box B ($20\,\text{kg}$) are side by side on a horizontal floor. A is pushed horizontally with a force of $120\,\text{N}$, and A pushes B ahead of it. The coefficient of kinetic friction between each box and the floor is $0.15$.

How large is the force with which A pushes B?

N
Hint 1: a guiding question

The boxes move together. First calculate the acceleration of the whole system: which horizontal forces are external?

Hint 2: what is the connection
$a$$= \dfrac{F - \mu(m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})g}{m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}}$

Then draw the free-body diagram of B alone.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The equation of motion of B: $P - \mu m_\text{B} g = m_\text{B} a$.

Show the solution

Given

$m_\text{A} = 30\,\text{kg}$, $m_\text{B} = 20\,\text{kg}$, push $F = 120\,\text{N}$, coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu = 0.15$ for both, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the force $P$ with which A pushes B

Principle

The boxes move together, so they have the same acceleration. The acceleration comes from the whole system, in which the forces between A and B are internal and cancel each other (third law). Then B alone is chosen as the object, and the force of A becomes external (chapter Equation of motion for connected bodies).

Free-body diagram

Box B as a rectangle, positive direction to the right. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface upwards, the push of A $\overline{P}$ from the left side to the right and kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the rear corner of the bottom surface to the left.

Formula

System: $F - \mu(m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})g = (m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})a$, so

$a$$= \dfrac{F - \mu(m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})g}{m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}}$

B: $P - \mu m_\text{B} g = m_\text{B} a$, so

$P$$= m_\text{B}(a + \mu g)$
$= \dfrac{m_\text{B}}{m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}}\,F$

Conversions

None needed, the quantities are in base units.

Substitution

$a$$= \dfrac{120\,\text{N} - 0.15 \cdot 50\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{50\,\text{kg}}$
$= \dfrac{46.425\,\text{N}}{50\,\text{kg}}$
$= 0.9285\,\text{m/s}^2$
$P$$= 20\,\text{kg} \cdot (0.9285\,\text{m/s}^2 + 1.4715\,\text{m/s}^2)$
$= 48.0\,\text{N}$

Answer

$P \approx 48\,\text{N}$

Two significant figures like the coefficient of friction $0.15$.

Reasonableness

When the coefficient of friction is the same, B gets the same fraction of the push as its fraction of the total mass: $\tfrac{20}{50} \cdot 120\,\text{N} = 48\,\text{N}$. The force is smaller than the push $120\,\text{N}$, as it should be. A pushes B with a force of $48\,\text{N}$, and B pushes A backwards with a force of the same magnitude.

4. Explanation task

A heavy and a light wooden box, whose bottoms are of the same wood, are on a plank. The plank is tilted slowly. Explain with a free-body diagram and an equation of motion why the boxes start to slide at the same angle of inclination, although a larger weight acts on the heavier one.

Model answer

The box is chosen as the object. The forces on it are the weight $\overline{G}$ straight down, the normal force of the plank $\overline{N}$ perpendicular to the plank and static friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ up the plank. With the $x$ axis along the plank, the component of the weight $mg\sin\alpha$ tends to take the box down, and perpendicular to the plank $N = mg\cos\alpha$. Static friction can be at most $\mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg\cos\alpha$. The box starts to slide when $mg\sin\alpha$ grows as large as this upper limit: $mg\sin\alpha_0 = \mu_0 mg\cos\alpha_0$. For the heavier box both the component pulling it down and the upper limit of friction are larger, in the same ratio as the mass. The mass cancels, and $\tan\alpha_0 = \mu_0$: the limiting angle depends only on the coefficient of static friction of the surfaces. The result holds in the model $F_{\mu_0} = \mu_0 N$, in which friction does not depend on the area of the contact surface.
