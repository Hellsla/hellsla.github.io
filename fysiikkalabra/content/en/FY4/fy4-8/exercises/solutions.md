## fy4-8-L1-01
**Given:** drop height $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is ignored
**Find:** the speed $v$ at the water surface in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Only the weight acts on the stone, so only the weight does work and the mechanical energy is conserved. The zero level is chosen at the water surface: at the start the stone has only potential energy, at the end only kinetic energy.
**Formula:** $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$
**Conversions:** The height is in metres, so no conversions are needed. The mass is not needed, because it cancels.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 12.0\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{235.44\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 15.34\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $15.3\,\text{m/s}$, three significant figures like the height $12.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate with round numbers: $\sqrt{2 \cdot 10 \cdot 12}\,\text{m/s} = \sqrt{240}\,\text{m/s} \approx 15.5\,\text{m/s}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. The speed is about $55\,\text{km/h}$, which suits a drop from the height of a four-storey building.

## fy4-8-L1-02
**Given:** speed at the bottom $v = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, speed at the highest point $0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; friction and air resistance are ignored
**Find:** the rise $h$ above the bottom of the ramp in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** The weight and the normal force of the ramp act on the skateboarder. The normal force is perpendicular to the motion and does no work, so the mechanical energy is conserved. The zero level is at the bottom of the ramp: the kinetic energy turns entirely into potential energy when the skateboarder stops at the highest point.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2} mv^2 = mgh$, so $h = \dfrac{v^2}{2 \cdot g}$
**Conversions:** The speed is in $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed. The mass cancels.
**Substitution:** $h = \dfrac{(6.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{36\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{19.62\,\text{m/s}^2} = 1.835\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $1.8\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the speed $6.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** When falling $1.8\,\text{m}$, the speed is $\sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 1.835}\,\text{m/s} = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, so the calculation works backwards too. In reality friction and air resistance reduce the rise slightly.

## fy4-8-L1-03
**Given:** distance $s = 4.0\,\text{m}$, frictional force $F_\mu = 35\,\text{N}$ against the motion
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the frictional force, with its sign, in $\text{J}$
**Principle:** The frictional force points against the motion, so its component in the direction of the motion is $-F_\mu$. A force that opposes the motion does negative work.
**Formula:** $W = F_\parallel s$, and $F_\parallel = -F_\mu$, so $W = -F_\mu s$
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the distance in metres, so no conversions are needed; $1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = -35\,\text{N} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m} = -140\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $-140\,\text{J}$, that is $-1.4 \cdot 10^2\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the distance $4.0\,\text{m}$ and the force $35\,\text{N}$.
**Reasonableness:** The work is negative, because friction decreases the energy of the box: $140\,\text{J}$ turns into internal energy of the box and the floor. Order of magnitude: about $40\,\text{N}$ times $4\,\text{m}$ is about $160\,\text{J}$.

## fy4-8-L1-04
**Given:** $m = 65\,\text{kg}$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$, final speed $v_2 = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the work $W_\text{net}$ done by the net force in $\text{kJ}$
**Principle:** The work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy. The net force or the distance need not be known, because the initial and final speeds tell the change in kinetic energy.
**Formula:** $W_\text{net} = \Delta E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2$, and $v_1 = 0$, so $W_\text{net} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $W_\text{net} = \tfrac12 \cdot 65\,\text{kg} \cdot (8.0\,\text{m/s})^2 = 2080\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $2.1\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $65\,\text{kg}$ and the speed $8.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The work is positive, because the kinetic energy of the sprinter increases. Estimate: $\tfrac12 \cdot 60 \cdot 64\,\text{J} \approx 1900\,\text{J}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude.

## fy4-8-L1-05
**Given:** mechanical energy at the top of the hill $E_{\text{mech},1} = 12.4\,\text{kJ}$ and at the bottom $E_{\text{mech},2} = 9.8\,\text{kJ}$
**Find:** the work $W_\text{other}$ done together by friction and air resistance, with its sign, in $\text{kJ}$
**Principle:** By the work–energy principle the change in mechanical energy equals the work done by forces other than the weight. Here the other forces are friction and air resistance; the normal force of the snow does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion.
**Formula:** $E_{\text{mech},1} + W_\text{other} = E_{\text{mech},2}$, so $W_\text{other} = E_{\text{mech},2} - E_{\text{mech},1}$
**Conversions:** Both energies are in kilojoules, so the answer comes directly in kilojoules.
**Substitution:** $W_\text{other} = 9.8\,\text{kJ} - 12.4\,\text{kJ} = -2.6\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $-2.6\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures, because the energies are known to a tenth of a kilojoule and their difference is $2.6\,\text{kJ}$.
**Reasonableness:** The work is negative, because friction and air resistance oppose the motion and the mechanical energy decreases. The skier lost about a fifth of the mechanical energy, which is believable in downhill skiing.

## fy4-8-L2-01
**Given:** height $h = 15.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 8.00\,\text{m/s}$ downward, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is ignored
**Find:** the speed $v_2$ at the ground in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** After the throw only the weight acts on the ball, so the mechanical energy is conserved. The zero level is chosen at the ground: at the start the ball has both kinetic and potential energy, at the end only kinetic energy. Kinetic energy does not depend on the direction of the velocity.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2 + mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2gh}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The mass cancels.
**Substitution:** First $v_1^2 = (8.00\,\text{m/s})^2 = 64.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$ and $2gh = 2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 15.0\,\text{m} = 294.3\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{(64.0 + 294.3)\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 18.93\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $18.9\,\text{m/s}$, three significant figures like the height $15.0\,\text{m}$ and the speed $8.00\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** A plain drop would give $\sqrt{294.3}\,\text{m/s} \approx 17.2\,\text{m/s}$, and the throw adds speed, so the result must be greater than this. The speeds do not simply add, however: $18.9\,\text{m/s}$ is much less than $8.0 + 17.2\,\text{m/s}$, because energies add, not speeds.

## fy4-8-L2-02
**Given:** $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, initial speed $v_1 = 72\,\text{km/h}$, final speed $v_2 = 0$, frictional force $F_\mu = 6.0\,\text{kN}$ against the motion; the road is horizontal and air resistance is ignored
**Find:** the braking distance $s$ in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** The road is horizontal, so the potential energy does not change. The weight and the normal force of the road are perpendicular to the motion and do no work. The only force that does work is friction, which is also the net force, so its work is the change in kinetic energy.
**Formula:** $-F_\mu s = 0 - \tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2$, so $s = \dfrac{mv_1^2}{2F_\mu}$
**Conversions:** $v_1 = 72\,\text{km/h} = 72 : 3.6\,\text{m/s} = 20\,\text{m/s}$ and $F_\mu = 6.0\,\text{kN} = 6.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{N} = 6000\,\text{N}$
**Substitution:** $s = \dfrac{1200\,\text{kg} \cdot (20\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 6000\,\text{N}} = \dfrac{480\,000\,\text{J}}{12\,000\,\text{N}} = 40\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $40\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the speed $72\,\text{km/h}$ and the force $6.0\,\text{kN}$.
**Reasonableness:** The deceleration is $6000\,\text{N} : 1200\,\text{kg} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the equations of motion give the same distance: $\tfrac{(20\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2} = 40\,\text{m}$. Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, so at double the speed the braking distance would be four times as long.

## fy4-8-L2-03
**Given:** $m = 250\,\text{kg}$, lifting height $h = 18.0\,\text{m}$, speed constant, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the work $W_\text{other}$ done by the rope in $\text{kJ}$
**Principle:** The weight and the tension force of the rope act on the load. The rope is a force other than the weight, so we use the work–energy principle. The speed is constant, so the kinetic energy does not change, and the work of the rope increases only the potential energy. The zero level is chosen at the starting height of the load.
**Formula:** $E_{\text{k},1} + E_{\text{p},1} + W_\text{other} = E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2}$, and $E_{\text{k},1} = E_{\text{k},2}$ and $E_{\text{p},1} = 0$, so $W_\text{other} = mgh$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W_\text{other} = 250\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 18.0\,\text{m} = 44\,145\,\text{J} = 44.1\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $44.1\,\text{kJ}$, three significant figures like the lifting height $18.0\,\text{m}$ and the mass $250\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** At a constant speed the force of the rope equals the weight, about $2.5\,\text{kN}$, and $2.5\,\text{kN} \cdot 18\,\text{m} = 45\,\text{kJ}$. The work is positive, because the rope pulls in the direction of the motion and increases the mechanical energy of the load.

## fy4-8-L3-01
**Given:** $m = 80\,\text{kg}$, speed at the top of the hill $v_1 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$ and at the bottom $v_2 = 13.0\,\text{m/s}$, height $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, length of the road $s = 150\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the average resistive force $F_\text{res}$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The weight, the normal force of the road, and air resistance and rolling resistance act on the cyclist. The normal force does no work. The resistive forces are forces other than the weight, so the work–energy principle gives their work. The resistive force points against the motion all the way, so its work is $-F_\text{res} s$. The zero level is chosen at the bottom of the hill.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2 + mgh - F_\text{res} s = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$, so $F_\text{res} = \dfrac{\tfrac{m}{2}(v_1^2 - v_2^2) + mgh}{s}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** First $\tfrac{m}{2}(v_1^2 - v_2^2) = \tfrac12 \cdot 80\,\text{kg} \cdot (16 - 169)\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = -6120\,\text{J}$ and $mgh = 80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 12.0\,\text{m} = 9417.6\,\text{J}$, so $F_\text{res} = \dfrac{-6120\,\text{J} + 9417.6\,\text{J}}{150\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{3297.6\,\text{J}}{150\,\text{m}} = 21.98\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $22\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $80\,\text{kg}$ and the speed $4.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The resistive forces took $3.3\,\text{kJ}$, about a third of the potential energy $9.4\,\text{kJ}$. At a speed of about $13\,\text{m/s}$ the air resistance on a cyclist is a few tens of newtons, so the average $22\,\text{N}$ for the whole hill is believable.

## fy4-8-L3-04
**Given:** $m = 70\,\text{kg}$, height difference $h = 6.0\,\text{m}$, length of the slope $L = 45\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$ and speed $0$ after stopping; horizontal stopping distance $s = 29.4;\ 31.2;\ 28.7;\ 30.5;\ 29.9;\ 30.7\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the frictional force $F$ is constant, air resistance is ignored
**Find:** a) the mean stopping distance $s_\text{mean}$; b) the average frictional force $F$ in $\text{N}$; c) the speed $v$ at the bottom of the slope
**Principle:** The weight, the normal force of the snow and friction act on the snowboarder. The normal force does no work, so friction is the only other force and we use the work–energy principle. Friction opposes the motion all the time, so its work is $-F$ times the whole distance travelled, $L + s_\text{mean}$. The zero level is the bottom of the slope. At the start and at the end the speed is zero, so all of the initial potential energy $mgh$ turns into internal energy.
**Formula:** a) $s_\text{mean} = \dfrac{\text{sum}}{6}$. b) $mgh - F\left(L + s_\text{mean}\right) = 0$, so $F = \dfrac{mgh}{L + s_\text{mean}}$. c) On the slope $mgh - F L = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\left(mgh - F L\right)}{m}}$.
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** a) The sum of the stopping distances is $180.4\,\text{m}$, so $s_\text{mean} = 180.4\,\text{m} : 6 = 30.07\,\text{m}$. b) $mgh = 70\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} = 4120\,\text{J}$ and $L + s_\text{mean} = 45\,\text{m} + 30.07\,\text{m} = 75.07\,\text{m}$, so $F = 4120\,\text{J} : 75.07\,\text{m} = 54.9\,\text{N}$. c) The work of friction on the slope is $F L = 54.9\,\text{N} \cdot 45\,\text{m} = 2470\,\text{J}$, so $\tfrac{1}{2} mv^2 = 4120\,\text{J} - 2470\,\text{J} = 1650\,\text{J}$ and $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 1650\,\text{J} : 70\,\text{kg}} = 6.87\,\text{m/s}$.
**Answer:** b) $F \approx 55\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $70\,\text{kg}$ and the height $6.0\,\text{m}$. c) The speed at the bottom of the slope is about $6.9\,\text{m/s}$, and it is smaller than the speed on a frictionless slope, $\sqrt{2gh} = 10.8\,\text{m/s}$, because friction has already turned part of the potential energy into internal energy on the slope: the mechanical energy at the bottom of the slope is $1650\,\text{J}$ and not $4120\,\text{J}$.
**Reasonableness:** The frictional force $55\,\text{N}$ is about $8\,\%$ of the snowboarder's weight $687\,\text{N}$, that is the coefficient of friction is about $0.08$, which suits slippery snow. On the slope friction turns $2470\,\text{J} : 4120\,\text{J} \approx 60\,\%$ of the energy into internal energy, which corresponds to the share of the slope in the whole distance, $45 : 75.07 \approx 60\,\%$.

## fy4-8-L3-05
**Given:** $m = 0.057\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; heights at the top $h_0 = 1.20\,\text{m}$ and after the bounces $h_1 = 0.77;\ h_2 = 0.49;\ h_3 = 0.32;\ h_4 = 0.20\,\text{m}$; air resistance is ignored
**Find:** a) the mechanical energy of the ball at the top points and the share conserved in each bounce; b) the mean of the shares in percent; c) the mechanical energy turned into internal energy in the first bounce, and a justification about the conservation of energy
**Principle:** At the top the ball is momentarily at rest, so the kinetic energy is zero and $E_\text{mech} = mgh$ (the floor as the zero level). During the flight only the weight acts, so the mechanical energy is conserved, and the changes happen in the bounces. The mass and $g$ cancel from the share, so the share is the ratio of the heights, $\dfrac{E_{n+1}}{E_n} = \dfrac{h_{n+1}}{h_n}$.
**Formula:** $E_n = mgh_n$, the share $r_n = \dfrac{h_{n+1}}{h_n}$, and the energy change in the first bounce $\Delta E = E_0 - E_1 = mg\left(h_0 - h_1\right)$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units. The share is changed to percent by multiplying by one hundred.
**Substitution:** a) The energies are $E_0 = 0.057 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 1.20\,\text{J} = 0.671\,\text{J}$, $E_1 = 0.431\,\text{J}$, $E_2 = 0.274\,\text{J}$, $E_3 = 0.179\,\text{J}$ and $E_4 = 0.112\,\text{J}$. The shares are $r_0 = 0.77 : 1.20 = 0.642$, $r_1 = 0.636$, $r_2 = 0.653$ and $r_3 = 0.625$. b) The sum of the shares is $2.556$, so the mean is $2.556 : 4 = 0.639 = 63.9\,\%$. c) $\Delta E = 0.057\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (1.20 - 0.77)\,\text{m} = 0.240\,\text{J}$.
**Answer:** b) In a bounce the ball keeps on average about $64\,\%$ of its mechanical energy, two significant figures like the heights. c) In the first bounce about $0.24\,\text{J}$ of mechanical energy turns into internal energy. Justification: the mechanical energy of the ball is not conserved in the bounces, because it decreases every time (the heights decrease: $1.20 \to 0.77 \to 0.49 \to 0.32 \to 0.20\,\text{m}$), but the total energy is conserved: in a bounce the interaction between the ball and the floor turns part of the mechanical energy into internal energy (warming) of the ball and the floor, and into sound.
**Reasonableness:** The shares $0.625$–$0.653$ are almost the same, so the ball loses about the same share in every bounce, which is believable for a tennis ball. After four bounces $0.20 : 1.20 \approx 17\,\%$ of the initial energy is left, and $0.639^4 \approx 0.167$ gives the same.
