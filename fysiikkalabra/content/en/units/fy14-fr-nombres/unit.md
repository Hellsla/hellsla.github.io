On a visit to ITER you will buy things: a coffee, a sandwich, a bus ticket, a postcard. Every time, someone tells
you a price in French, quickly. In this lesson you learn to understand that price and to say it yourself. This is a
scenario for practice, and every price in the lesson is invented.

## Counting to 100

Most French numbers are built like Finnish ones: you put the parts together.

- **0–16** have their own words: *<span lang="fr">zéro, un, deux, trois, quatre, cinq, six, sept, huit, neuf, dix, onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize</span>*.
- **17–19** are *ten and seven* and so on: *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">dix-sept</span>, <span class="nobr">dix-huit</span>, <span class="nobr">dix-neuf</span></span>*.
- **The tens to 60:** *<span lang="fr">vingt, trente, quarante, cinquante, soixante</span>*. Then you add the units with a hyphen: *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">vingt-deux</span></span>*, *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">trente-cinq</span></span>*.
  For 21, 31, 41, 51 and 61 you say *and one*: *<span lang="fr">vingt et un</span>*, *<span lang="fr">soixante et un</span>*.

From 70 to 99, France counts with **twenties**, a little like the old English *four score* ($4 \cdot 20$) for 80:

| Number | French | How it is built |
|---|---|---|
| 70 | <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">soixante-dix</span></span> | $60 + 10$ |
| 71 | <span lang="fr">soixante et onze</span> | $60 + 11$ |
| 75 | <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">soixante-quinze</span></span> | $60 + 15$ |
| 80 | <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingts</span></span> | $4 \cdot 20$ |
| 81 | <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingt-un</span></span> | $4 \cdot 20 + 1$ (no *et*) |
| 90 | <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingt-dix</span></span> | $4 \cdot 20 + 10$ |
| 97 | <span class="wrap" lang="fr">quatre-vingt-dix-sept</span> | $4 \cdot 20 + 17$ |
| 100 | <span lang="fr">cent</span> | |

So from 70 and from 90 you count on with the numbers 10–19. *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">Quatre-vingts</span></span>* has an *s* only when no number follows it.

In Belgium and Switzerland people say **<span lang="fr">septante</span>** (70) and **<span lang="fr">nonante</span>** (90). In three Swiss cantons (Vaud,
Valais, Fribourg) 80 is **<span lang="fr">huitante</span>**. In France you will hear *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">soixante-dix</span></span>* and *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingt-dix</span></span>*.

## Prices in euros

France uses the **euro** (€), and one euro is 100 **<span lang="fr">centimes</span>**. In France people say *<span lang="fr">centime</span>*, not *cent*.
French writes prices with a **decimal comma** and puts the euro sign after the number: <span lang="fr">2,50 €</span>, <span lang="fr">0,90 €</span>.

To say a price, put **<span lang="fr">euros</span>** between the euros and the cents:

- <span lang="fr">2,50 €</span> = <span lang="fr">deux euros cinquante</span>
- <span lang="fr">1,80 €</span> = <span lang="fr">un euro <span class="nobr">quatre-vingts</span></span>
- <span lang="fr">0,90 €</span> = <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingt-dix</span> centimes</span>

When you do not catch a price, ask. These phrases are polite, with *<span lang="fr">vous</span>*:

## Sounds of the numbers

The hints in italics use Finnish letters, as in the first lesson: capitals show the stressed syllable, *õ*, *ã* and
*ẽ* are nasal vowels, *è* is an open *e*, *ö* is close to Finnish *ö* and *ž* is the *s* of English *pleasure*.

1. **Final consonants.** *<span lang="fr">Six</span>* and *<span lang="fr">dix</span>* end in a clear *s* when they stand alone (*SIS*, *DIS*), but the *s* is
   silent before a consonant: <span lang="fr">six croissants</span> *si krwa-SÃ*. *<span lang="fr">Sept</span>* is *SET* and *<span lang="fr">huit</span>* is *YIT*: the French
   *u* is the Finnish *y*.
2. **<span lang="fr">Vingt</span> and <span lang="fr">cent</span>.** The final *gt* and *t* are silent: *VẼ*, *SÃ*. In 21–29 the *t* of *<span lang="fr">vingt</span>* is heard
   (*vẽt-DÖ*), but never in 81–99: <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingt-un</span></span> is *ka-trö-vẽ-Ẽ*.
3. **Liaison with <span lang="fr">euros</span>.** *<span lang="fr">Euro</span>* starts with a vowel, so a silent final consonant is linked to it:
   <span lang="fr">deux euros</span> *dö-zö-RO*, <span lang="fr">trois euros</span> *trwa-zö-RO*, <span lang="fr">six euros</span> *si-zö-RO*, <span lang="fr">dix euros</span> *di-zö-RO*,
   <span lang="fr">un euro</span> *ẽ-nö-RO*, <span lang="fr">vingt euros</span> *vẽ-tö-RO*.
4. **Stress at the end.** A French number is one long word with the stress on its last syllable:
   <span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">quatre-vingt-dix-sept</span></span> *ka-trö-vẽ-di-SET*. Listen for the end: it tells you the units.
