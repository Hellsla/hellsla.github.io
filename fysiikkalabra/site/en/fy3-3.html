Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature

Why does a bicycle tyre feel harder on a hot summer day than on a frosty day, even though no more air has been pumped into it?

In this lesson you learn

  • You can describe a gas as a thermodynamic system whose state is given by temperature, pressure and volume.
  • You can use the micro model to explain what pressure and temperature are.
  • You can justify why temperatures are compared in kelvins from absolute zero.

Prior knowledge

  • Celsius scale: water freezes at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and boils at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at normal pressure (lower secondary school physics)
  • Pumping extra air into a tyre makes it harder (everyday experience)

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 7.

1Think about it

A bicycle is left outside in a frosty night, and in the morning the tyre gives way under your thumb. In the afternoon the same bicycle has stood in the sun, and the tyre is hard. The valve has been closed all the time, so the tyre holds the same air, and the rubber stretches so little that the volume of the tyre stays almost the same. The only thing that has changed is the temperature of the air.

What happens to the air inside the tyre when it warms up, and why does it then push harder on the walls of the tyre?

Optional Review: why does a tyre get harder?
You pump more air into a bicycle tyre. Why does the tyre get harder?

  • An empty tyre does not get harder even if you tap it. The hardness comes from the air inside the tyre, which pushes the rubber outwards.

  • Exactly: when more air is brought into the same space, its pressure on the walls of the tyre increases. In the opening phenomenon, however, no air is added. Something else makes the same air push harder on the walls.

  • Pumping increases the amount of air in the tyre; no air disappears anywhere. The air is squeezed into a smaller space, but that is exactly why it pushes harder on the walls of the tyre.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    When you study a phenomenon, you separate the part of the world you are looking at from the rest. The separated part is the thermodynamic system, and everything outside it is the surroundings. The air in a bicycle tyre is an example of a system. If no matter can leave or enter the system, the system is closed. The state of a system is described by state variables: temperature, pressure and volume, and the amount of substance. Pressure is force per unit area; its unit is the pascal (Pa), and 1 kPa = 1000 Pa. Pressure is studied in more detail later. When you change one state variable of a gas system, at least one of the others changes too.

    The micro model of matter explains why this happens. A gas consists of particles that move all the time in all directions and collide with each other and with the walls of the container. The temperature of a gas tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles: the hotter the gas, the faster the particles move on average. Pressure arises when particles hit the wall and each collision pushes the wall a little. When the volume decreases and the temperature stays the same, the particles have less room and hit the wall more often, so the pressure increases, but a single collision does not become harder. When the temperature rises but the volume stays the same, the particles hit the wall both more often and harder, so the pressure increases. This is why the tyre feels harder in summer.

    The figure is schematic: the length of an arrow shows the speed of a particle. The containers are the same size and contain the same number of particles.

    Temperature is measured on the Celsius scale, where $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the freezing point of water and $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the boiling point of water at normal pressure, and on the Kelvin scale. The zero of the Kelvin scale is absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature. At absolute zero the average kinetic energy of the particles is at its smallest, and nothing can be colder. A Celsius degree and a kelvin are the same size, so a temperature difference is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius. When the volume and the amount of gas stay the same and the gas is dilute, that is, far from liquefying, the pressure of the gas is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins. The average kinetic energy of the particles of a dilute gas is also directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins, so ratios of temperatures are compared in kelvins. The connection between pressure, volume and temperature is studied in more detail in the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

    Limits of the model

    The statement "temperature tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles" holds as such for a dilute gas whose particles interact with each other only when they collide. Then the temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the translational motion of the particles. In a liquid and in a solid the particles are close to each other and vibrate, pulled by their neighbours. Temperature describes the strength of the thermal motion of the particles in them too, but the energy of the substance also includes the energy of the interactions between the particles. So temperature is not the same thing as the energy of a substance: ice and water can have the same temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, although water of the same mass has more internal energy than ice (chapters Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy and Changes of state). Even at absolute zero not all motion of the particles stops, but its energy is the smallest possible.

    Kelvin scale

    The conversion of a Celsius temperature $\theta$ to kelvins is:

    $$T = \theta + 273.15$$

    where $T$ is the temperature in kelvins (K) and $\theta$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius (°C). In practice the number 273 is often enough. For kelvins you do not use the word degree: you say "300 kelvins", not "300 degrees kelvin".

    Absolute zero is therefore $0\,\text{K}$ = $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. A temperature in kelvins cannot be negative, but a Celsius temperature can.

    The figure is drawn to scale. The upper scale is the Kelvin scale and the lower scale is the Celsius scale; the interval from $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is as long as the interval from $273.15\,\text{K}$ to $373.15\,\text{K}$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the temperature doubles when it rises from $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The physically correct comparison is made in kelvins: from $283\,\text{K}$ to $293\,\text{K}$. The ratio of the temperatures is 293/283 ≈ 1.035, so the increase is about $3.5\,\%$. This is why the average kinetic energy of the particles does not double. Another mistake is to think that $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the lowest possible temperature. It is only the freezing point of water, and the lowest possible temperature is $0\,\text{K}$.

    Summary

    • A thermodynamic system is a separated part of the world. Its state is described by state variables: temperature, pressure, volume and amount of substance.
    • In a gas system, temperature, pressure and volume depend on each other: if you change one, at least one of the others changes too.
    • Micro model of a gas: temperature tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles, and pressure arises from the collisions of the particles with the wall. In a liquid and in a solid the energy also includes the energy of the interactions between the particles, so temperature is not the same thing as the energy of a substance.
    • When the volume and the amount of substance stay the same, the pressure of a dilute gas is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins. Continuing the line through the data points, that is, extrapolation, gives an estimate of absolute zero.
    • The conversion is $T = \theta + 273.15$. Absolute zero is $0\,\text{K}$ = $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A closed rigid container holds gas, so its volume and the amount of substance stay the same. The pressure of the gas is measured at different temperatures:

      $\theta$ (°C) 0 20 40 60 80 100
      $p$ (kPa) 100 107 115 122 129 137

      Use extrapolation to find the temperature at which the pressure of the gas would be zero.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The table has six data points $(\theta,\, p)$, and the pressure is $p_0 = 100\,\text{kPa}$ at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. You are asked for the temperature $\theta_0$ at which the pressure would be zero. On a graph the points lie on a straight line: for every $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the pressure increases by $7$–$8\,\text{kPa}$. The line is continued to the left, outside the measured range, that is, extrapolated.

      The vertical axis is drawn at the left edge of the figure, at about $-300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and not at $\theta = 0$. The solid line is the measured range and the dashed line is the extrapolation.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The container is closed and rigid, so the amount of gas and the volume stay the same, and the pressure depends only on the temperature. The data points are on a straight line, so the pressure changes steadily with the temperature. The equation of the line gives an estimate of the pressure also outside the measured range, if you assume that the dependence stays the same. The pressure is zero where the line crosses the temperature axis.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The slope $k$ is calculated from two points of the line that are far apart. Here the data points lie on the line within the reading accuracy, so the end points of the measured range are also points of the line. In the equation of the line, $p_0$ is the pressure at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Set $p = 0$ and solve for $\theta_0$:

      $$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta \theta} \\[4pt] p &= p_0 + k\theta \\ 0 &= p_0 + k\theta_0 \\ \theta_0 &= -\frac{p_0}{k} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. No conversions are needed. The pressures are already in kilopascals, and the temperature difference $\Delta\theta$ is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, so the unit of the slope $\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$ is also $\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}}$. The result comes out directly in degrees Celsius, because the temperatures in the table are in degrees Celsius.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the slope and then the zero point:

      $$\begin{aligned} &k = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta \theta} \\ &= \frac{(137 - 100)\,\text{kPa}}{(100 - 0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= 0.37\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \\[6pt] &\theta_0 = -\frac{p_0}{k} \\ &= -\frac{100\,\text{kPa}}{0.37\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}} \\ &= -270.27\,{}^\circ\text{C} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The temperature at zero pressure is about $-270\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result is given with two significant figures, because the slope $0.37\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$ is known only to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The result is close to absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$: only about $3\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, or one per cent, away from it. An independent check: if the pressure is proportional to the temperature in kelvins, $p = 100\,\text{kPa}$ corresponds to $273.15\,\text{K}$, so the pressure at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ would be $100\,\text{kPa} \cdot \tfrac{373.15}{273.15} = 136.6\,\text{kPa}$, and the measured value is $137\,\text{kPa}$. According to the micro model, at zero pressure the motion of the particles would be so small that no pressure would arise any more. Real gases liquefy before this temperature is reached, so the continuation of the line is only a model and describes a limiting value.

      Optional Find the error
      In a rigid closed tank the pressure of a gas is 120 kPa at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The gas warms up to $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.A student reasons: "The temperature doubles, so the pressure doubles: $p = 2 \cdot 120\,\text{kPa} = 240\,\text{kPa}$." Where is the error?

      • Correct. The pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins, not to the Celsius temperature: $p_2 = 120\,\text{kPa} \cdot \dfrac{313.15\,\text{K}}{293.15\,\text{K}} \approx 128\,\text{kPa}$. The zero of the Celsius scale is only the freezing point of water.

      • Even at constant volume the pressure increases when the particles move faster. The student was right that the pressure increases; the error is in how much.

      • You cannot add kelvins or degrees Celsius to kilopascals, because they are different quantities. The pressure changes in the same proportion as the temperature in kelvins: by about $7\,\%$, to about 128 kPa.

      • Physically the temperature does not double: in kelvins it rises from $293\,\text{K}$ to $313\,\text{K}$, only about $7\,\%$. The pressure increases in the same proportion, to about 128 kPa.

      Optional Apply to a new situation

      A glass jar is sealed with an airtight lid in a room where the air temperature is $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the pressure is 101 kPa. The jar is heated in a water bath until the temperature of the air inside it is $80\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The volume of the jar and the amount of air stay the same.

      What is the pressure of the air in the jar? Give your answer in kilopascals.

      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which state variables stay the same and which change? On which scale must the ratio of the temperatures be calculated?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      When the volume and the amount of substance stay the same, the pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins: $\dfrac{p_2}{p_1} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, so $p_2 = p_1 \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      air in a closed jar, $\theta_1 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $p_1 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, $\theta_2 = 80\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; the volume of the jar and the amount of air stay the same

      Find

      pressure of the air $p_2$ in kilopascals

      Principle

      The volume and the amount of air are constant, so the pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins: the particles of the warmer air move faster and hit the wall more often and harder. The proportionality does not hold for the Celsius temperature.

      Formula

      $\dfrac{p_2}{p_1} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, so

      $$p_2 = p_1\,\frac{T_2}{T_1}$$

      Conversions

      The temperatures are converted to kelvins: $T_1 = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = 80 + 273.15 = 353.15\,\text{K}$. The pressure stays in kilopascals.

      Substitution

      $p_2$$= 101\,\text{kPa} \cdot \dfrac{353.15\,\text{K}}{293.15\,\text{K}}$
      $= 121.67\,\text{kPa}$

      Answer

      $122\,\text{kPa}$

      Three significant figures like the pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$.

      Reasonableness

      The temperature in kelvins rises by $60\,\text{K}$, or about $\dfrac{60}{293} \approx 20\,\%$, so the pressure rises by about $0.20 \cdot 101\,\text{kPa} \approx 21\,\text{kPa}$, and $101 + 21 \approx 122\,\text{kPa}$. The temperature rises, so the pressure must increase. The ratio of Celsius temperatures $\tfrac{80}{20} = 4$ would give an unreasonable $404\,\text{kPa}$. The pressure in the jar is greater than the outdoor air pressure of $101\,\text{kPa}$, so the lid bulges outwards.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      The temperature of the air is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What does the micro model say about the particles of the air?

      • Water freezes at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, but air does not. For example, nitrogen, the main part of air, stays a gas down to about $-196\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and its particles move freely also at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      • $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is only the freezing point of water. In kelvins it is $273\,\text{K}$, so the particles are moving. The motion of the particles is at its smallest only at absolute zero, $0\,\text{K}$ = $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      • Correct. $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ = $273.15\,\text{K}$ is far from absolute zero, and the average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the temperature in kelvins.

      • The particles of a gas move all the time, also without heating. Heating only increases their average kinetic energy.

      2. Increase in temperature

      The temperature of the air rises from $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What happens to the average kinetic energy of the particles?

      • The zero of the Celsius scale has been chosen to be the freezing point of water, not absolute zero. The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the temperature in kelvins, and in kelvins the temperature rises only from $283\,\text{K}$ to $293\,\text{K}$.

      • A temperature change of $10\,\text{K}$ is not a percentage as such. The relative increase is found by comparing the temperatures in kelvins: $10\,\text{K}/283.15\,\text{K} \approx 3.5\,\%$.

      • Correct. The average kinetic energy is proportional to the temperature in kelvins, so its ratio is $\dfrac{293.15\,\text{K}}{283.15\,\text{K}} \approx 1.035$, which means an increase of about $3.5\,\%$.

      The table shows the theoretical values of the simulation for the pressure (a relative number) when the volume is 1.0 dm³. They are values of the model, not measurements.

      $\theta$ (°C) −123 27 177

      The points are on the same line. Continue the line and find the temperature at which the pressure would be zero. Give your answer in degrees Celsius.

      Hint 1: a guiding question

      How much does the pressure change when the temperature rises by one degree Celsius?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The slope is $k = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta \theta}$, and the pressure is zero when it has decreased from 100 to zero:

      $\theta_0$$= 27\,\text{°C} - \dfrac{100}{k}$
      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $k$$= \dfrac{150 - 50}{177\,\text{°C} - (-123\,\text{°C})}$

      Show the solution

      Given

      points $(-123\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $50)$, $(27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $100)$ and $(177\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $150)$ on the same line; $p$ is a relative number without a unit

      Find

      temperature $\theta_0$ at which $p = 0$, in degrees Celsius

      Principle

      The points are on the same line, so the pressure changes steadily with the temperature. When the line is continued downwards outside the measured range, that is, extrapolated, the zero pressure is found where the line crosses the temperature axis.

      Reading the graph

      Read two points from the table, for example the end points $(-123\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $50)$ and $(177\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $150)$. The third point $(27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $100)$ is on the same line, so the model is linear. Continue the line to the left until $p = 0$.

      Formula

      $k$$= \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$

      and line $p = p_a + k(\theta - \theta_a)$ through the point $(\theta_a,\, p_a) = (27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $100)$. When $p = 0$:

      $$\theta_0 = \theta_a - \frac{p_a}{k}$$

      Conversions

      No conversions are needed: the temperature difference $\theta_2 - \theta_1$ is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, and the result is wanted in degrees Celsius. The unit of the slope is $\tfrac{1}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$.

      Substitution

      $k$$= \dfrac{150 - 50}{(177 - (-123))\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
      $= \dfrac{100}{300\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
      $= \dfrac{1}{3}\,\tfrac{1}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

      and

      $\theta_0$$= 27\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{100}{\tfrac{1}{3}\,\tfrac{1}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
      $= 27\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
      $= -273\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

      Answer

      $-273\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

      Three significant figures like the temperatures in the table.

      Reasonableness

      The pressure drops $50$ units for every $150\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so from the point $(-123\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $50)$ it is another $150\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to zero: $-123 - 150 = -273\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result is absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ or $0\,\text{K}$, because in the model the pressure is proportional to the temperature in kelvins.

        Level 1 5 exercises

        1. The temperature of a room is $21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What is the temperature in kelvins?

          K
          Answer

          294 K

          Show the solution

          Given

          $\theta = 21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Find

          temperature in kelvins, $T$

          Principle

          A kelvin and a degree Celsius are the same size, and the scales differ only in their zero point: $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is $273.15\,\text{K}$. So you get the reading in kelvins by adding $273.15$ to the Celsius reading.

          Formula

          $T = \theta + 273.15$, and this is already solved for the quantity to find, $T$

          Conversions

          The Celsius temperature is converted to kelvins in this formula; no other unit conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $T = 21 + 273.15 = 294.15\,\text{K}$

          Answer

          $294\,\text{K}$

          In whole kelvins, because the temperature $21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is given in whole degrees.

          Reasonableness

          Room temperature is about $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the estimate is $20 + 273 \approx 293\,\text{K}$, and the result is close to it. Check by converting back: $294.15 - 273.15 = 21$, so the conversion returns the starting value. A temperature in kelvins is positive, as it should be.

        2. The temperature of a gas is $350\,\text{K}$. What is the temperature in degrees Celsius?

          °C
          Answer

          77 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          $T = 350\,\text{K}$

          Find

          temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta$

          Principle

          The intervals of the Kelvin and Celsius scales are the same size, and the difference between the zero points is $273.15$. So you get the Celsius reading by subtracting $273.15$ from the reading in kelvins.

          Formula

          $T = \theta + 273.15$, so $\theta = T - 273.15$

          Conversions

          The temperature in kelvins is converted to degrees Celsius with this formula; no other unit conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\theta$$= 350 - 273.15$
          $= 76.85\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $77\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          In whole degrees, because $350\,\text{K}$ is given in whole kelvins.

          Reasonableness

          The boiling point of water at normal pressure is $373\,\text{K}$, or $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and $350\,\text{K}$ is $23\,\text{K}$ below it, so the estimate is $100 - 23 = 77\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result is also above zero, because $350\,\text{K} > 273\,\text{K}$. Check by converting back: $76.85 + 273.15 = 350$.

        3. The boiling point of liquid nitrogen is $77.4\,\text{K}$. What is it in degrees Celsius?

          −196 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          $T = 77.4\,\text{K}$

          Find

          temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta$

          Principle

          The intervals of the Celsius and Kelvin scales are the same size, and the difference between the zero points is $273.15$. So $\theta$ is found by subtracting $273.15$ from the reading in kelvins. The formula also works when the result is negative.

          Formula

          $T = \theta + 273.15$, so $\theta = T - 273.15$

          Conversions

          The temperature in kelvins is converted to degrees Celsius with this formula; no other unit conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\theta$$= 77.4 - 273.15$
          $= -195.75\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $-196\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Three significant figures like the value $77.4\,\text{K}$ (the exact constant $273.15$ does not limit the result; to one decimal place the result would be $-195.8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$).

          Reasonableness

          Nitrogen is the main component of air and liquefies only at very low temperatures: the result is clearly below the freezing point of water, $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, but above absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Check by converting back: $-195.75 + 273.15 = 77.4\,\text{K}$. Estimate: $77 - 273 \approx -196$.

        4. The temperature of water rises from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $45\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. How large is the change in temperature in kelvins?

          K
          Answer

          25 K

          Show the solution

          Given

          $\theta_1 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\theta_2 = 45\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Find

          change in temperature in kelvins, $\Delta T$

          Principle

          A degree Celsius and a kelvin are the same size, and the zero points of the scales differ only by the constant $273.15$. The constant cancels in the subtraction, so a change in temperature is the same on both scales.

          Formula

          $\Delta T$$= T_2 - T_1$
          $= (\theta_2 + 273.15) - (\theta_1 + 273.15)$

          so $\Delta T = \theta_2 - \theta_1$

          Conversions

          No conversion is needed, because a change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins; only the unit changes.

          Substitution

          $\Delta T = 45 - 20 = 25\,\text{K}$

          Answer

          $25\,\text{K}$

          Two significant figures like the given temperatures.

          Reasonableness

          Check in kelvins: $T_1 = 293.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = 318.15\,\text{K}$, so the difference is $318.15 - 293.15 = 25\,\text{K}$. The sign is positive, because the water warms up.

        Level 2 4 exercises

        1. The temperature of a gas is $288\,\text{K}$. The gas is heated, and its temperature rises by $45\,\text{K}$. What is the final temperature in degrees Celsius?

          °C
          Answer

          60 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          $T_1 = 288\,\text{K}$, temperature rise $\Delta T = 45\,\text{K}$

          Find

          final temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta_2$

          Principle

          A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius, so the rise can be added directly to the temperature in kelvins. Only at the end is the result converted to degrees Celsius.

          Formula

          $T_2 = T_1 + \Delta T$ and $\theta_2 = T_2 - 273.15$, so $\theta_2 = T_1 + \Delta T - 273.15$

          Conversions

          The rise $45\,\text{K}$ is added as it is. The final temperature is converted from kelvins to degrees Celsius by subtracting $273.15$.

          Substitution

          $T_2 = 288 + 45 = 333\,\text{K}$, and

          $\theta_2$$= 333 - 273.15$
          $= 59.85\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $60\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          In whole degrees, because the initial values are given in whole kelvins.

          Reasonableness

          The initial temperature $288\,\text{K}$ is about $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and a rise of $45\,\text{K}$ gives about $15 + 45 = 60\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Exact check: $288 - 273.15 = 14.85\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $14.85 + 45 = 59.85\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The gas warms up, so the final temperature is higher than the initial temperature.

        2. In a closed rigid container the pressure of a gas is 90.0 kPa at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and 123 kPa at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Assume that the pressure depends linearly on the temperature.

          What is the pressure at $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$?

          kPa
          Answer

          102 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          $(\theta_1,\, p_1) = (0\,{}^\circ\text{C},\ 90.0\,\text{kPa})$ and $(\theta_2,\, p_2) = (100\,{}^\circ\text{C},\ 123\,\text{kPa})$; the pressure depends linearly on the temperature

          Find

          pressure $p$ at the temperature $\theta = 35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ in kilopascals

          Principle

          The container is closed and rigid, so the amount of gas and the volume stay the same and the pressure changes only with the temperature. The problem allows a linear model, so the points determine a straight line, and you read the pressure at $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ from the line.

          Formula

          slope

          $k$$= \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$

          and line $p = p_1 + k\theta$, because $\theta_1 = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; so

          $p$$= p_1 + \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}\,\theta$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the pressures are in the same unit, and the temperature difference $\theta_2 - \theta_1$ is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins. The unit of the slope is $\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$.

          Substitution

          $k$$= \dfrac{(123 - 90.0)\,\text{kPa}}{(100 - 0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= 0.33\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

          and

          $p$$= 90.0\,\text{kPa} + 0.33\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot 35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 101.55\,\text{kPa}$

          Answer

          $102\,\text{kPa}$

          The pressure difference $\Delta p = 123 - 90.0 = 33\,\text{kPa}$ has two figures, so $k = 0.33\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$ and $k\theta \approx 12\,\text{kPa}$; the sum $90.0 + 12 = 102\,\text{kPa}$ is rounded to whole kilopascals.

          Reasonableness

          The temperature $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is $35\,\%$ of the way from $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the pressure must be $35\,\%$ of the way through the range $90.0\ldots123\,\text{kPa}$: $90.0 + 0.35 \cdot 33 = 101.55\,\text{kPa}$. The result is between the pressures of the points and below the mean of the range, $106.5\,\text{kPa}$.

        3. The graph shows the pressures of a gas measured at different temperatures in a closed rigid container. Assume that the pressure depends linearly on the temperature also outside the measured range.

          What would the pressure be at $-100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$?

          kPa
          Answer

          47.5 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          reading from the graph, the points $(0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $75\,\text{kPa})$ and $(100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $102.5\,\text{kPa})$; the pressure depends linearly on the temperature, also outside the measured range

          Find

          pressure $p$ at the temperature $\theta = -100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ in kilopascals

          Principle

          In a rigid closed container the amount of gas and the volume stay the same, so the pressure depends only on the temperature. The points are on a straight line, so the line can be continued outside the measured range, that is, extrapolated.

          Reading the graph

          The data points are at temperatures $0\ldots 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The first point is at a crossing of the grid, $(0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $75\,\text{kPa})$, and the last point is slightly above the $100\,\text{kPa}$ line, at $102.5\,\text{kPa}$. From these two points the slope of the line is calculated, and the line is continued to the left to the temperature $-100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. If you draw the line by eye, you get a value of $45$–$50\,\text{kPa}$, which is accepted.

          Formula

          $k$$= \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$

          and $p = p_1 + k(\theta - \theta_1)$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the pressures are in kilopascals, and the temperature differences are the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins.

          Substitution

          $k$$= \dfrac{(102.5 - 75)\,\text{kPa}}{(100 - 0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
          $= 0.275\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

          and

          $p$$= 75\,\text{kPa} + 0.275\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot (-100 - 0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 75\,\text{kPa} - 27.5\,\text{kPa}$
          $= 47.5\,\text{kPa}$

          Answer

          $p \approx 48\,\text{kPa}$

          Two significant figures, because the pressure difference $27.5\,\text{kPa}$ read from the graph is a three-figure value but the reading accuracy is about $1\,\text{kPa}$.

          Reasonableness

          The temperature is below the measured range, so the pressure must be lower than the lowest measured value, $75\,\text{kPa}$, but clearly above zero. An independent check: if the pressure is proportional to the temperature in kelvins,

          $p$$\approx 75\,\text{kPa} \cdot \dfrac{173.15\,\text{K}}{273.15\,\text{K}}$
          $= 47.5\,\text{kPa}$

          which is the same.

        4. The temperature of the night side of the Moon is $-173\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and of the day side $127\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          How many times greater is the temperature in kelvins of the day side than the temperature in kelvins of the night side?

          kertaa
          Answer

          4.0 kertaa

          Show the solution

          Given

          $\theta_\text{night} = -173\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $\theta_\text{day} = 127\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Find

          ratio of the temperatures in kelvins $\dfrac{T_\text{day}}{T_\text{night}}$

          Principle

          The ratio is asked for the temperatures in kelvins, because only the zero of the Kelvin scale is absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature. The ratio of Celsius temperatures has no physical meaning.

          Formula

          $T = \theta + 273.15$, and the ratio

          $\dfrac{T_\text{day}}{T_\text{night}}$$= \dfrac{\theta_\text{day} + 273.15}{\theta_\text{night} + 273.15}$

          Conversions

          Both temperatures are converted to kelvins by adding $273.15$:

          $T_\text{night}$$= -173 + 273.15$
          $= 100.15\,\text{K}$

          and

          $T_\text{day}$$= 127 + 273.15$
          $= 400.15\,\text{K}$

          Substitution

          $\dfrac{T_\text{day}}{T_\text{night}}$$= \dfrac{400.15\,\text{K}}{100.15\,\text{K}}$
          $= 3.9955$

          Answer

          $4.00$

          Times. Whole degrees give temperatures of $100\,\text{K}$ and $400\,\text{K}$ to the nearest kelvin, three significant figures in each, so the quotient also has three.

          Reasonableness

          In kelvins the temperatures are about $100\,\text{K}$ and $400\,\text{K}$, so the ratio is about $4$. The day side is warmer, so the ratio is above $1$. The ratio of the Celsius temperatures would be $\tfrac{127}{-173} \approx -0.73$, which is a meaningless comparison.

        1. The total pressure (not gauge pressure) of the air in a bicycle tyre is 300 kPa at $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The tyre does not leak, and its volume stays the same. The pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins.

          What is the pressure in the tyre at $35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$?

          kPa
          Answer

          321 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          $p_1 = 300\,\text{kPa}$ (total pressure), $\theta_1 = 15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\theta_2 = 35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Find

          pressure $p_2$ at the temperature $\theta_2$ in kilopascals

          Principle

          The tyre does not leak and its volume does not change, so the amount of air and the volume are constant. Then the pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins: the particles of the warmer gas move faster and hit the wall more often and harder. The proportionality does not hold for the Celsius temperature.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{p_2}{p_1} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, so $p_2 = p_1\,\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$

          Conversions

          The temperatures are converted to kelvins: $T_1 = 15 + 273.15 = 288.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = 35 + 273.15 = 308.15\,\text{K}$. The pressure stays in kilopascals, because it appears only in a ratio.

          Substitution

          $p_2$$= 300\,\text{kPa} \cdot \dfrac{308.15\,\text{K}}{288.15\,\text{K}}$
          $= 320.8\,\text{kPa}$

          Answer

          $321\,\text{kPa}$

          Three significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          The temperature in kelvins rises by $20\,\text{K}$, or about $\dfrac{20}{288} \approx 6.9\,\%$, so the pressure rises by about $0.069 \cdot 300\,\text{kPa} \approx 21\,\text{kPa}$, and $300 + 21 = 321\,\text{kPa}$. The temperature rises, so the pressure must increase. The ratio of Celsius temperatures $\tfrac{35}{15}$ would give an unreasonable $700\,\text{kPa}$.

        2. In a closed rigid container the pressure of a gas is 150 kPa at $27.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The container is heated until the pressure is 200 kPa. The pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins.

          What is the final temperature in degrees Celsius?

          °C
          Answer

          127 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          $p_1 = 150\,\text{kPa}$, $\theta_1 = 27.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $p_2 = 200\,\text{kPa}$

          Find

          final temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta_2$

          Principle

          The container is closed and rigid, so the volume and the amount of gas stay the same, and the pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins. So the ratio of the pressures equals the ratio of the temperatures in kelvins.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{p_2}{p_1} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, so $T_2 = T_1\,\dfrac{p_2}{p_1}$, and $\theta_2 = T_2 - 273.15$

          Conversions

          The initial temperature is converted to kelvins:

          $T_1$$= 27.0 + 273.15$
          $= 300.15\,\text{K}$

          At the end the result is converted to degrees Celsius by subtracting $273.15$. The pressures do not need converting, because they are in the same unit.

          Substitution

          $T_2$$= 300.15\,\text{K} \cdot \dfrac{200\,\text{kPa}}{150\,\text{kPa}}$
          $= 400.2\,\text{K}$

          and

          $\theta_2$$= 400.2 - 273.15$
          $= 127.05\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $127\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Three significant figures like the value $27.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          Reasonableness

          The pressure increases by a factor of $\tfrac{4}{3}$, so the temperature in kelvins must increase by the same factor: about $300\,\text{K} \to 400\,\text{K}$, or $127\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Check: $\tfrac{p}{T}$ at the start is $\tfrac{150}{300.15} = 0.4998\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}}$ and at the end $\tfrac{200}{400.2} = 0.4998\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{K}}$. The ratio of Celsius temperatures would wrongly give $36\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

        3. The temperature of a gas is $20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The gas is cooled until the average kinetic energy of its particles is half of the original. The average kinetic energy is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins.

          What is the final temperature in degrees Celsius?

          °C
          Answer

          −127 °C

          Show the solution

          Given

          $\theta_1 = 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and average kinetic energy $E_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}E_1$

          Find

          final temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta_2$

          Principle

          According to the task, the average kinetic energy of the particles of the gas is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins; this is so in a dilute gas whose particles interact with each other only when they collide. The proportionality applies to the temperature in kelvins, because only the zero of the Kelvin scale is absolute zero. When the energy is halved, the temperature in kelvins is also halved. You must not halve the Celsius temperature.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{E_2}{E_1}$$= \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$
          $= \dfrac{1}{2}$

          so $T_2 = \dfrac{T_1}{2}$, and $\theta_2 = T_2 - 273.15$

          Conversions

          The initial temperature is converted to kelvins:

          $T_1$$= 20.0 + 273.15$
          $= 293.15\,\text{K}$

          At the end $T_2$ is converted back to degrees Celsius by subtracting $273.15$.

          Substitution

          $T_2$$= \dfrac{293.15\,\text{K}}{2}$
          $= 146.575\,\text{K}$

          and

          $\theta_2$$= 146.575 - 273.15$
          $= -126.575\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Answer

          $-127\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Three significant figures like the value $20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          Reasonableness

          The gas must cool, so the result is below $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and it is above absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Check: $\tfrac{146.575\,\text{K}}{293.15\,\text{K}} = 0.5$. Halving the Celsius temperature would give $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, but then the temperature in kelvins would be only $283\,\text{K}$, or $97\,\%$ of the initial value, not half.
