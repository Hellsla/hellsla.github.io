Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces

Why can a sled press on the snow differently when it is pulled upward at an angle by a rope than when it simply stands still?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • identify the forces acting on an object and draw them in a free-body diagram.
  • tell forces apart from their components and reason out the combined effect of forces.
  • form the equations of motion with the help of a free-body diagram.

Prior knowledge

  • Newton's second law $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$ from the chapter Newton's second law
  • The sine and cosine of a right triangle

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

Two children pull a sled on ice. One pulls on a short rope obliquely upward, and the other pushes the sled from behind with a handle that points obliquely downward. Both use an equal force. Friction on ice is small, but the runners of the sled press on the ice in different ways.

Which forces act on the sled in each case, and does the sled press on the ice with the same force as when it stands still?

Optional Review: a sliding puck
An ice hockey puck has been hit, and it is now sliding alone on the ice toward the goal.Which forces act on the puck during the slide, when air drag is ignored?

  • Correct. Only objects that touch the puck or pull on it act on it: the Earth and the ice. The stick is no longer touching the puck, so its force ended when the hit ended. In the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws you learn that motion continues without a force pulling forward.

  • The force of the hit acted only while the stick was touching the puck. No object acts forward on the sliding puck. The puck moves because it has a velocity, not because something pushes it.

  • The puck does not sink through the ice, so the ice supports it: the normal force is as large as the weight. There is no forward force, because no object pushes the puck during the slide.

3Investigate

In the simulation a sled on ice is pulled by a rope or pushed from a handle. On the left is the situation diagram and on the right the free-body diagram of the sled, with the lengths of the arrows to scale.

Simulation

How to use

Choose Situation (pull or push) and adjust the force $F$ and the angle $\alpha$. The free-body diagram updates immediately. Press Start and the sled starts to move; press Reset and it stops at the start. The Show the components of the force F box shows the components of the force $F$ with dashed lines. Speed slows down or speeds up the motion without changing the readings. The readings are under the picture.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation. The ice is horizontal and friction is ignored, so only the weight $\overline{G}$, the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the ice and the force $\overline{F}$ of the rope or handle act on the sled. The force is so small that the sled does not lift off the ice. There is no air drag. The blue arrow of the velocity is in the situation diagram, not in the free-body diagram, because velocity is not a force.

How the calculation works

The components of the force are $F_x = F\cos\alpha$ and $F_y = F\sin\alpha$. In a pull $F_y$ points up and in a push down. Vertically the sled does not accelerate, so $N + F_y - G = 0$ in a pull and $N - F_y - G = 0$ in a push, with $G = mg$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. Horizontally $F_x = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{F\cos\alpha}{m}$.

Why is the normal force in a push larger than the weight $196\,\text{N}$?

  • Correct. Vertically $N - F_y - G = 0$, so $N = G + F_y$. In a pull the component pointed up and the normal force decreased.

Conclusion

Using your measurements and the free-body diagram, explain why the normal force and the acceleration decrease when the pulling angle grows and the force stays the same.

Model answer

The rope force $F = 100\,\text{N}$ is split into the components $F_x = F\cos\alpha$ and $F_y = F\sin\alpha$. As the angle grows, the vertical component grows and supports a larger part of the weight, so the normal force $N = G - F\sin\alpha$ from the ice decreases: in the model values $196\,\text{N}$ in a horizontal pull and $146\,\text{N}$ at an angle of $30^\circ$. At the same time the horizontal component decreases, and since there is no friction, only it accelerates the sled: $a = \dfrac{F\cos\alpha}{m}$ is $5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ at $0^\circ$ and $2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ at $60^\circ$. So a force affects the motion not only by its magnitude but also by its direction.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Usually several forces act on an object at the same time. The Earth pulls it down, the surface supports it, a rope pulls and the ground resists the motion. Each force describes one interaction between the object and some other object. The motion is decided by the combined effect of these forces, the net force $\Sigma\overline{F}$, which is the vector sum of the forces. In the chapter Newton's second law you learn that the net force determines the acceleration: $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$.

    To include every force and to keep anything extra out of the calculation, the forces are first drawn in a free-body diagram. It is a different picture from the situation diagram. The situation diagram shows the whole situation: the surface, the rope, the person pulling and the direction of motion. The free-body diagram shows only the chosen object, simplified to a rectangle, and the forces acting on it, as arrows. The direction of an arrow is the direction of the force, and the lengths of the arrows tell which force is larger.

    In the figure, a box is pulled along by a rope that points obliquely upward. The force of the rope points both forward and upward. The upward part helps to hold the box up, so the floor does not have to support it as much as when the box stands still. That is why the normal force $N$ is smaller than the weight $G$. The velocity is not drawn in the free-body diagram, because velocity is not a force.

    Drawing a free-body diagram

    A free-body diagram is always drawn with the same eight steps. They are worth going through every time, even when the situation looks easy.

    1. Choose the object. Decide whose forces you draw. Only forces that act on this object go into the diagram. Forces that the object itself exerts on others do not belong in it.
    2. Find the interactions. List everything that acts on the object: the Earth (weight), every surface the object touches (normal force and friction), every rope (tension), a push or a pull, and air or water (drag and buoyancy). Every force has a cause outside the object. Motion alone does not cause a force.
    3. Draw the object and the forces. Draw the object simplified to a rectangle. Draw one arrow for each force, starting at the point where the force acts. The arrow of a pushing force may also end at that point.
    4. The weight $\overline{G}$ points straight down.
    5. The normal force $\overline{N}$ is perpendicular to the contact surface and pushes the object away from the surface. It does not always point up: a ceiling pushes down and a wall pushes sideways.
    6. The friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ is parallel to the contact surface. Its direction comes from the direction in which the object slides, or tends to slide, relative to the surface. It does not always oppose the velocity. The friction arrow starts at a corner of the contact surface, outside the object, or ends there. It is not drawn along a side of the object from the middle of the side.
    7. The tension $\overline{T}$ acts along the rope and pulls the object toward the rope.
    8. Air drag is drawn only when the situation and its assumptions call for it.
    9. Name the forces. Give each force its own symbol. Two similar forces are told apart with subscripts, for example $\overline{T}_1$ and $\overline{T}_2$.
    10. Think about the magnitudes. At rest and in uniform motion the net force is zero, so the arrows cancel each other. In accelerating motion the net force points in the direction of the acceleration, which is not always the direction of the velocity. Do not invent exact lengths if the data does not fix them.
    11. Only forces. Velocity, acceleration and displacement are not forces. If they are needed, they are drawn beside the object as thin arrows in different colours: in this course forces are red, velocity is blue and acceleration is orange. The net force is not one more force.
    12. Components are not new forces. When a force is split into components, the components together replace the force in the calculation. They are drawn with a dashed line so that they are not mistaken for forces.
    13. Equation of motion. Only now are the axes chosen and Newton's second law written component by component from the forces and signs of the diagram.

    The figure shows four situations in which the directions of the forces often surprise people. On a slope the normal force is perpendicular to the slope, not vertical, and friction holds the box in place along the slope. On a helium balloon pressed against the ceiling the ceiling pushes downward.

    In a free-body diagram the object is always a simplified rectangle, and each force starts from its point of action: the weight from the centre, the normal force and friction from the contact surface, and the tension from where the rope is attached. The arrow of a pushing force may also end at the point where the push acts. In the situation diagram objects are drawn as they look, but in the free-body diagram only the rectangle and the forces are drawn.

    The marking instructions of the matriculation examinations give task-specific points for the free-body diagram. For example, in the spring 2025 helium-balloon task a missing, extra or wrongly directed force cost all the points of the diagram, and so did drawing velocity or acceleration as a force on the balloon. In the autumn 2025 examination a missing name cost one point. The exact scoring varies from task to task, but every examination requires the same things: all the forces, no invented forces, correct directions and unambiguous names.

    Equation of motion by components

    Newton's second law concerns the net force:

    $$\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$$

    where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the vector sum of the forces acting on the object (N), $m$ is the mass of the object (kg) and $\overline{a}$ is its acceleration (m/s²). When the forces are not along one line, two perpendicular axes are chosen and the law is written separately for each axis direction:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Sigma F_x &= m a_x \\ \Sigma F_y &= m a_y \end{aligned}$$

    where $\Sigma F_x$ and $\Sigma F_y$ are the sums of the components of the forces (N) and $a_x$ and $a_y$ are the components of the acceleration (m/s²). A component pointing along the axis is positive and one pointing against it is negative. It is wise to choose the axes so that one of them points in the direction of the acceleration.

    The components of an oblique force $F$ come from a right triangle, when $\alpha$ is the angle between the force and the $x$-axis:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_x &= F\cos\alpha \\ F_y &= F\sin\alpha \end{aligned}$$

    where $F$ is the magnitude of the force (N), $F_x$ and $F_y$ are its components (N) and $\alpha$ is the angle. The components are not new forces. They replace the force $F$ in the calculation.

    Common misconception

    A moving object does not need a force in the direction of its motion. Only the weight, the normal force and friction act on a sliding box, and there is no "force of motion": there is no object that would cause it. Another common mistake is to think that the normal force is always equal to the weight. The normal force is exactly as large as the situation requires: in an oblique pull it is smaller than the weight, and in an oblique push or in an elevator accelerating upward it is larger. A third mistake is to draw the components as forces in addition to the force itself, which puts the same force into the calculation twice.

    Summary

    • A free-body diagram contains only the chosen object, as a rectangle, and the forces acting on it. The situation diagram and the free-body diagram are different pictures.
    • Every force has a cause: the Earth, a surface, a rope, a pusher or the air. Velocity and acceleration are not forces, and they are drawn in different colours from the forces.
    • The normal force is perpendicular to the surface, friction is parallel to the surface and tension is along the rope.
    • The net force is the vector sum of the forces, and $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$ is written by components: $\Sigma F_x = m a_x$ and $\Sigma F_y = m a_y$.
    • The components of an oblique force are $F\cos\alpha$ and $F\sin\alpha$. They replace the force in the calculation and are not new forces.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A sled of mass $12\,\text{kg}$ is pulled on horizontal snow by a rope that makes an angle of $35^\circ$ with the horizontal. The tension in the rope is $80\,\text{N}$ and the friction force from the snow is $30\,\text{N}$. What is the acceleration of the sled, and what is the normal force from the snow?

      Step 1/8

      Given and find. $m = 12\,\text{kg}$, $T = 80\,\text{N}$, $\alpha = 35^\circ$, $F_\mu = 30\,\text{N}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the acceleration $a$ and the normal force $N$.

      Step 2/8

      Principle. Choose the sled as the object. The Earth (weight), the snow (normal force and friction) and the rope (tension) act on it. Newton's second law is written in two directions: horizontally the sled accelerates, vertically it stays on the surface of the snow, so $a_y = 0$.

      Step 3/8

      Free-body diagram. The sled is drawn as a rectangle, and the forces start from their points of action. The axes are chosen so that $x$ points in the direction of motion and $y$ points up. The force of the rope is split into the components $T_x = T\cos\alpha$ and $T_y = T\sin\alpha$, drawn with dashed lines.

      Step 4/8

      Rearranged formula. The equations of motion are read from the diagram, and the asked quantities are solved:

      $$\begin{aligned} &x\text{:}\ \ T\cos\alpha - F_\mu = m a \\ &y\text{:}\ \ N + T\sin\alpha - mg = 0 \\ &a = \frac{T\cos\alpha - F_\mu}{m} \\ &N = mg - T\sin\alpha \end{aligned}$$

      Step 5/8

      Unit conversions. None are needed: all quantities are in SI units, and $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} = \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$.

      Step 6/8

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{T\cos\alpha - F_\mu}{m} \\ \phantom{a} &= \frac{80\,\text{N}\cdot\cos 35^\circ - 30\,\text{N}}{12\,\text{kg}} \\ \phantom{a} &\approx 2.96\,\text{m/s}^2 \\[4pt] N &= mg - T\sin\alpha \\ \phantom{N} &= 12\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ &\quad\ - 80\,\text{N}\cdot\sin 35^\circ \\ \phantom{N} &\approx 71.8\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 7/8

      Result. The acceleration is $a \approx 3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the normal force is $N \approx 72\,\text{N}$, given with two significant figures like the data $80\,\text{N}$, $30\,\text{N}$ and $12\,\text{kg}$.

      Step 8/8

      Reasonableness. The weight of the sled is $mg \approx 118\,\text{N}$. The normal force $72\,\text{N}$ is smaller, because the vertical component of the rope force, $T_y \approx 46\,\text{N}$, supports part of the weight, and $72\,\text{N} + 46\,\text{N} \approx 118\,\text{N}$. Without friction the acceleration would be $65.5\,\text{N} : 12\,\text{kg} \approx 5.5\,\text{m/s}^2$, so friction reduces it, as it should.

      Find the error

      A box has been given a shove, and now it slides alone to the right on a horizontal floor and slows down. A student drew the free-body diagram shown in the figure.What is wrong in the diagram?

      • In a free-body diagram the weight is drawn from the centre, as in the diagram. The floor acts from the bottom surface with the normal force and friction. The error is in the force $\overline{F}$: nothing is pushing the box any more.

      • Friction opposes sliding relative to the surface. The box slides to the right, so friction points to the left, as in the diagram. The error is elsewhere: the force $\overline{F}$ has no cause.

      • Correct. The shove ended when the hand left the box. Only the weight, the normal force from the floor and friction act on the sliding box. Since horizontally there is only friction, the box slows down.

      • Vertically the box does not accelerate, so the normal force and the weight are equal, as in the diagram. Motion in the horizontal direction does not change this. The error is in the force $\overline{F}$, which has no cause.

      Choose the correct free-body diagram

      A person stands in an elevator. The elevator starts moving upward and speeds up.In which diagram is the free-body diagram of the person correct? In the free-body diagrams the person is drawn as a rectangle. Compare the arrows carefully: each wrong diagram has one error.

      • Equally long arrows mean that the net force is zero. Then the person would be at rest or moving uniformly. In an accelerating elevator the normal force must be larger than the weight.

      • Then the net force would point down, and the acceleration of the person would be downward. That happens when an elevator moving upward slows down, not when it starts accelerating upward.

      • Correct. The person accelerates upward, so the net force must point up: $N - G = ma$, that is $N > G$.

      • Velocity is not a force. No object causes a force $\overline{v}$, so it does not belong in the free-body diagram. Otherwise the diagram would be correct.

      Apply to a new situation

      A fisher lifts a fish of $2.0\,\text{kg}$ with a line straight up so that the fish accelerates upward at $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

      How large is the tension in the line? Air drag is ignored. Answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Draw the free-body diagram of the fish. Which two forces act on the fish, and which one is larger when the fish accelerates up?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Choose the $y$-axis up. The equation of motion is $T - mg = ma$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $T$$= m(g + a)$
      $= 2.0\,\text{kg}\cdot(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2)$
      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$, acceleration upward $a = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the tension $T$ in the line in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The fish accelerates, so the sum of the forces acting on it is not zero. By Newton's second law the net force is $ma$ in the direction of the acceleration, that is upward.

      Free-body diagram

      The fish as a rectangle, $y$-axis up. The tension $\overline{T}$ of the line up and the weight $\overline{G}$ down. $\overline{T}$ is longer, because the net force points up. The acceleration is drawn separately, not as a force.

      Formula

      $T - mg = ma$, so $T = m(g + a)$

      Conversions

      The mass is in kilograms and the accelerations in $\text{m/s}^2$, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $T$$= 2.0\,\text{kg}\cdot(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2)$
      $= 2.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 11.31\,\text{m/s}^2$
      $= 22.62\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $23\,\text{N}$

      Two significant figures like the mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and the acceleration $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

      Reasonableness

      The weight of the fish is about $20\,\text{N}$. An accelerating lift requires more, and $23\,\text{N}$ is a little larger. The excess of $3\,\text{N}$ is $ma = 2.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. If the fish were lowered with the same acceleration, the line would need only $m(g - a) \approx 17\,\text{N}$.

      1. Concept

      A book rests on a horizontal table. Which statement about the free-body diagram of the book is correct?

      • The force of the book on the table acts on the table, not on the book. Only forces that act on the chosen object go into the free-body diagram.

      • Correct. The Earth and the table act on the book. The forces are equal because of Newton's second law: the acceleration is zero, so the net force is zero.

      • The forces of a force pair act on different objects. The pair of the normal force from the table is the force of the book on the table, and the pair of the weight is the gravitational pull of the book on the Earth. The weight and the normal force are equal because the book does not accelerate.

      2. Calculation

      A shop sign of mass $8.0\,\text{kg}$ hangs from two equally long cables. Each cable makes an angle of $50^\circ$ with the horizontal, and the sign is at rest.

      How large is the tension in each cable? Answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Draw the free-body diagram of the sign: the weight and two tensions $\overline{T}_1$ and $\overline{T}_2$. The situation is symmetric, so $T_1 = T_2 = T$.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The horizontal components cancel each other. Vertically $2T\sin 50^\circ - mg = 0$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $T = \dfrac{mg}{2\sin 50^\circ}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 8.0\,\text{kg}$, angle of the cables to the horizontal $\alpha = 50^\circ$, the sign is at rest; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the tension $T$ in one cable in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The sign is at rest, so by Newton's second law the sum of the forces is zero in both directions. The situation is symmetric, so the tensions in the cables are equal.

      Free-body diagram

      The sign as a rectangle, $x$ to the right and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ down, the tension $\overline{T}_1$ obliquely up and to the left and $\overline{T}_2$ obliquely up and to the right, both $50^\circ$ from the horizontal. Their horizontal components $T\cos 50^\circ$ cancel each other, and the vertical components $T\sin 50^\circ$ point up.

      Formula

      $y$: $2T\sin\alpha - mg = 0$, so $T = \dfrac{mg}{2\sin\alpha}$

      Conversions

      The mass is in kilograms, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $T$$= \dfrac{8.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{2\cdot\sin 50^\circ}$
      $= \dfrac{78.48\,\text{N}}{1.532}$
      $= 51.22\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $51\,\text{N}$

      Two significant figures like the mass $8.0\,\text{kg}$.

      Reasonableness

      If the cables were vertical, each would carry half of the weight, $39\,\text{N}$. Oblique cables need more, because only part of the force is vertical, so $51\,\text{N} > 39\,\text{N}$ is reasonable. The flatter the cables, the larger the tension needed.

        Exit ticket

        Finally, three quick questions. Answer first on your own and open the answer only afterwards.

        1. A ball has been thrown straight up, and it is just at the highest point of its path. Which forces act on the ball when air drag is ignored?
          Answer

          Only the weight $\overline{G}$, which points down. The thrower's hand is no longer touching the ball, so there is no upward force. At the highest point the velocity is zero for a moment, but the acceleration is still $g$ downward.

        2. Why are the components of a force drawn with a dashed line in the free-body diagram, and what happens if both the force and its components are used in the calculation?
          Answer

          The components are not new forces, but together they replace the force. The dashed line sets them apart from the forces. If both the force and its components are included in the calculation, the same force is counted twice and the result is wrong.

        3. A box is pulled on a horizontal floor by a rope that makes an angle $\alpha$ with the horizontal. The tension in the rope is $T$, the friction force $F_\mu$ and the mass of the box $m$. Write the vertical equation of motion and solve the normal force $N$ from it.
          Answer

          Vertically the box does not accelerate, so $N + T\sin\alpha - mg = 0$ and $N = mg - T\sin\alpha$. Friction is horizontal, so it does not belong in the vertical equation.

        4. Two children pull a sled of $50\,\text{kg}$ on ice with ropes that are horizontal and perpendicular to each other. One pulls with a force of $30\,\text{N}$ and the other with $40\,\text{N}$. Friction is ignored.

          m/s²
          Answer

          1.0 m/s²

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 50\,\text{kg}$, $F_1 = 30\,\text{N}$ and $F_2 = 40\,\text{N}$ horizontal, perpendicular to each other; no friction

          the magnitude $a$ of the acceleration of the sled in $\text{m/s}^2$

          The net force is the vector sum of the forces, and by Newton's second law it determines the acceleration. Vertically the weight and the normal force from the ice cancel each other.

The buoyancy arrow is as long as the weight and normal force arrows together. There is no air drag, because the balloon is not moving.

          Formula

          $F_\text{b} - mg - N = 0$, so $N = F_\text{b} - mg$

          Conversions

          $m$$= 4.0\,\text{g}$
          $= 4.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.0040\,\text{kg}$

          Substitution

          $N$$= 0.10\,\text{N} - 0.0040\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 0.10\,\text{N} - 0.03924\,\text{N}$
          $= 0.06076\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $0.061\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $4.0\,\text{g}$ and the buoyancy $0.10\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          The buoyancy is larger than the weight, $0.039\,\text{N}$, so without the ceiling the balloon would rise. The ceiling stops it by pushing down exactly by the excess: $0.039\,\text{N} + 0.061\,\text{N} = 0.10\,\text{N}$. Here the normal force points down, not up.
