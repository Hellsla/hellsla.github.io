Determining absolute zero

Why can the lowest possible temperature be found in a school laboratory, even though a thermometer does not come anywhere near it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • turn measurement data into a straight line and extend the line to zero pressure.
  • determine absolute zero experimentally by extrapolation.
  • estimate the uncertainty of a result using the steepest and the shallowest line that fit.

Prior knowledge

  • When the volume and the amount of gas stay the same, the pressure of a dilute gas is proportional to the temperature in kelvins, and absolute zero is the zero point of the kelvin scalechapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature
  • The temperature of a gas tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles, and pressure comes from particles colliding with the wallchapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 7.

1Think about it

On a frosty night, a car tyre is softer in the morning than it was the previous afternoon: when the air cools, its pressure decreases. Could the pressure keep decreasing if the cooling continued? In a school laboratory, you rarely get below $-20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and yet physics knows at which temperature the pressure of a gas would be zero.

How can the lowest possible temperature be determined from measurements made at quite ordinary temperatures?

  • The ratio of temperatures is calculated in kelvins, not in degrees Celsius. The readings in kelvins are $293\,\text{K}$ and $313\,\text{K}$, so the temperature does not double, and neither does the pressure.

Given

points read from the line $A = (20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $108.6\,\text{kPa})$ and $B = (90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $134.3\,\text{kPa})$

Find

the temperature $\theta_0$ in degrees Celsius at which the line gives a pressure of zero

Principle

The flask is sealed and rigid, so the amount and volume of the gas stay constant and the pressure is a linear function of temperature, $p = p_0 + k\theta$. So two points on the line are enough to determine the line, and the line can be extended (extrapolated) all the way to zero pressure.

Reading the graph

Points A and B are read far apart from the best-fit line, because a long distance reduces the effect of a reading error on the slope. The pressure of a point is read from the vertical axis at its temperature: A at $\theta = 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and B at $\theta = 90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The readings are given in the problem, and the vertical axis of the picture starts at $95\,\text{kPa}$, so the zero point is not read from the picture.

Formula

The slope, the pressure of the line at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the temperature at zero pressure:

$$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{p_B - p_A}{\theta_B - \theta_A} \\ p_0 &= p_A - k\theta_A \\ \theta_0 &= -\frac{p_0}{k} \end{aligned}$$

Conversions

No conversions are needed: the pressures are in kilopascals, so the kPa cancels in the quotient $p_0/k$ and the result is in degrees Celsius. The temperature difference $90.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the same in kelvins.

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{(134.3 - 108.6)\,\text{kPa}}{(90.0 - 20.0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= \dfrac{25.7\,\text{kPa}}{70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= 0.36714\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$

Then

$p_0$$= 108.6\,\text{kPa} - 0.36714\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
$= 101.26\,\text{kPa}$
$\theta_0$$= -\dfrac{101.26\,\text{kPa}}{0.36714\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}}$
$= -275.8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Answer

$-276\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Three significant figures, because the pressure difference $25.7\,\text{kPa}$ and the temperature difference $70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are known to three figures; finally the subtraction $20.0 - 296$ gives the result in whole degrees.

Reasonableness

Estimate: the pressure rises by about $0.37\,\text{kPa}$ per degree, so $101\,\text{kPa}$ runs out after about $101/0.37 \approx 270$ degrees. The result is close to $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the difference is small compared with the extrapolation distance of $270\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.     4Theory

    Key idea

    In a closed rigid container, the volume and the amount of gas do not change, so the pressure depends only on temperature. In a gas thermometer, a glass flask stands in a water bath. As the bath warms up, you read the bath temperature $\theta$ from a thermometer and the gas pressure $p$ from a pressure gauge.

    When you plot the measurements on a graph, the points lie almost on a straight line: the pressure rises by almost the same amount for every degree Celsius. In real data sets the points are usually not exactly on one line, because of measurement uncertainty. Measurement uncertainty describes the uncertainty attached to a measurement result. It does not usually mean an absolute maximum possible deviation from the true value. Here the uncertainty of the pressure reading is estimated as $\pm 1\,\text{kPa}$. The error limit that a manufacturer states for a gauge is a different thing: it is a property of the device, and the uncertainty of the measurement is also affected by, for example, reading the scale and the bath temperature settling. So you draw the line through the middle of the group of points, so that it passes through the uncertainty bars, not necessarily through every point.

    You extend the line beyond the measurement range towards colder temperatures until the pressure is zero. This is called extrapolation: the result comes from a region where nothing has been measured. According to the microscopic model, pressure comes from particles colliding with the wall. In the ideal gas model, the pressure would be zero at the temperature where the average kinetic energy of the particles is at its lowest. The temperature where the line meets zero pressure is the experiment's estimate of absolute zero. It is only an estimate: a real gas liquefies before that point, and the extension is a model.

    Temperature at zero pressure

    Within the measurement range, the pressure of a gas is a linear function of temperature:

    $$p = p_0 + k\theta$$

    where $p$ is the pressure (kPa), $p_0$ is the pressure of the line at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (kPa), $k$ is the slope of the line (kPa/°C) and $\theta$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius. You calculate the slope from two points read far apart from the drawn line:

    $$k = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$$

    where $(\theta_1, p_1)$ and $(\theta_2, p_2)$ are points on the line. Zero pressure is reached when $p = 0$. The temperature is then $\theta_0$, and the equation gives:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0 &= p_0 + k\theta_0 \\ \theta_0 &= -\frac{p_0}{k} \end{aligned}$$

    You get the same result from any point $(\theta_1, p_1)$ on the line by substituting $p = 0$ into the equation $p = p_1 + k(\theta - \theta_1)$:

    $$\theta_0 = \theta_1 - \frac{p_1}{k}$$

    You estimate the uncertainty by drawing two limit lines that still pass through all the uncertainty bars: the steepest and the shallowest. For each you calculate the temperature at zero pressure, and the smaller and larger values give an interval. You give the result using the midpoint and half of the interval:

    $$\Delta\theta_0 = \frac{\theta_{0,\text{max}} - \theta_{0,\text{min}}}{2}$$

    where $\theta_{0,\text{max}}$ is the larger (less negative) and $\theta_{0,\text{min}}$ the smaller temperature at zero pressure. The result has the form $\theta_0 \pm \Delta\theta_0$. The interval is an estimate, not a guaranteed limit: it takes into account the uncertainty of the points but not whether the dependence stays linear outside the measurement range.

    The picture is drawn to scale. The limit lines run close to each other in the measurement range but move apart when you extrapolate, so the uncertainty grows as you extend the line. A more precise pressure gauge, more measurement points and a wider measurement range reduce the uncertainty. Once the result is known, the zero of the kelvin scale is placed there: the temperature in kelvins is $T = \theta - \theta_0$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that absolute zero has been measured because it is on the graph. Only the seven points in the range 10–$100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are measured; the rest is an extension of the line, and the extension is an assumption that the pressure behaves in the same way at colder temperatures. Another mistake is to consider the result a failure if it is not exactly $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result $-276\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ differs from the accepted value by about 3 degrees when the uncertainty is 18 degrees, so it agrees with the value. A third mistake is to draw a wiggly line through every point: the variation of the points up and down is caused by measurement uncertainty, and the line should be drawn through the middle of the group of points.

    Summary

    • In a closed rigid container, the pressure of a dilute gas is a linear function of temperature within the measurement range: $p = p_0 + k\theta$. You turn the measurement data into a straight line by plotting $p$ as a function of temperature and drawing the line through the middle of the group of points.
    • Measurement uncertainty describes the uncertainty attached to a measurement result, and it is not usually an absolute maximum possible deviation from the true value. The line does not have to hit every point, only pass through the uncertainty bars.
    • Extrapolation extends the line beyond the measurement range: the temperature at zero pressure is $\theta_0 = -p_0/k$.
    • You estimate the uncertainty from the limit lines: $\Delta\theta_0 = (\theta_{0,\text{max}} - \theta_{0,\text{min}})/2$. The result $\theta_0 \pm \Delta\theta_0$ agrees with the accepted value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ if that value lies in the interval.

        Three significant figures, because the temperature $77.0\,\text{K}$ is given to three figures ($273.15\,\text{K}$ is a definition and does not limit). The line cannot be extended all the way to zero, because nitrogen condenses into a liquid at about $67\,\text{K}$. Then the amount of gas decreases, the pressure falls faster than the line predicts, and the condition "amount of substance constant" no longer holds.

        Reasonableness

        The temperature $77.0\,\text{K}$ is about $0.28$ times the temperature $273.15\,\text{K}$, and the pressure is in the same proportion: about $0.28 \cdot 100 = 28\,\text{kPa}$. At the temperature $67\,\text{K}$ the line would give a pressure of $100 \cdot 67/273.15 = 24.5\,\text{kPa}$, but at about that temperature the gas starts to condense into a liquid, and after that the pressure no longer follows the line. So extrapolation is reliable only as far as the assumption of a gas holds true.

        kertaa

        Show the solution

        Given

        group A's result $\theta_0 = -262.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and uncertainty $\Delta\theta_0 = 6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; the accepted value $\theta_\text{acc} = -273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

        Find

        the ratio $n$ of the deviation to the uncertainty (how many times $\Delta\theta_0$ fits between the result and the accepted value) and the reason for the disagreement

        Principle

        In this chapter a result is considered consistent with the accepted value if the value lies in the uncertainty interval $\theta_0 \pm \Delta\theta_0$, that is, when the deviation is at most one uncertainty. The ratio $n$ tells you how many times larger the deviation is than the uncertainty. The uncertainty is not an absolute limit, so a deviation alone does not prove an error, but the larger $n$ is, the more likely it is that the measurement has a systematic error or that the uncertainty was estimated too small.

        $n$$= \dfrac{|\theta_0 - \theta_\text{acc}|}{\Delta\theta_0}$

        Conversions

        All temperatures are in degrees Celsius, and temperature differences are the same in kelvins. The ratio $n$ is dimensionless, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $n$$= \dfrac{|-262.00\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C})|}{6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
        $= \dfrac{11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
        $= 1.858$

        Answer

        $1.86$

        Times, three significant figures, because the difference $11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the uncertainty $6.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are given to three figures (the result $-262.00\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the accepted value are given to the hundredth).

        Reasonableness

        The deviation $11.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is almost two uncertainties ($2 \cdot 6.00 = 12.0$). The uncertainty interval $-268\ldots -256\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ does not contain the accepted value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so by this rule the result is not consistent with it. The cause may be a systematic error, for example an imprecise thermometer, an air leak or a change in the volume of the flask, or an uncertainty that was estimated too small.         Given

        Find

        a) the uncertainty $\Delta\theta_0$ of the zero-pressure temperature for each series, in degrees Celsius; b) the effect of the width of the measurement range and the consistency with the value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

        Principle

        The pressure at each point is known within $p \pm \Delta p$. The steepest line passes through the lower end of the lower point and the upper end of the upper point, the shallowest the other way round; both fit the data. Their zero-pressure temperatures bound an interval, and the uncertainty is half of the interval. The model assumes that in a sealed rigid container the pressure is a linear function of temperature also outside the measured range.

        Reading the graph

        Two points are read from each series. The steepest line passes through the points $(\theta_1$; $p_1 - \Delta p)$ and $(\theta_2$; $p_2 + \Delta p)$, the shallowest through the points $(\theta_1$; $p_1 + \Delta p)$ and $(\theta_2$; $p_2 - \Delta p)$.

        Formula

        The pressure of the line at the point $\theta_1$ is $p_a$ and at the point $\theta_2$ it is $p_b$. Then

        $k$$= \dfrac{p_b - p_a}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$
        $\theta_0$$= \theta_1 - \dfrac{p_a}{k}$
        $\Delta\theta_0$$= \dfrac{\theta_{0,\text{max}} - \theta_{0,\text{min}}}{2}$

        Conversions

        No conversions are needed: the kilopascals cancel in the quotient $p_a/k$, and the temperature differences are the same in kelvins.

        Series A, steepest:

        $$\begin{aligned} k &= \dfrac{(131.0 - 107.0)\,\text{kPa}}{(85 - 25)\,{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= 0.4000\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C} \\ \theta_0 &= 25\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{107.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.4000\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= -242.5\,{}^\circ\text{C} \end{aligned}$$

        $$\begin{aligned} k &= \dfrac{(129.0 - 109.0)\,\text{kPa}}{(85 - 25)\,{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= 0.3333\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C} \\ \theta_0 &= 25\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{109.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.3333\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= -302.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} \end{aligned}$$

        So

        $\Delta\theta_{0,\text{A}}$$= \dfrac{(-242.5 + 302.0)\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{2}$
        $= 29.75\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

        Series B, shallowest:

        $$\begin{aligned} k &= \dfrac{(180.5 - 109.0)\,\text{kPa}}{(225 - 25)\,{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= 0.3575\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C} \\ \theta_0 &= 25\,{}^\circ\text{C} - \dfrac{109.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.3575\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}} \\ &= -279.90\,{}^\circ\text{C} \end{aligned}$$

        The uncertainty of series B is $10.7\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; three significant figures, because the pressures are given to four figures and the difference of the extreme values, $21.46\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, is known to three. The uncertainty of series A is about $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The results are $\theta_{0,\text{A}} = -272 \pm 30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $\theta_{0,\text{B}} = -269 \pm 11\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

        Model answer (b)

        The measurement range of series B is $200\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ wide and that of series A $60\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the uncertainty of B is only about a third of the uncertainty of A. The same pressure uncertainty $\pm 1.0\,\text{kPa}$ affects the slope less the longer the interval from which it is calculated, and a small error in the slope grows in extrapolation. So a wider measurement range reduces the uncertainty. The interval of each series contains the value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (A: $-302\ldots -243\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, B: $-280\ldots -258\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), so the results are consistent with the accepted value. The interval is an estimate, and it assumes that the pressure stays linear also outside the measured range.

        Reasonableness

        The ratio of the widths of the ranges is $200:60 \approx 3.3$ and the ratio of the uncertainties $29.75:10.73 \approx 2.8$, so the uncertainty decreases roughly in step with the increase in width. The midpoints of the intervals of the series, $-272\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $-269\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, are close to each other and close to the value $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
