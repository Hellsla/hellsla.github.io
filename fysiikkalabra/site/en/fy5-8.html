Spring force and spring energy

Why does a pinball launcher shoot the ball faster the further its spring is compressed?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • recognise a harmonic force and work out its direction.
  • determine the spring constant from the gradient of a measurement graph.
  • calculate the magnitude of a harmonic force and the elastic potential energy of a spring.

Prior knowledge

  • In periodic motion an object moves back and forth on both sides of the equilibrium positionchapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude
  • Two objects exert forces of equal magnitude and opposite direction on each otherchapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws
  • A stretched spring has elastic potential energychapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy

1Think about it

In a pinball machine the ball is shot onto the playing field by a launcher. The player pulls the plunger knob back, which compresses the plunger's spring, and lets go. A short pull sends the ball out gently, a long pull so fast that the ball goes round the whole field. When you hold the knob still, you can feel the spring in your hand.

In which direction does the spring push the plunger, and how much more energy is stored in the spring when the compression is doubled?

Optional Recap: Newton's third law
A hand pulls the end of a spring fixed to a wall to the right with a force of $5.0\,\text{N}$, and the spring is at rest.With what force and in which direction does the spring pull the hand?

  • The forces of an interaction have opposite directions. The hand pulls the spring to the right, so the spring pulls the hand to the left with a force of the same magnitude, $5.0\,\text{N}$.

  • The forces on a spring at rest cancel each other, but the spring still acts on both of its ends. By Newton's third law the spring pulls the hand with a force of equal magnitude, $5.0\,\text{N}$, in the opposite direction, that is to the left.

  • Correct. As you learned in the chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws, two objects exert forces of equal magnitude and opposite direction on each other. The force of the hand acts on the spring, the force of the spring on the hand. In this chapter that distinction is important.

  • Stretching does not reduce the reaction force. By Newton's third law the forces are always equal in magnitude: the spring pulls the hand to the left with a force of $5.0\,\text{N}$.

How the calculation works

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A spring pulls back towards the equilibrium position

    In the chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude an object moved back and forth on both sides of the equilibrium position. Now we find out what kind of force always pulls the object back towards the equilibrium position. Consider a trolley attached to a horizontal spring. When the spring is at its rest length, it exerts no force on the trolley, and the trolley can be at rest. This point is the equilibrium position of the trolley. Let the $x$-axis point along the spring with its origin at the equilibrium position. The position $x$ of the trolley is then its displacement from the equilibrium position: to the right of the equilibrium position $x > 0$ and to the left $x < 0$.

    When the trolley is pulled to the right, the spring stretches and pulls the trolley to the left. When the trolley is pushed to the left, the spring is compressed and pushes the trolley to the right. In both cases the force of the spring points towards the equilibrium position, that is opposite to the displacement. This is why the force of a spring is also called a restoring force.

    Harmonic force

    When a spring is neither stretched nor compressed too much, the magnitude of the spring force is directly proportional to the displacement: double the displacement gives double the force. A force whose magnitude is directly proportional to the displacement of an object from the equilibrium position and which always points towards the equilibrium position is called a harmonic force. The force of a spring is its most common example. The restoring force of a pendulum is also approximately harmonic for small swings. In the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum we see what kind of motion a harmonic force produces.

    The force of the spring and the force that stretches the spring

    When a hand stretches a spring, two objects interact: the hand pulls the spring, and the spring pulls the hand. By Newton's third law these forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but they act on different objects. The force $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ that stretches the spring acts on the spring (the subscript v comes from the Finnish venyttävä, stretching, as in the simulation). The spring force $\overline{F}$ acts on the hand or on the object at the end of the spring. So only the spring force is drawn in the free-body diagram of the object.

    When the trolley is held still at displacement $x$, a hand pulls or pushes the trolley, and the trolley pulls or pushes the spring with a force of equal magnitude. The force of the hand and the force of the spring on the trolley cancel each other. A force meter used to stretch the spring shows the magnitude of the stretching force, and it is equal to the magnitude of the spring force.

    Harmonic force

    When the origin of the $x$-axis is at the point where the spring is at its rest length, and $x$ is the extension or compression of the spring from its rest length, the $x$-component of the force that the spring exerts on an object is

    $$F = -kx$$

    where $F$ is the force of the spring on the object ($\text{N}$), $x$ the extension or compression of the spring from its rest length ($\text{m}$) and $k$ the spring constant of the spring ($\text{N/m}$). The minus sign gives the direction: the force is always opposite to the displacement. When $x > 0$, $F < 0$, and when $x < 0$, $F > 0$. The magnitude of the force is $k|x|$. The spring constant tells the stiffness of the spring, that is how many newtons of force are needed per metre of extension. The spring of a car's suspension can have tens of kilonewtons per metre, the spring of a toy only a few newtons per metre. For a horizontal spring the rest-length position is also the equilibrium position of the object, so the displacement from the equilibrium position is then $x$. The law holds only when the spring is neither stretched too much nor compressed too much.

    The spring constant from a measurement

    The spring constant is determined by measuring. Weights are hung from the spring, and for each weight the extension $x$ of the spring is measured. A weight hanging at rest pulls the spring with a force equal to the weight $mg$ of the weight. This is the force stretching the spring, whose magnitude is

    $$F_\text{v} = kx$$

    where $F_\text{v}$ is the stretching force ($\text{N}$) and $x$ the extension ($\text{m}$). The measurement points are plotted in an $(x, F_\text{v})$ coordinate system and a straight line is fitted to them. In real data series the measurement points do not usually lie exactly on one line because of measurement uncertainty. The line passes through the origin, because without a stretching force the spring is at its rest length. The gradient (slope) of the line is the spring constant $k$.

    The gradient is read from two points of the fitted line, not from two measurement points. In the example data of the graph the line passes through the points $(0.025\,\text{m}$; $1.0\,\text{N})$ and $(0.100\,\text{m}$; $4.0\,\text{N})$, so $k = \tfrac{3.0\,\text{N}}{0.075\,\text{m}} = 40\,\text{N/m}$. The points are chosen far apart, so that the uncertainty of the reading affects the result little. You can make the same measurement yourself in the laboratory investigation Determining the spring constant (page in Finnish).

    Elastic potential energy

    In the chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy it was stated that a stretched spring has elastic potential energy. Now its magnitude can be calculated. When a spring is stretched slowly from its rest length to extension $x$, the stretching force does work, and the work is stored in the spring as potential energy. The work of a constant force, $W = Fs$, is the area of a rectangle in an $(s, F)$ graph (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The stretching force is not constant, however, but grows steadily from zero to $kx$. So the work is the triangle between the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph and the $x$-axis, with base $x$ and height $kx$. The elastic potential energy is

    $$E_\text{p} = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$$

    where $E_\text{p}$ is the elastic potential energy ($\text{J}$), $k$ the spring constant ($\text{N/m}$) and $x$ the extension or compression ($\text{m}$). At the rest length the elastic potential energy is zero.

    The triangle for double the extension is twice as wide and twice as tall, so four triangles of the original size fit in it. So the potential energy grows with the square of the extension, even though the force grows only in direct proportion. Because $x$ is squared, the energy is the same when the spring is stretched or compressed by the same amount. The units agree: $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot\text{m}^2 = \text{N}\cdot\text{m} = \text{J}$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think the force of the spring and the force stretching the spring are the same force. They are a Newton's third law pair: the stretching force acts on the spring and the spring force on the object, so only the spring force is drawn in the free-body diagram of the object. Another mistake is to read the minus sign in the expression $F = -kx$ as meaning that the force is always negative or small. The minus sign only gives the direction: the force points against the displacement, towards the equilibrium position, and is positive when $x < 0$. A third mistake is to assume that double the extension doubles the elastic potential energy. The force doubles, but the energy becomes four times as large, because it depends on the square of the extension.

    Summary

    • A harmonic force is directly proportional to the displacement of an object from the equilibrium position and always points towards the equilibrium position: $F = -kx$.
    • The minus sign gives the direction: the force is opposite to the displacement.
    • The spring constant $k$ tells the stiffness of the spring. It is the gradient of the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph, read from two points of the fitted line.
    • The force stretching a spring acts on the spring and the spring force on the object: they are a Newton's third law pair.
    • The elastic potential energy is the area of the triangle under the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph, $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. Double the extension gives four times the energy.

      Example 1: Weighing a fish with a spring scale

      A fisherman weighs a fish with a spring balance. When a fish of mass $1.20\,\text{kg}$ hangs at rest from the hook of the balance, the spring of the balance has stretched $3.0\,\text{cm}$. What is the spring constant of the spring, and how much elastic potential energy is stored in the spring? The masses of the spring and the hook are neglected.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Mass of the fish $m = 1.20\,\text{kg}$, extension of the spring $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We are asked for the spring constant $k$ and the elastic potential energy $E_\text{p}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Choose the fish as the object. The Earth (weight $\overline{G}$) and the spring (spring force $\overline{F}$) act on it. The fish is at rest, so the forces cancel each other, and the magnitude of the spring force is $mg$. By Newton's third law the fish pulls the spring with a force of equal magnitude, so the force stretching the spring is $F_\text{v} = mg$. From this and the extension we get the spring constant. In the free-body diagram the $y$-axis points up.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The equation of motion is read from the free-body diagram: $F - mg = 0$, so the force stretching the spring is $F_\text{v} = kx = mg$. The spring constant is solved from this, and the energy is obtained from the extension:

      $$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{mg}{x} \\ E_\text{p} &= \frac{1}{2}kx^2 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The extension is converted to metres:

      $x$$= 3.0\,\text{cm}$
      $= 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.030\,\text{m}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{mg}{x} \\ \phantom{k} &= \frac{1.20\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.030\,\text{m}} \\ \phantom{k} &= 392.4\,\text{N/m} \\[4pt] E_\text{p} &= \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 \\ \phantom{E_\text{p}} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 392.4\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \\ &\phantom{E_\text{p} =}\cdot (0.030\,\text{m})^2 \\ \phantom{E_\text{p}} &= 0.1766\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The spring constant is $k \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{N/m}$ and the elastic potential energy $E_\text{p} \approx 0.18\,\text{J}$, to two significant figures like the extension $3.0\,\text{cm}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The energy can also be calculated from the triangle of the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph:

      $\tfrac{1}{2}F_\text{v}x$$= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 11.77\,\text{N}\cdot 0.030\,\text{m}$
      $\approx 0.18\,\text{J}$

      the same result. A spring of about $400\,\text{N/m}$ stretches a centimetre under a force of about $4\,\text{N}$, which suits a balance used to weigh fish of a few kilograms. The energy is small because the extension is small.

The object pulls the spring with the force $\overline{F}_2$, and the spring pulls the object with the force $\overline{F}_1$: they are a Newton's third law pair, and only $\overline{F}_1$ acts on the object. Without $\overline{F}_2$ the weight and the force of the spring cancel each other, as they should at rest.

      • The weight acts at the centre of mass of the object, so it is drawn from the centre, as in the diagram. The error is the force $\overline{F}_2$: it is the force of the object on the spring, and it does not belong in the free-body diagram of the object.

      • The object is at rest, so the forces on it must cancel each other: the force of the spring is equal to the weight. The fault of the diagram is that it also has the force $\overline{F}_2$ acting on the spring.

      • A stretched spring pulls the object towards its equilibrium position, that is up, opposite to the extension. $\overline{F}_1$ is correct. The error is the force $\overline{F}_2$, which acts on the spring and not on the object.

      Example 4: Apply to a new situation

      The spring of a pinball launcher has a spring constant of $250\,\text{N/m}$. The player pulls the plunger $6.0\,\text{cm}$ back, so that the spring is compressed by the same distance, and lets go. The spring sets the plunger and the $80\,\text{g}$ ball in front of it in motion, and the ball leaves the plunger when the spring is at its rest length. Friction, the mass of the plunger and the tilt of the table are neglected.

      At what speed does the ball leave?

      m/s
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Into what does the elastic potential energy change when the spring returns to its rest length?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The elastic potential energy changes into the kinetic energy of the ball: $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Solve $v = x\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$ and convert the centimetres and grams to SI units.

      Show the solution

      Given

      spring constant $k = 250\,\text{N/m}$, compression $x = 6.0\,\text{cm}$, mass of the ball $m = 80\,\text{g}$; the ball is initially at rest

      Find

      the launch speed $v$ of the ball in $\text{m/s}$

      Principle

      There is no friction, the mass of the plunger is neglected and the table is horizontal, so mechanical energy is conserved: the elastic potential energy changes entirely into the kinetic energy of the ball. The ball leaves when the spring is at its rest length, when the energy of the spring is zero.

      Formula

      $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = x\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$

      Conversions

      $x$$= 6.0\,\text{cm}$
      $= 6.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.060\,\text{m}$
      $m$$= 80\,\text{g}$
      $= 80 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
      $= 0.080\,\text{kg}$

      Substitution

      $v$$= 0.060\,\text{m}\cdot\sqrt{\dfrac{250\,\text{N/m}}{0.080\,\text{kg}}}$
      $= 0.060\,\text{m}\cdot 55.90\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
      $= 3.354\,\text{m/s}$

      Answer

      $3.4\,\text{m/s}$

      Two significant figures like the compression $6.0\,\text{cm}$ and the mass $80\,\text{g}$.

      Reasonableness

      The energy of the spring is

      $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 250\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.060\,\text{m})^2$
      $= 0.45\,\text{J}$

      and the kinetic energy of the ball is

      $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 0.080\,\text{kg}\cdot(3.354\,\text{m/s})^2$
      $\approx 0.45\,\text{J}$

      the same. About $12\,\text{km/h}$ is a believable launch speed for a pinball. Double the compression would give four times the energy and double the speed.

        $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{p} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 302\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.50\,\text{m})^2 \\ &= 37.75\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

        The kinetic energy of the arrow and the efficiency are

        $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{k} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 0.025\,\text{kg}\cdot(47.0\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &= 27.61\,\text{J} \\ \eta &= \frac{27.61\,\text{J}}{37.75\,\text{J}} = 0.731 \end{aligned}$$

        Answer

        $73\,\%$

        Of the stored energy changed into the kinetic energy of the arrow, two significant figures like the mass of the arrow $25\,\text{g}$. The rest, about $27\,\%$ or about $10\,\text{J}$, probably went into the kinetic energy of the spring and the string, vibration, sound and heat: the spring and the string move themselves and finally stop. The answer is based on the assumption that the graph is a line over the whole draw distance.

        Reasonableness

        The efficiency is below $100\,\%$, as it must be, and an efficiency above $70\,\%$ is usual for real crossbows. The speed is checked the other way round: if all of the $37.75\,\text{J}$ went to the arrow, its speed would be

        $$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\cdot 37.75\,\text{J}}{0.025\,\text{kg}}} = 55.0\,\text{m/s}$$

        a little more than the measured $47.0\,\text{m/s}$.

      1. Spring A is stiffer than spring B. Which spring has the steeper $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph, and why?
        Answer

        Spring A's. The gradient of the graph is the spring constant $k$, and stretching a stiffer spring the same distance needs a larger force, so its line rises more steeply.

      2. A spring whose spring constant is $200\,\text{N/m}$ is first compressed $0.10\,\text{m}$ and then stretched $0.10\,\text{m}$. In which situation is more elastic potential energy stored in it?
        Answer

        The energy is the same,

        $E_\text{p}$$= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 200\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.10\,\text{m})^2$
        $= 1.0\,\text{J}$

        in both, because the displacement is squared and its sign does not matter. The direction of the force, on the other hand, is opposite.

      3. A hand stretches a spring. Which two forces form a Newton's third law pair here, and which objects do they act on?
        Answer

        The force of the hand on the spring, that is the force stretching the spring $\overline{F}_\text{v}$, acts on the spring, and the force of the spring on the hand $\overline{F}$ acts on the hand. They are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but because they act on different objects, they do not cancel each other.

      The expression $F = -kx$ has a minus sign. What does it tell you about the force of the spring?

      • The spring constant is always positive, and it tells the stiffness of the spring. The minus sign tells that the force is opposite to the displacement.

      • Only when $x > 0$. When the spring is compressed, that is $x < 0$, $-kx$ is positive and the force points in the positive direction of the $x$-axis, towards the equilibrium position.

      • Correct. When $x > 0$ the force is negative, and when $x < 0$ it is positive. So the force always points towards the equilibrium position.

      3. Calculation task: Sign and magnitude

      An object at the end of a spring is acted on by a spring force $F = -6.0\,\text{N}$ when the object is at $x = 0.12\,\text{m}$. The spring is horizontal, and the origin is at the equilibrium position, where the spring is at its rest length.

      How large is the spring force $F$, with its sign, when the object is at $x = -0.20\,\text{m}$?

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      First calculate the spring constant from the readings of the first situation.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $F = -kx$, so $k = -\dfrac{F}{x}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $k = 50\,\text{N/m}$. Then substitute $x = -0.20\,\text{m}$ into the expression $F = -kx$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      $F_1 = -6.0\,\text{N}$ at $x_1 = 0.12\,\text{m}$; new position $x_2 = -0.20\,\text{m}$; horizontal spring, origin at the equilibrium position, that is at the rest length of the spring

      Find

      the spring force $F_2$ with its sign, in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The force of the spring is harmonic: $F = -kx$, and the spring constant is the same at both positions. The spring constant is obtained from the first situation. The sign tells the direction.

      Formula

      $F_1 = -kx_1$, so $k = -\dfrac{F_1}{x_1}$, and $F_2 = -kx_2$

      Conversions

      The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $k$$= -\dfrac{-6.0\,\text{N}}{0.12\,\text{m}}$
      $= 50\,\text{N/m}$
      $F_2$$= -50\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(-0.20\,\text{m})$
      $= 10\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $F_2 = +10\,\text{N}$

      That is $10\,\text{N}$ in the positive direction of the $x$-axis, two significant figures like the initial data.

      Reasonableness

      The object is on the negative side of the equilibrium position, so the force must point in the positive direction, towards the equilibrium position. The displacement is $\tfrac{0.20}{0.12} \approx 1.7$ times as large, and so is the magnitude of the force: $1.7 \cdot 6.0\,\text{N} = 10\,\text{N}$.

      4. Explanation task: Draw it yourself

      A box of mass $6.0\,\text{kg}$ is pulled along a horizontal floor by a horizontal spring so that the box slides at a constant speed. The kinetic friction of the floor on the box is $18\,\text{N}$, and the spring constant of the spring is $120\,\text{N/m}$. Draw the free-body diagram of the box on paper: the box as a rectangle and each force from its point of application. Write the equations of motion and calculate how much the spring has stretched.

      Model diagram

      The Earth, the floor and the spring act on the box. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre, and the support force of the floor $\overline{N}$ up from the lower surface. The spring force $\overline{F}$ pulls the box horizontally in the direction of the pull, and kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ points against it from the corner of the lower surface. The force that stretches the spring is not drawn, because it acts on the spring. The speed is constant, so the forces cancel each other. With $x$ pointing in the direction of the pull and $y$ up: $y$: $N - mg = 0$, so $N = 6.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 59\,\text{N}$. $x$: $kx - F_\mu = 0$, so $x = \dfrac{F_\mu}{k} = \dfrac{18\,\text{N}}{120\,\text{N/m}} = 0.15\,\text{m}$. The spring has stretched $15\,\text{cm}$.
