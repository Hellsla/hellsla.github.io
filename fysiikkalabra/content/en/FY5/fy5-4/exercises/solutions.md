## fy5-4-L1-01
**Given:** tangential speed $v = 20\,\text{m/s}$, radius of the bend $r = 80\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The car drives round the bend at a steady speed, so it is in uniform circular motion. The direction of the velocity changes, and the acceleration points towards the centre of the bend. Its magnitude depends only on the tangential speed and the radius.
**Formula:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$
**Conversions:** The speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the radius in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{(20\,\text{m/s})^2}{80\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{400\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{80\,\text{m}} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ towards the centre of the bend, two significant figures like the speed $20\,\text{m/s}$ and the radius $80\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration is about half of the free-fall acceleration $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. A passenger feels it as a clear sideways push, but on a dry road the grip is enough for it. The unit $\tfrac{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{\text{m}} = \text{m/s}^2$ is the unit of acceleration.

## fy5-4-L1-02
**Given:** tangential speed $v = 8.00\,\text{m/s}$, radius of the bend $r = 36.5\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The speed of the runner stays the same, but the direction of the velocity turns in the bend. So the runner is in uniform circular motion, and the acceleration points towards the centre of the bend.
**Formula:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{(8.00\,\text{m/s})^2}{36.5\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{64.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{36.5\,\text{m}} = 1.7534\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $1.75\,\text{m/s}^2$, three significant figures like the speed $8.00\,\text{m/s}$ and the radius $36.5\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration is less than a fifth of the free-fall acceleration, so the runner can produce it with the friction between the shoes and the track by leaning the body a little. The cornering speeds of sprinters are of the same order of magnitude.

## fy5-4-L1-03
**Given:** $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, $r = 1.2\,\text{m}$, $v = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, horizontal path
**Find:** the net force $\Sigma F$ on the stone in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The stone is in uniform circular motion, so its acceleration is the centripetal acceleration towards the centre. By Newton's second law the net force is the product of the mass and the acceleration, and it points in the same direction.
**Formula:** $\Sigma F = m a_\text{n}$ and $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $\Sigma F = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\Sigma F = 0.50\,\text{kg}\cdot\dfrac{(4.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{1.2\,\text{m}} = 0.50\,\text{kg}\cdot 13.33\,\text{m/s}^2 = 6.667\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $6.7\,\text{N}$ towards the centre of the path, two significant figures like the given data.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the stone is about $4.9\,\text{N}$, so the horizontal net force is slightly greater than the weight. The string also supports the stone, so the tension of the string is still greater, about $\sqrt{6.7^2 + 4.9^2}\,\text{N} \approx 8.3\,\text{N}$. This is clearly felt in the hand but easy to hold. The unit $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2 = \text{N}$.

## fy5-4-L1-04
**Given:** centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = 2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$, radius $r = 25\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the tangential speed $v$ in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** In a roundabout the car goes round a circle. If the speed is steady, the motion is uniform circular motion, and the centripetal acceleration depends on the tangential speed and the radius.
**Formula:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $v^2 = a_\text{n} r$ and $v = \sqrt{a_\text{n} r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{2.5\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 25\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{62.5\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 7.906\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $7.9\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the acceleration and the radius.
**Reasonableness:** $7.9\,\text{m/s}$ is about $28\,\text{km/h}$, a normal speed in a roundabout. Check: $\tfrac{(7.9\,\text{m/s})^2}{25\,\text{m}} \approx 2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

## fy5-4-L1-05
**Given:** radius of the drum $r = 0.25\,\text{m}$, tangential speed of the garment $v = 15\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** In the spin cycle the drum turns steadily, so a garment at the rim of the drum is in uniform circular motion and accelerates towards the axis of the drum.
**Formula:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{(15\,\text{m/s})^2}{0.25\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{225\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{0.25\,\text{m}} = 900\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $900\,\text{m/s}^2$ or $9.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures like the radius $0.25\,\text{m}$ and the speed $15\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration is about $90$ times the free-fall acceleration. The wall of the drum pushes the garment towards the axis with a correspondingly large force, but the water escapes through the holes of the drum and nothing keeps it on the circular path. That is why the water separates from the clothes.

## fy5-4-L2-01
**Given:** combined mass $m = 80\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$, radius of the arc at the bottom of the dip $r = 16\,\text{m}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected
**Find:** the support force $N$ of the road in $\text{kN}$
**Principle:** At the bottom of the dip the cyclist and the bicycle are for a moment on a circular path whose centre is above the road. By Newton's second law the net force points up towards the centre and equals $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. In the vertical direction only the weight and the support force of the road act on them, so the support force must be greater than the weight.
**Force diagram:** The cyclist and the bicycle as one object, a rectangle on the road, $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards and the support force $\overline{N}$ of the road from the bottom face upwards, longer than the weight. The acceleration separately as a thin orange arrow up towards the centre of the arc.
```voimakuvio
pinta 0
G 784,8
N 1104,8
a ylös
v oikea
akselit 0
```
**Formula:** $y$: $N - mg = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $N = m\left(g + \dfrac{v^2}{r}\right)$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units; the result is finally converted to kilonewtons, $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $N = 80\,\text{kg}\cdot\left(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + \dfrac{(8.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{16\,\text{m}}\right) = 80\,\text{kg}\cdot 13.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1104.8\,\text{N} = 1.1048\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $1.1\,\text{kN}$ upwards, two significant figures like the speed $8.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the radius $16\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight is $80\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 785\,\text{N}$, and the support force is about $1.4$ times it, because $\tfrac{v^2}{r} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ is about $0.4$ times $g$. The cyclist feels pressed into the saddle. At the top of a hill at the same speed the support force would correspondingly be smaller than the weight.

## fy5-4-L2-02
**Given:** period $T = 5.0\,\text{s}$, distance from the centre $r = 3.0\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n}$ of the child in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The roundabout turns steadily, so the child is in uniform circular motion. By the chapter *Moving in a circle: period and speed*, the tangential speed is the length of one revolution divided by the period, and the centripetal acceleration is found from the tangential speed.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ and $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{2\pi\cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s}} = 3.770\,\text{m/s}$ and $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{(3.770\,\text{m/s})^2}{3.0\,\text{m}} = 4.737\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $4.7\,\text{m/s}^2$ towards the centre of the roundabout, two significant figures like the period $5.0\,\text{s}$ and the radius $3.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration is about half of the free-fall acceleration, so the child has to hold on firmly. Closer to the centre, with the same period, both the tangential speed and the acceleration would be smaller.

## fy5-4-L2-03
**Given:** $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, $v = 12\,\text{m/s}$, radius of the arc at the top $r = 30\,\text{m}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected
**Find:** the support force $N$ of the road in $\text{kN}$
**Principle:** At the top of the hill the car is for a moment on a circular path whose centre is below the road. By Newton's second law the net force points down towards the centre and equals $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. In the vertical direction only the weight and the support force act on the car, so the weight is greater than the support force.
**Force diagram:** The car as a rectangle on the road, $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards and the support force $\overline{N}$ of the road from the bottom face upwards, shorter than the weight. The acceleration separately as a thin orange arrow down towards the centre of the arc.
```voimakuvio
pinta 0
G 11772
N 6012
a alas
v oikea
akselit 0
```
**Formula:** $y$: $N - mg = -m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $N = m\left(g - \dfrac{v^2}{r}\right)$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units; the result is finally converted to kilonewtons, $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $N = 1200\,\text{kg}\cdot\left(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - \dfrac{(12\,\text{m/s})^2}{30\,\text{m}}\right) = 1200\,\text{kg}\cdot 5.01\,\text{m/s}^2 = 6012\,\text{N} = 6.012\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{kN}$ upwards, two significant figures like the speed $12\,\text{m/s}$ and the radius $30\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the car is $11.8\,\text{kN}$, and the support force is only about half of it, so the passengers feel light. The support force is still positive, because $\tfrac{v^2}{r} = 4.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is smaller than $g$: the car does not leave the road.

## fy5-4-L2-04
**Given:** $m = 7.26\,\text{kg}$, radius $r = 1.8\,\text{m}$, period $T = 0.50\,\text{s}$, the path is a horizontal circle
**Find:** the net force $\Sigma F$ on the hammer in $\text{kN}$
**Principle:** In the model the hammer is in uniform circular motion, so by Newton's second law the net force points towards the centre of the circle and equals $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. The tangential speed is found from the period.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ and $\Sigma F = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units; the result is finally converted to kilonewtons, $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{2\pi\cdot 1.8\,\text{m}}{0.50\,\text{s}} = 22.62\,\text{m/s}$ and $\Sigma F = 7.26\,\text{kg}\cdot\dfrac{(22.62\,\text{m/s})^2}{1.8\,\text{m}} = 2064\,\text{N} = 2.064\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $2.1\,\text{kN}$ towards the centre, two significant figures like the radius $1.8\,\text{m}$ and the period $0.50\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The force is almost $30$ times the weight of the hammer, $71\,\text{N}$, so the thrower has to lean strongly backwards. Because the force is so much greater than the weight, the wire is almost horizontal, and the model of a horizontal circle is reasonable.

## fy5-4-L3-01
**Given:** radius of the bend $r = 60\,\text{m}$, coefficient of static friction $\mu_0 = 0.10$, horizontal road; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected
**Find:** the greatest speed $v$ in $\text{km/h}$
**Principle:** In a bend the only horizontal force on the car is the static friction of the road, and by Newton's second law it must be $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre. Static friction can be at most $\mu_0 N$. At the greatest speed the friction is at its maximum.
**Force diagram:** The car seen from behind as a rectangle, the centre of the bend on the right, $x$ towards the centre and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the support force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom face upwards and equally long, and the maximum static friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the corner of the bottom face towards the centre, one tenth of the length of the weight. The acceleration separately towards the centre.
```voimakuvio
pinta 0
G 100
N 100
F_mu 10 oikea
a oikea
akselit 0
```
**Formula:** $y$: $N - mg = 0$ and $x$: $\mu_0 N = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $\mu_0 mg = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $v = \sqrt{\mu_0 g r}$
**Conversions:** The result is finally converted to $\text{km/h}$: $1\,\text{m/s} = \dfrac{0.001\,\text{km}}{\tfrac{1}{3600}\,\text{h}} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{0.10\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 60\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{58.86\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 7.672\,\text{m/s} = 7.672\cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} = 27.62\,\text{km/h}$
**Answer:** $28\,\text{km/h}$, two significant figures like the radius $60\,\text{m}$ and the coefficient $0.10$.
**Reasonableness:** On ice a bend must be taken very slowly, and on dry asphalt ($\mu_0 \approx 0.8$) the same bend could be taken at $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.8$ times the speed, about $78\,\text{km/h}$. The mass cancelled, so even a heavy car does not stay in the bend any better.

## fy5-4-L3-02
**Given:** $m = 0.40\,\text{kg}$, $r = 0.80\,\text{m}$, greatest tension of the string $T_\text{max} = 25\,\text{N}$, the table is smooth and horizontal
**Find:** the greatest rotation frequency $f$ in $\text{Hz}$
**Principle:** The puck is in uniform circular motion. In the plane of the table only the tension of the string acts on it, so by Newton's second law the tension is $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the peg. The greater the speed, the greater the tension, so the greatest allowed tension gives the greatest speed. The rotation frequency $f$ is the number of revolutions per second, so in one second the puck travels the distance $f \cdot 2\pi r$, that is $v = 2\pi r f$.
**Force diagram:** The puck seen from the side as a rectangle on the table, the peg on the right, $x$ towards the peg and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the support force $\overline{N}$ of the table from the bottom face upwards and equally long, and the tension $\overline{T}$ of the string from the right side towards the peg. The acceleration separately towards the peg.
```voimakuvio
pinta 0
G 3,924
N 3,924
T 25 0
a oikea
akselit 0
```
**Formula:** $x$: $T = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, where $T$ is the tension of the string, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T r}{m}}$; $v = 2\pi r f$, so $f = \dfrac{v}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed; $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{1/s}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{25\,\text{N}\cdot 0.80\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{50\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 7.071\,\text{m/s}$ and $f = \dfrac{7.071\,\text{m/s}}{2\pi\cdot 0.80\,\text{m}} = 1.407\,\text{Hz}$
**Answer:** $1.4\,\text{Hz}$, two significant figures like the mass, the radius and the strength of the string.
**Reasonableness:** The puck makes a little over one revolution per second, and the period is about $0.71\,\text{s}$, which is possible when whirled by hand. The tension grows with the square of the speed, so a small increase in the frequency would soon break the string.

## fy5-4-L3-03
**Given:** design speed $v = 80\,\text{km/h}$, sideways friction at most $0.25\,mg$, horizontal road; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and rolling resistance are neglected
**Find:** the smallest radius $r$ of the bend in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** In a bend the only horizontal force on the car is the friction of the road, and by Newton's second law it must be $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre. The smaller the radius, the greater the friction needed, so the smallest allowed radius is found when the friction is the greatest allowed.
**Force diagram:** The car seen from behind as a rectangle, the centre of the bend on the right, $x$ towards the centre and $y$ up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre downwards, the support force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom face upwards and equally long, and the friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the corner of the bottom face towards the centre, a quarter of the length of the weight. The acceleration separately towards the centre.
```voimakuvio
pinta 0
G 100
N 100
F_mu 25 oikea
a oikea
akselit 0
```
**Formula:** $y$: $N - mg = 0$ and $x$: $F_\mu = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$. The condition $F_\mu \le 0.25\,mg$ gives $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} \le 0.25\,mg$, so $r \ge \dfrac{v^2}{0.25\,g}$
**Conversions:** $v = 80\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{80\cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 22.22\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $r = \dfrac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{0.25\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{493.8\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{2.4525\,\text{m/s}^2} = 201.4\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $200\,\text{m}$ or $2.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the speed $80\,\text{km/h}$ and the coefficient $0.25$.
**Reasonableness:** The radii of the bends of country roads are hundreds of metres, so the result is believable. The radius grows with the square of the speed: for $120\,\text{km/h}$ a radius $2.25$ times as large would be needed, about $450\,\text{m}$. The mass cancelled, so the same radius is enough for all cars.
