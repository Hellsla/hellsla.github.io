## fy8-11-L1-01
**Given:** atomic masses $m_\text{Li} = 6.0151229\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{n} = 1.0086649\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{He} = 4.0026033\,\text{u}$ and $m_\text{T} = 3.0160493\,\text{u}$
**Asked:** the reaction energy $Q$ in megaelectronvolts
**Principle:** The reaction releases an energy that corresponds to the difference between the masses of the starting materials and the products. The sum of the atomic numbers is conserved ($3 + 0 = 2 + 1$), so the electrons in the atomic masses cancel and the atomic masses can be used directly.
**Formula:** $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$, from which $Q = (m_\text{Li} + m_\text{n} - m_\text{He} - m_\text{T})\,c^2$
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, so the mass difference in atomic mass units is multiplied by $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ and $c^2$ cancels.
**Substitution:** $Q = (7.0237878\,\text{u} - 7.0186526\,\text{u})\,c^2 = 0.0051352\,\text{u} \cdot c^2 = 0.0051352 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 4.7834\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** $4.78\,\text{MeV}$, three significant figures; the precision of the mass difference $0.0051352\,\text{u}$ would allow even more.
**Reasonableness:** The mass difference is about $0.005\,\text{u}$, and $0.005 \cdot 900\,\text{MeV} = 4.5\,\text{MeV}$. The result is smaller than in the fusion of deuterium and tritium ($17.6\,\text{MeV}$) but clearly positive, so the reaction releases energy.

**Principle:** Equivalence of mass and energy: when the mass of the products of a reaction is smaller than that of the starting materials, the energy corresponding to the difference is released as the kinetic energy of the products and as radiation.

**Substitution:** $Q = 0.1915\,\text{u} \cdot c^2 = 0.1915 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 178.38\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** $178\,\text{MeV}$, three significant figures; the mass difference $0.1915\,\text{u}$ is given with four, but a more precise value for the energy of a single fission is not needed.
**Reasonableness:** With rounded numbers $0.2 \cdot 900\,\text{MeV} = 180\,\text{MeV}$. The result is of the same order of magnitude as the fission energy of the example of the chapter, $173\,\text{MeV}$, and the average of about $200\,\text{MeV}$.

## fy8-11-L1-03
**Given:** number of neutrons at the start $N_0 = 1000$, multiplication factor $k = 0.90$, generation $g = 10$
**Asked:** the average number of neutrons $N_{10}$ in generation $10$
**Principle:** Each neutron produces on average $k$ neutrons in the next generation, so the number is multiplied by $k$ in every generation. Because $k < 1$, the chain gradually dies out.
**Formula:** $N_g = N_0 k^g$
**Conversions:** None are needed: the number of neutrons and the multiplication factor are pure numbers.
**Substitution:** $N_{10} = 1000 \cdot 0.90^{10} = 1000 \cdot 0.34868 = 348.68$
**Answer:** $349$ neutrons, and the number is an average, because the fate of individual neutrons is random.
**Reasonableness:** In every generation $10\,\%$ of the neutrons are lost. If the decrease were always $100$ neutrons, the number would be zero, but $10\,\%$ is taken of an ever smaller number, so about a third is still left.

## fy8-11-L1-06
**Asked:** what the job of the moderator of a nuclear reactor is.
**Principle:** The neutrons produced in fission are very fast, but a uranium-235 nucleus is more likely to capture a slow neutron than a fast one. The moderator is a material whose light nuclei the neutrons collide with and lose kinetic energy. The moderator neither removes neutrons nor transfers heat: the former is the job of the control rods and the latter that of the coolant.
**Model answer:** The moderator slows down the fast neutrons produced in fissions, because slow neutrons are more likely than fast ones to cause fissions of uranium-235. In this way the chain reaction keeps going. The number of neutrons and the multiplication factor are adjusted with the control rods, which capture neutrons, and the heat is transferred to the turbine by the coolant. A neutron does not split into parts as it slows down; it loses kinetic energy in collisions.

## fy8-11-L1-07
**Asked:** why fusion requires a high temperature.
**Principle:** Atomic nuclei have a positive charge, so they repel each other electrically. Nuclear forces act only over a very short distance, so the nuclei have to get that close to each other. At a high temperature the kinetic energy of the particles is large, and some of the nuclei get close enough in spite of the repulsion.
**Model answer:** The nuclei repel each other electrically, so they need a large kinetic energy to get close to each other. When the nuclei are close enough, the short-range nuclear forces overcome the electric repulsion and the nuclei join together. Temperature does not make the nuclei radioactive or reduce their mass, and at such a high temperature matter is not a liquid but plasma. The decrease in mass takes place only in the fusion reaction, and it releases energy.

## fy8-11-L2-07
**Asked:** what the error is in the student's reasoning when the student concludes that no energy is released in fission.
**Principle:** The number of nucleons is conserved ($236 = 236$), so the student's starting point is correct. However, mass is not just the number of nucleons times a constant; the binding energy affects it. In the products the binding energy per nucleon is greater than in uranium, so the total mass of the products is smaller. By the equivalence of mass and energy, the mass difference is released as energy, $Q = \Delta m\,c^2$.
**Model answer:** The number of nucleons is conserved, but the nucleons of the products are more tightly bound than those of uranium-235, so the total mass of the products is smaller than that of the starting materials. The mass difference is released as energy, so energy is released in fission. Nucleons do not turn into energy or disappear, and mass and energy are equivalent, not separate. If the nucleons of the products were more loosely bound, the mass would increase and no energy would be released.

## fy8-11-L3-04
**Given:** activity per tonne as a function of time (table, example data; $t$ is counted from about $10$ years after removal), target value $A_\text{limit} = 100\,\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}}$, half-life of plutonium-239 $24\,100\,\text{a}$
**Asked:** a) the half-life $T_{1/2}$, b) the time $t$ at which the activity is $100\,\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}}$, c) an explanation
**Principle:** By the decay law $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$, so $\ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t$ is a linear function of time with slope $-\lambda$. A straight line is fitted to the points, and the half-life is $T_{1/2} = \tfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$. When the line is extended, the time at which the activity is $100\,\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}}$ is obtained by solving $t$ from the decay law.
**Formula:** $\ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t$, $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$ and $t = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}\ln\dfrac{A_0}{A_\text{limit}}$
**Conversions:** None are needed: the time is in years and the activity in the unit $\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}}$ throughout the problem.
**Reading the graph:** The points $(t, \ln A)$ are $(0\,\text{a}; 10.309)$, $(10\,\text{a}; 10.082)$, $(20\,\text{a}; 9.865)$, $(30\,\text{a}; 9.619)$, $(40\,\text{a}; 9.388)$ and $(50\,\text{a}; 9.175)$. They lie approximately on a straight line, but measurement points do not usually lie exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty. The line is drawn between the points. The equation of the fitted line is $\ln A = 10.311 - 0.02285\,\tfrac{1}{\text{a}} \cdot t$, so $\lambda = 0.02285\,\tfrac{1}{\text{a}}$ and $A_0 = e^{10.311}\,\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}} = 30\,060\,\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}}$. Two points far from each other, for example $(0\,\text{a}; 10.309)$ and $(50\,\text{a}; 9.175)$, give approximately the same line.
**Substitution:** a) $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{0.02285\,\tfrac{1}{\text{a}}} = 30.3\,\text{a}$. b) $t = \dfrac{1}{0.02285\,\tfrac{1}{\text{a}}}\ln\dfrac{30\,060}{100} = 43.77\,\text{a} \cdot 5.706 = 249.7\,\text{a}$. c) The activity of plutonium-239 falls to half only in $24\,100$ years, so in $250$ years it has decreased by only about $1\,\%$: $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{250/24100} = 0.993$.
**Answer:** $250\,\text{a}$ as the result of part b) (the extrapolation extends from $50$ years to $250$ years); the half-life is $30\,\text{a}$, and two significant figures are enough, because fitting a line is imprecise. Justification for part c): after about $250$ years the activity is determined by long-lived nuclides such as plutonium-239, whose activity decreases only slowly. The activity of plutonium-239 itself falls to a thousandth only in about $240\,000$ years (problem L3-03), and this is why the fuel is isolated in Onkalo for a long time.
**Reasonableness:** In the table the activity at $30$ years is $15\,050$, about half of the initial value $30\,000$, so the half-life is about $30\,\text{a}$. Eight half-lives reduce the activity by a factor of $2^{8} = 256$, and $30\,000 : 256 \approx 117$, so $100\,\tfrac{\text{TBq}}{\text{t}}$ is reached after about $250\,\text{a}$. The model describes only the fission products.

## fy8-11-L3-05
**Given:** (the problem continues problem L3-01 with another reactor) net electrical power $P_\text{e} = 507\,\text{MW}$, efficiency $\eta = 0.33$, operating time $92\,\%$ of the year ($365\,\text{d}$), energy of a fission $Q = 200\,\text{MeV}$, $m_\text{U} = 235.0439282\,\text{u}$, $1\,\text{u} = 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, heating value of coal $24\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Asked:** a) the thermal power $P_\text{th}$ and the power $P_\text{waste}$ transferred to the cooling water, b) the mass $m$ of uranium-235 that undergoes fission in a year, c) the mass of the corresponding coal
**Principle:** The efficiency is the electrical power divided by the thermal power, so the thermal power is obtained from the electrical power. The part of the thermal power that is not turned into electricity is transferred to the cooling water. By the assumption, all the thermal power comes from fissions of uranium-235. The energy released in a year divided by the energy of one fission is the number of fissions, and each fission uses up one uranium-235 atom.
**Formula:** $\eta = \dfrac{P_\text{e}}{P_\text{th}}$, $P_\text{waste} = P_\text{th} - P_\text{e}$, $E = 0.92\,P_\text{th}\,t$, $N = \dfrac{E}{Q}$, $m = N\,m_\text{U}$ and $m_\text{coal} = \dfrac{E}{\text{heating value}}$
**Conversions:** $t = 365 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 31\,536\,000\,\text{s}$; $Q = 200 \cdot 10^{6} \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 3.204 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{J}$; $m_\text{U} = 235.0439282 \cdot 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg} = 3.9030 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$; $24\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} = 24 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
**Substitution:** a) $P_\text{th} = \dfrac{507\,\text{MW}}{0.33} = 1536\,\text{MW}$ and $P_\text{waste} = 1536\,\text{MW} - 507\,\text{MW} = 1029\,\text{MW}$. b) $E = 0.92 \cdot 1536.4 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W} \cdot 31\,536\,000\,\text{s} = 4.457 \cdot 10^{16}\,\text{J}$, so $N = \dfrac{4.457 \cdot 10^{16}\,\text{J}}{3.204 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{J}} = 1.391 \cdot 10^{27}$ and $m = 1.391 \cdot 10^{27} \cdot 3.9030 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg} = 543.0\,\text{kg}$. c) $m_\text{coal} = \dfrac{4.457 \cdot 10^{16}\,\text{J}}{24 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = 1.857 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{kg} = 1.86 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{t}$
**Answer:** $540\,\text{kg}$ as the result of part b); two significant figures, because the efficiency $0.33$ and the operating time $92\,\%$ are given with two figures. In part a) the thermal power is $1500\,\text{MW}$ and $1000\,\text{MW}$ is transferred to the cooling water (the same two significant figures). In part c) about $1.9$ million tonnes of coal would be needed: in nuclear reactions the energy is millions of electronvolts per atom and in combustion of the order of an electronvolt, so about $3.4$ million times as much mass of coal is needed as of uranium-235.
**Reasonableness:** According to the chapter, a gram of uranium-235 releases about $82\,\text{GJ}$. The energy $4.457 \cdot 10^{16}\,\text{J}$ divided by $8.2 \cdot 10^{10}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}}$ is about $5.4 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{g} = 540\,\text{kg}$, which matches. The power transferred to the cooling water is greater than the electrical power, which fits the efficiency $0.33$, because $1029 > 507$.
