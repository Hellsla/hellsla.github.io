Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies

Why does a plucked guitar string sound at a definite pitch, and why does the pitch rise when the string is tightened or its vibrating part is shortened by pressing it against the fingerboard?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • recognise the nodes and antinodes of a standing wave and explain how a standing wave forms from a wave and its reflection.
  • calculate the natural frequencies of a string and of an air column from the length and the wave speed.
  • compare the natural frequencies of a tube open at both ends and a tube closed at one end.

Prior knowledge

  • The speed, the frequency and the wavelength of a wave are related by $v = f\lambda$chapter How waves form and travel
  • A wave is reflected from the fixed end of a rope in the opposite phasechapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap
  • The displacements of waves that meet add up, and waves in opposite phase cancel each otherchapter When waves combine: superposition and interference

1Think about it

Two students experiment in the gym with a long skipping rope. One end of the rope is tied to a wall bar, and the other student shakes the free end up and down with small movements. With slow, uneven shaking the rope wriggles messily. When the shaking rate is just right, the rope settles into one calm arc that swings with a large amplitude. When the rate is increased, the arc breaks up, until at a new, faster rate the rope shows two arcs and, between them, a point that does not move at all.

Why does the rope settle into a calm pattern only at certain shaking rates, and what determines those rates?

Optional Recap: reflection from a fixed end
The right end of a rope is fixed to a wall. A single wave pulse above the rest position of the rope travels along the rope towards the wall.What kind of pulse returns from the wall?

  • Correct. At a fixed end the wave is reflected in the opposite phase (chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap): the wall pulls the rope in the direction opposite to the pulse, so the end stays in place. Today we investigate what happens when the incident and the reflected wave travel in the rope at the same time.

  • That is what happens at a free end, which is able to move. A fixed end does not move, so the wall pulls the rope in the opposite direction and the pulse is turned below the rest position.

  • A fixed end cannot take in energy, because it does not move. Therefore almost the whole pulse is reflected back, and it is turned below the rest position at the same time.

  • The incident and the reflected pulse add up only at the moment when they overlap near the wall. One pulse of the same size returns, turned below the rest position.

Assumptions of the model

The picture shows the steady state that forms after the driving has gone on for a long time: the wave travelling towards the right end and its reflection have added up. The motion is drawn greatly slowed down, because the real frequency is hundreds of hertz, and the displacements are magnified. The amplitude of the driving source itself is $1.0\,\text{mm}$ in the model for the string, and a small damping limits the greatest displacement to about $17\,\text{mm}$; in real instruments the ratio depends on the materials. In a tube the displacement means the displacement of the air along the tube, so the nodes and antinodes are displacement nodes and antinodes. At the open end of the tube the antinode at the natural frequencies is in the model exactly at the mouth of the tube (there is no end correction), and the speed of sound in air is $340\,\text{m/s}$. All numbers are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

The wavelength is $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$. When $s = L - x$ is the distance from the right end and $k = \tfrac{2\pi f}{v} - i\alpha$ is the wave number with a small damping $\alpha = \tfrac{0.06}{L}$, the amplitude of the displacement at the point $x$ is $U = a\tfrac{\sin ks}{\sin kL}$ for the string, $U = a\tfrac{\cos ks}{\sin kL}$ for the open tube and $U = a\tfrac{\sin ks}{\cos kL}$ for the closed tube, where $a = 1.0\,\text{mm}$. These satisfy the conditions at the ends: at a fixed and at a closed end the displacement is zero, and at an open end it is greatest. The greatest displacement $A$ is the greatest value of $|U|$ along the string or the tube. Nodes are points where $|U|$ is at its smallest and below $15\,\%$ of $A$, and antinodes points where it is at its greatest and above $85\,\%$ of $A$. The natural frequencies are not fed into the model: the displacement grows large by itself when the denominator $\sin kL$ or $\cos kL$ is almost zero.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A wave and its reflection

    When the end of a rope is shaken steadily, a wave travels along the rope towards its fixed end. At the fixed end the wave is reflected back in the opposite phase (chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap). The rope then carries two waves with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude but opposite directions of travel. Their displacements add up at every point (chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference). Here the reflection is assumed to be ideal, so that the wave loses no energy. The sum does not look like a travelling wave: certain points stay in place all the time, and between them the rope swings back and forth. This kind of wave motion is called a standing wave.

    Nodes and antinodes

    The points of a standing wave where the displacement is zero at every moment are called nodes. There the incident and the reflected wave are always in opposite phase and cancel each other. Halfway between the nodes the waves are always in phase and reinforce each other. At these antinodes the amplitude of the oscillation is greatest, twice the amplitude of a single wave. The distance between two adjacent nodes is half a wavelength, $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$, and from a node to the next antinode it is $\tfrac{\lambda}{4}$. In reality the reflected wave is slightly weaker than the incident one, so even the nodes vibrate a little.

    The part of the rope between two nodes moves as a whole in the same direction, and the neighbouring part always moves in the opposite direction. So no wave crest travels anywhere. Because the incident and the reflected wave carry the same amount of energy in each direction, a standing wave does not transfer energy along the rope.

    Natural frequencies

    Both ends of a guitar string are fixed, so both ends are nodes. A standing wave can form only if a whole number of half wavelengths fits on the length of the string. At other wavelengths the waves reflecting back and forth in the string are in different phases on different round trips and largely cancel each other. The frequencies at which a standing wave forms are called the natural frequencies of the string. When the string is driven at a natural frequency, every round trip of the reflection strengthens the motion and the amplitude grows large. This phenomenon is called resonance.

    The lowest natural frequency is the fundamental frequency. A plucked string vibrates at many of its natural frequencies at the same time, and the pitch we hear is determined by the fundamental frequency. The air column in a wind instrument behaves in the same way: its natural frequencies depend on the length of the tube and on whether the end of the tube is open or closed.

    Natural frequencies of a string and of an air column

    A string fixed at both ends

    When the length of the vibrating part of the string is $L$, there is a node at each end, and $n$ half wavelengths fit on the string:

    $$\begin{aligned} L &= n \cdot \frac{\lambda_n}{2} \\ \lambda_n &= \frac{2L}{n} \end{aligned}$$

    The speed, the frequency and the wavelength of a wave are related by $v = f\lambda$ (chapter How waves form and travel). From this we get the natural frequencies of the string

    $$\begin{aligned} f_n &= \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = n \cdot \frac{v}{2L} = n f_1 \\ n &= 1, 2, 3, \ldots \end{aligned}$$

    where $f_n$ is the $n$th natural frequency (Hz), $v$ the speed of the wave in the string (m/s), $L$ the length of the vibrating part of the string (m) and $\lambda_n$ the wavelength (m). The fundamental frequency is $f_1 = \tfrac{v}{2L}$, and the other natural frequencies are its multiples. The natural frequencies above the fundamental frequency are called overtones. At the natural frequency $f_n$ the string has $n$ antinodes and $n + 1$ nodes, counting the ends.

    An instrument and its pitch

    The formula tells how the pitch of a string instrument is changed. When the player presses the string against the fingerboard, the vibrating part $L$ becomes shorter and the fundamental frequency increases. When the string is tightened with the tuning peg, the speed $v$ of the wave in the string increases and the fundamental frequency rises. At the same tension a thick, heavy string carries a wave more slowly than a thin one, which is why bass strings sound low. The vibrating string makes the body of the instrument and the air vibrate at the same frequency, so the frequency of the sound we hear is the frequency of the string's vibration.

    An air column in a tube

    In a flute or an organ pipe the standing wave forms in the air inside the tube. Sound is longitudinal wave motion, so the air oscillates along the tube. At the closed end of the tube the air cannot move, so there is a displacement node. At the open end the air can move freely, and there is a displacement antinode. In reality the antinode is slightly outside the mouth of the tube, but this so-called end correction is neglected here. In this chapter the speed of sound in air is taken to be $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$.

    When the tube is open at both ends, there is an antinode at each end, and again $n$ half wavelengths fit in the tube. The natural frequencies are the same as for a string, $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{2L}$, where $v$ is the speed of sound in air.

    When the tube is closed at one end, there is a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end. From a node to an antinode is $\tfrac{\lambda}{4}$, so the length of the tube must be an odd number of quarter wavelengths:

    $$\begin{aligned} L &= n \cdot \frac{\lambda_n}{4} \\ f_n &= n \cdot \frac{v}{4L} \\ n &= 1, 3, 5, \ldots \end{aligned}$$

    A closed tube therefore has only the odd natural frequencies $f_1$, $3f_1$, $5f_1$ and so on. Its fundamental frequency $f_1 = \tfrac{v}{4L}$ is half the fundamental frequency of an open tube of the same length, so a closed organ pipe sounds an octave lower than an open pipe of the same length.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that at the fundamental frequency a whole wavelength fits on the string, that is, $\lambda_1 = L$. At the fundamental frequency, however, the string has only one antinode in the middle and nodes at the ends, so the length of the string is half a wavelength and $\lambda_1 = 2L$. For example, the wavelength corresponding to the fundamental frequency of a $1.20\,\text{m}$ string is $2.40\,\text{m}$. For the same reason the distance between adjacent nodes is $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$, not $\lambda$.

    Another mistake is to calculate the natural frequencies of a tube closed at one end in the same way as for a string. The fundamental frequency of a closed tube is $\tfrac{v}{4L}$, and its next natural frequency is $3f_1$, not $2f_1$.

    Summary

    • A standing wave forms when a wave and its reflection travel in opposite directions and add up. At the nodes the displacement is always zero, and at the antinodes the amplitude is greatest.
    • The distance between adjacent nodes is $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$, and from a node to an antinode it is $\tfrac{\lambda}{4}$.
    • The natural frequencies of a string fixed at both ends and of a tube open at both ends are $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{2L}$, where $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ The lowest of them is the fundamental frequency $f_1$.
    • In a tube closed at one end the closed end is a node and the open end an antinode, and the natural frequencies are $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{4L}$, where $n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$
    • A shorter or tighter string and a shorter tube sound higher. The chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect examines how the fundamental frequency and the overtones show up in the frequency analysis of a sound.

      Example 1: A guitar string

      The vibrating part of a guitar's A string, from the nut to the bridge, is $0.650\,\text{m}$ long, and the fundamental frequency of the string is $110\,\text{Hz}$. What is the speed of the wave in the string? How short must the vibrating part be made by pressing the string against the fingerboard so that the fundamental frequency is $165\,\text{Hz}$? The tension of the string does not change when it is pressed.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The length of the string is $L = 0.650\,\text{m}$ and the fundamental frequency is $f_1 = 110\,\text{Hz}$; the new fundamental frequency is $f_1' = 165\,\text{Hz}$. We want the speed $v$ of the wave in the string and the new length $L'$ of the vibrating part.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The string is fixed at both ends, so at the fundamental frequency half a wavelength fits on its length and $f_1 = \tfrac{v}{2L}$. When the string is pressed against the fingerboard, its tension and thickness stay the same, so the speed $v$ of the wave does not change. Only the length of the vibrating part changes.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. From the formula for the fundamental frequency we first solve $v$ and then the new length $L'$:

      $$\begin{aligned} f_1 &= \frac{v}{2L} \;\Rightarrow\; v = 2 L f_1 \\ f_1' &= \frac{v}{2L'} \;\Rightarrow\; L' = \frac{v}{2 f_1'} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. None are needed: the length is in metres and the frequency in hertz, and $\text{m} \cdot \text{Hz} = \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} &v = 2 L f_1 \\ &= 2 \cdot 0.650\,\text{m} \cdot 110\,\text{Hz} \\ &= 143\,\text{m/s} \\[6pt] &L' = \frac{v}{2 f_1'} \\ &= \frac{143\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 165\,\text{Hz}} \\ &= 0.4333\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The speed of the wave in the string is $143\,\text{m/s}$, and the vibrating part must be $0.433\,\text{m}$ long. The results are given to three significant figures, like the length of the string $0.650\,\text{m}$ and the frequencies.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The new fundamental frequency is $165\,\text{Hz} : 110\,\text{Hz} = 1.5$ times the old one, and since $f_1$ is inversely proportional to the length, the string must become shorter in the same ratio: $0.650\,\text{m} : 1.5 = 0.433\,\text{m}$. A higher note needs a shorter string, as is observed when playing. At the fundamental frequency $\lambda_1 = 2L = 1.30\,\text{m}$, so

      $f_1 \lambda_1$$= 110\,\text{Hz} \cdot 1.30\,\text{m}$
      $= 143\,\text{m/s}$

      which is the same as the calculated speed of the wave.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      A student calculates the fundamental frequency of an organ pipe closed at its upper end when the length of the pipe is $0.85\,\text{m}$ and the speed of sound $340\,\text{m/s}$:

      $$\begin{aligned} f_1 &= \frac{v}{2L} \\ &= \frac{340\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 0.85\,\text{m}} = 200\,\text{Hz} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • Correct. The formula $\tfrac{v}{2L}$ holds when the condition at both ends is the same, a node or an antinode. At the fundamental frequency of a closed pipe only a quarter of a wavelength fits in the pipe, so $\lambda_1 = 4L = 3.4\,\text{m}$ and $f_1 = 100\,\text{Hz}$.

      • The speed of sound in air does not depend on the direction in which the sound travels. The travelling back and forth already shows in the relation between the length and the wavelength, and in a closed pipe it leads to the condition $L = \tfrac{\lambda}{4}$, which makes the frequency lower, $100\,\text{Hz}$.

      • The length in metres and the speed in metres per second give hertz directly, so no conversion is needed. The error is in the formula: the fundamental frequency of a closed pipe is $\tfrac{v}{4L} = 100\,\text{Hz}$, because there is a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end.

      • The end of the pipe decides where the node is and where the antinode. At the closed end the air cannot move, so there is a node, and at the fundamental frequency only a quarter of a wavelength fits in the pipe. Therefore $f_1 = \tfrac{v}{4L} = 100\,\text{Hz}$.

      A didgeridoo is a long wooden tube that the player blows into with the lips pressed against the mouth of the tube. The lips close this end of the tube, so the instrument works roughly as a tube closed at one end. The length of the tube is $1.50\,\text{m}$.

      Hint 1: a guiding question

      At which end of the tube is the node and at which the antinode? How large a part of a wavelength fits in the tube at the fundamental frequency?

      Show the solution

      Given

      tube closed at one end, length $L = 1.50\,\text{m}$; speed of sound in air $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$

      Find

      the second lowest natural frequency in hertz

      Principle

      The lips close the end of the tube, so the air cannot move there and there is a displacement node; at the open end there is an antinode. Therefore only an odd number of quarter wavelengths fits in the tube, and the natural frequencies are odd multiples of the fundamental frequency. The second lowest corresponds to $n = 3$.

      Formula

      $f_n = n \cdot \dfrac{v}{4L}$, where $n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$, so for $n = 3$: $f_3 = \dfrac{3v}{4L}$

      Conversions

      None are needed: the length is in metres and the speed in metres per second.

      Substitution

      $f_3$$= \dfrac{3 \cdot 340\,\text{m/s}}{4 \cdot 1.50\,\text{m}}$
      $= \dfrac{1020\,\text{m/s}}{6.00\,\text{m}}$

      $170\,\text{Hz}$

      Three significant figures as in the length of the tube $1.50\,\text{m}$ and the speed of sound.

      Reasonableness

      The fundamental frequency is $\tfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{6.00\,\text{m}} = 56.7\,\text{Hz}$, which suits the low, humming sound of a didgeridoo, and $3 \cdot 56.7\,\text{Hz} = 170\,\text{Hz}$. At this frequency three quarters of a wavelength fit in the tube:

      $\lambda$$= \tfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{170\,\text{Hz}}$
      $= 2.00\,\text{m}$

      $\tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 2.00\,\text{m} = 1.50\,\text{m}$.

      A strong growth of the amplitude when the frequency of an external drive matches the system's natural frequency.

        The vibrating part of the lowest string of a double bass is $1.05\,\text{m}$ long, and a wave travels in the string at the speed $86.1\,\text{m/s}$. The string is fixed at both ends.

        What is the fundamental frequency of the string?

        Hz
        Answer

        $41.0\,\text{Hz}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        length of the vibrating part of the string $L = 1.05\,\text{m}$, speed of the wave in the string $v = 86.1\,\text{m/s}$; the string is fixed at both ends

        Find

        the fundamental frequency $f_1$ of the string in hertz

        Principle

        There is a node at each fixed end. At the fundamental frequency the string has one antinode, so half a wavelength fits on the string: $\lambda_1 = 2L$. The frequency follows from the basic wave equation $v = f\lambda$.

        Formula

        $f_n = n \cdot \dfrac{v}{2L}$, so at the fundamental frequency $n = 1$: $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{2L}$

        Conversions

        None are needed: the length is in metres and the speed in metres per second, so

        $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{m}}$$= \tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
        $= \text{Hz}$

        Substitution

        $f_1$$= \dfrac{86.1\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 1.05\,\text{m}}$
        $= \dfrac{86.1\,\text{m/s}}{2.10\,\text{m}}$
        $= 41.0\,\text{Hz}$

        Answer

        $41.0\,\text{Hz}$

        Three significant figures as in the length $1.05\,\text{m}$ and the speed $86.1\,\text{m/s}$.

        Reasonableness

        The lowest note of a double bass is very low, about $40\,\text{Hz}$, and the result matches it. A check the other way round: the wavelength is

        $\lambda_1$$= 2.10\,\text{m}$
        $f_1 \lambda_1$$= 41.0\,\text{Hz} \cdot 2.10\,\text{m}$
        $= 86.1\,\text{m/s}$

        which is the given speed.

        Answer

        Principle

        At the natural frequencies of a string fixed at both ends, $n$ half wavelengths fit on the string and the string has $n$ antinodes. The natural frequencies are multiples of the fundamental frequency, because the speed of the wave and the length of the string stay the same.

        Reasonableness

        Four antinodes mean that the wavelength is a quarter of the wavelength at the fundamental frequency. Since $v = f\lambda$ stays the same, the frequency must be four times as high. The frequency $392\,\text{Hz}$ is two octaves above the fundamental frequency, because $98.0\,\text{Hz}$ doubled twice is $392\,\text{Hz}$.

        Show the solution

        Find

        which statement describes an antinode of a standing wave correctly

        A standing wave forms when waves travelling in opposite directions add up.         The vibrating part of a guitar string is $0.650\,\text{m}$ long. When the guitarist tightens the string with the tuning peg, the speed of the wave in the string increases from $141.0\,\text{m/s}$ to $148.0\,\text{m/s}$.

        By how many hertz does the fundamental frequency of the string rise?

        Hz
        Answer

        $5.4\,\text{Hz}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        length of the vibrating part of the string $L = 0.650\,\text{m}$; speed of the wave before tightening $v_1 = 141.0\,\text{m/s}$ and after it $v_2 = 148.0\,\text{m/s}$

        Find

        the change in the fundamental frequency $\Delta f = f_\text{2} - f_\text{1}$ in hertz

        Principle

        The string is fixed at both ends, so at the fundamental frequency $f = \tfrac{v}{2L}$. Tightening changes only the speed of the wave; the length of the vibrating part of the string stays the same. The fundamental frequency is therefore directly proportional to the speed.

        Formula

        $f = \dfrac{v}{2L}$, so

        $\Delta f$$= \dfrac{v_2}{2L} - \dfrac{v_1}{2L}$

        giving $\Delta f = \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{2L}$

        Conversions

        None are needed: the speeds are in metres per second and the length in metres.

        Substitution

        $\Delta f$$= \dfrac{148.0\,\text{m/s} - 141.0\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 0.650\,\text{m}}$
        $= \dfrac{7.0\,\text{m/s}}{1.300\,\text{m}}$
        $= 5.385\,\text{Hz}$

        Answer

        $5.4\,\text{Hz}$

        Two significant figures, because the difference of the speeds $7.0\,\text{m/s}$ is known to two figures.

        Reasonableness

        The fundamental frequencies are $108.46\,\text{Hz}$ before and $113.85\,\text{Hz}$ after, and their difference is the same $5.39\,\text{Hz} \approx 5.4\,\text{Hz}$. The speed increases by about $5\,\%$, so the frequency also increases by about $5\,\%$: $0.05 \cdot 108\,\text{Hz} \approx 5\,\text{Hz}$. The frequency rises, as is heard when a string is tightened.

        The fundamental frequency of an organ pipe open at both ends is $262\,\text{Hz}$. The organ builder closes the upper end of the pipe with a plug.

        Hz
        Answer

        Show the solution

        Given

        fundamental frequency of the open organ pipe $f_\text{o} = 262\,\text{Hz}$; the length of the pipe $L$ and the speed of sound $v$ stay the same when the upper end is closed

        Find

        the fundamental frequency $f_\text{c}$ of the closed pipe in hertz

        In an open pipe there is a displacement antinode at each end, so at the fundamental frequency $L = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}$. The same length therefore corresponds to a wavelength twice as long, and since the speed of sound is the same, the frequency is halved.

        $f_\text{o} = \dfrac{v}{2L}$ and $f_\text{c} = \dfrac{v}{4L}$, so dividing,

        $\dfrac{f_\text{c}}{f_\text{o}}$$= \dfrac{2L}{4L}$
        $= \dfrac{1}{2}$

        Conversions

        None are needed: the length and the speed are not needed as numbers, because they cancel.

        Substitution

        $= 131\,\text{Hz}$

        $131\,\text{Hz}$

        Three significant figures as in the fundamental frequency $262\,\text{Hz}$.

        A halved frequency is a note an octave lower, as it should be for a stopped pipe.       Optional 4. Explanation task: Pitch

      Using a standing wave, explain why the note of a guitar rises when the string is pressed against the fingerboard, and why it also rises when the string is tightened.

      Model answer

      Both ends of a guitar string are fixed, so the ends are nodes, and at the fundamental frequency the length of the vibrating part of the string is half a wavelength: $\lambda_1 = 2L$. When the string is pressed against the fingerboard, the vibrating part becomes shorter, so the wavelength also becomes shorter. The speed of the wave in the string does not change, so according to the equation $v = f\lambda$ the frequency increases: $f_1 = \tfrac{v}{2L}$ is inversely proportional to the length. When the string is tightened, the wavelength stays the same, but the wave travels faster in the tighter string. A greater $v$ at the same wavelength means a greater frequency. The note we hear rises in both cases, because the pitch is determined by the fundamental frequency.
