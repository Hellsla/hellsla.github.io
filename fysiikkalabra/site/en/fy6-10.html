Electric charge and Coulomb's law

Why does a balloon rubbed on a wool sweater stick to a wall, although the wall has no electric charge?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain the charging of objects and induction as the transfer and conservation of charge.
  • calculate the force between two point charges with Coulomb's law and work out its direction.
  • describe the electric field of a point charge with field lines.

Prior knowledge

  • Electric current is moving charge, $I = Q : t$, and the charge of an electron is $-e$chapter Electric circuit, current and voltage
  • A force and its reaction force are equal in magnitude and opposite in directionchapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws

Rub a balloon on a wool sweater for a moment and hold it against a wall. The balloon stays stuck to the wall without tape and stays there for a long time. In the same way, a plastic comb rubbed on hair lifts small bits of paper from a table. The wall and the paper are not charged, and nothing has been rubbed on them.

Prediction 3: why does the balloon stick to the wall?

Four students explain why a negatively charged balloon stays stuck to a neutral wall. Whose explanation is correct?

  • Exactly. The wall is an insulator, so the charges cannot move far, but every molecule becomes polarised. Because the electric force weakens as the distance grows, the pull of the nearby positive ends beats the repulsion of the more distant negative ends.

  • Rubbing does not make the balloon sticky: the balloon comes off easily when its charge leaks away, for example because of damp air. What holds the balloon to the wall is the electric force that arises when the charge of the balloon polarises the molecules of the wall.

  • The electric force is not like gravity, which pulls on all objects: like charges repel each other. The neutral wall is drawn towards the balloon only because the charge of the balloon shifts the charges in the wall's molecules.

  • A negative balloon repels electrons and does not attract them, and no charge moves from the wall to the balloon. The wall stays neutral as a whole: its molecules become polarised, and the positive ends are closer to the balloon than the negative ones.

Assumptions of the model

The spheres are small compared with their distance, so in the model they are point charges in air, and their charges stay in place. In real spheres the charge shifts slightly on the surface when they are close together because of induction, so at very short distances the force differs from the model's value. The model also has no induction: when the charge of one sphere is zero, there is no force, although a real uncharged metal sphere would move towards a charged one. How the calculation works

The magnitude of the force is calculated with Coulomb's law

$$F = k\,\frac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$$

The same $F$ acts on both spheres in opposite directions: away from the other sphere for charges of the same sign and towards the other sphere for charges of opposite signs. The field lines are drawn in small steps along the direction of the common field of the two point charges.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Electric charge and elementary charge

    The atoms of matter contain positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons (chapter Electric circuit, current and voltage). Electric charge is a property of particles that makes them act on each other through the electric interaction: charges of the same sign repel each other and charges of opposite signs attract each other. The symbol of charge is $Q$ or $q$, and its unit is the coulomb $\text{C}$. The charge of a proton is $+e$ and that of an electron $-e$, where $e$ is the elementary charge, the smallest charge that occurs free. A neutron has no charge.

    An ordinary object has as many protons as electrons, so its total charge is zero: the object is electrically neutral. An object is charged when it has more or fewer electrons than protons. Charge is always made of whole elementary charges:

    $$Q = n e$$

    where $Q$ is the magnitude of the charge of the object ($\text{C}$), $n$ is the number of extra or missing electrons and $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ is the elementary charge. The coulomb is a large charge, since it takes $6.24 \cdot 10^{18}$ elementary charges. The charge of a balloon charged by rubbing is typically tens of nanocoulombs: a charge of $-50\,\text{nC}$ means about $3.1 \cdot 10^{11}$ extra electrons.

    Conservation of charge

    Charge is neither created nor destroyed. The total charge of an isolated system, that is, the sum of all its charges, stays the same, even though charge moves from one object to another. This is the law of conservation of charge. When a balloon is rubbed on wool, some of the electrons of the wool move to the surface of the balloon. The balloon gets a negative charge, and the wool is left with an equal positive charge, because it is missing the same number of electrons. In solids it is the electrons that move; the protons stay in the nuclei of the atoms.

    Charging objects

    An object can be charged in three ways.

    • Rubbing. Two different materials are rubbed against each other, and electrons move from the material that holds them more weakly to the other material. The objects become equally charged with opposite signs.
    • Contact. A charged object touches a conductor, and some of the charge moves to the conductor. Two identical metal spheres share their common charge equally in contact: if the charges are first $+12\,\text{nC}$ and $0\,\text{nC}$, the charge of each is $+6\,\text{nC}$ after the contact.
    • Induction. A charged object is brought near a conductor without touching it. The free electrons of the conductor move, so a charge of opposite sign appears on the near side of the conductor and a charge of the same sign on the far side. The total charge of the conductor does not change. However, if the conductor is connected to the ground for a moment with a wire, some electrons move to the ground or from the ground to the conductor, and the conductor is left charged.

    Why a charged object attracts a neutral object

    Induction explains why a charged object also attracts a neutral object. The charge of opposite sign on a metal sphere is nearer to the charged rod than the charge of the same sign. The electric force weakens as the distance grows, so the attraction is greater than the repulsion, and the sphere is drawn towards the rod.

    In an insulator the electrons cannot move from one object to another, but the charges in each molecule shift slightly: the molecule becomes polarised. Next to a negatively charged balloon, the positive ends of the molecules in the wall turn towards the balloon. The wall stays neutral, but the positive ends nearby attract the balloon more than the negative ends farther away repel it. That is why the balloon stays on the wall and the comb lifts bits of paper.

    Electric current is moving charge

    When charge moves from one place to another, an electric current flows. In a conductor the current is the charge that passes through the cross-section in a unit of time, $I = Q : t$ (chapter Electric circuit, current and voltage). The same holds for a spark and for lightning. When you touch a door handle on a dry winter day, a charge, typically under a microcoulomb, is discharged from your body in a spark. The discharge lasts only about a microsecond, so the current is for a moment of the order of an ampere, although the charge that moves is small.

    There is an electric field around a charge

    A charged object acts on another charged object without touching it. This is described with an electric field: a charge changes the space around it so that an electric force acts on another charge there. The field is illustrated with field lines. The direction of a field line is the direction in which the force on a small positive test charge points; the force on a negative charge points against the field line.

    A point charge is a charged object whose size is small compared with the distances considered. From a positive point charge the field lines go out radially, and into a negative point charge they come in radially. Near the charge the lines are close together and the field is strong; farther away the lines spread out and the field weakens. The strength of the field is handled with calculations in the chapter Uniform electric field.

    Coulomb's law

    The magnitude of the electric force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. This is Coulomb's law:

    $$F = k\,\frac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$$

    where $F$ is the magnitude of the force ($\text{N}$), $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges ($\text{C}$), $r$ is the distance between the charges ($\text{m}$) and $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$ is the constant of Coulomb's law. The law holds for point charges and for uniformly charged spheres, whose distance is measured between the centres. The value of the constant holds in a vacuum; in air the force is almost the same.

    Force and reaction force

    Coulomb's law gives the magnitude of the force. The direction of the force is worked out from the signs of the charges: the forces lie on the line between the charges, and charges of the same sign repel and charges of opposite signs attract each other. The charge $q_1$ acts on the charge $q_2$ with the force $\overline{F}_{12}$, and the charge $q_2$ acts on the charge $q_1$ with the force $\overline{F}_{21}$. They are a force and its reaction force, $\overline{F}_{12} = -\overline{F}_{21}$ (chapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws), so they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, also when the charges are of different sizes. In the formula of the law the charges appear symmetrically, so the same $F$ acts on both charges.

    The square of the distance

    The force depends strongly on the distance. When the distance doubles, the denominator $r^2$ becomes four times as large and the force drops to a quarter. At three times the distance the force is a ninth and at four times the distance a sixteenth. On the charges, in contrast, the force is directly proportional: when one charge doubles, the force doubles. In the graph each charge is $20\,\text{nC}$. At a distance of $3\,\text{cm}$ the force is $4.0\,\text{mN}$ and at a distance of $6\,\text{cm}$ only $1.0\,\text{mN}$.

    The forces of several charges

    When several other charges act on a charge, the force of each charge is calculated separately with Coulomb's law, as if the others were not there. The net force on the charge is the vector sum of these forces:

    $$\overline{F} = \overline{F}_1 + \overline{F}_2$$

    where $\overline{F}$ is the net force ($\text{N}$) and $\overline{F}_1$ and $\overline{F}_2$ are the forces that two other charges exert on it ($\text{N}$). When all the charges are on the same line, forces in the same direction are added and forces in opposite directions are subtracted from each other.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a larger charge acts on a smaller one with a greater force than the smaller acts on the larger. The forces between the charges are, however, a force and its reaction force: they are always equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Coulomb's law contains the product of the charges, so doubling both charges quadruples the same force that acts on both.

    Another mistake is to think that no electric force acts on a neutral object. A charged object attracts a neutral object, because induction or polarisation brings the charge of opposite sign nearer than the charge of the same sign. A third mistake is to believe that rubbing creates charge. Rubbing only moves electrons from one object to another, and the total charge is conserved.

    Summary

    • Charge is a multiple of the elementary charge, $Q = ne$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. Charges of the same sign repel and charges of opposite signs attract each other.
    • Charge is conserved: in rubbing and contact, electrons only move from one object to another. Two identical metal spheres share their common charge equally in contact.
    • In induction a charged object moves the charges of a conductor, and in an insulator it polarises the molecules, so a charged object attracts a neutral object.
    • Electric current is moving charge, $I = Q : t$.
    • The field lines of a point charge are radial: outwards from a positive charge and inwards into a negative one, and the field weakens as the distance grows.
    • Coulomb's law $F = k\,\tfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$: double the distance gives a quarter of the force. A force and its reaction force are equal, and the forces of several charges are added as vectors.

      Worked example

      Problem

      Two small metal spheres are on insulating rods so that the distance between the centres of the spheres is $6.0\,\text{cm}$. The charge of one sphere is $+12\,\text{nC}$ and that of the other $-8.0\,\text{nC}$. How large is the electric force between the spheres, and do the spheres attract or repel each other?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $q_1 = +12\,\text{nC}$, $q_2 = -8.0\,\text{nC}$ and $r = 6.0\,\text{cm}$. We are asked for the magnitude of the force $F$ and whether the spheres attract or repel each other.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The spheres are small compared with their distance, so they can be treated as point charges, and Coulomb's law gives the magnitude of the force. The direction of the force is worked out from the signs: charges of opposite signs attract each other. An equal force acts on each sphere.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$F = k\,\frac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$$

      Step 4/7

      Conversions.

      $q_1$$= 12\,\text{nC}$
      $= 12 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$
      $\lvert q_2 \rvert$$= 8.0\,\text{nC}$
      $= 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$
      $r$$= 6.0\,\text{cm}$
      $= 6.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.060\,\text{m}$

      The absolute value of the product of the charges is

      $\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert$$= 12 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$
      $= 9.6 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{C}^2$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} F &= k\,\frac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2} \\ &= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{9.6 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{C}^2}{(0.060\,\text{m})^2} \\ &= 2.397 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The force is $2.4 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N} = 0.24\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures like the charge $8.0\,\text{nC}$ and the distance $6.0\,\text{cm}$. The spheres attract each other, because their charges have opposite signs.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. With rounded numbers $10^{10} \cdot 10^{-16} : 0.004 \approx 2.5 \cdot 10^{-4}$, so the order of magnitude is right. The force is equal to the weight of a mass of about $24\,\text{mg}$, so it is small, but it is enough to move light objects. At half the distance the force would be four times as large, $0.96\,\text{mN}$.

      Find the error

      The charges of two small spheres are $5.0\,\text{nC}$, and the distance between their centres is $2.0\,\text{cm}$.A student calculates the force between the spheres like this:

      $$\begin{aligned} F &= k\,\frac{q^2}{r^2} \\ &= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{(5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C})^2}{2.0^2} \\ &= 5.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • Coulomb's law contains the product of the charges $q_1 q_2$. When the charges are equal, the product is $(5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C})^2$, as in the calculation. The error is in the unit of the distance.

      • In Coulomb's law the distance is raised to the second power, $F = k\,\tfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$, so the square belongs in the calculation. The error is in the unit: centimetres must be converted to metres before substituting.

      • Correct. The unit of the constant $k$ contains $\text{m}^2$, so the distance must be substituted in metres: $r = 2.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.020\,\text{m}$. Then the denominator is $(0.020\,\text{m})^2 = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$ and not $4.0$, and the force is $10\,000$ times as large, $5.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$. The error can also be seen from the units: the denominator is missing $\text{m}^2$.

      • There is an error in the calculation: the distance was substituted in centimetres, although the unit of the constant $k$ contains metres. The correct denominator is $(0.020\,\text{m})^2$, and the force is $5.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$.

        The object is missing two electrons, and that is why it is positive.

      2. Both charges of two point charges are doubled, and at the same time the distance between them is doubled. How does the force between them change?
        Answer

        The force does not change. The product of the charges becomes four times as large, and the denominator $r^2$ of Coulomb's law also becomes four times as large, so they cancel: $\tfrac{2 \cdot 2}{2^2} = 1$.

      1. Concept

      A plastic comb is pulled through dry hair, and the comb becomes negatively charged. What happens to the hair?

      • Correct. In rubbing, electrons move from the hair to the comb. Charge is conserved, so the hair is left with a positive charge equal to the negative charge the comb gained. That is why the hair rises towards the comb.

      • If both were negative, the total charge would have grown out of nothing. The electrons moved from the hair to the comb, so the hair is missing electrons and is positive.

      • Rubbing does not create charge. The negative charge of the comb is electrons that moved from the hair, so the hair is left with an equal positive charge.

      • In solids the protons stay in the nuclei of the atoms. The hair is positive because electrons moved from it to the comb.

      2. Read the graph

      The graph shows the force $F$ between two identical, equally charged small spheres as a function of the distance $r$ between them. Determine the charge of the spheres from the graph. Give the answer in nanocoulombs.

      nC
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Choose a point on the graph whose values you can read accurately from the grid.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      At the point $r = 3.0\,\text{cm}$ the force is $9.0\,\text{mN}$. When the charges are equal, Coulomb's law is $F = k\,\tfrac{q^2}{r^2}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Solve $q = r\sqrt{\tfrac{F}{k}}$ and substitute $r = 0.030\,\text{m}$ and $F = 9.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      the points of the graph; the spheres are identical and equally charged, $q_1 = q_2 = q$; $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$

      Find

      the charge of the spheres $q$ in nanocoulombs

      Principle

      The spheres are small compared with the distance, so the force between them comes from Coulomb's law. When the charges are equal, the product of the charges is $q^2$, and $r$ and $F$ of one point read from the graph are enough to solve the charge.

      Reading the graph

      The point $r = 3.0\,\text{cm}$ is on a vertical grid line, and the force there is on the horizontal line of $9.0\,\text{mN}$. The largest force was chosen because its relative reading uncertainty is the smallest.

      Formula

      $F = k\,\dfrac{q^2}{r^2}$, so $q = r\sqrt{\dfrac{F}{k}}$

      Conversions

      $r$$= 3.0\,\text{cm}$
      $= 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.030\,\text{m}$
      $F$$= 9.0\,\text{mN}$
      $= 9.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$

      finally $1\,\text{C} = 10^{9}\,\text{nC}$.

      Substitution

      $\dfrac{F}{k}$$= \dfrac{9.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}}{8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}}$
      $= 1.001 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\tfrac{\text{C}^2}{\text{m}^2}$
      $q$$= 0.030\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{1.001 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\tfrac{\text{C}^2}{\text{m}^2}}$
      $= 3.00 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{C}$
      $= 30.0\,\text{nC}$

      Answer

      $30\,\text{nC}$

      Two significant figures, because the force $9.0\,\text{mN}$ is read from the graph with two significant figures.

      Reasonableness

      Checked with another point, $k\,\tfrac{q^2}{r^2}$ gives at a distance of $9.0\,\text{cm}$ the force

      $8.988 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot (3.0 \cdot 10^{-8})^2 : 0.090^2\,\text{N}$
      $= 1.0\,\text{mN}$

      as in the graph. At three times the distance the force is a ninth.

      Optional 3. Explain induction

      A negatively charged plastic rod is brought near an empty aluminium drink can lying on its side on a table. The can starts to roll towards the rod, although it is uncharged and the rod does not touch it. Explain why.

      Model answer

      Aluminium is a conductor with free electrons. The negative charge of the rod repels them, so electrons move to the side of the can farther from the rod. A positive charge is left on the side facing the rod and an equal negative charge on the far side, so the can is still neutral as a whole. This is called induction. The positive charge is nearer to the rod than the negative one, and the electric force weakens as the distance grows. So the attraction of the rod on the positive side of the can is greater than the repulsion on the negative side, and a net force towards the rod acts on the can.

        1. Exercise 10-6Core

          Two small spheres, each with a charge of $+30\,\text{nC}$, repel each other with a force of $2.0\,\text{mN}$.

          How far apart are the centres of the spheres? Give the answer in centimetres.

          cm
          Answer

          $6.4\,\text{cm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          charges $q_1 = q_2 = +30\,\text{nC}$, force $F = 2.0\,\text{mN}$; $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$

          Find

          the distance between the centres of the spheres $r$ in centimetres

          Principle

          The spheres are small, so Coulomb's law holds when the distance is measured between the centres. The distance is solved from the law: the force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, so the distance comes as a square root.

          Formula

          $F$$= k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$

          so

          $r$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{k \lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{F}}$

          Conversions

          $q_1$$= q_2$
          $= 30 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$
          $F$$= 2.0\,\text{mN}$
          $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$

          finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.

          Substitution

          $r^2$$= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \dfrac{(30 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C})^2}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}}$
          $= 4.045 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2$

          so

          $r$$= \sqrt{4.045 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= 0.0636\,\text{m}$
          $= 6.36\,\text{cm}$

          Answer

          $6.4\,\text{cm}$

          Two significant figures like the charges and the force.

          Reasonableness

          Substituting back:

          $8.988 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 9.0 \cdot 10^{-16} : 0.0636^2\,\text{N}$
          $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$

          In addition, spheres of $20\,\text{nC}$ at a distance of $6.0\,\text{cm}$ repel each other with a force of $1.0\,\text{mN}$, so with larger charges a force twice as large at the same distance is believable.

        1. Exercise 10-10

          A point charge $q_1 = +20\,\text{nC}$ is at $x = 0$ and a point charge $q_2 = -20\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 10\,\text{cm}$. A point charge $q_3 = +5.0\,\text{nC}$ is brought to the midpoint between them, to $x = 5.0\,\text{cm}$.

          How large is the net force on the charge $q_3$? Give the answer in millinewtons.

          mN
          Answer

          $0.72\,\text{mN}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $q_1 = +20\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 0$, $q_2 = -20\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 10\,\text{cm}$, $q_3 = +5.0\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 5.0\,\text{cm}$; $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$

          Find

          the magnitude of the net force $F$ on the charge $q_3$ in millinewtons

          Principle

          The force of each charge is calculated separately with Coulomb's law, and the net force is their vector sum. The positive $q_1$ repels the positive $q_3$ in the positive $x$-direction, and the negative $q_2$ attracts $q_3$ towards itself, also in the positive $x$-direction. The forces are in the same direction, so their magnitudes are added. The distances and the magnitudes of the charges are the same, so the forces are equal.

          Formula

          $F_1$$= k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_1 q_3 \rvert}{r^2}$
          $F_2$$= k\,\dfrac{\lvert q_2 q_3 \rvert}{r^2}$

          $F = F_1 + F_2$

          Conversions

          $r = 5.0\,\text{cm} = 0.050\,\text{m}$, $1\,\text{nC} = 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$ and finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.

          Substitution

          $F_1$$= F_2$
          $= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \dfrac{20 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.050\,\text{m})^2}$
          $= 3.595 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$
          $F$$= 2 \cdot 3.595 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$
          $= 7.190 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$
          $= 0.7190\,\text{mN}$

          Answer

          $0.72\,\text{mN}$

          In the positive $x$-direction, that is, towards the charge $q_2$, two significant figures like the charge $5.0\,\text{nC}$ and the distance.

          Reasonableness

          If $q_2$ were positive, the forces would be opposite and would cancel. Between charges of opposite signs the forces reinforce each other, so the net force is twice the force of one charge.

          the ratio of the forces $F_\text{e} : F_\text{g}$ and why it does not depend on the distance

          Principle

          The charge of an electron is $-e$ and that of a proton $+e$, so the electric force comes from Coulomb's law. Both forces are inversely proportional to the square of the distance, so $r^2$ cancels in the ratio, and the ratio is the same at all distances.

          Formula

          $F_\text{e}$$= k\,\dfrac{e^2}{r^2}$
          $F_\text{g}$$= G\,\dfrac{m_\text{e} m_\text{p}}{r^2}$

          so

          $\dfrac{F_\text{e}}{F_\text{g}}$$= \dfrac{k e^2}{G m_\text{e} m_\text{p}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: all the quantities are in base units, and the units cancel, since

          $\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \text{C}^2$$= \tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot \text{kg}^2$

          Substitution

          $k e^2$$= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot (1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C})^2$
          $= 2.307 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2$
          $G m_\text{e} m_\text{p}$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$
          $= 1.017 \cdot 10^{-67}\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2$

          so

          $\dfrac{F_\text{e}}{F_\text{g}}$$= \dfrac{2.307 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}{1.017 \cdot 10^{-67}\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2}$
          $= 2.27 \cdot 10^{39}$

          Answer

          $2.27 \cdot 10^{39}$

          Three significant figures (a chosen precision). Check your explanation: the ratio does not depend on the distance, because the expression of each force contains the same $r^2$.

          Reasonableness

          Estimate: $10^{10} \cdot 2.6 \cdot 10^{-38} : 10^{-67} \approx 2.6 \cdot 10^{39}$. Between particles gravity is negligibly weak; in everyday life it shows only because the mass of the Earth is huge and its charge is almost zero.

        3. Exercise 10-12

          A point charge $+4.0\,\text{nC}$ is at $x = 0$ and a point charge $+1.0\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 12\,\text{cm}$. A third point charge is brought to a point on the $x$-axis between them.

          At which point is there no net force on the third charge? Give the answer in centimetres from the point $x = 0$.

          cm
          Answer

          $8.0\,\text{cm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $Q_1 = +4.0\,\text{nC}$ at $x = 0$, $Q_2 = +1.0\,\text{nC}$ at $d = 12\,\text{cm}$; the third charge is between them

          Find

          the point $x$ where no net force acts on the third charge, in centimetres

          Principle

          Both charges are positive, so between them they push the third charge in opposite directions. The net force is zero when the forces are equal. In Coulomb's law the magnitude of the third charge and $k$ are the same in both forces, so they cancel, and the result does not depend on the magnitude or the sign of the third charge.

          Formula

          $k\,\dfrac{Q_1 q}{x^2}$$= k\,\dfrac{Q_2 q}{(d - x)^2}$

          so

          $\dfrac{x}{d - x}$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{Q_1}{Q_2}}$
          $x$$= \dfrac{d \sqrt{Q_1 : Q_2}}{1 + \sqrt{Q_1 : Q_2}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: in the ratio of the charges the nanocoulombs cancel, and the point comes in centimetres.

          Substitution

          $\sqrt{\dfrac{4.0\,\text{nC}}{1.0\,\text{nC}}}$$= 2$
          $x$$= \dfrac{12\,\text{cm} \cdot 2}{1 + 2}$
          $= 8.0\,\text{cm}$

          Answer

          $8.0\,\text{cm}$

          Two significant figures like the charges.

          Reasonableness

          The point is twice as far from the larger charge ($8.0\,\text{cm}$) as from the smaller one ($4.0\,\text{cm}$). A charge four times as large and a distance twice as large give the same force, since $\tfrac{4}{2^2} = \tfrac{1}{1^2}$. The equilibrium point is nearer the smaller charge, as it should be.
