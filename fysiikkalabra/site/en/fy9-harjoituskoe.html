Practice exam

Why is it worth doing a six-hour exam once in full before the real exam?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve the problems of a six-hour exam and divide your time between its parts.
  • choose the problems that best suit what you know.
  • assess your answers with a marking scheme and choose the chapters you will review again.

1Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Exam instructions

Structure

The practice exam is built like the physics matriculation exam. You have six hours. The exam has $11$ problems in three parts, and you answer seven:

Part What you answer Points
Part I compulsory, $10$ short items $20$ p
Part II $4$ problems out of seven $4 \cdot 15$ p
Part III $2$ data-based problems out of three $2 \cdot 20$ p

The maximum score of the exam is $120$ p. If you answer more problems in Part II or III than the instructions say, count only the ones you chose before opening the model answers when you assess the practice exam.

Points in Part I

Each of the ten items of Part I is worth $2$ p, $20$ p in total:

  • items 1–4, multiple choice: $4 \cdot 2$ p $= 8$ p;
  • items 5–7, statement and justification: $3 \cdot 2$ p $= 6$ p;
  • items 8–10, short calculations: $3 \cdot 2$ p $= 6$ p.

Parts II and III

The problems of Parts II and III are at the end of the page under Problems, and their title says which part each problem belongs to. The problems of Part II are worth $15$ p and the data-based problems of Part III $20$ p. The points of each part of a problem are marked on it. Write a complete solution for every calculation: the given and requested quantities, the principle, the formula solved for the unknown, the substitution with units, the answer to the right precision and a reasonableness check.

Aids

Use the same aids as in the real exam: a calculator, a spreadsheet, a graphing program and a formula editor, together with the exam's tables and formulas. Your notes, the textbook, the internet and the model answers on this page stay closed throughout the exam. The programs and materials allowed in the real exam are given in the regulations of the Matriculation Examination Board, so check them before the exam.

Time plan

Six hours are $360\,\text{min}$. Divide your time roughly according to the points and keep time for checking at the end:

  • Read and choose, $15\,\text{min}$. Read all the problems. From Part II choose the four, and from Part III the two, whose phenomenon you recognise and whose every part you can begin to answer.
  • Part I, $50\,\text{min}$. About five minutes per item. If an item does not open up, move on to the next one and come back to it at the end.
  • Part II, $160\,\text{min}$. About $40\,\text{min}$ per problem.
  • Part III, $110\,\text{min}$. About $55\,\text{min}$ per problem. In a data-based problem drawing the graph takes time, so start with it.
  • Check, $25\,\text{min}$. Go through the units, the significant figures and that every question has been answered.

During the exam, write down how long each problem took. In the self-assessment you will see where the time went.

Self-assessment

Assess the exam only when the six hours have passed or you have answered all the problems you chose.

Part I

The page checks the items of Part I. Give yourself $2$ p for a multiple-choice item and a statement if your first choice was correct, otherwise $0$ p. In the real exam you cannot change your answer after seeing feedback, so do not change your choice. A short calculation gives $2$ p if the result is correct and your solution has the formula, the substitution with units and the answer with its unit, and $1$ p if the method is correct but the result is wrong or the unit is missing.

Parts II and III

  1. Open the model answer and marking scheme of a problem only after you have marked the problem as done.
  2. Go through the marking scheme criterion by criterion. Give the points of a criterion only if your answer contains what the criterion requires. A good intention or a correct number without justification is not enough.
  3. Read the section Common mistakes: if you made one of them, check that it did not earn you points. The section Also accepted tells which other solutions are correct.
  4. Add up the points of each problem and of the whole exam, at most $120$ p.

What next

Every problem has a list of the chapters whose skills it uses. If you got less than half the points of a problem, review these chapters and do one more problem from them. Also compare your use of time with the plan: if some part took much more time, practise its type of problem before the real exam.

Part I: ten short items (20 p)

Part I is compulsory. It has ten items from modules FY1–FY8, $2$ p each. Items 1–4 are multiple choice, in items 5–7 you choose whether a statement is true and on what grounds, and items 8–10 are short calculations. Part I 1. A phone screen (2 p)
The length of a phone screen is measured with a ruler as $14.62\,\text{cm}$ and its width with a tape measure as $6.8\,\text{cm}$.How is the area of the screen given?

  • The calculator shows all the digits, but the result is no more precise than the given values. The width is known to only two digits, so the result has two significant figures: $99\,\text{cm}^2$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Rounding is not to the nearest ten but to significant figures. Two significant figures give $99\,\text{cm}^2$; $100\,\text{cm}^2$ is rounded too much. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. The result of a multiplication is given to as many significant figures as the least precise given value, here the width $6.8\,\text{cm}$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • The precision is set by the least precise given value, not the most precise one. The width $6.8\,\text{cm}$ has two significant figures, so the area is $99\,\text{cm}^2$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Part I 2. A chain of energy conversions (2 p)
Electricity is produced in a condensing power plant with an efficiency of $40\,\%$. In transmission $5\,\%$ of the electrical energy is lost, and the efficiency of an LED lamp is $30\,\%$.What fraction of the energy of the fuel becomes light in the lamp?

  • The weakest stage alone does not determine the result, because the losses in the power plant and in transmission also reduce the energy that reaches the lamp. The overall efficiency is $0.40 \cdot 0.95 \cdot 0.30 \approx 11\,\%$. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

  • The mean does not tell how much energy is left at the end of the chain. Each stage uses only the energy the previous stage gave, so the efficiencies are multiplied: about $11\,\%$. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

  • Energy is also converted into forms other than useful ones in the power plant ($60\,\%$) and in the lamp ($70\,\%$). The efficiencies are multiplied: about $11\,\%$. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

  • Correct. Each stage receives only what the previous one gave: $0.40 \cdot 0.95 \cdot 0.30 \approx 0.11$. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

Part I 3. A ball at its highest point (2 p)
A ball is thrown straight up. Air resistance is neglected. Which statement about the motion of the ball at its highest point is correct?

  • If the acceleration were zero, the ball would stay hovering at the highest point. The weight acts on the ball all the time, so the acceleration is $g$ downwards even when the velocity is zero. Review this in the chapter Newton's second law.

  • It is the velocity that changes direction, not the acceleration. The weight points down all the time, so the acceleration is $g$ downwards throughout the flight. Review this in the chapter Newton's second law.

  • The throwing force acts only during the throw, while the ball is in the hand. During the flight only the weight acts on the ball, so the ball is not in equilibrium. Review this in the chapter Newton's second law.

  • Correct. The only force is the weight, so the acceleration is $g$ downwards throughout the flight, also at the highest point. Review this in the chapter Newton's second law.

Part I 4. A magnet in a copper pipe (2 p)
A small strong magnet is dropped into a vertical copper pipe. It falls through the pipe much more slowly than a piece of iron of the same size.What explains this?

  • The pipe is electrically neutral, and the magnet has no electric charge. The moving magnet induces currents in the pipe, and their magnetic field slows it down. Review this in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

  • Copper is not ferromagnetic and is not magnetised like iron: a magnet does not stick to copper. The slowing is due to induction. Review this in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

  • Correct. The magnetic flux changes in the wall of the pipe, and by Lenz's law the magnetic field of the induced eddy currents opposes the change that produces them. Review this in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

  • A piece of iron of the same size falls quickly through the same gap, so the air does not explain the difference. The slowing is due to eddy currents. Review this in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

Part I 5. Statement: a gas cylinder warms up (2 p)
Statement: “When the gas in a closed steel cylinder warms from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the pressure of the gas doubles.” Choose the correct assessment and justification.

  • At constant volume the pressure increases with the temperature, because the particles hit the walls more often and harder. The increase is about $7\,\%$. Review this in the chapter Ideal gas law.

  • The pressure is proportional to the absolute temperature, not to the Celsius temperature. From $293\,\text{K}$ to $313\,\text{K}$ the pressure increases by only about $7\,\%$. Review this in the chapter Ideal gas law.

  • The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature, which increases by only about $7\,\%$. Neither the speed nor the pressure doubles. Review this in the chapter Ideal gas law.

  • Correct. At constant volume $\tfrac{p_2}{p_1} = \tfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \tfrac{313\,\text{K}}{293\,\text{K}} \approx 1.07$, so the pressure increases by about $7\,\%$. Review this in the chapter Ideal gas law.

Part I 6. Statement: the speeds of satellites (2 p)
Statement: “A satellite orbiting the Earth in a low circular orbit moves faster than a satellite in a high circular orbit.” Choose the correct assessment and justification.

  • Correct. Gravity provides the centripetal acceleration: $G\tfrac{mM}{r^2} = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{GM}{r}}$, and the speed increases as the radius decreases. Review this in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • A longer orbit is travelled more slowly, not faster: high up gravity is weaker and $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{GM}{r}}$ is smaller. The period increases both because the orbit is longer and because the speed is smaller. Review this in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • Air resistance brakes the satellite; it does not push it. The statement is true, but the reason is gravity: $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{GM}{r}}$. Review this in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • The orbital speed depends on the radius of the orbit: $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{GM}{r}}$. In a low orbit the speed is greater. Review this in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

Part I 7. Statement: a second lamp in parallel (2 p)
A lamp is connected to the terminals of a battery. Statement: “When a second identical lamp is connected in parallel with the lamp, the first lamp becomes clearly dimmer, because the current is shared between two lamps.” The battery is new, and its internal resistance is small. Choose the correct assessment and justification.

  • The current of the battery increases, but it is shared between the lamps. The voltage and current of the first lamp stay almost the same, so it does not become brighter. Review this in the chapter Resistors in series and in parallel.

  • The total resistance of a parallel connection is smaller than the resistance of either lamp, so the current of the battery increases. The voltage and current of the lamp stay almost unchanged. Review this in the chapter Resistors in series and in parallel.

  • Correct. Lamps connected in parallel have the same voltage, the terminal voltage of the battery, which decreases only a little because of the internal resistance. The first lamp therefore becomes only slightly dimmer. Review this in the chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance.

  • A battery does not give a constant current. Its terminal voltage stays almost the same, so each lamp gets the same current as the first one had before, and the current of the battery doubles. Review this in the chapter Resistors in series and in parallel.

Part I 8. Oscillation of a spring (2 p)

A $250\,\text{g}$ mass is hung on a spring with a spring constant of $40.0\,\text{N/m}$. The mass is set oscillating vertically. Calculate the period of the oscillation. The mass of the spring is neglected.

s
Show the solution

Given

spring constant $k = 40.0\,\text{N/m}$, mass $m = 250\,\text{g}$

Find

the period $T$

Principle

The spring force on the mass is proportional to the displacement from the equilibrium position, so the motion is simple harmonic oscillation. Gravity only shifts the equilibrium position and does not change the period. The mass of the spring is neglected.

Formula

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$

Conversions

$m$$= 250\,\text{g}$
$= 250 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$
$= 0.250\,\text{kg}$

Substitution

$T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{0.250\,\text{kg}}{40.0\,\text{N/m}}}$
$= 2\pi \cdot 0.07906\,\text{s}$
$= 0.4967\,\text{s}$

Answer

$T \approx 0.497\,\text{s}$

Three significant figures like the spring constant and the mass.

Reasonableness

Unit:

$\sqrt{\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{N/m}}}$$= \sqrt{\tfrac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2}}$
$= \text{s}$

About two oscillations per second fits the motion of a school spring and mass.

Part I 9. A fan heater (2 p)

The power of a fan heater is $1.50\,\text{kW}$ when its voltage is $230\,\text{V}$. Calculate the resistance of the heating element of the heater.

Ω
Show the solution

Given

power $P = 1.50\,\text{kW}$, voltage $U = 230\,\text{V}$

Find

the resistance $R$

Principle

Electric power is the product of voltage and current, and the current through a resistor obeys Ohm's law. When the current is eliminated, the power depends only on the voltage and the resistance.

Formula

$P = UI$ and $I = \dfrac{U}{R}$, so $P = \dfrac{U^2}{R}$, that is $R = \dfrac{U^2}{P}$

Conversions

$P$$= 1.50\,\text{kW}$
$= 1.50 \cdot 1000\,\text{W}$
$= 1500\,\text{W}$

Substitution

$R$$= \dfrac{(230\,\text{V})^2}{1500\,\text{W}}$
$= \dfrac{52\,900\,\text{V}^2}{1500\,\text{W}}$
$= 35.27\,\Omega$

Answer

$R \approx 35.3\,\Omega$

Three significant figures like the power and the voltage.

Reasonableness

The current is $\tfrac{230\,\text{V}}{35.3\,\Omega} = 6.5\,\text{A}$ and $230\,\text{V} \cdot 6.5\,\text{A} \approx 1500\,\text{W}$. The current is below the $10$–$16\,\text{A}$ limit of an ordinary socket, as it should be for a household appliance.

Part I 10. A violet laser (2 p)

The laser of a Blu-ray player emits violet light with a wavelength of $405\,\text{nm}$. Calculate the energy of one photon in electronvolts.

eV
Show the solution

Given

wavelength $\lambda = 405\,\text{nm}$, Planck constant $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, speed of light $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$

Find

the energy of a photon $E$ in electronvolts

Principle

Light carries energy in quanta, photons. The energy of one photon is proportional to the frequency of the light, and the frequency follows from the wavelength, because the speed of light is $c = f\lambda$.

Formula

$E = hf$ and $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, so $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

Conversions

$\lambda$$= 405\,\text{nm}$
$= 405 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$

$1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

Substitution

$hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$
$= 1.986 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

so

$E$$= \dfrac{1.986 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{405 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
$= 4.905 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

and in electronvolts

$E$$= \dfrac{4.905 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J/eV}}$
$= 3.062\,\text{eV}$

Answer

$E \approx 3.06\,\text{eV}$

Three significant figures like the wavelength.

Reasonableness

The photons of visible light have energies of about $1.6$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$, and violet is near the short-wavelength end, so the energy is at the upper end of the range. Check: $\tfrac{1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm}}{405\,\text{nm}} = 3.06\,\text{eV}$.

Exercise 7.1-1 Part II: Sauna stove and steam 15 points

Made-up example data · The numbers have been chosen to be realistic, but they are not a measurement.

The sauna of a summer cottage has an electric stove with a power of $6.0\,\text{kW}$. The stove holds $25\,\text{kg}$ of sauna stones, whose specific heat capacity is $0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$. The stove is on for $50\,\text{min}$ before bathing, and during that time the temperature of the stones rises from $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $280\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

During bathing ten ladlefuls, in total $1.2\,\text{l}$ of water at $18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, are thrown onto the stove. The water evaporates completely on the hot stones. Assume that the steam is at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ when it forms and that all the energy needed for the evaporation comes from the stones. The specific heat capacity of water is $4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$ and its specific latent heat of vaporisation is $2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

  1. a) How much energy is stored in the stones during the preheating? 3 p
  2. b) What fraction of the electrical energy the stove uses during the preheating is in the stones at the end? 3 p
  3. c) Having seen the result of part b, a neighbour says: “So the efficiency of the stove is only about $30\,\%$, which means it is a poor appliance.” Assess the claim. 3 p
  4. d) How much energy is transferred from the stones to the water when all ten ladlefuls evaporate? 3 p
  5. e) After water is thrown on the stove, the skin feels as if it were burning, even though the air temperature in the sauna hardly rises. Explain this. 3 p

Skills from the chapters: Heating and cooling of matter: $Q = cm\Delta T$, Changes of state, Efficiency, Electrical energy at home

Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Given

mass of the stones $m_\text{k} = 25\,\text{kg}$, specific heat capacity $c_\text{k} = 0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$, the temperature rises from $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $280\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Find

the energy stored in the stones $Q_\text{k}$

Principle

The stones are solid all the time and their phase does not change, so the increase in their internal energy is a quantity of heat that depends on the mass, the specific heat capacity and the change in temperature.

Formula

$Q_\text{k} = c_\text{k} m_\text{k} \Delta T$

Conversions

$c_\text{k}$$= 0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$
$= 0.80 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$
$= 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$

A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius:

$\Delta T$$= 280\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
$= 265\,\text{K}$

Substitution

$Q_\text{k}$$= 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{kg} \cdot 265\,\text{K}$
$= 5\,300\,000\,\text{J}$
$= 5.30\,\text{MJ}$

Answer

$Q_\text{k} \approx 5.3\,\text{MJ}$

Two significant figures, because the specific heat capacity $0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$ and the mass $25\,\text{kg}$ are known to two digits.

Reasonableness

Roughly $25 \cdot 800 \cdot 300\,\text{J} = 6\,\text{MJ}$, so the order of magnitude is right. The energy corresponds to about $1.5\,\text{kWh}$, so the stove would produce it at full power in about $15\,\text{min}$.

b)

Given

power of the stove $P = 6.0\,\text{kW}$, time $t = 50\,\text{min}$, $Q_\text{k} = 5.30\,\text{MJ}$ (part a)

Find

the fraction of the electrical energy stored in the stones

Principle

The stove converts electrical energy into internal energy at the power $P$, so the electrical energy is the power multiplied by the time. The fraction is the energy stored in the stones divided by all the electrical energy.

Formula

$E = Pt$ and fraction $= \dfrac{Q_\text{k}}{E} = \dfrac{Q_\text{k}}{Pt}$

Conversions

$t$$= 50 \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
$= 3000\,\text{s}$
$P$$= 6.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{W}$
$= 6000\,\text{W}$

Substitution

$E$$= 6000\,\text{W} \cdot 3000\,\text{s}$
$= 18\,000\,000\,\text{J}$
$= 18.0\,\text{MJ}$

and fraction $= \dfrac{5.30\,\text{MJ}}{18.0\,\text{MJ}} = 0.2944$

Answer

About $29\,\%$ of the electrical energy is in the stones at the end, two significant figures like the power and the result of part a.

Reasonableness

The fraction is below $100\,\%$, as it must be. The electrical energy $18\,\text{MJ}$ is $\tfrac{18\,\text{MJ}}{3.6\,\text{MJ/kWh}} = 5.0\,\text{kWh}$, which is a typical consumption for heating a sauna.

c)

Find

whether the efficiency of the stove is only about $30\,\%$

Principle

Efficiency is the useful energy obtained divided by the energy used. It depends on what is counted as useful and where the boundary of the system is drawn. In an electric resistor practically all the electrical energy becomes internal energy.

Model answer

The stove is an electric resistor, so practically all the electrical energy it uses, $18\,\text{MJ}$, becomes internal energy of the sauna. About $29\,\%$ of it is in the stones, but the rest heats the air, the benches and the walls of the sauna, and only part of it leaks out through the walls and the ventilation. In heating a sauna, the warming of the air and the walls is also useful, so the figure of part b is the stones' share of the energy, not the efficiency of the stove. The claim does not hold: as a heater of the sauna the stove has an efficiency of almost $100\,\%$, and the only waste is the energy transferred out of the sauna.

d)

Given

mass of the water $m = 1.2\,\text{kg}$ (density of water $1\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{l}}$), initial temperature $18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c_\text{v} = 4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$, $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$; the steam forms at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Find

the energy $Q$ transferred from the stones to the water

Principle

The water first warms to its boiling point, with no change of phase, and then evaporates at constant temperature. The energy needed for both stages comes from the stones, so they are added together.

Formula

$Q = c_\text{v} m \Delta T + r m$

Conversions

$1.2\,\text{l}$ of water is $1.2\,\text{kg}$.

$c_\text{v}$$= 4.19 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$
$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$
$r$$= 2260 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
$= 2.26 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
$\Delta T$$= 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
$= 82\,\text{K}$

Substitution

$Q$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 1.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 82\,\text{K} + 2.26 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 1.2\,\text{kg}$
$= 412\,296\,\text{J} + 2\,712\,000\,\text{J}$
$= 3\,124\,296\,\text{J}$

Answer

$Q \approx 3.1\,\text{MJ}$

Two significant figures like the amount of water $1.2\,\text{l}$.

Reasonableness

The evaporation takes almost $90\,\%$ of the energy, which fits the fact that the latent heat of vaporisation is over five times the energy needed to heat the water to its boiling point. Without heating from the stove the stones would cool by

$\tfrac{3.12\,\text{MJ}}{25\,\text{kg} \cdot 800\,\text{J/(kg K)}}$
$\approx 160\,\text{K}$

the stove produces the same energy in $\tfrac{3.12\,\text{MJ}}{6000\,\text{W}} \approx 9\,\text{min}$, so the stove keeps the stones hot when water is thrown on them now and then.

e)

Find

why the skin feels as if it were burning after water is thrown on the stove, although the air temperature hardly rises

Principle

Steam condenses when it meets a surface cooler than itself. Condensation releases the same energy that was needed for the evaporation.

Model answer

The water vapour formed on the stove spreads into the air of the sauna and meets the skin, whose temperature is only about $35$–$40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The vapour condenses into water on the skin, and for every kilogram that condenses about $2.3\,\text{MJ}$ of energy is released and transferred to the skin. The skin therefore receives a lot of energy in a short time, although the air temperature in the sauna hardly changes. In addition, moist skin does not cool by sweating, because sweat does not evaporate as well into humid air. That is why the steam feels burning.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the final temperature $280\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, or $553\,\text{K}$ in kelvins, is taken as the change in temperature, although the formula needs the change $\Delta T = 265\,\text{K}$.
  • In part b the time is left in minutes, which makes the energy $300\,\text{kJ}$ and the fraction over $100\,\%$.
  • In part c the claim is accepted because “the rest of the energy is wasted”, although the air and the walls also warm up.
  • In part d the warming of the water to its boiling point or the evaporation is forgotten; the warming alone gives only $0.41\,\text{MJ}$.
  • In part e it is explained that the steam is “hotter than the air”, without mentioning condensation and the energy it releases.

Also accepted

  • In part c an answer stating that efficiency depends on what is defined as useful, and that the fraction of energy stored in the stones is a different thing from the efficiency of the stove.
  • In part d the answer $3.12\,\text{MJ}$ when the precision is justified with the heat capacity of the water; the full precision point requires the justification.

Model answer

a)

Find

the free-body diagram of the box during braking, when the box stays at rest relative to the bed

Principle

Only the interactions acting on the box are drawn in the free-body diagram: the Earth (weight) and the bed (normal force and friction). The box slows down together with the trailer, so its acceleration points backwards, and by Newton's second law the net force must point in the same direction.

Model answer

The forces on the box are the weight $\overline{G}$ down, the normal force $\overline{N}$ of the bed up and the static friction $\overline{F}_{\mu 0}$ of the bed horizontally backwards, that is against the direction of travel of the car. The box does not accelerate vertically, so $N = G$. The only horizontal force is static friction, and it must point backwards, because it slows the box down as much as the brakes slow down the trailer. There is no forward “force of motion”: the box would continue moving forwards if there were no friction.

b)

Given

coefficient of static friction $\mu_0 = 0.45$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the greatest deceleration $a_\text{max}$ at which the box does not slide

Principle

The box stays at rest relative to the bed if it has the same deceleration as the trailer. That deceleration is given by the only horizontal force, static friction. Static friction can be at most $\mu_0 N$, and on a horizontal bed $N = mg$.

Formula

$F_{\mu 0} = ma$ and $F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$, so $ma \le \mu_0 mg$, that is $a_\text{max} = \mu_0 g$

Conversions

None needed.

Substitution

$a_\text{max}$$= 0.45 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 4.4145\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a_\text{max} \approx 4.4\,\text{m/s}^2$

Two significant figures like the coefficient of friction.

Reasonableness

The deceleration is a little less than half the free-fall acceleration, which is gentle braking. In emergency braking on dry asphalt the deceleration is about $7$–$9\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the box would then slide.

c)

Given

initial speed $v_0 = 80\,\text{km/h}$, final speed $0$, deceleration at most $a_\text{max} = 4.4145\,\text{m/s}^2$ (part b)

Find

the shortest braking distance $s$

Principle

The braking is uniformly decelerated motion. The smaller the deceleration, the longer the distance, so the shortest allowed distance is obtained with the greatest allowed deceleration.

Formula

$v^2 = v_0^2 - 2as$ and $v = 0$, so $s = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a_\text{max}}$

Conversions

$v_0$$= 80\,\text{km/h}$
$= \dfrac{80 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 22.22\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$s$$= \dfrac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 4.4145\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= \dfrac{493.8\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{8.829\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= 55.93\,\text{m}$

Answer

$s \approx 56\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the coefficient of friction and the speed.

Reasonableness

The braking lasts $\tfrac{22.2\,\text{m/s}}{4.41\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 5.0\,\text{s}$, and at the average speed $11.1\,\text{m/s}$ this corresponds to $56\,\text{m}$. In emergency braking with a deceleration of $7\,\text{m/s}^2$ the distance would be only about $35\,\text{m}$.

d)

Find

whether increasing the mass of the box helps to keep the box in place

Principle

The maximum static friction is $\mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$, and the force $ma$ is needed to slow the box down. Both are proportional to the mass.

Model answer

A heavier box presses on the bed harder, so the maximum possible static friction $\mu_0 mg$ increases. But a heavier box also needs a greater force $ma$ to slow it down, and this increases in the same proportion. The mass cancels from the condition $ma \le \mu_0 mg$, so the greatest allowed deceleration $a_\text{max} = \mu_0 g$ does not depend on the mass. The suggestion therefore does not help. Methods that work are tying the box to the bed with straps, or increasing the coefficient of friction, for example with a rubber non-slip mat under the box.

  • In part b one tries to calculate or guess the mass, although it cancels from the equation.
  • In part c the speed is left in $\text{km/h}$, which makes the distance $725$ with no sensible unit.
  • In part c the formula $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ is used without calculating the time.
  • In part d it is only stated that “a heavy box stays put better”, without justifying the ratio of the forces.

Also accepted

  • In part a a free-body diagram without the acceleration arrow, when the text gives the direction of the acceleration.
  • In part c a solution via the time: $t = \tfrac{v_0}{a}$ and $s = v_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$.

Model answer

a)

Given

length of the open pipe $L = 1.31\,\text{m}$, speed of sound at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ $v = 343.2\,\text{m/s}$

Find

the fundamental frequency $f_1$

Principle

A standing wave of the air column forms in the pipe. An open pipe has a displacement antinode at both ends, and in the fundamental mode there is one node between them, so the length of the pipe is half a wavelength. The frequency follows from the wave equation $v = f\lambda$.

Formula

$\lambda_1$$= 2L$
$f_1$$= \dfrac{v}{\lambda_1}$
$= \dfrac{v}{2L}$

Conversions

Substitution

$f_1$$= \dfrac{343.2\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 1.31\,\text{m}}$

Answer

$f_1 \approx 131\,\text{Hz}$

Three significant figures like the length of the pipe.

Reasonableness

Humans hear from about $20\,\text{Hz}$ to $20\,\text{kHz}$, and $131\,\text{Hz}$ is a low note, as can be expected from a pipe about a metre long.

Given

A solid metal contracts as it cools, and the change in length is proportional to the original length and to the change in temperature. The fundamental frequency is inversely proportional to the length, so a shortening pipe raises the frequency by the same relative amount.

Formula

$\Delta L$$= \alpha L_0 \Delta T$
$\dfrac{\Delta f}{f}$$\approx -\dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0}$

e)

Find

Principle

The temperature of the cord rises until it transfers as much power to the surroundings as is produced in it. The transfer of heat depends on how much of the surface is freely in contact with the air.

Model answer

The same heating power, about $110\,\text{W}$, is produced in the cord whether it is on the reel or spread out. A cord that is spread out gives off heat to the air from its whole surface, so it warms only a little. a)

Given

Find

the critical angle $\alpha_\text{r}$

Principle

When light passes from water into air, it is refracted away from the normal of the surface. At the critical angle the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$: the ray travels along the surface. Snell's law holds at the critical angle too.

Formula

$n_1 \sin\alpha_\text{r} = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$, so

$\sin\alpha_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$

Conversions

None needed.

Substitution

Three significant figures like the refractive index.

Model answer

a)

Find

the equation of the beta decay of carbon-14 and what happens in the nucleus

Principle

In beta-minus decay a neutron of the nucleus changes into a proton, and the nucleus emits an electron and an antineutrino. The mass number and the charge number are conserved in the reaction.

Model answer

$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{\phantom{-}0}_{-1}\text{e} + \overline{\nu}_\text{e}$. Mass numbers: $14 = 14 + 0$, and charge numbers: $6 = 7 + (-1)$. The nucleus of carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons. In the decay one neutron changes into a proton, so the daughter nucleus has seven protons and seven neutrons: it is nitrogen-14. The nucleus emits an electron, that is a beta particle, and an antineutrino.

b)

Given

the age of the canoe $t$

Principle

When the tree is felled, carbon-14 only decays and no new carbon-14 comes in. The activity is proportional to the number of nuclei, so by the law of radioactive decay it halves in every half-life. It is assumed that the carbon-14 content of the air was the same while the tree was growing as it is now.

from which with a logarithm

$\ln\dfrac{A_0}{A}$$= \dfrac{t}{T_{1/2}} \ln 2$

that is

$t$$= T_{1/2} \cdot \dfrac{\ln(A_0 / A)}{\ln 2}$

Conversions

Substitution

$\dfrac{A_0}{A}$$= \dfrac{0.226}{0.198}$
$= 1.1414$
$t$$= 5730\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{\ln 1.1414}{\ln 2}$

$= 1093\,\text{a}$

$t \approx 1.09 \cdot 10^3$

Years, that is about $1100$ years, three significant figures like the activities.

The activity has decreased by only about $12\,\%$, which is much less than half, so the age must be clearly less than one half-life. A thousand-year-old canoe fits the end of the Iron Age, when dugout canoes were in common use.

The age is calculated with the largest and the smallest value of the activity. A larger activity means a younger sample. The uncertainty of the age is half the difference between the extreme values.

The method can date a sample only if the change in activity is clearly greater than the measurement uncertainty and the remaining activity can be distinguished from the background radiation.

The change is lost in the uncertainty.   • In part c the uncertainty is given as $2\,\%$ of the age, the uncertainty of the activity, that is about $20$ years.

Research question: how does the bounce height of a squash ball depend on the temperature of the ball when the drop height stays the same? Model answer

Find

why the stack of filters finally falls at a constant speed

Principle

Newton's second law: the acceleration is the net force divided by the mass. Air resistance increases as the speed increases, while the weight stays the same.

Model answer

The forces on the stack are the weight $\overline{G}$ downwards and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ upwards. Just after the release the speed is small, so the air resistance is small and the net force points down: the stack accelerates downwards and its speed increases. As the speed increases, the air resistance increases, so the net force and the acceleration decrease. Finally the air resistance is equal to the weight. Then the net force is zero, the acceleration is zero and, by Newton's first law, the stack continues at a steady speed, the terminal speed.

Model answer

a)

Find

a graph whose points lie on a straight line, a line fitted to it, and a justification for the choice of axes

Graph

The period of a mass–spring oscillator is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$, so $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\,m$: the square of the period is proportional to the oscillating mass. That is why $T^2$ is chosen for the vertical axis and $m$ for the horizontal axis. From the table first $T = \tfrac{t_{10}}{10}$ and then $T^2$ are calculated:

$m\ (\text{kg})$ $T\ (\text{s})$ $T^2\ (\text{s}^2)$
$0.100$ $0.437$ $0.191$
$0.200$ $0.586$ $0.343$
$0.300$ $0.713$ $0.508$
$0.400$ $0.819$ $0.671$
$0.500$ $0.903$ $0.815$
$0.600$ $0.990$ $0.980$

The line is not forced through the origin, because the spring itself also oscillates.

Model answer

The points lie on a straight line, so $T^2$ depends linearly on the mass. The fitted line $T^2 = 1.578\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}} \cdot m + 0.0324\,\text{s}^2$ crosses the vertical axis slightly above the origin.

b)

Given

the fitted line of part a

Find

the spring constant $k$

Principle

According to the model $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}(m + m_\text{j})$, the slope of the line is $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$. The slope is read from the fitted line at two points far apart.

Reading the graph

The points $(0\,\text{kg}$; $0.032\,\text{s}^2)$ and $(0.600\,\text{kg}$; $0.979\,\text{s}^2)$ are read from the line, so the slope is

$\dfrac{0.979\,\text{s}^2 - 0.032\,\text{s}^2}{0.600\,\text{kg}}$
$= 1.578\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}}$

Formula

slope $= \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$, so $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\text{slope}}$

Conversions

None needed: $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}^2} = \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$.

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{4\pi^2}{1.578\,\text{s}^2/\text{kg}}$
$= 25.01\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}^2}$

Answer

$k \approx 25.0\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$

Three significant figures like the measurements.

Reasonableness

Such a spring stretches by $\tfrac{9.81\,\text{N}}{25\,\text{N/m}} \approx 0.39\,\text{m}$ with a $1\,\text{kg}$ mass, which fits a school coil spring.

c)

Given

the line of part a: slope $1.578\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}}$, constant term $b = 0.0324\,\text{s}^2$; mass of the spring $61\,\text{g}$

Find

why the line does not pass through the origin, and how large a mass $m_\text{j}$ of the spring oscillates with the mass

Principle

Besides the mass, the spring also moves: its lower end moves with the mass and its upper end stays still. The motion of the spring therefore acts as if part of its mass were in the hanging mass, and the oscillating mass is $m + m_\text{j}$. Then $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}(m + m_\text{j})$, and the line crosses the mass axis at $m = -m_\text{j}$.

Reading the graph

When $T^2 = 0$, the fit gives $0 = 1.578\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}} \cdot m + 0.0324\,\text{s}^2$, so the line crosses the mass axis at $m = -\tfrac{b}{\text{slope}}$.

Formula

$m_\text{j} = \dfrac{b}{\text{slope}}$

Conversions

Finally $1\,\text{kg} = 1000\,\text{g}$.

Substitution

$m_\text{j}$$= \dfrac{0.0324\,\text{s}^2}{1.578\,\text{s}^2/\text{kg}}$
$= 0.0205\,\text{kg}$
$= 20.5\,\text{g}$

Answer

$m_\text{j} \approx 21\,\text{g}$

Two significant figures, because the constant term is small and its relative uncertainty large. It is about a third of the spring's own mass, $61\,\text{g}$.

Reasonableness

The lower end of the spring moves as much as the mass and the upper end not at all, so it makes sense that only part of the spring's mass contributes. $\tfrac{61\,\text{g}}{3} \approx 20\,\text{g}$ agrees with the result.

d)

Given

mass $m = 0.300\,\text{kg}$, extension $x = 11.8\,\text{cm}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; $k = 25.0\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$ from part b

Find

the spring constant from the extension measurement and a comparison with part b

Principle

At rest the forces on the mass are the spring force up and the weight down, and they are equal. The spring force is proportional to the extension (Hooke's law).

Formula

$kx = mg$, so $k = \dfrac{mg}{x}$

Conversions

$x$$= 11.8\,\text{cm}$
$= 11.8 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
$= 0.118\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{0.300\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.118\,\text{m}}$
$= 24.94\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$

Answer

$k \approx 24.9\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$

Three significant figures like the mass and the extension. The result differs from the value of part b, $25.0\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$, by only about $0.3\,\%$, so the methods support each other.

Reasonableness

An uncertainty in reading the extension of, for example, $\pm 2\,\text{mm}$ is about $2\,\%$, which is larger than the difference, so the results agree.

e)

Find

whether the period doubles when the mass is doubled

Principle

The periods in the data for masses of which one is twice the other are compared with each other and with the model $T \propto \sqrt{m + m_\text{j}}$.

Model answer

In the data the mass doubles, for example, from $0.300\,\text{kg}$ to $0.600\,\text{kg}$, and the period increases from $0.713\,\text{s}$ to $0.990\,\text{s}$, that is only by a factor of $\tfrac{0.990}{0.713} \approx 1.39$. The claim is wrong: the period is proportional to the square root of the oscillating mass, so without the mass of the spring doubling the mass would increase the period by a factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. When the effect of the spring's mass from part c, $m_\text{j} \approx 0.020\,\text{kg}$, is added to both masses, the model gives $\sqrt{0.620 : 0.320} \approx 1.39$, which agrees with the data. The difference is therefore due to the spring's own mass, which does not double.

Common mistakes

  • In part a $T$ is plotted as a function of the mass and a straight line is fitted to the curved set of points.
  • In part a one forgets to divide by ten, which makes $T^2$ a hundred times too large and the spring constant a hundredth.
  • In part b the slope itself, or its reciprocal without the factor $4\pi^2$, is taken as the spring constant.
  • In part c the deviation from the origin is explained by measurement error alone, although all the points lie consistently on the line.
  • In part d the extension is left in centimetres, which makes the spring constant $0.249\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$.

Also accepted

  • In part c determining the effective mass of the spring directly from where the line crosses the mass axis on the graph, when the reading is about $-20\,\text{g}$.
  • In part e a comparison with the masses $0.100\,\text{kg}$ and $0.200\,\text{kg}$ (ratio $1.34$), when the difference from $\sqrt{2}$ is explained with the mass of the spring.
