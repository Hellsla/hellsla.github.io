Fusion in the energy system

Why is it so hard to say whether fusion will ever power our homes?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • compare fusion with fission and with renewable energy as parts of an energy system.
  • calculate the energy per kilogram of fusion fuel and the tritium a power plant burns.
  • distinguish established results from institutional plans and corporate claims.

Prior knowledge

  • D–T fusion releases $17.6\ \text{MeV}$, of which $3.5\ \text{MeV}$ goes to the helium nucleus and $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ to the neutronchapter Fusion reactions: from the Sun to deuterium and tritium
  • The fusion gain $Q$ compares fusion power with heating power, not with electricitychapter Inside ITER: the machine and fusion gain

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 1.

1Think about it

Every few months a headline says that fusion has made a breakthrough. Laboratories report new records, governments announce power plants for the 2040s, and companies promise electricity even sooner. At the same time, the world needs to cut its carbon dioxide emissions now, and wind and solar power are growing fast.

If fusion is so powerful, why can nobody tell you the year when it will power your home?

Review: what Q means

In the chapter Inside ITER: the machine and fusion gain, you met the fusion gain $Q = P_\text{fusion} / P_\text{heating}$. ITER aims for $Q = 10$. What does this mean?

  • $Q$ is a ratio of two powers, not of two times. A long pulse is a different goal: WEST and EAST hold records for duration.

  • Right. ITER aims for $500\ \text{MW}$ of fusion power from $50\ \text{MW}$ of heating. $Q$ says nothing about electricity: today you will see why that matters for the energy system.

  • The $500\ \text{MW}$ is heat from fusion, and the $50\ \text{MW}$ is heating power into the plasma. Running ITER takes over $300\ \text{MW}$ of electricity, and it makes none.

  • That would be an engineering gain. $Q$ compares fusion power with heating power only. ITER will not produce electricity at all: its heat is released, not turned into power.

    3Investigate

    The world's energy challenge

    People use energy for heat, transport, industry and electricity. In 2024 the world used roughly $590\ \text{EJ}$ of primary energy (energy in the form we find it, such as coal, oil, wind or uranium), and more than four fifths of it still came from fossil fuels. Burning them releases carbon dioxide, the main cause of climate change. The IPCC concluded in 2018 that, to limit warming to $1.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$, net carbon dioxide emissions must reach net zero around 2050.

    Electricity is a growing part of the answer: electric cars, heat pumps and data centres all use it. In 2025, world electricity demand grew by $2.8\ \%$. An electricity grid must match supply and demand every second. Some sources are dispatchable: operators can switch them up or down when needed (gas, hydro, nuclear). Wind and solar power are variable: their output depends on the weather and the time of day. A system with much wind and solar also needs storage, flexible demand, strong grids or firm low-carbon power that runs whatever the weather.

      Review the theory summary.

      Results, plans and claims

      News about fusion mixes three kinds of statements. Learn to tell them apart.

      • An established result has already happened and has been measured, usually reported by a laboratory and checked by other scientists. Example: on 5 December 2022 the National Ignition Facility (NIF) released $3.15\ \text{MJ}$ from a fuel target hit by $2.05\ \text{MJ}$ of laser light.
      • An institutional plan is what a government, an international organisation or a public laboratory intends to do. Plans change: ITER's 2024 baseline moved deuterium–tritium operation to 2039.
      • A corporate claim is a goal or forecast announced by a private company. Companies need investors, so their announcements are also advertising. A claim is not necessarily false, but it is not yet evidence.

      A good reader also asks when a statement was made. Every changing fact in this unit carries its date.

      Uncertainties: engineering, money and time

      No fusion device has yet delivered electricity to a grid (as of November 2026). The engineering questions include materials that survive years of fast neutrons, breeding enough tritium, removing intense heat, and keeping a machine running reliably for months. Scientific gain $Q$ compares fusion power with heating power only; a power plant must also pay for its magnets, lasers, pumps and conversion losses.

      The economic questions are just as open. In 2006 ITER's construction was estimated at $5.9$ billion euros; later estimates of construction and operation together are $18\text{–}22$ billion euros. Nobody can yet give the price of fusion electricity: published figures for future power plants are estimates, not prices. Money comes from two directions: public funding (ITER, national programmes such as the UK's £2.5 billion announced in June 2025) and private investment. In a 2026 survey of 56 fusion companies, the Fusion Industry Association counted USD 14.24 billion raised in total, USD 4.48 billion of it in the year to July 2026.

        4Theory summary

        Key idea

        Fusion could add firm, low-carbon power to a future energy system. It would emit no carbon dioxide while running, and its fuel is plentiful. But it competes with sources that already work and are getting cheaper: wind, solar, hydro and fission. Whether fusion becomes part of the system depends on results that have not happened yet: reliable machines, enough tritium, durable materials and an affordable price. So the honest answer to the opening question is a conditional one: fusion could help, if these problems are solved. No fusion plant has yet delivered electricity, and the public prototype plans (STEP, 2040) come after the 2030 target, so climate plans cannot count on fusion for the cuts needed by 2050.

        Fuel: deuterium, lithium and tritium

        A deuterium–tritium (D–T) power plant burns two isotopes of hydrogen: ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}\ (3.5\ \text{MeV}) + \text{n}\ (14.1\ \text{MeV})$. Deuterium is in all water: about one hydrogen atom in 6420 in sea water is deuterium, about $0.035\ \text{g}$ in every litre. The energy per kilogram is enormous. In its last experiments in 2023, JET released $69.26\ \text{MJ}$ of heat in one pulse from only $0.21\ \text{mg}$ of fuel, about as much energy as $1.5\ \text{kg}$ of petrol gives when it burns.

        Tritium is the problem. It is radioactive, with a half-life of $12.3$ years (it decays to helium-3 by beta decay), so almost none exists in nature. The world's civilian stock was only about $25\ \text{kg}$ in 2024, mostly from Canadian fission reactors. A power plant must therefore breed its own tritium. A breeding blanket that contains lithium surrounds the plasma: a fusion neutron and a lithium-6 nucleus give a new tritium nucleus and helium, ${}^{6}_{3}\text{Li} + \text{n} \to {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}.$ The blanket must make slightly more than one new tritium nucleus for every one burned; this has not yet been shown at power-plant scale, and ITER will test small test blanket modules.

        Fusion and fission compared

        Property Fission Fusion (D–T)
        reaction heavy nuclei split light nuclei join
        fuel uranium deuterium, lithium
        in electricity (2025) $8.9\ \%$ none yet
        chain reaction yes, controlled no
        main waste spent fuel, long-lived activated structure

        In a fission reactor, a chain reaction of neutrons keeps going and must be controlled. A fusion plasma holds only a few grams of fuel at a time, and any disturbance cools it within seconds, so it cannot run away. The product of the reaction, helium, is not radioactive.

        Fusion still has real hazards. Tritium can escape into the environment, so it must be contained. The $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ neutrons make the steel and other materials of the machine radioactive. This neutron activation gives large volumes of low- and intermediate-level waste, but no long-lived high-level waste from the fuel. Most of the activity fades within about 100 years. Strong magnets, very cold helium and great heat are further, industrial hazards.

          Worked example

          Problem

          How much energy is released by the fusion of one kilogram of D–T fuel? Compare it with petrol, which gives $46.4\ \text{MJ}$ per kilogram when it burns.

          Step 1/7

          Given: One D–T reaction releases ${E_1 = 17.6\ \text{MeV}}$. The masses of the nuclei are about ${m_\text{D} = 2.014\ \text{u}}$ and ${m_\text{T} = 3.016\ \text{u}}$, with ${1\ \text{u} = 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}}$ and ${1\ \text{MeV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{J}}$. Petrol: ${e_\text{petrol} = 46.4\ \text{MJ/kg}}$. We want the energy per kilogram $e_\text{DT}$ and the ratio $e_\text{DT} : e_\text{petrol}$.

          Step 2/7

          Principle: Each reaction uses one deuterium and one tritium nucleus, so the fuel mass of one reaction is ${m_1 = m_\text{D} + m_\text{T}}$. The energy per kilogram is the energy of one reaction divided by the fuel mass of one reaction. The electrons' mass is very small and is left out.

          Step 3/7

          Formula: Solved in symbols:

          $$\begin{aligned} e_\text{DT} &= \frac{E_1}{m_\text{D} + m_\text{T}} \\[6pt] \text{ratio} &= \frac{e_\text{DT}}{e_\text{petrol}} \end{aligned}$$

          Step 4/7

          Unit conversions: The energy of one reaction in joules: ${E_1 = 17.6 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{J}}$ ${= 2.820 \cdot 10^{-12}\ \text{J}}$. The fuel mass in kilograms: ${m_1 = 5.030\ \text{u}}$ ${= 5.030 \cdot 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}}$ ${= 8.355 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}}$. Petrol: ${46.4\ \text{MJ/kg} = 4.64 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{J/kg}}$.

          Step 5/7

          Substitution: Energy per kilogram, then the ratio:

          $$\begin{aligned} e_\text{DT} &= \frac{2.820 \cdot 10^{-12}\ \text{J}}{8.355 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}} \\ &= 3.3747 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J/kg} \\[6pt] \text{ratio} &= \frac{3.3747 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J/kg}}{4.64 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{J/kg}} \\ &= 7.27 \cdot 10^{6} \end{aligned}$$

          Step 6/7

          Answer: One kilogram of D–T fuel releases about ${3.37 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J}}$, about seven million times as much as one kilogram of petrol. Three significant figures fit, because the reaction energy $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ has three.

          Step 7/7

          Reasonableness: JET's record pulse released $69.26\ \text{MJ}$ from $0.21\ \text{mg}$ of fuel, which is ${69.26 \cdot 10^{6}\ \text{J} : 0.21 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{kg}}$ ${\approx 3.3 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J/kg}}$, the same as our result. A chemical reaction releases a few electronvolts per molecule, a nuclear reaction millions of electronvolts, so a ratio of millions is expected. (ITER's website says "nearly four million times" compared with coal, oil or gas; such comparisons depend on the fuel chosen and on what mass is counted, but every version gives millions.)

          How much tritium does a power plant burn?

          A fusion power plant releases a fusion power of $1.0\ \text{GW}$ for one year, $3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{s}$. Each D–T reaction releases $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ and burns one tritium nucleus of mass $3.016\ \text{u}$.

          How many kilograms of tritium does the plant burn in a year? Compare your answer with the world's civilian stock of about $25\ \text{kg}$ (2024).

          $\text{kg}$
          Hint 1: a guiding question

          First find the energy released in a year: $E = Pt$.

          Hint 2: what is the connection

          The number of reactions is $N = E : E_1$, where $E_1$ is the energy of one reaction in joules.

          Hint 3: an intermediate step

          $E_1 = 17.6 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ $= 2.820 \cdot 10^{-12}\ \text{J}$ and $E = 3.156 \cdot 10^{16}\ \text{J}$, so $N = 1.119 \cdot 10^{28}$.

          Show the solution

          Given

          Fusion power ${P = 1.0\ \text{GW} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{W}}$, time ${t = 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{s}}$, energy of one reaction ${E_1 = 17.6\ \text{MeV}}$, mass of a tritium nucleus ${m_\text{T} = 3.016\ \text{u}}$. We want the mass of tritium $m$ burned in a year.

          Principle

          Every reaction burns one tritium nucleus. The number of reactions is the total energy divided by the energy of one reaction, and the mass is that number times the mass of one nucleus.

          Formula

          $$\begin{aligned} N &= \frac{Pt}{E_1} \\ m &= N m_\text{T} = \frac{Pt\, m_\text{T}}{E_1} \end{aligned}$$

          Unit conversions

          ${E_1 = 17.6 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{J}}$ ${= 2.820 \cdot 10^{-12}\ \text{J}}$ and ${m_\text{T} = 3.016 \cdot 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}}$ ${= 5.010 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}}$.

          Substitution

          $$\begin{aligned} N &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{W} \cdot 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{s}}{2.820 \cdot 10^{-12}\ \text{J}} \\ &= 1.119 \cdot 10^{28} \\[6pt] m &= 1.119 \cdot 10^{28} \cdot 5.010 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} \\ &= 56.1\ \text{kg} \end{aligned}$$

          Answer

          The plant burns about ${m \approx 56\ \text{kg}}$ of tritium a year. Two significant figures are enough, because the power $1.0\ \text{GW}$ has two.

          Reasonableness

          One gigawatt for a year is $3.2 \cdot 10^{16}\ \text{J}$; with $3.4 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J}$ per kilogram of D–T fuel this is about $94\ \text{kg}$ of fuel, and tritium is $3.016 : 5.030 \approx 60\ \%$ of that mass, about $56\ \text{kg}$. It is more than twice the world's civilian stock of about $25\ \text{kg}$: a power plant cannot buy its tritium, it must breed it.

          Optional Find the error
          A student writes: "In December 2022 the National Ignition Facility got $3.15\ \text{MJ}$ out from $2.05\ \text{MJ}$ in.So laser fusion now makes more electricity than it uses." Where is the error?

          • The numbers are right: $3.15\ \text{MJ}$ out from $2.05\ \text{MJ}$ of laser light, a target gain of about $1.5$. The error is the step from target gain to electricity.

          • Right. The gain of about $1.5$ compares fusion energy with the laser energy on the target. The lasers drew about $300\ \text{MJ}$ of electricity, so the whole facility used about a hundred times more energy than the target released, and it turned none of it into electricity.

          • The date is right: 5 December 2022. In 2025 NIF reached a larger yield, $8.6\ \text{MJ}$. The error is in the conclusion about electricity.

          • A gain above 1 at the target is a scientific milestone, not net electricity. The lasers drew about $300\ \text{MJ}$ from the grid, and NIF has no equipment to turn heat into electricity.

          Common misconception

          "Fusion is clean, so it makes no radioactive waste." This is false. The fuel's product, helium, is harmless, but tritium is radioactive and the neutrons activate the machine itself. The waste is short-lived compared with spent fission fuel, not absent.

          A second mistake is to read a plan or a company's goal as a result. "Will deliver electricity in 2028" is a claim about the future. Only a measured, checked event is an established result.

          6Check your understanding

          1. Concept: can a fusion plant run away?

          Why can a fusion plasma not run away like an uncontrolled fission chain reaction?

          • Right. The fuel is fed in continuously, a few grams at a time, and the plasma must be kept extremely hot. If anything goes wrong, it cools and the reactions stop. Tritium, activation and heat remain real hazards.

          • D–T fusion does make neutrons ($14.1\ \text{MeV}$ each); they activate the machine. But fusion is not a chain reaction: a neutron does not start the next fusion.

          • The magnets confine the plasma, but they are not a safety barrier against energy. The reason is the fuel: only grams of it are present, and the plasma cools and stops when disturbed.

          • The plasma is the hottest thing on Earth, over $100$ million kelvin. It is safe because it holds very little fuel and stops when it cools, not because it is cold.

          2. Calculation: tritium decays

          In 2024 the world's civilian stock of tritium was about $25\ \text{kg}$.If none were used and none were added, about how much would be left after ten years? The half-life of tritium is $12.3$ years.

          • Decay is not that fast. After $10$ years the factor is $(1/2)^{10/12.3} = 0.570$, so about $14\ \text{kg}$ is left.

          • Half of the stock is left after one half-life, $12.3$ years. Ten years is less than one half-life, so more than half is left: $25\ \text{kg} \cdot (1/2)^{10/12.3} \approx 14\ \text{kg}$.

          • Right. $m = 25\ \text{kg} \cdot (1/2)^{10/12.3} = 25\ \text{kg} \cdot 0.570 \approx 14\ \text{kg}$. Stored tritium loses about $5\ \%$ a year, another reason why a power plant must breed its own.

          • Tritium decays whether it is used or not: about $5\ \%$ a year. After ten years, $25\ \text{kg} \cdot (1/2)^{10/12.3} \approx 14\ \text{kg}$ remain.

          A website says: "Fusion power produces no radioactive waste at all." Which evaluation is best?

          • Helium is harmless, but the reaction also makes a fast neutron. Neutrons activate the materials of the machine, and the tritium fuel itself is radioactive.

          • Tritium is radioactive, with a half-life of $12.3$ years, and the neutrons activate the machine. Both must be managed.

          4. Write: evaluate a headline

          Imagine a news site writes in 2026: "Fusion breakthrough: clean energy for everyone by 2035!" Write five to seven sentences that evaluate the headline.           Model answer

          The headline is partly right, because fusion experiments have produced real results: NIF released more energy than its laser light delivered in 2022, and JET released $69\ \text{MJ}$ in one pulse in 2023. However, "by 2035" is only a plan or a claim, not a result, and no fusion device has yet delivered electricity to a grid. ITER, for example, plans deuterium–tritium operation only from 2039, and it will not make electricity. Fusion also has unsolved problems: breeding enough tritium, materials that survive the neutrons and an affordable cost. "Clean" is also too simple, because the machine becomes radioactive, although the waste is short-lived. A more accurate headline would be: "Fusion records grow, but no one can yet say when fusion electricity will come."
