Ideas for development

These workshops are waiting for a developer. You can take one as your own planning task: write it out with the planning template, test it and bring it to the teacher.

  • Idea for development

    How clean can you make water?

    Participants build a filter from sand, gravel and cloth and find out how clear they can make dirty water.

    Audience
    Lower secondary
    Topic
    Water and chemistry
    Duration
    about $30\,\text{min}$ (planned duration)
    Equipment
    Water, everyday items

    Idea: The groups get dirty water and materials such as gravel, sand, coffee filter paper and cotton cloth. They design a filter from a cut plastic bottle, test it and compare how clear the filtered water is. Finally they consider why clear water is still not safe to drink and how a waterworks cleans water.

    To develop before use

    • A recipe for dirty water that is safe and repeatable (for example soil and water).
    • A fair way to compare clarity, for example a line drawn on white paper that must be seen through a glass of water.
    • Background on the stages of water treatment from a reliable source.
    • Tidying up and disposing of the waste water.

    Safety notes

    • Filtered water is never drunk or tasted.
    • The plastic bottles are cut in advance, because a cut edge can be sharp.
    • A wet floor is dried at once.
    • Hands are washed after handling the dirty water and soil.
  • Idea for development

    Build the slowest possible ball run

    Participants build a track from cardboard and tape on which a ball rolls down as slowly as possible.

    Audience
    Primary school
    Topic
    Motion and energy
    Duration
    about $30\,\text{min}$ (planned duration)
    Equipment
    Everyday items

    Idea: Usually people try to make a track fast, but here the goal is the opposite. The groups build a track on a wall or a table on which a ball or a marble rolls for as long as possible. Timing is done with a phone's stopwatch. The challenge starts a discussion about friction, slope and energy. The opening challenge of Ahjo 2 is the same idea for upper secondary students and can be used as the basis for the workshop.

    To develop before use

    • An equipment list and a support for the track that does not need fixing to a wall.
    • An explanation for primary pupils of why the ball slows down in the bends.
    • Fair rules: the same starting height and ball size for everyone.

    Safety notes

    • Small balls are not suitable for children under three, and they are picked up from the floor so that no one slips.
    • Scissors and the utility knife only for the tutor.

    Ahjo 2: opening challenge · Weight, friction and the inclined plane · Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle

  • Idea for development

    The oddities of air pressure

    A glass of water held upside down by a piece of card, suction cups that will not come apart, and other experiments where the air pushes.

    Audience
    Lower secondary
    Topic
    Air pressure
    Duration
    about $30\,\text{min}$ (planned duration)
    Equipment
    Water, everyday items

    Idea: The groups go round stations where air pressure does something surprising: a glass of water is turned upside down with a card over it, suction cups are pressed together and pulled apart, and a straw is held in a glass of water with a finger over its top. At every station they predict first. Finally the tutor explains that air pushes in all directions and that pressure is force per unit area.

    To develop before use

    • Instructions and equipment for the stations that work reliably.
    • A picture of pressure that suits lower secondary students, and its link to Ruukkilabra's chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.
    • Tidying up: the stations use water.

    Safety notes

    • No experiments that need heating (for example crushing a can by heating it), because they cause a risk of burns.
    • Plastic glasses.
    • A wet floor is dried at once.

    Pressure and hydrostatic pressure

  • Idea for development

    Program your first robot

    Participants program a small robot or microcontroller to make its first movement or light.

    Audience
    Lower secondary
    Topic
    Programming and robotics
    Duration
    about $45\,\text{min}$ (planned duration)
    Equipment
    Computer or tablet, robot or microcontroller

    Idea: The participants get a ready, simple program that they change: first a light blinks faster, then the robot moves forward and turns. The aim is a first success within a few minutes and then a change of their own. Ahjo Makerspace's Arduino guides and the robot's test program are a good basis.

    To develop before use

    • Which devices are available. Ahjo's equipment is still on its way, so the devices are agreed with the teacher.
    • A ready starter program and instructions that a lower secondary student can follow.
    • A backup plan if a computer or the connection to the device does not work.

    Safety notes

    • Only low voltage and batteries or USB power.
    • A marked area on the floor for a moving robot, so that no one trips.

    Ahjo: Arduino guide

  • Idea for development

    Into the invisible world with a microscope

    Participants examine everyday samples with a microscope or a magnifying glass: salt, sugar, fabric and a plant leaf.

    Audience
    Primary school
    Topic
    Living nature
    Duration
    about $30\,\text{min}$ (planned duration)
    Equipment
    Microscope

    Idea: The groups first guess what a familiar thing looks like magnified and draw their guess. Then they look at the sample with a microscope or a magnifying glass and draw what they saw. The workshop practises careful observation and telling an observation from an assumption.

    To develop before use

    • What kind of microscopes or phone clip-on magnifiers are available.
    • Samples that show up well at low magnification.
    • Using living organisms: if they are used, they are returned to nature.

    Safety notes

    • Glass slides can break. Use plastic ones if possible.
    • Do not touch the hot part near the microscope's lamp.
    • Samples are not tasted.
    • A mirror-lit microscope is never pointed at the sun.

How to plan and test a workshop

Use the workshop planning template. The ready workshops show what a finished plan looks like.

  1. Choose a phenomenon and an audience

    Which phenomenon interests you, and who is the workshop for? A primary pupil needs shorter steps and more doing than a lower secondary student.

  2. Write the goal in one sentence

    What can the participant do or notice after the workshop? For example: ”The participant notices that the bulb lights only when the circuit is closed.”

  3. Plan the hook

    Start with a phenomenon or a question that sparks curiosity in under a minute.

  4. Plan the activity

    A task where the participants predict, try and observe. First success within a few minutes, then a challenge.

  5. Write the questions and the closing

    Three good questions and a closing in which the participants say what they noticed.

  6. Make a safety plan and an equipment list

    Go through the safety checklist. Show the plan to the teacher before testing.

  7. Test

    Try the workshop first yourself and then with other tutors or friends. Time it and ask for feedback with the feedback form.

  8. Improve

    Write in your portfolio what happened in the test and what you changed. Changing the plan after a test is the most important part of planning.

Working time. A workload of about $4 \cdot 75\,\text{min}$ is set aside for planning and testing. Taking a ready workshop into use takes less time than planning your own, so when you use a ready workshop, make it your own: add your own hook, your own differentiation or a new step.

Adding a new workshop to the bank

When a workshop you planned has been tested and the teacher has approved it, it can be added to the bank. In Ruukkilabra each workshop is its own file, so adding one needs no changes to the site's code.

This part is done when

  • you have a written workshop plan or an adapted ready workshop
  • you have tested the workshop at least once with others
  • you have changed the plan based on the test
  • the plan, the test results and the changes are in your portfolio
