## Quick start

You make a small robot that moves without a program. It gets going in about ten minutes, and all you need is a toothbrush head, a vibration motor, a coin cell and double-sided tape. No devices are used, so no device induction is needed.

1. With double-sided tape, fix the vibration motor on the back of the brush head, near its front end.
2. Tape the coin cell behind the motor with its plus side up, so that one of the motor's leads stays under the cell.
3. Bend the motor's other lead onto the top of the cell and press it in place with a small piece of tape. The motor starts to vibrate.
4. Put the robot on the table. It sets off.

### Try

Make the robot move on the table.

### Modify

Give the robot its own look and build it a track or an arena from paper or cardboard.

### Challenge yourself

Measure the robot's speed with three repetitions and find out how the angle of the bristles affects it. The instructions are below in the Learn more part.

## Challenge

Build a robot that moves by shaking. On the shaft of the vibration motor there is an off-centre weight that makes the whole robot vibrate. When the bristles slant, every vibration pushes the robot a little forward. The small robot shows how a motor turns electrical energy into motion and how the structure steers the motion.

The work takes about 30 minutes. It is worth setting aside another half hour for building an arena and a race.

## Learning goals

After the work you can

- connect a motor to a battery and switch the power off
- explain how the off-centre weight and the slanting bristles make the robot move
- change the robot's structure and observe the effect of the change
- measure a speed with three repetitions and calculate the mean.

## Starting level and prior skills

You need no earlier experience. Calculating speed with $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$ helps in the challenge. If needed, revise the chapter [Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs](fy1-5.html).

## Equipment and materials

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Toothbrush head with the handle cut off | 1 |
| Vibration motor, coin or cylinder type, $3\,\text{V}$ | 1 |
| Coin cell CR2032 ($3\,\text{V}$) | 1 |
| Double-sided tape and ordinary tape | 1 roll |
| Ruler or tape measure and a stopwatch | 1 + 1 |
| Pipe cleaners, paper or stickers for decoration | as you like |

Your teacher cuts the toothbrush handles off in advance. The leads of a vibration motor are often already stripped.

## Safety and induction

The work needs no device induction. Still, follow these precautions:

- A coin cell is dangerous if swallowed. Keep the cells in their box and give them back to your teacher at the end of the lesson.
- Do not join the motor's leads together on top of the cell. A short circuit drains the cell and makes it warm.
- When you finish, remove the motor's lead from the cell, so that the cell does not run down.

## Steps

1. Check that the handle of the brush head has been cut off and the edge is smooth. If the edge is sharp, ask your teacher to sand it.
2. Cut a piece of double-sided tape the size of the back of the brush head and stick it on.
3. Fix the vibration motor on the tape at the front end of the brush. If the motor has a visible spinning off-centre weight, it must be able to spin freely.
4. Bend the motor's leads so that one runs under the place of the cell and the other stays up.
5. Put the coin cell on the tape behind the motor with its plus side up, so that the lower lead stays under the cell. The bottom of the cell is the minus terminal.
6. Bend the upper lead onto the top of the cell, the plus terminal, and fix it with a small piece of tape. The motor starts to vibrate. A vibration motor turns either way, so the order of the leads does not matter.
7. Put the robot on a flat table. If it falls over, move the cell so that the centre of mass is in the middle of the brush.
8. When you finish, remove the upper lead from the top of the cell.

## Test and measure

- How it works: on the shaft of the motor there is an off-centre weight whose centre of mass is not on the shaft. When the weight spins, it shakes the whole robot up and down. Every time the robot gets lighter, the slanting bristles spring back and push it in the direction the bristles slant.
- Mark a distance of $50\,\text{cm}$ on the table. Measure how long the robot takes to travel the distance, and repeat three times. Calculate the speed $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$ for each repetition and their mean.
- Challenge yourself: compare two brushes whose bristles are at different angles, or cut the bristles of one brush shorter. Measure the speed of each with three repetitions and present the results as a table. How does the angle of the bristles affect the speed?

## If something does not work

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The motor does not start | A lead does not touch the terminal of the cell | Press the lead more firmly with tape. Check that the lower lead is stripped where it lies under the cell. |
| The motor does not start | The off-centre weight hits the tape | Move the motor so that the weight spins freely. |
| The robot goes round in circles | The weight is on one side | Move the cell or the motor nearer the middle. |
| The robot falls over | The centre of mass is too high | Put the cell as low and as central as you can. |
| The robot vibrates on the spot | The bristles are straight | Slant the bristles the same way by pressing the brush against the table and pulling it backwards. |

## Extension

1. A switch. Add a paper switch to the robot as in the [paper circuit card](ahjo-valokortti.html), so that you can switch the power off without removing the lead.
2. A robot arena. Build a cardboard arena with obstacles together with your group. Who can get their robot through the route by changing only its structure?
3. A two-motor robot. Think how a robot with two brushes and two motors could be steered to turn. Sketch the solution before you build.

## Document your work

Record in your project diary

- a photo or a short video of the robot moving
- the results as a table: distance, time and speed of each repetition, and the mean
- one change to the structure and what followed from it.

## For the teacher: preparation

- This is a draft: the guide has not been tried in Ahjo. Build one robot yourself before the lesson and check that the vibration motor you have chosen starts with a CR2032 cell.
- Cut the toothbrush handles off in advance with side cutters or a small saw and sand the sharp edges. The students need no cutting tools in this work.
- There are two types of vibration motor: a flat coin motor and a cylinder motor with a visible off-centre weight on the end of its shaft. Both will do. The cylinder type often gives stronger motion.
- Hand out the coin cells at the start of the lesson and collect them at the end. A swallowed coin cell is life-threatening, so keep them in a locked box.
- The work suits lesson 11 of Ahjo 1 as an alternative task or an extra challenge. It links the work with design and the physics chapter [Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs](fy1-5.html).
- The bristle robot is a common makerspace starter project. The text and figure of the guide were made for Ruukkilabra.
