Newspapers, websites and signs in Provence often write about ITER and fusion. In this lesson you read a short
French news text, written for this course in the style of a regional newspaper, with real and checked facts. You
read it without a dictionary and without a translation app.

## Why you understand more than you think

You can already recognise many French words, especially through English, Swedish and international scientific vocabulary. English took a very large number of words
from French and Latin, Swedish took many from French too (*trottoar* from *<span lang="fr">trottoir</span>*, *paraply* from *<span lang="fr">parapluie</span>*,
*byrå* from *<span lang="fr">bureau</span>*), and Finnish took international words through Swedish (*insinööri* from Swedish *ingenjör*,
which comes from French *<span lang="fr">ingénieur</span>*). Science uses the same international words everywhere: *<span lang="fr">plasma</span>*,
*<span lang="fr">tokamak</span>*, *<span lang="fr">physiciens</span>*, *<span lang="fr">projet</span>*.

Good readers of a foreign language use five strategies:

1. **Start with what is easy:** the title, the place, the names, the dates and the numbers. They tell you the topic
   before you read a single sentence.
2. **Use look-alike words** in English, Swedish or Finnish. Read them aloud if the spelling hides them.
3. **Use word endings.** Many French endings have a regular English partner: *<span lang="fr">‑ité</span>* = *‑ity* (<span lang="fr">électricité</span>),
   *<span lang="fr">‑ique</span>* = *‑ic* (<span lang="fr">scientifique</span>), *<span lang="fr">‑eur</span>* = *‑or* (<span lang="fr">réacteur</span>), *<span lang="fr">‑ment</span>* = *‑ly* (<span lang="fr">seulement</span>, only).
4. **Guess from the context.** What does the sentence need? In <span lang="fr">la machine utilise des aimants très puissants pour garder le plasma loin des parois</span>,
   the *<span lang="fr">aimants</span>* hold a hot gas, so they must be **magnets**.
5. **Skip what you do not need.** You rarely need every word to get the message.

## False friends

Some French words look like English words but mean something else. They are called **false friends**:

| French | Means (and not) |
|---|---|
| <span lang="fr">actuel, actuellement</span> | current, currently (not *actual, actually*) |
| <span lang="fr">une expérience</span> | an experiment, or an experience |
| <span lang="fr">un milliard</span> | a billion, $10^{9}$ (not *a million*) |
| <span lang="fr">un billion</span> | $10^{12}$, a trillion (not *a billion*) |
| <span lang="fr">éventuellement</span> | possibly (not *eventually*) |
| <span lang="fr">une librairie</span> | a bookshop (not *a library*) |
| <span lang="fr">le calendrier</span> | a calendar, but also a schedule |

When a look-alike gives a sentence a strange meaning, check it against the context.
