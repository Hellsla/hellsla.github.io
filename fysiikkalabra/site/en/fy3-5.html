Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy

Why does a bicycle pump get hot when you pump up a tyre, even though nothing is heating the pump?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • distinguish heat, work and internal energy: heat and work transfer energy, while internal energy is a property of the system.
  • apply the first law of thermodynamics.

1Think about it

You pump a bicycle tyre to a high pressure. At the end, the lower end of the pump, where the air is compressed, feels warm in your hand. After the pump has rested for a while, it is at room temperature again. On the other hand, when you let air out of the tyre through the valve, the valve feels cool, even though nothing is cooling it.

Where does the air in the pump get extra energy if it is not heated, and where does that energy go when the pump cools down?

  • The amount of substance is a state variable of its own. The temperature can rise even though the number of particles stays the same; then the same particles move faster on average.

  • Correct: according to the micro model, temperature tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles. A higher temperature means faster-moving particles.

  • A single particle does not have a temperature. Temperature describes the average kinetic energy of a large group of particles.

Assumptions of the model

The numbers are values from the model, not measurements from a real pump. The gas in the model corresponds to $0.50\,\text{g}$ of air: its internal energy increases by $0.359\,\text{J}$ for each kelvin. The gas has $40$ particles that move in a plane and do not collide with each other. Air molecules also rotate, so each particle has rotational energy in addition to kinetic energy, which the picture does not show. A fixed wall sends a particle that hits it off in a random direction and redistributes its energy between motion and rotation; an insulated wall does not change the total energy of the particle. In this way the gas stays in equilibrium, and a slow insulated push corresponds to a slow compression of air without heat transfer. For this, physics gives $T_2 = T_1 (V_1/V_2)^{\gamma - 1} = 293\,\text{K} \cdot (4/3)^{0.4} \approx 329\,\text{K}$, when $\gamma \approx 1.4$ for air; the simulation gives $330\,\text{K}$. The small difference is mainly because there are only $40$ particles: with another random initial state the work would vary by about $11$–$15\,\text{J}$. In a real pump the piston is much slower than the molecules (about $500\,\text{m/s}$), so there the difference arises mainly from whether heat has time to be transferred out.

How the calculation works

A particle that hits the piston bounces off like a ball off a bat: if the piston moves towards it at speed $u$, the speed of the particle in the direction of the piston increases by $2u$, $v_x' = 2u - v_x$. The work $W$ of the piston is the kinetic energy the particles gain in this way, equal to the force of the piston times the distance. When the piston is pulled out, the particles bounce off slower and $W$ is negative. In an insulated cylinder $Q = 0$ and $\Delta U = W$. A conducting wall is at room temperature $293\,\text{K}$: a particle that hits it bounces off with less energy if the gas is warmer than the room, and with more if the gas is colder. The energy transferred in this way is the quantity of heat $Q$, and $\Delta U = Q + W$. The slow piston moves about $50$ times slower than the particles on average, the fast one only about $3.5$ times slower. The particles hit a fast piston at a greater relative speed, and the energy does not have time to spread evenly, so the gas resists the piston more and over the same distance the work is greater; in an insulated cylinder the temperature rises by exactly as much more as the work, $\Delta T = W/C$. The temperature is calculated from the total energy of the particles and the pressure $p/p_0$ from the temperature and the volume.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    In chapter Work and energy, work was described as a way of transferring energy, and Joule's experiment showed that mechanical work can heat water: the work done by the weight increased the internal energy of the water, even though the water was not heated. Water does not contain "heat". The internal energy $U$ of a system is the sum of the kinetic energy of its particles and the energy of the interactions between the particles. In chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy, this energy was called thermal energy; a more precise name is internal energy. Temperature describes how strongly the particles move because of thermal motion: in a gas, it tells you the average kinetic energy of the particles. When the temperature of a substance rises and its state of matter does not change, the particles move more strongly and the internal energy increases. Even so, temperature and internal energy are not the same thing, because when the state of matter changes, the internal energy changes even though the temperature stays the same: when, for example, pure ice melts at normal pressure, the internal energy increases, but the temperature stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Internal energy also depends on the amount of substance: a big lake contains much more internal energy than a cup of hot tea, even though the lake is colder.

    You can change the internal energy of a system in two ways. The first is work: when the piston of a pump compresses air, the surroundings do work on the gas, and the internal energy of the gas increases, because the moving piston hits the particles like a bat hits a ball and they bounce off faster. In the simulation you can see this at the particle level. This is why the pump gets hot. The second is heat: when two objects at different temperatures touch, energy transfers by itself from the hotter one to the colder one until the temperatures are the same. Energy transferred in this way is called heat, and the amount of energy transferred is the quantity of heat $Q$. So heat is a transfer of energy, not a property of matter: an object has a temperature and internal energy, but it does not have heat.

    Energy is conserved. When energy is transferred into a system as heat or work and the kinetic or potential energy of the system does not change, its internal energy increases by exactly the amount of energy transferred, and when energy is transferred out, the internal energy decreases by the same amount. This is the first law of thermodynamics: the law of conservation of energy written for heat and work. An isolated system is a closed system into which, and out of which, no energy is transferred either as heat or as work. Its total energy stays constant, even if energy changes form inside it.

    First law of thermodynamics

    The change in internal energy is the sum of the quantity of heat transferred into the system and the work done on the system:

    $$\Delta U = Q + W$$

    where $\Delta U$ is the change in internal energy (J), $Q$ is the quantity of heat transferred into the system (J) and $W$ is the work done on the system by the surroundings (J). The signs tell you the direction: $Q$ is positive when heat is transferred into the system and negative when it is transferred out; $W$ is positive when the surroundings do work on the system (for example, compress a gas) and negative when the system does work on the surroundings (the gas expands and pushes the piston). All the terms are in joules, the same unit as work in chapter Work and energy, and the work done by a piston on a gas is calculated in the same way as there, as the product of force and distance.

    Common misconception

    In everyday language we say that an object "has heat" or that heat is "stored". In physics, heat is the transfer of energy because of a temperature difference, and what is stored in an object is internal energy. Another mistake is to treat temperature and internal energy as the same thing: a hot spark has a temperature of thousands of degrees, but its internal energy is small, because it contains very little matter. A third mistake is to think that internal energy can be changed only by heating. The air in a pump gets hot without any heat source, because work is done on it.

    Summary

    • The internal energy $U$ is the sum of the kinetic energy of the particles of a system and the energy of the interactions between the particles. It increases when the temperature rises while the state of matter stays the same, and it also depends on the amount of substance. Temperature and internal energy are not the same thing.
    • Heat is the transfer of energy from hotter to colder because of a temperature difference; the energy transferred is the quantity of heat $Q$. An object does not "have heat".
    • Heat and work change internal energy: $\Delta U = Q + W$. Energy transferred in is positive and energy transferred out is negative.
    • Energy is conserved: this is the first law of thermodynamics. The total energy of an isolated system stays constant.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A pump cylinder contains air. The piston is pushed down, so the surroundings do $150\,\text{J}$ of work on the air. At the same time, a quantity of heat of $40\,\text{J}$ is transferred from the warming air through the cylinder walls to the surroundings. By how much does the internal energy of the air change?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The air is the system. The surroundings do work on the system, so $W = +150\,\text{J}$. Heat is transferred out of the system, so $Q = -40\,\text{J}$. The quantity to find is the change in internal energy $\Delta U$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Energy is conserved: the internal energy of the system changes by exactly the amount of energy transferred into it as heat and work. This is the first law of thermodynamics, and it applies because the air is a clearly bounded system, and energy crosses its boundary only as work and heat.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. In the first law, the quantity to find $\Delta U$ is already alone on the left-hand side, so the equation does not need rearranging:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta U &= Q + W \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. None needed: both $Q$ and $W$ are given in joules, and the result is wanted in joules. The signs, however, must be decided: energy into the system is positive and energy out of the system is negative.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their signs:

      $$\begin{aligned} &\Delta U = Q + W \\ &= -40\,\text{J} + 150\,\text{J} \\ &= 110\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The internal energy of the air increases by $110\,\text{J}$. The result is given in whole joules, because both the work and the heat were given in whole joules.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The work brought $150\,\text{J}$ into the air and only $40\,\text{J}$ left as heat, so the internal energy must increase and the temperature of the air must rise. If the same $150\,\text{J}$ of work were done so slowly that all the energy from the work had time to leave as heat, then $Q = -150\,\text{J}$ and $\Delta U = 0$, and the temperature would not change. Now less heat leaves, so the result lies between this limiting case and the work alone, $150\,\text{J}$.

      Optional Find the error
      A quantity of heat of $250\,\text{J}$ is transferred into the gas in a cylinder, and the gas expands, doing $90\,\text{J}$ of work on the piston. A student calculates: $\Delta U = Q + W = 250\,\text{J} + 90\,\text{J} = 340\,\text{J}$.Where is the error?

      • Correct. In the formula $\Delta U = Q + W$, the work $W$ is the work done on the system by the surroundings. When the gas itself pushes the piston, energy leaves the gas, so $W$ is negative.

      • Heat and work are two ways of transferring energy, and both are measured in joules. So they can be added together, as long as the signs give the direction correctly.

      • $Q$ tells you the direction of the heat, not of the motion. Heat is transferred into the gas, so $Q = +250\,\text{J}$ is correct. The error is in the other term.

      • Check in which direction energy is transferred as work. An expanding gas does work on the surroundings, so the work takes energy away from the gas instead of bringing it in.

      the total change in internal energy $\Delta U$ in joules

      $= 30\,\text{J}$

The result is positive because more came in than went out, and the gas warms up.

          Energy is conserved, so $\Delta U = Q + W$. The signs show the direction: energy into the system is positive and energy out of the system is negative. Heat leaving the air decreases the internal energy, so $Q$ is negative.

          Formula

          $\Delta U = Q + W$, and the quantity to find $\Delta U$ is already on the left-hand side of the equation

          Conversions

          None needed: both transfers are given in joules.

          Substitution

          a) The student gave the heat a plus sign, although heat is transferred out of the air and the correct value is $Q = -10\,\text{J}$. The sign of the work, $W = +30\,\text{J}$, is correct. b)

          $\Delta U$$= -10\,\text{J} + 30\,\text{J}$
          $= 20\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $20\,\text{J}$

          In whole joules, because the transfers are given in whole joules.

          Reasonableness

          The work brought $30\,\text{J}$ into the air and $10\,\text{J}$ left as heat, so the internal energy must increase by less than the work: $20\,\text{J} < 30\,\text{J}$. The student's result was larger than the work, although energy left the air, which cannot be right.

        1. Exercise 6-16

          The nozzle of a bicycle pump is blocked with a finger, so no air can escape. The piston is pushed very slowly, and it pushes the air a distance of $0.15\,\text{m}$ with an average force of $120\,\text{N}$. The temperature of the air in the pump stays at room temperature throughout, and the air can be treated as an ideal gas.

          How large a quantity of heat $Q$ is transferred into the air? Give the answer with its sign and justify why the internal energy of the air does not change.

          J
          Answer

          $-18\,\text{J}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the system is the air in the pump (the amount does not change); $F = 120\,\text{N}$, $s = 0.15\,\text{m}$, the temperature stays constant

          Find

          the quantity of heat $Q$ transferred into the air, with its sign, and why $\Delta U$ does not change

          Principle

          Energy is conserved, so $\Delta U = Q + W$. According to the problem, the temperature stays constant and the amount of air does not change, so the kinetic energy of the particles and their number are unchanged, and because the air can be treated as an ideal gas, its internal energy does not change: $\Delta U = 0$. Then the energy brought in by the work must be transferred out as heat: $Q = -W$.

          Formula

          $W = Fs$ and $\Delta U = Q + W = 0$, so $Q = -W = -Fs$

          Conversions

          None needed: the force is in newtons and the distance in metres, so the product is in joules.

          Substitution

          $W$$= 120\,\text{N} \cdot 0.15\,\text{m}$
          $= 18\,\text{J}$

          (work is done on the air, so $W > 0$). So $Q = -18\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $-18\,\text{J}$

          Two significant figures, like the data $0.15\,\text{m}$ and $120\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          Because the piston is pushed slowly, the energy brought in by the work has time to be transferred as heat through the pump walls to the room, and the temperature stays constant. Balance: $-18\,\text{J} + 18\,\text{J} = 0$. Limiting case: if the piston were pushed quickly, there would be no time for heat to be transferred, $Q \approx 0$, and the pump would get hot.

        2. Exercise 6-17

          In a physics lesson at upper secondary school, a measurement like Joule's experiment is done. A $5.0\,\text{kg}$ weight hanging on a string descends slowly at a constant speed and turns a paddle wheel in water. The water container is very well insulated. The weight is always lifted back up and released, 12 times in total.

          From what height must the weight descend each time so that the internal energy of the water increases by $1.0\,\text{kJ}$ in total? Assume that the energy lost by the weight is transferred to the water as work done by the paddle wheel.

          m
          Answer

          $1.7\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the system is the water; mass of the weight $m = 5.0\,\text{kg}$, number of descents $n = 12$, $\Delta U = 1.0\,\text{kJ}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, insulated container

          Find

          the height $h$ of one descent

          Principle

          Energy is conserved, so $\Delta U = Q + W$. The container is insulated, so no heat is transferred and $Q = 0$. The weight descends at a constant speed, so the rope pulls it with a force $F = G = mg$, and when it descends a height $h$ the rope and the paddle wheel do work $W_1 = Fh = mgh$ on the water. Then $\Delta U = W$.

          Formula

          work of one descent $W_1 = mgh$, so $\Delta U = nmgh$ and $h = \dfrac{\Delta U}{nmg}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta U$$= 1.0\,\text{kJ}$
          $= 1000\,\text{J}$

          Finally $1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\tfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$, so the unit of the quotient is the metre.

          Substitution

          $h$$= \dfrac{1000\,\text{J}}{12 \cdot 5.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{1000\,\text{J}}{588.6\,\text{N}}$
          $= 1.6989\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $1.7\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures, like the mass $5.0\,\text{kg}$ and the energy $1.0\,\text{kJ}$.

          Reasonableness

          A drop of about 1.7 metres fits in a classroom. The work of one descent is

          $5.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.7\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 83\,\text{J}$

          and $12 \cdot 83\,\text{J} \approx 1000\,\text{J}$. Joule's experiment needs many repetitions, because one kilogram of water would warm up by only $\tfrac{1000\,\text{J}}{4190\,\text{J/K}} \approx 0.24\,\text{K}$.

        3. Exercise 6-18

          A gas in a cylinder is taken in two different ways from the same initial temperature to the same final temperature; the amount of gas does not change. The gas can be treated as an ideal gas, in which the energy of the interactions between the particles is negligibly small. In way A a quantity of heat of $400\,\text{J}$ is transferred to the gas, and the gas does $150\,\text{J}$ of work on the surroundings. In way B the gas does $60\,\text{J}$ of work on the surroundings.

          How large a quantity of heat is transferred to the gas in way B? Justify why the change in the internal energy of the gas is the same in both ways.

          J
          Answer

          $310\,\text{J}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the system is the gas; way A: $Q_\text{A} = +400\,\text{J}$, $W_\text{A} = -150\,\text{J}$ (the gas does work); way B: $W_\text{B} = -60\,\text{J}$; same initial and final temperature, same amount of gas

          Find

          the quantity of heat $Q_\text{B}$, and why $\Delta U$ is the same in both ways

          Principle

          Energy is conserved, so $\Delta U = Q + W$ for both ways. According to the problem, the temperature and the amount of gas are the same in the initial and final states, so the kinetic energy of the particles is the same, and because the energy of the interactions is negligible in an ideal gas, the internal energy is the same too. So $\Delta U$ is the same in both ways, even though the heat and work differ: they depend on the path, while the internal energy depends only on the initial and final states.

          Formula

          $\Delta U_\text{A} = Q_\text{A} + W_\text{A}$ and $\Delta U_\text{B} = \Delta U_\text{A}$, so

          $Q_\text{B}$$= \Delta U_\text{A} - W_\text{B}$
          $= Q_\text{A} + W_\text{A} - W_\text{B}$

          Conversions

          None needed: all energies are given in joules.

          Substitution

          $\Delta U_\text{A}$$= 400\,\text{J} + (-150\,\text{J})$
          $= 250\,\text{J}$

          So

          $Q_\text{B}$$= 250\,\text{J} - (-60\,\text{J})$
          $= 310\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $310\,\text{J}$

          ; in the addition the result is as precise as the data, which are given in whole joules.

          Reasonableness

          In way B the gas does less work, so the same increase in internal energy $250\,\text{J}$ needs less heat than in way A: $310\,\text{J} < 400\,\text{J}$. Balance:

          $310\,\text{J} + (-60\,\text{J})$
          $= 250\,\text{J}$
          $= \Delta U_\text{A}$
