Force as a measure of interaction and weight G = mg

Why does an astronaut weigh only one sixth as much on the Moon as on Earth, even though no part of the astronaut has gone missing?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why force measures the strength of an interaction and how a force meter measures it in newtons.
  • tell weight and mass apart.
  • calculate the weight of an object from its mass on different celestial bodies.

Prior knowledge

  • Direct proportionality and a straight line through the origin, for example $s = vt$ (FY1)
  • Everyday experience: a heavy and a light object, a stretching spring or rubber band (everyday knowledge, not a previous lesson)

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 6.

1Think about it

A force meter is basically just a spring. Hang a $10\,\text{kg}$ sample box on it on Earth, and the spring stretches a certain distance. Take the same box and the same meter to the Moon: the spring stretches much less. The box contains as much matter as before, and the spring has not changed either. Something else has changed.

What does the spring of a force meter actually measure, and what changes in it when the place changes?

Optional Revision from FY1: direct proportionality
A cyclist rides at a constant speed and travels $2.0\,\text{km}$ in 6.0 minutes. How far does the cyclist ride at the same speed in 18 minutes?

  • You added the difference in times ($12\,\text{min}$) to the distance. In direct proportionality the quantities change by the same ratio, not by the same amount: the time triples, so the distance triples too, $3 \cdot 2.0\,\text{km} = 6.0\,\text{km}$.

  • The time grows from 6 minutes to 18 minutes, that is, it triples, not doubles. Multiply the distance by the same number: $3 \cdot 2.0\,\text{km} = 6.0\,\text{km}$.

  • The distance in kilometres is not the same number as the time in minutes. The speed is $2.0\,\text{km} / 6.0\,\text{min}$, that is, one third of a kilometre per minute; in 18 minutes the distance is $6.0\,\text{km}$.

  • Correct. In uniform motion, distance is directly proportional to time, $s = vt$: three times the time, three times the distance, $3 \cdot 2.0\,\text{km} = 6.0\,\text{km}$. You need the same reasoning in this lesson with the spring and weight.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    When two objects act on each other, they interact. For example, the Earth pulls an apple towards itself. Force tells you how strong the interaction is: the greater the force, the stronger the interaction. The unit of force is the newton (N), and about 1 N is the weight of a 100 g apple on Earth. At this stage only the magnitude of the force matters, and in the figures an arrow shows the direction in which the force points.

    You can measure a force with a force meter. It contains a spring that stretches more the greater the force acting on it.

    The extension is directly proportional to the force as long as the spring is not stretched too far: double the force stretches the spring double the distance. The graph (force, extension) is therefore a straight line through the origin, like the directly proportional models in FY1, for example uniform motion $s = v t$. If 5.0 N stretches a spring by 2.0 cm, then 10 N stretches it by 4.0 cm and 2.5 N stretches it by 1.0 cm.

    The scale is calibrated by hanging identical small objects on the spring, each with a mass of about $100\,\text{g}$, that is, a weight of about 1 N:

    • One object stretches the spring by a certain distance, and the position is marked 1 N.
    • Each object pulls on the spring equally hard, so two objects pull with double the force and stretch it double the distance. This position is marked 2 N, and so on.
    • After that you can read the force directly in newtons from the extension.

    The weight of an object is a measure of the strength of the interaction between the Earth and the object. It is a force, and its unit is the newton. Mass is something quite different: it tells you how much matter an object contains, and its unit is the kilogram. Mass is the same everywhere, but weight depends on where the object is. The Moon is a much smaller celestial body than the Earth, and the interaction between the Moon and the astronaut is weaker than between the Earth and the astronaut, so the astronaut weighs less on the Moon.

    Weight

    Two identical objects stretch the spring of a force meter twice as much as one, so weight is directly proportional to mass:

    $$G = m g$$

    where $G$ is the weight (N), $m$ the mass (kg) and $g$ the acceleration of free fall (m/s²), $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ on the surface of the Earth. When mass is in kilograms and $g$ in m/s², the weight comes out in newtons, because 1 N = $1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$ (this is justified in FY4). So the weight of a 1 kg mass on Earth is 9.81 N. Elsewhere $g$ is different. The values in the table apply to the surface of the celestial body (for Jupiter, the level of the cloud tops):

    Celestial body $g$ at the surface (m/s²)
    Moon 1.62
    Mars 3.71
    Earth 9.81
    Jupiter 24.8

    The Moon's $g$ is about one sixth of the Earth's value (1.62/9.81 ≈ 0.17 ≈ 1/6), so the same mass weighs about one sixth as much on the Moon as on Earth. This answers the question about the astronaut at the beginning.

    In the graph, weight is plotted as a function of mass. For each celestial body, weight is directly proportional to mass, so the graph is a straight line through the origin. The greater $g$ is, the steeper the line: for the same mass, weight is greatest on Jupiter and smallest on the Moon.

    Common misconception

    In everyday language people say "I weigh 70 kilos", but the kilogram is a unit of mass, not of weight. In physics the mass is $70\,\text{kg}$ and the weight on Earth is about 690 N. Another mistake is to think that an object's mass changes on the Moon. The mass stays the same, and only the weight decreases. Note also that a force meter measures force, not mass: its reading changes when the same object is taken to another place. A bathroom scale still shows kilograms, because it measures force and its scale has been made on Earth by dividing the force by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. On the Moon such a scale would show only about one sixth of the mass.

    Summary

    • Force tells you the strength of an interaction, and its unit is the newton (N). The extension of a force meter's spring is directly proportional to the force.
    • Weight is a force: $G = m g$. Mass tells you how much matter an object contains, and it is the same everywhere.
    • $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ on the surface of the Earth, so the weight of a 1 kg mass on Earth is 9.81 N. On the Moon $g = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$ and on Mars $3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      A research robot has a mass of $120\,\text{kg}$. What is its weight on Earth and on Mars?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The mass is $m = 120\,\text{kg}$, and from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Mars} = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the weights $G_\text{Earth}$ and $G_\text{Mars}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Weight is a measure of the strength of the interaction: it is directly proportional to mass, and the constant is the $g$ of the celestial body on whose surface the robot is. The robot's mass is the same in both places, so only $g$ changes.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side of the formula, so the formula does not need rearranging. The same mass is multiplied by the acceleration of free fall at each place:

      $$\begin{aligned} G_\text{Earth} &= m g_\text{Earth} \\ G_\text{Mars} &= m g_\text{Mars} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The mass is already in kilograms and $g$ in m/s², so no conversions are needed: $1\,\text{kg} \cdot 1\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1\,\text{N}$, and the result comes out directly in newtons.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. The values are substituted with their units into both formulas:

      $$\begin{aligned} &G_\text{Earth} = m g_\text{Earth} \\ &= 120\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ &= 1177.2\,\text{N} \\[6pt] &G_\text{Mars} = m g_\text{Mars} \\ &= 120\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ &= 445.2\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. To three significant figures, the robot's weight on Earth is $G_\text{Earth} \approx 1180\,\text{N}$, or about 1.18 kN, and on Mars $G_\text{Mars} \approx 445\,\text{N}$. Three figures, because the mass $120\,\text{kg}$ and the $g$ values are given to three figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The weight of a 1 kg mass on Earth is about 10 N, so the weight of a $120\,\text{kg}$ mass is about 1200 N. Mars's $g$ is a little over one third of the Earth's value (3.71/9.81 ≈ 0.38), and 445 N is a little over one third of 1180 N. On Mars the weight is smaller because the interaction between Mars and the robot is weaker than between the Earth and the robot. The mass is the same in both.

      Optional Find the error
      A student calculates the weight of a $500\,\text{g}$ bag of flour on Earth like this: $G = mg = 500 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 4905\,\text{N}$. Where is the error?

      • The value $1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$ applies to the Moon. The bag of flour is weighed on Earth, so $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is correct. The error is in the unit of mass.

      • Weight is a force, and the unit of force is the newton. The kilogram is a unit of mass. The mass of the bag is $500\,\text{g}$, but its weight is $0.500\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 4.91\,\text{N}$.

      • Correct. The weight comes out in newtons only when the mass is in kilograms, because $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$. The reasonableness check says the same: the weight of a half-kilo bag is about 5 N, not almost 5000 N.

      • Check the order of magnitude: the weight of 1 kg on Earth is about 10 N, so $500\,\text{g}$ cannot weigh almost 5000 N. The mass was substituted in grams, although the formula needs kilograms.

      the reading on the Moon, that is, the weight $G_\text{Moon} = 12\,\text{N}$; from the table $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      $G_\text{Moon} = m g_\text{Moon}$ and $G_\text{Earth} = m g_\text{Earth}$, so

      $m$$= \dfrac{G_\text{Moon}}{g_\text{Moon}}$

      3. Graph and calculation

      Weights were measured for different masses on an unknown celestial body. The $(m, G)$ graph is a straight line through the origin that passes through the point $(50\,\text{kg}, 440\,\text{N})$.

      What is the acceleration of free fall on this celestial body? Answer in m/s².

      m/s²
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What does the steepness of the line tell you when weight is directly proportional to mass?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Solving the formula $G = mg$ gives $g = \dfrac{G}{m}$, that is, the slope of the line.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $g$$= \dfrac{440\,\text{N}}{50\,\text{kg}}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      the $(m, G)$ graph is a straight line through the origin that passes through the point $(50\,\text{kg}, 440\,\text{N})$

      Find

      the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

      Principle

      Weight is directly proportional to mass, $G = m g$, so the graph is a straight line through the origin. The slope of the line $\dfrac{G}{m}$ is the same at every point, and it is exactly the acceleration of free fall $g$.

      Reading the graph

      Read one point from the line, $m = 50\,\text{kg}$ and $G = 440\,\text{N}$. Because the line passes through the origin, the slope is the weight at the point divided by its mass. No other points are needed, because the ratio is the same everywhere.

      Formula

      $G = m g$, so

      $$g = \frac{G}{m}$$

      Conversions

      No conversions are needed: the weight is in newtons and the mass in kilograms, and $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2$, so $\dfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}}$ is $\text{m/s}^2$.

      Substitution

      $g$$= \dfrac{440\,\text{N}}{50\,\text{kg}}$
      $= 8.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Answer

      $8.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Two significant figures like the mass $50\,\text{kg}$ and the weight $440\,\text{N}$.

      Reasonableness

      The value is close to the Earth's value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ but slightly smaller, so it is none of the celestial bodies in the table. Check by calculating back: $50\,\text{kg} \cdot 8.8\,\text{m/s}^2 = 440\,\text{N}$, so the point is on the line.

        2. Core

          The spring of a force meter stretches 2.0 cm when an object with a weight of 4.0 N is hung on it.

          How many centimetres does the spring stretch when an object with a weight of 12 N is hung on it?

          cm
          Answer

          6.0 cm

          Show the solution

          Given

          $F_1 = 4.0\,\text{N}$ and its extension $x_1 = 2.0\,\text{cm}$; a second force $F = 12\,\text{N}$

          Find

          the extension $x$ in centimetres

          Principle

          The extension of a force meter's spring is directly proportional to the force stretching it (the spring is not stretched too far), so when the force is multiplied, the extension is multiplied by the same number. That is why we can use a ratio.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{x}{x_1} = \dfrac{F}{F_1}$, so $x = \dfrac{x_1 F}{F_1}$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the extension comes out in the same unit as $x_1$ (centimetres), and the newtons cancel in the ratio $\dfrac{F}{F_1}$.

          Substitution

          $x$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 12\,\text{N}}{4.0\,\text{N}}$
          $= 6.0\,\text{cm}$

          Answer

          $6.0\,\text{cm}$

          Two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          The force is $\dfrac{12}{4.0} = 3$ times as great, so the extension must be $3 \cdot 2.0\,\text{cm} = 6.0\,\text{cm}$. The spring constant agrees too:

          $\dfrac{2.0\,\text{cm}}{4.0\,\text{N}}$$= 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{N}}$

          and $0.50 \cdot 12 = 6.0$.

        3. The mass of a measuring instrument left on the surface of the Moon is $30\,\text{kg}$.

          What is its weight on the Moon? Read the value of $g$ from the table of the lesson.

          N
          Answer

          49 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 30\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the weight on the Moon $G$ in newtons

          Principle

          Weight is a measure of the strength of the interaction between the Moon and the instrument, and it is directly proportional to mass. The instrument is on the surface of the Moon, so the constant is the Moon's $g$, not the Earth's.

          Formula

          $G = m g_\text{Moon}$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side.

          Conversions

          The mass is in kilograms and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes out directly in newtons.

          Substitution

          $G$$= 30\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 48.6\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $49\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $30\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          On Earth the instrument's weight would be $30\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 294.3\,\text{N}$. The Moon's $g$ is about one sixth of the Earth's value, and

          $\dfrac{294.3\,\text{N}}{6}$$\approx 49\,\text{N}$

          so the result agrees.

        4. The graph shows the calibration measurements of the spring of a force meter. When an unknown object is hung on the spring, it stretches $3.5\,\text{cm}$.

          How great a force stretches the spring?

          N
          Answer

          7.0 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          the measurement points of the graph at grid crossings; the extension of the spring with the object $x = 3.5\,\text{cm}$

          Find

          the force $F$ in newtons

          Principle

          The extension of the spring is directly proportional to the force (the spring is not stretched too far), so the points lie on a straight line through the origin and the ratio $x/F$ is the same everywhere. The unknown force does not have to be read between the points by eye: it is calculated from the ratio.

          Reading the graph

          Read two points from the grid crossings: $(4\,\text{N}$; $2\,\text{cm})$ and $(10\,\text{N}$; $5\,\text{cm})$. In both, $x/F = 0.5\,\text{cm/N}$, so the points lie on the same straight line through the origin. The extension $3.5\,\text{cm}$ is between the points $(6\,\text{N}$; $3\,\text{cm})$ and $(8\,\text{N}$; $4\,\text{cm})$, so the force must be between $6\,\text{N}$ and $8\,\text{N}$. An answer read by eye, $6.8$–$7.2\,\text{N}$, is accepted.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{x}{F} = \dfrac{x_1}{F_1}$, so $F = \dfrac{F_1 x}{x_1}$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the centimetres cancel in the ratio $\dfrac{x}{x_1}$ and the force comes out in newtons.

          Substitution

          $F$$= \dfrac{4\,\text{N} \cdot 3.5\,\text{cm}}{2\,\text{cm}}$
          $= 7.0\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $F = 7.0\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the extension $3.5\,\text{cm}$.

          Reasonableness

          The extension $3.5\,\text{cm}$ is $1.75$ times that of the point $(4\,\text{N}$; $2\,\text{cm})$, and $1.75 \cdot 4\,\text{N} = 7.0\,\text{N}$. The result is between $6\,\text{N}$ and $8\,\text{N}$, as it should be.

        5. The mass of an object is $2.5\,\text{kg}$. What is its weight on Jupiter, where $g = 24.8\,\text{m/s}^2$?

          N
          Answer

          62 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 2.5\,\text{kg}$, $g_\text{Jupiter} = 24.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the weight on Jupiter $G$ in newtons

          Principle

          Weight is directly proportional to mass, and the constant is the $g$ of the celestial body on whose surface (here the cloud tops) the object is. Mass is the same everywhere, and only $g$ changes.

          Formula

          $G = m g_\text{Jupiter}$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side.

          Conversions

          The mass is in kilograms and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes out directly in newtons.

          Substitution

          $G$$= 2.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 24.8\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 62.0\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $62\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $2.5\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          On Earth the weight would be $2.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 24.5\,\text{N}$. Jupiter's $g$ is $\dfrac{24.8}{9.81} \approx 2.5$ times the Earth's value, and $2.5 \cdot 24.5\,\text{N} \approx 62\,\text{N}$, so $62\,\text{N}$ agrees.

        1. Core

          A force meter shows 49 N when an object is hung on it on Earth. What is the mass of the object?

          kg
          Answer

          5.0 kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          the force meter reading, that is, the weight $G = 49\,\text{N}$ on Earth; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the mass $m$ of the object in kilograms

          Principle

          A force meter measures force, and the object hangs from the meter on Earth, so the reading is the object's weight $G = m g$. The mass is obtained by dividing the weight by the Earth's $g$.

          Formula

          $G = m g$, so $m = \dfrac{G}{g}$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2$, so $\dfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m/s}^2}$ is a kilogram.

          Substitution

          $m$$= \dfrac{49\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 4.995\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $5.0\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures like the weight $49\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          The weight of a 1 kg mass is about $10\,\text{N}$, so $49\,\text{N}$ corresponds to a mass of about $5\,\text{kg}$. Check by calculating back:

          $5.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 49.05\,\text{N}$
          $\approx 49\,\text{N}$
        2. Core

          The mass of a hen's egg is $60\,\text{g}$. What is its weight on Earth in newtons?

          N
          Answer

          0.59 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 60\,\text{g}$; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ (Earth)

          Find

          the weight $G$ in newtons

          Principle

          Weight is directly proportional to mass, $G = m g$. The egg is on the surface of the Earth, so we use the Earth's $g$. The formula needs the mass in kilograms, so the grams must be converted.

          Formula

          $G = m g$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side.

          Conversions

          $1\,\text{g} = 0.001\,\text{kg}$, so $m = 60\,\text{g} = 0.060\,\text{kg}$. This way the result comes out in newtons.

          Substitution

          $G$$= 0.060\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 0.5886\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $0.59\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $60\,\text{g}$.

          Reasonableness

          The weight of an object of about $100\,\text{g}$ is $1\,\text{N}$, so the weight of $60\,\text{g}$ is about $0.6\,\text{N}$. If the mass had not been converted, we would have got $60 \cdot 9.81 \approx 589$, a value a thousand times too large, which is impossible for an egg.

        3. The mass of an astronaut with equipment is $145\,\text{kg}$. By how much is his weight on the Moon ($g = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$) smaller than on Earth ($g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$)?

          N
          Answer

          1190 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 145\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the decrease in weight $\Delta G = G_\text{Earth} - G_\text{Moon}$ in newtons

          Principle

          The mass is the same in both places, and the weight in each is $G = m g$ with the $g$ of that place. The difference is obtained by subtracting the weight on the Moon from the weight on Earth.

          Formula

          $\Delta G = m g_\text{Earth} - m g_\text{Moon}$, so $\Delta G = m (g_\text{Earth} - g_\text{Moon})$

          Conversions

          The mass is in kilograms and the $g$ values in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes out in newtons and no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\Delta G$$= 145\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2)$
          $= 145\,\text{kg} \cdot 8.19\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 1187.55\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $1190\,\text{N}$

          Three significant figures like the mass $145\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          On Earth the weight is about $145 \cdot 9.81\,\text{N} \approx 1422\,\text{N}$ and on the Moon about $145 \cdot 1.62\,\text{N} \approx 235\,\text{N}$, and the difference is $1422\,\text{N} - 235\,\text{N} \approx 1187\,\text{N}$. On the Moon the weight is

          $\dfrac{1.62}{9.81}$$\approx \dfrac{1}{6}$

          of the weight on Earth, so it decreases by about $\dfrac{5}{6}$, that is, $83\,\%$.

        4. The spring of a force meter stretches 1.5 cm when a $0.20\,\text{kg}$ object is hung on it on Earth. How great a force stretches the spring 4.5 cm?

          N
          Answer

          5.9 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$ and its extension $x_1 = 1.5\,\text{cm}$ on Earth; a new extension $x = 4.5\,\text{cm}$; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the stretching force $F$ in newtons

          Principle

          The weight of the object $G = m g$ is the force that stretches the spring by $x_1$. The extension is directly proportional to the force (the spring is not stretched too far), so the force at the new extension is obtained from the ratio.

          Formula

          $G = m g$ and $\dfrac{F}{G} = \dfrac{x}{x_1}$, so $F = \dfrac{m g x}{x_1}$

          Conversions

          The mass is already in kilograms. The centimetres cancel in the ratio $\dfrac{x}{x_1}$, so the extensions do not need to be converted to metres.

          Substitution

          $F$$= \dfrac{0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 4.5\,\text{cm}}{1.5\,\text{cm}}$
          $= 1.962\,\text{N} \cdot \dfrac{4.5}{1.5}$
          $= 5.886\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $5.9\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $0.20\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          The extension is $\dfrac{4.5}{1.5} = 3$ times as great, so the force must be three times as great too: $3 \cdot 1.962\,\text{N} = 5.886\,\text{N}$. Rough estimate: $0.2\,\text{kg}$ weighs about $2\,\text{N}$ and $3 \cdot 2\,\text{N} = 6\,\text{N}$.

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. An object is hung on a force meter on Earth, and the meter shows 18 N. The same object is taken to Mars ($g = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$).

          What does the same meter show there?

          N
          Answer

          6.8 N

          Show the solution

          Given

          $G_\text{Earth} = 18\,\text{N}$; from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Mars} = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the meter reading on Mars $G_\text{Mars}$ in newtons

          Principle

          The meter shows the weight. The mass of the object stays the same on Mars and only $g$ changes, so the weight changes in the same proportion as $g$. The mass is therefore constant here, and it links the two places.

          Formula

          $m$$= \dfrac{G_\text{Earth}}{g_\text{Earth}}$

          and $G_\text{Mars} = m g_\text{Mars}$, so

          $G_\text{Mars}$$= \dfrac{G_\text{Earth} g_\text{Mars}}{g_\text{Earth}}$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: $\dfrac{\text{m/s}^2}{\text{m/s}^2}$ cancels, and the weight stays in newtons.

          Substitution

          $m$$= \dfrac{18\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 1.835\,\text{kg}$

          so

          $G_\text{Mars}$$= 1.835\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 6.807\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $6.8\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the weight $18\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          Mars's $g$ is $\dfrac{3.71}{9.81} \approx 0.38$ of the Earth's value, and $0.38 \cdot 18\,\text{N} \approx 6.8\,\text{N}$. The weight decreases because the interaction on Mars is weaker, so the meter must show less than on Earth.

        2. On Earth a lifter can lift at most a mass of $22.0\,\text{kg}$, that is, the lifter can produce a force equal to the weight of this mass.

          How great a mass could the lifter lift on the Moon ($g = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$) with the same force?

          kg
          Answer

          133 kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m_\text{Earth} = 22.0\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the mass that can be lifted on the Moon $m_\text{Moon}$ in kilograms

          Principle

          The lifter can produce the same force $F$ in both places, and the weight of the mass lifted must be at most this force. On the Moon $g$ is smaller, so the same force is enough for a greater mass.

          Formula

          $F = m_\text{Earth} g_\text{Earth}$ and $F = m_\text{Moon} g_\text{Moon}$, so

          $m_\text{Moon}$$= \dfrac{m_\text{Earth} g_\text{Earth}}{g_\text{Moon}}$

          Conversions

          The mass is in kilograms and the $g$ values in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the force comes out in newtons and the mass in kilograms without conversions.

          Substitution

          $m_\text{Moon}$$= \dfrac{22.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{215.82\,\text{N}}{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 133.2\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $133\,\text{kg}$

          Three significant figures like the mass $22.0\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          The Moon's $g$ is about one sixth of the Earth's value, so the mass must be about $6 \cdot 22.0\,\text{kg} = 132\,\text{kg}$. Check by calculating back: $133.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2 = 215.8\,\text{N}$, the same as the weight of $22.0\,\text{kg}$ on Earth.

        3. The scale of a force meter has been made on Earth so that it shows, in kilograms, the number obtained by dividing the force by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. On Mars ($g = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$) the meter shows $26\,\text{kg}$ for an object hung on it.

          What is the mass of the object?

          kg
          Answer

          69 kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          the meter reading $L = 26\,\text{kg}$ on Mars; from the table $g_\text{Mars} = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$; the scale shows the force divided by the Earth's value $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the true mass $m$ of the object in kilograms

          Principle

          The meter measures force, not mass: its scale was made on Earth by dividing the force by the number $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. On Mars we must therefore first work out the force, that is, the weight of the object, from the reading, and the mass is obtained by dividing it by Mars's $g$.

          Formula

          $L = \dfrac{G}{g_\text{Earth}}$, so $G = L g_\text{Earth}$. On Mars $G = m g_\text{Mars}$, so

          $m$$= \dfrac{L g_\text{Earth}}{g_\text{Mars}}$

          Conversions

          The reading $26\,\text{kg}$ is interpreted as a number on the scale: the force is $G = 26\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ in newtons. No other conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $m$$= \dfrac{26\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{3.71\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{255.06\,\text{N}}{3.71\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 68.75\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $69\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures like the reading $26\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          On Mars $g$ is $\dfrac{3.71}{9.81} \approx 0.38$ of the Earth's value, so the reading is only about $0.38$ times the mass. The mass must be about

          $\dfrac{26\,\text{kg}}{0.38}$$\approx 69\,\text{kg}$

          clearly greater than the reading. Check by calculating back: $69\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 256\,\text{N}$ and

          $\dfrac{256\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$$\approx 26\,\text{kg}$
