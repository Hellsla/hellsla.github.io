Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting

Why do two groups get different speeds for the same toy car, even though both measure carefully?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • formulate a research question and a testable hypothesis.
  • plan a fair experiment: choose the variables, the measuring range and the repeats.
  • record measurements in a lab record and report a result with its uncertainty.

Prior knowledge

  • A measurement result has an uncertainty, and repeating the measurement gives a meanchapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement
  • A straight line is fitted to the data points, and its slope tells the rate of changechapter Graphing and fitting a straight line

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

Two groups measure the speed of the same toy car with a hand stopwatch. Group A runs the car once over a distance of $0.50\,\text{m}$, gets a time of $1.0\,\text{s}$ and calculates a speed of $0.50\,\text{m/s}$. Group B runs five different distances, three times each. Over $2.00\,\text{m}$ the mean time is $4.73\,\text{s}$, and group B calculates a speed of $0.42\,\text{m/s}$. The car and the batteries are the same.

Why do the results differ, and how should an investigation be designed so that its result can be trusted?

Optional Review: repeats and uncertainty
A student measures a walking time five times and gets $4.7\,\text{s}$, $4.9\,\text{s}$, $4.6\,\text{s}$, $4.8\,\text{s}$ and $4.7\,\text{s}$. What is the most sensible way to report the result?

  • A single extreme value is just one measurement among the others. When a measurement is repeated, the mean is a better estimate than the largest or smallest reading (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

  • The mean gives the result, but not how much it can vary. Without an uncertainty no one else can judge whether the result fits a prediction or another measurement (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

  • Correct. The mean is $23.7\,\text{s} : 5 = 4.74\,\text{s}$ and half of the range is $(4.9\,\text{s} - 4.6\,\text{s}) : 2 = 0.15\,\text{s}$. The first digit of the uncertainty is 1, so it is given with two figures and the mean to the same decimal place. Today you plan an experiment so that this uncertainty is small compared with the measurement.

  • Without the uncertainty the reader does not know how much the result can vary: the readings already vary by a tenth of a second. A result is given together with its uncertainty and with the precision that matches it (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

A fair experiment?

Group C investigates how the number of batteries affects the speed of a toy car. It first runs a new car with two batteries on the floor and then an old car with four batteries on a carpet. The speed is lower the second time.What is the most important flaw in the plan?

  • Two points is very few, and more values and repeats would be better. Even so, comparing two points would be sensible if the other variables were the same. The essential flaw is that the comparison is not fair.

  • Correct. In a fair experiment you change only the number of batteries and keep the car and the surface the same. Now the slowing down could be due to the carpet, the old car or the batteries, and the measurement does not separate them.

  • The investigator chooses the number of batteries, because that is the thing whose effect is being investigated. What is measured is the speed of the car. The worst flaw is still that other things change as well.

4Theory

Key idea

Physics is an experimental science. Physical claims are judged by observations and measurements. The methods of a good investigation are described so precisely that others can test the results and, where possible, repeat the measurements. An investigation is a systematic way of answering a question by measuring. It begins with a question and ends with a conclusion that the measurement results support. A good investigation is not one where the assumption turns out to be right, but one where the measurement could have shown the assumption to be wrong.

Research question and hypothesis

A research question is a question that a measurement can answer: "How does the travel time of a toy car depend on the length of the run?" The question "Which car is best?" is no good, because it cannot be measured.

A hypothesis is a reasoned, testable prediction that answers the research question. For example: "The car moves along the track at constant speed, so the distance it travels is directly proportional to time, $s = vt$." A hypothesis must lead to something measurable that could turn out to be wrong. The prediction "the car goes somehow" is not a hypothesis, because no measurement can disprove it.

Variables

A variable is a quantity or other factor that can vary in an investigation. In an experiment you change one variable, measure another and keep everything else the same:

  • independent variable: the experimenter chooses its values, here the distance $s$
  • dependent variable: its value is read from an instrument, here the time $t$
  • controlled variables: stay the same throughout the experiment, here the car, the batteries, the surface and the way of starting.

If two variables change at the same time, for example the distance and the batteries, you cannot tell what caused the change in the result. That is why an experiment is fair only when just the independent variable changes.

Planning before measuring

Background information on the phenomenon and the methods is gathered before planning, with source criticism: judge who produced the information and why, how it is justified, whether it is up to date and whether other sources support it. Before measuring, the plan answers these questions: what is measured and with which instrument, which values are chosen for the independent variable and how many, how many repeats are made for each value, and what is kept constant. Try the plan out first with a trial run. It tells you whether the measurement is possible at all, and it gives a rough estimate of the result.

Choose the measuring range so that the quantity being measured is clearly larger than the uncertainty of the measurement (see the section Relative uncertainty and choosing the measuring range). Choose several values at equal intervals, because one or two points do not show whether the dependence is a straight line.

Lab record

A lab record is a log made during the experiment, not from memory afterwards. It contains the research question, the date, the equipment and its accuracy, the controlled variables, the measurements as a table with units, and observations, for example "the car veered sideways".

Record the readings as they are, so that the record stays objective and is not shaped by your own expectations. Do not quietly delete or correct a measurement that looks odd: mark it and write down what happened. A short example:

$s\ (\text{m})$ $t_1\ (\text{s})$ $t_2\ (\text{s})$ $t_3\ (\text{s})$ note
0.50 1.3 1.1 1.4 hard to time
1.00 2.5 2.3 2.6
1.50 3.7 3.5 3.6

Report

A report describes the investigation so that someone else can repeat it and judge the conclusion. Its parts are:

  1. research question and hypothesis
  2. method: equipment, variables and procedure
  3. results: table, graph and the value of the quantity with its uncertainty
  4. discussion: sources of error and what you would do differently next time
  5. conclusion: the answer to the research question and whether the results support the hypothesis.

A conclusion is only as strong as the data. If the measurements agree with the prediction within the uncertainty, the hypothesis has gained support, but it has not been proved.

Relative uncertainty and choosing the measuring range

The uncertainty $\Delta t$ of a time measured by hand comes mainly from reaction time, and it is almost the same in a short and a long measurement. The quality of a measurement is therefore described by the relative uncertainty, the uncertainty compared with the measured value. To keep it at most $\varepsilon_\text{max}$, the measured time must be at least

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon &= \frac{\Delta t}{t} \\ t &\ge \frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \end{aligned}$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the relative uncertainty (often given as a percentage), $\Delta t$ the uncertainty of the time (s) and $t$ the measured time (s). With an uncertainty $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$ and a limit of $5\,\%$, the time must be at least $4\,\text{s}$.

The required distance follows from the model of uniform motion $s = vt$. If the measurement is made at several distances, a straight line is fitted to the data points (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line). When $t$ is on the horizontal axis and $s$ on the vertical axis, the slope of the line is the speed $v$. The shortest valid distance is the one at which the relative uncertainty just stays within the chosen limit $\varepsilon_\text{max}$. On a shorter distance the relative uncertainty exceeds this limit, and the result is too imprecise.

Common misconception

Many people think an investigation has failed if the measurement does not support the hypothesis. This is not so: if a measurement disproves the hypothesis, the investigator knows something they did not know before. A failed investigation is one from which nothing can be concluded, because the variables were not controlled or the measurements are too uncertain.

Another common mistake is to treat one measurement as enough. A single reading does not show how much the result varies, so the spread caused by random error cannot be estimated. A third mistake is to pick the measurements that fit the result and leave out the others: all measurements are recorded, and an outlier is explained or marked.

Summary

  • An investigation begins with a question that can be measured and a testable hypothesis that could turn out to be wrong.
  • In a fair experiment you change one variable, measure another and keep the others constant.
  • The measurement is planned in advance: measuring range, number of points, repeats and controlled variables. A trial run checks the plan.
  • The lab record contains the readings, units, equipment and observations, written during the experiment. No measurement is quietly deleted.
  • The relative uncertainty $\varepsilon = \Delta t : t$ gets smaller as the measured time gets longer; therefore the time must be at least $\Delta t : \varepsilon_\text{max}$.
  • A report gives the question, method, results, discussion and conclusion so that someone else can repeat the investigation.

    Example 1: Investigating a toy car's speed

    Students investigate the motion of a toy car. In a trial run the speed of the car is about $0.42\,\text{m/s}$. The time is measured with a hand stopwatch, and the timing uncertainty is $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$. The relative uncertainty of the time measurement should be at most $5.0\,\%$. What is the shortest distance the car must travel?

    Step 1/7

    Given and unknown. Speed $v = 0.42\,\text{m/s}$, uncertainty of the time $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$ and largest allowed relative uncertainty $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 5.0\,\% = 0.050$. We are asked for the shortest distance $s_\text{min}$.

    Step 2/7

    Principle. The relative uncertainty gets smaller as the measured time gets longer, because $\Delta t$ stays the same. First calculate the shortest allowed time from the uncertainty limit. The car moves at constant speed, so the model of uniform motion $s = vt$ gives the distance from the time.

    Step 3/7

    Rearranged formula. $$\begin{aligned} t_\text{min} &= \frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \\ s_\text{min} &= v\, t_\text{min} = \frac{v\,\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4/7

    Unit conversions. Not needed: the speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the distance comes out in metres. The percentage has been converted to a decimal number: $5.0\,\% = 0.050$.

    Step 5/7

    Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} t_\text{min} &= \frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \\ &= \frac{0.2\,\text{s}}{0.050} = 4\,\text{s} \\ s_\text{min} &= v\, t_\text{min} \\ &= 0.42\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 4\,\text{s} \\ &= 1.68\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 6/7

    Result. The distance must be at least $1.7\,\text{m}$. This is a design estimate whose precision is limited by the one-figure uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$. The minimum distance of a design estimate is therefore given to practical precision, here two figures like the speed $0.42\,\text{m/s}$, and rounded up so that the limit is surely met.

    Step 7/7

    Reasonableness. Over $1.7\,\text{m}$ the travel time of the car is about $1.7\,\text{m} : 0.42\,\text{m/s} \approx 4.05\,\text{s}$, so

    $\varepsilon$$= 0.2\,\text{s} : 4.05\,\text{s}$
    $\approx 4.9\,\%$

    which is just within the limit. Over $0.50\,\text{m}$ the time would be only $1.2\,\text{s}$ and the uncertainty about $17\,\%$, which is why the short distance was a poor choice.

    Optional Example 2: Find the error
    A student plans an experiment in which the speed of the car is $0.60\,\text{m/s}$ and the uncertainty of the time is $0.2\,\text{s}$. The limit of the relative uncertainty is $5\,\%$.They calculate the shortest distance:

    $$\begin{aligned} s_\text{min} &= \frac{v\,\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \\ &= \frac{0.60\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.2\,\text{s}}{5} \\ &= 0.024\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Where is the error?

    • Correct. The relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ is a quotient, so $5\,\%$ must be substituted as the number $0.050$. The error could have been noticed with a reasonableness check: you cannot measure a distance of a couple of centimetres with a hand stopwatch when the time is under a second.

    • The formula is correct. The shortest time is $t_\text{min} = \Delta t : \varepsilon_\text{max}$ and the distance is $s = v\,t_\text{min}$. Dividing the uncertainty by the speed would give a combination of seconds and speed that has no unit of distance.

    • No conversion is needed, because the speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the uncertainty in seconds; the seconds cancel and the result is in metres. The error is in how the percentage was handled.

    Example 4: Plan your own investigation

    Choose a phenomenon that you can investigate at school or at home and in which the quantities can be measured: for example the speed of walking or of a rolling ball, the cooling of water in a cup, or the stretching of a rubber band. Write a plan: research question, hypothesis, independent, dependent and controlled variables, measuring range and repeats, and how you will keep the lab record.

    Model answer

    Research question: how does the time a ball takes to roll depend on the distance on a level floor? The ball is always released from the same point over a short starting ramp, and timing starts when the ball passes a start line marked on the floor, by which point its speed has levelled off. Hypothesis: the speed of the ball on the floor stays approximately constant, so the travel time is directly proportional to the distance, $s = vt$. If the speed $s : t$ clearly decreases as the distance grows, the hypothesis is wrong. The independent variable is the distance from the start line (for example $1.6$–$3.2\,\text{m}$ at five equally spaced values), the dependent variable is the time with a hand stopwatch, and I keep constant the ball, the height of the ramp and the starting point, the surface and the way of starting (the ball is released without a push). In a trial run the speed of the ball is about $0.4\,\text{m/s}$. A real ball slows down slightly while rolling because of rolling resistance, so the experiment also tests whether the speed is constant enough. The uncertainty of timing by hand is about $0.2\,\text{s}$, so over the shortest distance the time must be at least $4\,\text{s}$ for the relative uncertainty to be at most $5\,\%$. The distance $1.6\,\text{m}$ gives a time of $4\,\text{s}$. I make three repeats for each distance and write every reading with its unit in the lab record, together with observations such as "the ball hit the wall". The trial run shows whether the distance is long enough.

      1. Exercise 8-17

        A student fits two lines to the measurement points (distance as a function of time): the steepest, whose slope is $0.463\,\text{m/s}$, and the shallowest, whose slope is $0.397\,\text{m/s}$. The measurement points fit between these lines. Half of the difference of the slopes of the lines is taken as the uncertainty.

        How large is the uncertainty of the speed?

        m/s
        Answer

        $0.033\,\text{m/s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        slope of the steepest line $v_\text{max} = 0.463\,\text{m/s}$, of the shallowest $v_\text{min} = 0.397\,\text{m/s}$

        Find

        the uncertainty $\Delta v$ of the speed in $\text{m/s}$

        Principle

        All lines that fit the data points within their uncertainties are plausible, so the speed is probably between the slopes of the steepest and the shallowest line. The best estimate is their mean, and the uncertainty is half of the difference.

        Formula

        $v$$= \dfrac{v_\text{max} + v_\text{min}}{2}$
        $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{v_\text{max} - v_\text{min}}{2}$

        Conversions

        The slopes are already in the same unit, $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{0.463\,\text{m/s} - 0.397\,\text{m/s}}{2}$
        $= \dfrac{0.066\,\text{m/s}}{2}$
        $= 0.033\,\text{m/s}$

        Answer

        $0.033\,\text{m/s}$

        (calculated value; the difference $0.066\,\text{m/s}$ is a two-figure number). As a stated uncertainty it is rounded to one significant figure, so the speed is $v = 0.43\,\text{m/s} \pm 0.03\,\text{m/s}$. The answer field accepts both.

        Reasonableness

        The uncertainty is about $\tfrac{0.033}{0.430} \approx 8\,\%$ of the speed, which suits motion measured with a hand stopwatch. If the hypothesis predicted $0.50\,\text{m/s}$, it would be more than two uncertainties above the measured value: the interval is $0.397$–$0.463\,\text{m/s}$, and the prediction does not fit in it.

      2. Exercise 8-18

        Hot water cools in a cup. The temperature is $80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $t = 0\,\text{min}$, $68.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $5.0\,\text{min}$, $52.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $15.0\,\text{min}$ and $47.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $20.0\,\text{min}$.

        The student's hypothesis is that the temperature falls steadily.

        How much greater is the rate of temperature fall during the first five minutes than during the last five minutes? Answer in K/min.

        K/min
        Answer

        $1.4\,\text{K/min}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        temperatures $80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at time $0\,\text{min}$, $68.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $5.0\,\text{min}$, $52.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $15.0\,\text{min}$ and $47.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $20.0\,\text{min}$

        Find

        the difference between the rates of fall during the first and the last five minutes in $\text{K/min}$

        Principle

        The hypothesis "the temperature falls steadily" predicts the same rate of fall everywhere. So calculate the rate of fall for two periods and compare them. A temperature change is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius.

        Formula

        $r$$= \dfrac{T_\text{start} - T_\text{end}}{t_\text{end} - t_\text{start}}$

        and the difference $r_1 - r_2$

        Conversions

        A fall in temperature in degrees Celsius equals the fall in kelvins, so a change of $1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is $1\,\text{K}$ and no conversion is needed.

        Substitution

        $r_1$$= \dfrac{80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 68.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= \dfrac{12.0\,\text{K}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= 2.4\,\text{K/min}$
        $r_2$$= \dfrac{52.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 47.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= \dfrac{5.0\,\text{K}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= 1.0\,\text{K/min}$

        so

        $r_1 - r_2$$= 2.4\,\text{K/min} - 1.0\,\text{K/min}$
        $= 1.4\,\text{K/min}$

        Answer

        $1.4\,\text{K/min}$

        Two significant figures, because the time intervals $5.0\,\text{min}$ and the fall $5.0\,\text{K}$ in the last interval are given with two significant figures.

        Reasonableness

        The first rate of fall is more than twice the last one, and this difference cannot come from the reading uncertainty alone, which from the precision of the readings is about $0.1\,\text{K}$. So the hypothesis of a steady fall does not hold: the temperature falls more slowly the closer the water is to room temperature.

      3. Exercise 8-19

        A time of $60\,\text{min}$ has been set aside for an investigation. Assembling and testing the equipment takes $12\,\text{min}$, and the measurement is made at six different distances. Each measurement repeat takes $2.0\,\text{min}$, including returning to the start and recording.

        How many repeats can be done for each distance, when the number of repeats is the same at every distance?

        repeats
        Answer

        $4$

        Show the solution

        Given

        total time $60\,\text{min}$, preparation $12\,\text{min}$, number of distances $N = 6$, one repeat $2.0\,\text{min}$

        Find

        the number of repeats $n$ for each distance

        Principle

        The time available for measuring is the total time minus the preparation. It is divided equally among all the repeats: there are $n$ repeats at each of the $N$ distances, and each repeat takes the same time.

        Formula

        $t_\text{tot} - t_\text{prep} = N\, n\, t_\text{rep}$, so

        $n$$= \dfrac{t_\text{tot} - t_\text{prep}}{N\, t_\text{rep}}$

        Conversions

        All times are in minutes, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $n$$= \dfrac{60\,\text{min} - 12\,\text{min}}{6 \cdot 2.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= \dfrac{48\,\text{min}}{12\,\text{min}}$
        $= 4.0$

        Answer

        $4$

        Repeats. The number of repeats is an exact whole number, and here the division comes out exactly.

        Reasonableness

        Four repeats at six distances make 24 measurements, and $24 \cdot 2.0\,\text{min} = 48\,\text{min}$ plus the preparation of $12\,\text{min}$ gives $60\,\text{min}$. No time is left unused, and the time set aside is not exceeded.

      4. Exercise 8-20

        A student investigated whether the mass of a ball affects the rolling time (compare the measurements of the light and heavy ball). The results of five repeats are the means $\bar{t}_{50} = 3.12\,\text{s} \pm 0.04\,\text{s}$ and $\bar{t}_{200} = 3.08\,\text{s} \pm 0.04\,\text{s}$ (the uncertainty is half of the range). The student's hypothesis was that the heavy ball rolls down the slope faster. The uncertainty of timing with a hand stopwatch was estimated at $0.15\,\text{s}$ for a single measurement. The estimate includes the reaction time, which can differ between measurement series, for example with a different timer or on a different occasion, so it does not fully show in the spread of five consecutive repeats.

        The discussion says: “The experiment went well. The mean of the heavy ball is smaller, so the hypothesis was correct. There were few errors.”

        How many times larger than the difference of the means is the hand-timing uncertainty $0.15\,\text{s}$? Use this and the intervals of the results to justify which claims of the discussion the data supports and which it does not, and write two better sentences for the discussion.

        Means of the travel times of the light and the heavy ball
        Ball$\bar{t}\,(\text{s})$$\Delta t\,(\text{s})$
        $50\,\text{g}$3.120.04
        $200\,\text{g}$3.080.04
        Answer

        $3.8$

        Show the solution

        Given

        $\bar{t}_{50} = 3.12\,\text{s} \pm 0.04\,\text{s}$, $\bar{t}_{200} = 3.08\,\text{s} \pm 0.04\,\text{s}$; hand-timing uncertainty $\Delta t_\text{hand} = 0.15\,\text{s}$; the claims of the discussion “the experiment went well”, “the hypothesis was correct” and “there were few errors”

        Find

        how many times larger than the difference of the means $\Delta t_\text{hand}$ is, and which claims the data supports

        Principle

        The discussion evaluates what the results show and how reliable they are. The difference of the means is compared with their uncertainties. Here the uncertainty intervals of the measured values overlap, so this data does not allow a reliable conclusion that the rolling times of the balls differ. The uncertainty of hand timing must also be taken into account when the results are interpreted. An experiment is not said to prove a hypothesis for certain, and the sentence “there were few errors” must not be left without numbers.

        Formula

        the difference $d = \bar{t}_{50} - \bar{t}_{200}$ and the ratio $k = \dfrac{\Delta t_\text{hand}}{d}$

        Conversions

        All times are in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $d$$= 3.12\,\text{s} - 3.08\,\text{s}$
        $= 0.04\,\text{s}$

        so

        $k$$= \dfrac{0.15\,\text{s}}{0.04\,\text{s}}$
        $= 3.75$
        $\approx 3.8$

        The intervals are $3.08$–$3.16\,\text{s}$ and $3.04$–$3.12\,\text{s}$, and they overlap in the interval $3.08$–$3.12\,\text{s}$.

        Answer

        about $3.8$ times. The data does not support the claim “the hypothesis was correct”, because the difference $0.04\,\text{s}$ is as large as the uncertainty $0.04\,\text{s}$ and the intervals overlap; a smaller mean alone is not enough. The claim “there were few errors” does not hold either: the hand-timing uncertainty is a multiple of the difference. The claim “the experiment went well” is too strong, because the experiment could not show a difference. Better sentences: “The difference of the means $0.04\,\text{s}$ is as large as the uncertainty $0.04\,\text{s}$, so the data does not show a difference in the travel times of the balls; however, this does not prove that mass has no effect.” and “The hand-timing uncertainty $0.15\,\text{s}$ is about $3.8$ times the difference, so in future a photogate should be used and the number of repeats increased.”

        Reasonableness

        When the uncertainty of the hand stopwatch is a multiple of the measured difference, a small difference ($0.04\,\text{s}$, about $1.3\,\%$ of the time) does not stand out reliably from the measurement error. The conclusion “no difference can be shown” is therefore suitably cautious. No deviating measurement was removed and the hypothesis was not declared certain.

      5. Exercise 8-21

        A student plans an investigation for the question “Does the cooling rate of water depend on the material of the container?” He pours the same number of decilitres of $80\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ water into three containers of the same size (glass, metal, plastic) and reads the temperature every minute. The independent (manipulated) variable is the material of the container and the dependent variable is the fall in temperature.

        The temperatures of the glass container in a preliminary experiment are in the table. Estimate the initial cooling rate from the change during the first three minutes of the table. The uncertainty of the fall in temperature is estimated at $\Delta T_\text{fall} = 1.0\,\text{K}$, and the relative uncertainty of the fall is wanted to be at most $5.0\,\%$. Assume that the initial cooling rate is about the same in all the containers as in the glass container and that it stays constant.

        How long must the measurement period be planned to be at least? Also name three variables to be controlled, with reasons, and tell how the cooling curves of the three containers are compared.

        Temperature of the water in the glass container
        $t\,(\text{min})$$T\,({}^\circ\text{C})$
        0.080.0
        1.077.9
        2.076.0
        3.074.0
        4.072.2
        min
        Answer

        $10\,\text{min}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        temperatures of the preliminary experiment in the glass container: $80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $0.0\,\text{min}$ and $74.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $3.0\,\text{min}$; uncertainty of the fall $\Delta T_\text{fall} = 1.0\,\text{K}$, largest allowed relative uncertainty $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 5.0\,\%$

        Find

        the minimum length $t$ of the measurement period in $\text{min}$, three variables to be controlled and the way of comparing the cooling curves

        Principle

        The initial cooling rate is estimated from the change during the first three minutes of the table. The relative uncertainty stays within the limit only if the measured fall is large enough compared with the uncertainty, just as in a time measurement where the uncertainty of the time is constant. First the smallest sufficient fall is calculated, and then the time in which the water cools by this amount at the initial rate.

        Formula

        $r$$= \dfrac{T_\text{start} - T_3}{t_3 - t_\text{start}}$
        $\;\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta T_\text{fall}}{T_\text{fall}}$
        $\le \varepsilon_\text{max}$

        from which

        $T_\text{fall}$$\ge \dfrac{\Delta T_\text{fall}}{\varepsilon_\text{max}}$

        $t = \dfrac{T_\text{fall}}{r}$

        Conversions

        A percent is a hundredth: $5.0\,\% = 0.050$. A fall in temperature is the same in kelvins and degrees Celsius, so $\text{K}$ is suitable as the unit.

        Substitution

        $r$$= \dfrac{80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 74.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{3.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{K}}{3.0\,\text{min}}$
        $= 2.0\,\text{K/min}$

        Then

        $T_\text{fall}$$\ge \dfrac{1.0\,\text{K}}{0.050}$
        $= 20\,\text{K}$

        so

        $t$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{K}}{2.0\,\text{K/min}}$
        $= 10\,\text{min}$

        Answer

        at least $10\,\text{min}$, two significant figures like the time interval $3.0\,\text{min}$ and the uncertainty $1.0\,\text{K}$. Variables to be controlled: the amount of water (otherwise the heat capacity and the surface area change), the initial temperature of the water (the cooling rate depends on the temperature difference to the room) and the surroundings, that is, the temperature of the room and the air flow, and whether there is a lid or not. The curves are drawn in the same graph and compared by their initial slopes (the cooling rate for the same fall in temperature $T_\text{fall}$, for example during the first $20\,\text{K}$ fall), taking the uncertainty into account.

        Reasonableness

        The fall in temperature must be $20\,\text{K}$, which is 20 times the uncertainty $1.0\,\text{K}$, so the relative uncertainty is $\tfrac{1}{20} = 5\,\%$. In reality cooling slows down (compare the earlier cooling exercise), and in the container that cools most slowly the fall is smaller, so $10\,\text{min}$ is a lower limit: the period should be planned longer and checked with a preliminary experiment.

      4. Calculation task: Calculating uncertainty

      A student measures a walking time five times: $9.8\,\text{s}$, $10.2\,\text{s}$, $10.0\,\text{s}$, $9.9\,\text{s}$ and $10.1\,\text{s}$. In this exercise the uncertainty is estimated simply and cautiously as half of the range.

      What is the relative uncertainty of the result as a percentage, when this estimate is used as the uncertainty?

      %
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the mean, and what are the largest and smallest readings?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The uncertainty is

      $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{t_\text{max} - t_\text{min}}{2}$

      and the relative uncertainty

      $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\bar{t}}$
      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $\bar{t}$$= 10.0\,\text{s}$
      $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{10.2\,\text{s} - 9.8\,\text{s}}{2}$
      $= 0.2\,\text{s}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      five times $9.8\,\text{s}$, $10.2\,\text{s}$, $10.0\,\text{s}$, $9.9\,\text{s}$ and $10.1\,\text{s}$

      Find

      the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the result as a percentage, when the uncertainty is half of the range

      Principle

      The repeats give a mean. The spread of the readings is estimated here simply as half of the range, and this is used as the estimate of the uncertainty. The relative uncertainty is the uncertainty divided by the mean.

      Formula

      $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{\sum t_i}{5}$
      $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{t_\text{max} - t_\text{min}}{2}$
      $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\bar{t}}$

      Conversions

      All times are in seconds, so no conversions are needed. The quotient is multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage.

      Substitution

      the sum of the times is $50.0\,\text{s}$, so

      $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{50.0\,\text{s}}{5}$
      $= 10.0\,\text{s}$
      $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{10.2\,\text{s} - 9.8\,\text{s}}{2}$
      $= 0.2\,\text{s}$

      so

      $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.2\,\text{s}}{10.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= 0.020$
      $= 2.0\,\%$

      Answer

      $2\,\%$

      One significant figure like the uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$ (calculator value $2.0\,\%$).

      Reasonableness

      Ten seconds is 50 times the uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$, so the fraction is about $\tfrac{1}{50} = 2\,\%$. All readings are within $0.2\,\text{s}$ of the mean, because the estimate describes the spread of the individual readings. The uncertainty of the mean itself is usually smaller than this, so the estimate is cautious.

      Optional 5. Explanation task: Explain the conclusion

      The conclusion of a student's report reads: "The hypothesis was proved correct, because the measurement gave the speed $0.42\,\text{m/s}$ as predicted." Explain what is too strong in the claim and how the conclusion should be worded.

      Model answer

      One experiment cannot prove a hypothesis correct. It can give it support if the result agrees with the prediction within the uncertainty, or refute it. A conclusion must contain the result with its uncertainty, for example $v = 0.42\,\text{m/s} \pm 0.02\,\text{m/s}$, and it must be compared with the prediction: the result fits the prediction, so the measurement supports the hypothesis. It must also say under what conditions the result was obtained, for example with a particular car and surface, and what was left uninvestigated, such as longer distances. This lets someone else judge how far the conclusion can be trusted.
