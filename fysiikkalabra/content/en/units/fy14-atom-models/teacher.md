## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–6 min, Think about it.** Read the hook (Thomson's "pudding" atom and the alpha particles). The review question on photon energy.
- **6–11 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, committed with a short reason. Ask two or three students to say their reason aloud; do not judge them yet.
- **11–30 min, Investigate.** Read *The gold-foil evidence* together, then the paths figure and its question in pairs. Then the hydrogen spectrum: the photon energy of the red line (students keep their answer) and the pair comparison of the two spectra.
- **30–42 min, Theory summary.** Key idea, Bohr's atom and the formula; the vocabulary match; back to the predictions. The biographies are optional.
- **42–60 min, Apply.** The worked example (closest approach) step by step; the $5 \to 2$ jump: students check that Bohr's model gives a measured line.
- **60–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the explanation on fusion with the sentence starters, the summary, the French corner in pairs.

## Plan for fast pairs and for running short of time
- **Fast pairs:** the optional error-spotting task (a jump to $n = 1$); the biographies of Marsden and Bohr; then calculate the wavelength of the jump $4 \to 2$ and find it in the measured table, or the ionisation wavelength $1240\ \text{eV} \cdot \text{nm} : 13.6\ \text{eV} \approx 91\ \text{nm}$.
- **Short of time:** keep the predictions, the gold-foil section, the red-line calculation, Bohr's atom and the $5 \to 2$ task. Do the worked example as a teacher demonstration (5 minutes) and give the French corner as homework.

## Organisation and preparation
- Pairs for the investigation and the spectrum comparison; predictions and checks individually.
- If you have them, show a hydrogen (or helium, neon) discharge tube and a filament lamp through cheap diffraction-grating glasses or a hand spectroscope: the contrast between lines and rainbow makes the whole lesson. Discharge tubes run on high voltage: only the teacher handles the power supply.
- The unit prepares the spectra station of the Fotoni lab visit (spectra of a filament lamp and gas discharge tubes). Ask students to keep their red-line photon energy for the visit.
- A marble model works as a demonstration of scattering: roll marbles at a hidden small target and count how few come back.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after writing. For a grade: the spectrum comparison names both kinds of spectrum with a wavelength or photon energy and infers fixed energy amounts; the fusion explanation uses the repulsion of two positive nuclei and a number from the worked example.
- Language: *nucleus / nuclei, energy level, line spectrum, transition, whereas*; comparing structures and hedging (*one possible explanation is …*).

## Common difficulties
- Students think a photon's energy is the energy of one level. Always ask: "Which two levels?"
- The negative energies: a level closer to zero is *higher*. The diagram helps; let students point to the levels.
- The size scales: femtometres ($10^{-15}\ \text{m}$) and picometres ($10^{-12}\ \text{m}$). Write them on the board with powers of ten.
- "The electron orbits like a planet": the Bohr model is a historical bridge; say clearly that quantum mechanics replaced the orbits (next unit).
- The 1909 count "about one in 8000" was measured with a platinum reflector; the later gold-foil measurements (1913) tested Rutherford's formula in detail.

## Extension ideas
- Balmer's formula $\lambda = B \cdot \dfrac{m^2}{m^2 - 4}$ with $B = 364.6\ \text{nm}$: show that it agrees with Bohr's levels for $m = 3, 4, 5, 6$.
- Spectral lines in astronomy and in fusion research: hydrogen and impurity lines tell physicists the temperature and the cleanliness of a plasma.
- Absorption: why does the Sun's spectrum have dark lines? Link to the third idea of Bohr's model.
