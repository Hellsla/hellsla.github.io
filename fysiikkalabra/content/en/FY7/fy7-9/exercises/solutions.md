## fy7-9-L1-01
**Given:** frequency of the radio wave $f = 94.0\,\text{MHz}$, speed of the wave $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** wavelength $\lambda$ in metres
**Principle:** A radio wave is an electromagnetic wave, which travels one wavelength during one period. So the speed, frequency and wavelength are related by $c = f\lambda$.
**Formula:** $c = f\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f}$
**Conversions:** $f = 94.0\,\text{MHz} = 94.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{Hz}$
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{94.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{Hz}} = 3.191\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $3.19\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the frequency $94.0\,\text{MHz}$.
**Reasonableness:** Units: $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{Hz}} = \tfrac{\text{m/s}}{1/\text{s}} = \text{m}$. The wavelength at about $100\,\text{MHz}$ in FM radio is about $3\,\text{m}$, and a slightly lower frequency gives a slightly longer wave. The value is over a metre, as it should be for radio waves.

## fy7-9-L1-02
**Given:** frequency of the microwaves $f = 2.45\,\text{GHz}$, speed of light $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** wavelength $\lambda$ in centimetres
**Principle:** Microwaves are electromagnetic radiation, which travels at the speed of light. The wave travels one wavelength during one period, so $c = f\lambda$.
**Formula:** $c = f\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f}$
**Conversions:** $f = 2.45\,\text{GHz} = 2.45 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}$. Finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{2.45 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}} = 0.1224\,\text{m} = 0.1224 \cdot 100\,\text{cm} = 12.24\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $12.2\,\text{cm}$, three significant figures like the frequency $2.45\,\text{GHz}$.
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength is between a millimetre and a metre, that is, in the region of microwaves.

## fy7-9-L1-03
**Given:** surface temperature of the skin $T = 307\,\text{K}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the skin is assumed to be a black body
**Find:** wavelength of the peak of the spectrum $\lambda_\text{max}$ in micrometres
**Principle:** The position of the peak of a black-body spectrum depends only on the surface temperature. Wien's displacement law fits because skin radiates approximately like a black body in the infrared region and the temperature is given in kelvins.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{b}{T}$
**Conversions:** The temperature is already in kelvins. Finally $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{307\,\text{K}} = 9.440 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} = 9.440\,\mu\text{m}$
**Answer:** $9.44\,\mu\text{m}$, three significant figures like the temperature $307\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** The peak is in the infrared region, far beyond the $750\,\text{nm} = 0.75\,\mu\text{m}$ of visible light, so the skin does not glow. The result is slightly shorter than the $9.7\,\mu\text{m}$ of a body at room temperature, because the skin is slightly warmer.

## fy7-9-L1-04
**Given:** wavelength of the peak of the spectrum $\lambda_\text{max} = 290\,\text{nm}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the star is assumed to be a black body
**Find:** surface temperature of the star $T$ in kelvins
**Principle:** A star radiates approximately like a black body, whose spectrum peak depends only on the surface temperature. So the temperature can be solved from Wien's displacement law when the peak is known.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda_\text{max} = 290\,\text{nm} = 290 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $T = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{290 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 9993\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $1.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the wavelength $290\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The peak is in the ultraviolet region, at a shorter wavelength than the $502\,\text{nm}$ of the Sun, so the star must be hotter than the Sun: $5772\,\text{K} \cdot 502 : 290 \approx 10\,000\,\text{K}$. Such a star looks bluish white.

## fy7-9-L1-05
**Given:** wavelength of the light $\lambda = 532\,\text{nm}$, speed of light $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** frequency of the light $f$ in terahertz
**Principle:** Light is an electromagnetic wave, which travels one wavelength during one period, so $c = f\lambda$. In air the speed of light is almost the same as in a vacuum.
**Formula:** $c = f\lambda$, so $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda = 532\,\text{nm} = 532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. Finally $1\,\text{THz} = 10^{12}\,\text{Hz}$.
**Substitution:** $f = \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 5.639 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz} = 563.9\,\text{THz}$
**Answer:** $564\,\text{THz}$, that is $5.64 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, three significant figures like the wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The frequencies of visible light are between about $4.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ (red, $750\,\text{nm}$) and $7.9 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ (violet, $380\,\text{nm}$), and green light lies between them. Units: $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{m}} = \tfrac{1}{\text{s}} = \text{Hz}$.

## fy7-9-L2-01
**Given:** temperature of the vault $t = 450\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the vault is assumed to be a black body
**Find:** wavelength of the peak of the spectrum $\lambda_\text{max}$ in micrometres
**Principle:** The hot vault radiates approximately like a black body, whose spectrum peak depends only on the temperature. In Wien's displacement law the temperature must be absolute, so degrees Celsius are first converted to kelvins.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{b}{T}$
**Conversions:** $T = (450 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 723.15\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{723.15\,\text{K}} = 4.007 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} = 4.007\,\mu\text{m}$
**Answer:** $4.01\,\mu\text{m}$, three significant figures like the temperature $450\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, that is $723\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** The peak is in the infrared region, so the vault hardly glows: a red glow visible in the dark begins only at about $800\,\text{K}$. The result lies between the $9.7\,\mu\text{m}$ of a body at room temperature and the $3.6\,\mu\text{m}$ of a dark-red glow, as the temperature does too.

## fy7-9-L2-02
**Given:** the spectrum curve in the graph; constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the body radiates like a black body
**Find:** surface temperature of the body $T$ in kelvins
**Principle:** The position of the peak of a black-body spectrum depends only on the surface temperature, so the temperature follows from Wien's displacement law once the wavelength of the peak has been read from the graph.
**Reading the graph:** The highest point of the curve is on the vertical grid line that is one square ($0.2\,\mu\text{m}$) to the right of the value $1$ (the grid lines are $0.2\,\mu\text{m}$ apart), so $\lambda_\text{max} = 1.2\,\mu\text{m}$. The height of the peak is not needed, because the temperature depends only on the position of the peak.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda_\text{max} = 1.2\,\mu\text{m} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $T = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{1.2 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 2415\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $2.4 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the wavelength $1.2\,\mu\text{m}$ read from the graph.
**Reasonableness:** The peak is in the infrared region but close to visible light, and the curve already rises at the visible wavelengths. So the body glows brightly, and the temperature is of the order of that of the filament of an incandescent lamp, about $2700\,\text{K}$.

## fy7-9-L2-03
**Given:** initial temperature $t_1 = 27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, final temperature $t_2 = 327\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the plate is assumed to be a black body
**Find:** shift of the peak of the spectrum $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_1 - \lambda_2$ in micrometres
**Principle:** According to Wien's displacement law the wavelength of the peak is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature, so on heating the peak shifts to shorter wavelengths. We calculate the peak at both temperatures and take the difference. The difference cannot be found from the temperature difference, because $\lambda_\text{max}$ does not depend linearly on the temperature.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{b}{T}$ and $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{b}{T_1} - \dfrac{b}{T_2}$
**Conversions:** $T_1 = (27 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 300.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = (327 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 600.15\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{300.15\,\text{K}} - \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{600.15\,\text{K}} = 9.655\,\mu\text{m} - 4.829\,\mu\text{m} = 4.826\,\mu\text{m}$
**Answer:** $4.83\,\mu\text{m}$ towards shorter wavelengths, three significant figures, because the temperatures are given to the nearest degree.
**Reasonableness:** The absolute temperature almost doubles, so the wavelength of the peak almost halves, from about $9.7\,\mu\text{m}$ to about $4.8\,\mu\text{m}$; the shift is therefore about half of the original wavelength. Both peaks are in the infrared region, so the plate does not glow.

## fy7-9-L2-04
**Given:** frequency of the peak of the spectrum $f = 6.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, speed of light $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the star is assumed to be a black body
**Find:** surface temperature of the star $T$ in kelvins
**Principle:** Wien's displacement law contains the wavelength of the peak, so first the frequency is converted to a wavelength with $c = f\lambda$, which holds for all electromagnetic radiation. Then the temperature is solved from Wien's displacement law.
**Formula:** $c = f\lambda_\text{max}$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{c}{f}$, and $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}} = \dfrac{b f}{c}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: all quantities are in SI units.
**Substitution:** $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{6.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$ and $T = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}} = 5796\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $5.8 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the frequency $6.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$.
**Reasonableness:** The peak is at $500\,\text{nm}$, in the middle of visible light, as for the Sun, and the temperature is almost the same as the $5772\,\text{K}$ of the surface of the Sun. So the star is similar to the Sun.

## fy7-9-L3-01
**Given:** temperature of the background radiation $T = 2.7255\,\text{K}$ (Fixsen 2009), constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the spectrum is a black-body spectrum
**Find:** wavelength of the peak of the spectrum $\lambda_\text{max}$ in millimetres and the region of the spectrum
**Principle:** Since the spectrum of the background radiation corresponds to black-body radiation, the position of its peak depends only on the temperature and follows from Wien's displacement law. The region is found by comparing the result with the boundaries of the regions of the spectrum.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{b}{T}$
**Conversions:** The temperature is already in kelvins. Finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{2.7255\,\text{K}} = 1.0633 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} = 1.0633\,\text{mm}$
**Answer:** $1.063\,\text{mm}$, four significant figures like the constant $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$. The wavelength is slightly over $1\,\text{mm}$, so the radiation belongs to microwaves.
**Reasonableness:** The temperature is about a hundred times lower than room temperature, $300\,\text{K}$, so the peak must be at a wavelength about a hundred times longer than the $9.7\,\mu\text{m}$ of a body at room temperature: $100 \cdot 9.7\,\mu\text{m} \approx 1\,\text{mm}$.

## fy7-9-L3-02
**Given:** wavelength range of the camera $8.0\,\mu\text{m}$–$14\,\mu\text{m}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the body is a black body
**Find:** the highest temperature $t$ in degrees Celsius at which the peak of the spectrum is still within the range
**Principle:** According to Wien's displacement law the peak of a hotter body is at a shorter wavelength. So the body with the highest temperature has its peak at the short end of the range, $\lambda_\text{max} = 8.0\,\mu\text{m}$; the peak of an even hotter body is outside the range.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$, and $t = T - 273.15\,\text{K}$ in degrees Celsius
**Conversions:** $\lambda_\text{max} = 8.0\,\mu\text{m} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$. Finally $273.15\,\text{K}$ is subtracted from the kelvins to get degrees Celsius.
**Substitution:** $T = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{8.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 362.25\,\text{K}$, so $t = (362.25 - 273.15)\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 89.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $89\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures like the wavelength $8.0\,\mu\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The other end of the range, $14\,\mu\text{m}$, gives the lowest temperature $2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K} : (14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}) = 207\,\text{K}$, about $-66\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The range $-66\,{}^\circ\text{C}$…$89\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ covers people, buildings and nature, which are what a thermal camera is used to image.

## fy7-9-L3-03
**Given:** time between the departure and the return of the laser pulse $t = 2.56\,\text{s}$, speed of light $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** distance of the reflector from the Earth $s$ in kilometres
**Principle:** Laser light is electromagnetic radiation, which travels through space and almost the whole atmosphere at the speed of light, steadily. In the measured time the light covers the distance twice, to the Moon and back, so the distance is $2s = c t$.
**Formula:** $2s = c t$, so $s = \dfrac{c t}{2}$
**Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $s = \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.56\,\text{s}}{2} = 3.84 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m} = 3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$
**Answer:** $3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$, three significant figures like the time $2.56\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The mean distance of the Moon from the Earth is about $384\,000\,\text{km}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. If you forget to divide the time by two, you get double the distance, which is too large.
