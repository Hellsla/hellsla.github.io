## fy7-6-L1-01
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 40\,\text{g}$, steady speed, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the braking force $F$ of the eddy currents in newtons
**Principle:** The speed is steady, so by Newton's first law the sum of the forces on the magnet is zero. Only the weight downwards and the braking force of the eddy currents upwards act on the magnet, so the braking force equals the weight.
**Formula:** $F - m g = 0$, so $F = m g$
**Conversions:** $m = 40\,\text{g} = 40 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.040\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $F = 0.040\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 0.3924\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $0.39\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $40\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** The force equals the weight of a $40\,\text{g}$ object, about $0.4\,\text{N}$. If the force were smaller, the magnet would accelerate; if greater, it would slow down. A steady speed requires a force exactly equal to the weight.

## fy7-6-L1-02
**Find:** the pole on the side of the ring facing the magnet, and whether the ring repels or attracts the magnet
**Principle:** By Lenz's law the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change that induces the current. An approaching magnet increases the flux through the ring, so the field of the ring opposes the increase.
**Model answer:** When the south pole approaches, the flux through the ring increases. The induced current circulates so that the ring becomes a magnet that opposes the arrival of the magnet: a south pole appears on the side of the ring facing the magnet. Like poles repel, so the ring repels the magnet and swings away from it. The same would happen if the approaching pole were a north pole; then the side of the ring facing the magnet would be a north pole. So the force of the ring always opposes the motion of the magnet, which corresponds to conservation of energy: whoever pushes the magnet does work, and the work is converted into electrical energy and internal energy of the ring.

## fy7-6-L1-03
**Given:** mass of the car $m = 450\,\text{kg}$, initial speed $v_1 = 22\,\text{m/s}$, final speed $v_2 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, horizontal track
**Find:** the energy $Q$ converted into internal energy of the copper plates, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The track is horizontal and other resistances are ignored, so the potential energy does not change and the only change in energy is the decrease in kinetic energy. Energy is conserved: the eddy currents convert the whole decrease in kinetic energy into internal energy of the plates of the brake.
**Formula:** $Q = \dfrac{m v_1^2}{2} - \dfrac{m v_2^2}{2}$, that is, $Q = \dfrac{m (v_1^2 - v_2^2)}{2}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in SI units. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 450\,\text{kg} \cdot \left((22\,\text{m/s})^2 - (6.0\,\text{m/s})^2\right) = 225\,\text{kg} \cdot 448\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = 100\,800\,\text{J} = 100.8\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $1.0 \cdot 10^2\,\text{kJ}$, that is, about $100\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the final speed $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the initial speed $22\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The initial kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 450\,\text{kg} \cdot (22\,\text{m/s})^2 \approx 109\,\text{kJ}$, and only about $8\,\text{kJ}$ is left to the car, because kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed. So the brake converts most of the kinetic energy into internal energy, as it should.

## fy7-6-L1-04
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 25\,\text{g}$, steady speed $v = 0.30\,\text{m/s}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the power $P$ at which the internal energy of the tube increases, in milliwatts
**Principle:** The speed is steady, so the braking force of the eddy currents equals the weight, $F = m g$. The kinetic energy does not change, so the work done by the braking force, which is the decrease in potential energy, is converted entirely into internal energy of the tube. Power is force times speed.
**Formula:** $F = m g$ and $P = F v$, so $P = m g v$
**Conversions:** $m = 25\,\text{g} = 25 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.025\,\text{kg}$. Finally $1\,\text{W} = 1000\,\text{mW}$.
**Substitution:** $P = 0.025\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.30\,\text{m/s} = 0.073575\,\text{W} = 73.575\,\text{mW}$
**Answer:** $74\,\text{mW}$, two significant figures like the mass $25\,\text{g}$ and the speed $0.30\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** With round numbers the weight is about $0.25\,\text{N}$ and $0.25\,\text{N} \cdot 0.3\,\text{m/s} \approx 0.075\,\text{W}$. The power is much smaller than that of a torch bulb, so the tube does not feel warm.

## fy7-6-L1-05
**Find:** why an induction hob heats the pan but not the glass surface
**Principle:** A changing magnetic field induces eddy currents in an electrically conducting body, and the currents convert electrical energy into internal energy of the conductor. A current needs a conductor in which it can circulate as a closed loop.
**Model answer:** The alternating current in the coil produces a magnetic field that changes direction tens of thousands of times a second. Induced currents, eddy currents, appear in the steel base of the pan penetrated by the field, and they circulate as closed loops in the metal of the base. Because of the resistance of the base, the currents convert electrical energy into internal energy of the base, and the base becomes hot and heats the food. Glass is an electrical insulator, so no currents appear in it, although the field passes through it. The glass warms up only where heat is conducted from the pan to the glass; next to the pan it stays cool.

## fy7-6-L1-06
**Find:** what happens in the copper ring when the north pole of the magnet is pulled away
**Principle:** By Lenz's law the field of the induced current opposes the change in flux. When the magnet moves away, the flux through the ring decreases, so the field of the induced current tries to keep the flux unchanged: at the centre of the ring it points in the same direction as the field of the magnet.
**Model answer:** The flux through the ring decreases, so the field of the induced current tries to maintain the flux: a south pole appears on the side of the ring facing the magnet. A south pole attracts a north pole, so the ring attracts the magnet and opposes its moving away. The option in which the field of the induced current opposes the field of the magnet and the ring repels the magnet describes an approaching magnet, when the flux increases. The option in which the flux increases as the magnet moves is wrong, because the flux of a receding magnet decreases. The option in which no current appears is wrong, because induction does not require a ferromagnetic material but an electrically conducting closed loop, and copper conducts electricity well. The ring opposes the motion of the magnet, so the person moving the magnet does work, which is converted into electrical energy and internal energy of the ring.

## fy7-6-L1-07
**Find:** why a magnet falls more slowly in an aluminium tube than in a plastic tube
**Principle:** A falling magnet changes the magnetic flux through the tube. In an electrically conducting tube this induces eddy currents, which by Lenz's law oppose the change in flux, that is, the motion of the magnet. In an insulator the current cannot circulate.
**Model answer:** Aluminium conducts electricity, so the change in flux caused by the falling magnet induces eddy currents in the wall of the tube. By Lenz's law their field opposes the motion of the magnet, and the braking force grows with the speed until it equals the weight and the magnet falls at a small steady speed. Plastic is an insulator, so no currents appear in its wall and there is no braking, and the magnet falls almost freely. The option in which aluminium attracts the magnet is wrong, because aluminium is not a ferromagnetic material and the braking arises only from the motion. The option in which the lightness of plastic increases gravity is wrong, because gravity depends only on the mass of the magnet. The option in which the roughness of aluminium causes friction is wrong, because friction does not depend on the electrical conductivity of the tube and eddy-current braking works without contact.

## fy7-6-L1-08
**Given:** number of turns $N = 120$, change in flux through one turn $\Delta\Phi = 2.5\,\text{mWb}$, time $\Delta t = 0.050\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the induced voltage $e$ in volts
**Principle:** The flux changes steadily, so by the law of induction a constant voltage appears in the coil, proportional to the number of turns and to the rate of change of the flux. The magnitude is calculated without the minus sign, because the minus sign only tells the direction given by Lenz's law.
**Formula:** $e = N \dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta\Phi = 2.5\,\text{mWb} = 2.5 \cdot 0.001\,\text{Wb} = 0.0025\,\text{Wb}$
**Substitution:** $e = 120 \cdot \dfrac{0.0025\,\text{Wb}}{0.050\,\text{s}} = 120 \cdot 0.050\,\dfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}} = 6.0\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{V}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** Unit: $\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}} = \tfrac{\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{s}} = \text{V}$. The flux changes by $0.050\,\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}}$, that is, $50\,\tfrac{\text{mWb}}{\text{s}}$, and $120$ turns multiply this by $120$, which is $6.0\,\text{V}$.

## fy7-6-L2-01
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 20\,\text{g}$, length of the tube $h = 1.50\,\text{m}$, falling time $t = 10\,\text{s}$, almost steady speed, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the average rate $P$ at which the internal energy of the tube increases, in milliwatts
**Principle:** The speed is almost steady over the whole distance, so it is obtained from the distance and the time, and the braking force equals the weight. In a steady fall the kinetic energy does not change, so potential energy is converted through the eddy currents into internal energy of the tube at the rate $P = F v$.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{h}{t}$, $F = m g$ and $P = F v$, so $P = m g v$
**Conversions:** $m = 20\,\text{g} = 20 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.020\,\text{kg}$. Finally $1\,\text{W} = 1000\,\text{mW}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{1.50\,\text{m}}{10\,\text{s}} = 0.15\,\text{m/s}$ and $P = 0.020\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.15\,\text{m/s} = 0.02943\,\text{W} = 29.43\,\text{mW}$
**Answer:** $29\,\text{mW}$, two significant figures like the mass $20\,\text{g}$ and the time $10\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** A check through energy: the energy converted into internal energy is $Q = m g h - \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = 0.2943\,\text{J} - 0.0002\,\text{J} \approx 0.294\,\text{J}$, and $0.294\,\text{J} : 10\,\text{s} \approx 0.029\,\text{W}$. The results are the same.

## fy7-6-L2-02
**Given:** number of turns $N = 200$, resistance $R = 15\,\Omega$, change in flux $\Delta\Phi = 3.0\,\text{mWb}$, time $\Delta t = 0.20\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the induced current $I$ in amperes
**Principle:** The flux changes steadily, so by the law of induction a constant induced voltage appears in the coil. The coil is closed, so the voltage drives a current through the resistance of the coil by Ohm's law. By Lenz's law the field of the current opposes the increase in flux, so the end of the coil facing the magnet becomes the same pole as the approaching pole. The magnitude is calculated without the minus sign.
**Formula:** $e = N \dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$ and $I = \dfrac{e}{R}$, so $I = \dfrac{N \Delta\Phi}{R \Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta\Phi = 3.0\,\text{mWb} = 3.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{Wb} = 0.0030\,\text{Wb}$
**Substitution:** $e = 200 \cdot \dfrac{0.0030\,\text{Wb}}{0.20\,\text{s}} = 3.0\,\text{V}$ and $I = \dfrac{3.0\,\text{V}}{15\,\Omega} = 0.20\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $0.20\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** Units: $\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}} = \tfrac{\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{s}} = \text{V}$ and $\tfrac{\text{V}}{\Omega} = \text{A}$. A voltage of a few volts and a current of a tenth of an ampere are reasonable for a magnet pushed in by hand.

## fy7-6-L2-03
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 20\,\text{g}$, braking force $F_\text{j} = k v$, where $k = 1.3\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{m}}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the final speed $v_\text{t}$ of the magnet in metres per second
**Principle:** At first the magnet accelerates, and the braking force grows with the speed. The speed stops growing when the braking force has grown equal to the weight: then the sum of the forces is zero, and the magnet falls at a steady speed.
**Formula:** $k v_\text{t} = m g$, so $v_\text{t} = \dfrac{m g}{k}$
**Conversions:** $m = 20\,\text{g} = 20 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.020\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $v_\text{t} = \dfrac{0.020\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.3\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{m}}} = \dfrac{0.1962\,\text{N}}{1.3\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{m}}} = 0.1509\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $0.15\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the coefficient $1.3\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{m}}$ and the mass $20\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** Unit: $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}/\text{m}} = \text{m/s}$. At a speed of $0.15\,\text{m/s}$ a tube one metre long takes about $7\,\text{s}$, which is a typical time in a copper-tube drop experiment. A heavier magnet would fall faster.

## fy7-6-L2-04
**Given:** braking force $F = 60\,\text{kN}$, speed of the train $v = 250\,\text{km/h}$
**Find:** the power $P$ at which kinetic energy is converted into internal energy, in megawatts
**Principle:** The braking force does work against the motion of the train. In a time $\Delta t$ the train travels a distance $v \Delta t$, and the work of the force is $F v \Delta t$, so the power of the work, that is, of the change in energy, is $P = F v$. The eddy currents convert this energy into internal energy of the brake and the rail.
**Formula:** $P = F v$
**Conversions:** $F = 60\,\text{kN} = 60 \cdot 1000\,\text{N} = 60\,000\,\text{N}$ and $v = 250\,\text{km/h} = 250 \cdot \dfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 69.44\,\text{m/s}$. Finally $1\,\text{MW} = 10^{6}\,\text{W}$.
**Substitution:** $P = 60\,000\,\text{N} \cdot 69.44\,\text{m/s} = 4\,166\,667\,\text{W} = 4.1667\,\text{MW}$
**Answer:** $4.2\,\text{MW}$, two significant figures like the force $60\,\text{kN}$.
**Reasonableness:** The power is large, the equivalent of hundreds of electric heaters, which suits the brake of a heavy train at high speed. As the speed of the train decreases, the power decreases too, because it is proportional to the speed, and the force of an eddy-current brake becomes smaller as well.

## fy7-6-L2-05

## fy7-6-L2-06
**Find:** the error in the student's reasoning, the pole that appears on the side of the ring facing the magnet, and why the incorrect rule would violate conservation of energy
**Principle:** By Lenz's law the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change in flux that induces the current. When the flux increases, the field tries to reduce the flux. Conservation of energy requires that whoever moves the magnet does work, from which the energy of the induced current comes.
**Model answer:** The student's error is to say that the induced current increases the change in flux. By Lenz's law the field of the current opposes the change: when the north pole approaches and the flux increases, the field of the induced current tries to reduce the flux, so a north pole appears on the side of the ring facing the magnet and the ring repels the magnet. With the student's rule the ring would attract the approaching magnet, so the magnet would accelerate towards the ring, the change in flux would become faster, the induced current would grow, and instead of braking, the attraction would grow stronger and stronger. The magnet would gain kinetic energy and the ring electrical energy without anything doing work or supplying energy, which violates conservation of energy. The correct rule works the other way round: the magnet must be pushed against the ring, and this work is converted into electrical energy and internal energy of the ring.

## fy7-6-L2-07
**Given:** number of turns $N = 300$, four measurements $(\Delta t, e)$: $(0.40\,\text{s}, 0.27\,\text{V})$, $(0.25\,\text{s}, 0.41\,\text{V})$, $(0.20\,\text{s}, 0.53\,\text{V})$ and $(0.10\,\text{s}, 1.02\,\text{V})$ (example data)
**Find:** the change in magnetic flux $\Delta\Phi$ in milliwebers
**Principle:** By the law of induction the average induced voltage satisfies $e = N \Delta\Phi : \Delta t$, so the product $e \cdot \Delta t = N \Delta\Phi$ is the same in every push, because the number of turns and the change in flux do not change. Because of the random variation of the measurements, the mean of the products gives the best estimate.
**Formula:** $e \cdot \Delta t = N \Delta\Phi$, so $\Delta\Phi = \dfrac{(e \cdot \Delta t)_{\text{avg}}}{N}$
**Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{Wb} = 1000\,\text{mWb}$. The unit $1\,\text{V} \cdot \text{s} = 1\,\text{Wb}$.
**Substitution:** The products are $0.40\,\text{s} \cdot 0.27\,\text{V} = 0.108\,\text{Wb}$, $0.25\,\text{s} \cdot 0.41\,\text{V} = 0.1025\,\text{Wb}$, $0.20\,\text{s} \cdot 0.53\,\text{V} = 0.106\,\text{Wb}$ and $0.10\,\text{s} \cdot 1.02\,\text{V} = 0.102\,\text{Wb}$. The sum is $0.4185\,\text{Wb}$, so the mean is $0.10463\,\text{Wb}$ and $\Delta\Phi = \dfrac{0.10463\,\text{Wb}}{300} = 3.488 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{Wb} = 0.3488\,\text{mWb}$
**Answer:** $0.35\,\text{mWb}$, two significant figures like the voltages. Part c): the product $e \cdot \Delta t$ is almost the same at all speeds, so $e = \dfrac{N \Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$, that is, the voltage is inversely proportional to the time.
**Reasonableness:** The product $e \cdot \Delta t$ is about $0.10\,\text{Wb}$ in every measurement (between $0.102\ldots0.108\,\text{Wb}$), although the time $\Delta t$ changes by a factor of four ($0.40\,\text{s}$ and $0.10\,\text{s}$) and the voltage increases correspondingly by about a factor of four ($0.27\,\text{V}$ and $1.02\,\text{V}$). When the product is constant, $e = \dfrac{\text{constant}}{\Delta t}$, so the voltage is inversely proportional to the time. A faster push thus induces a greater voltage.

## fy7-6-L3-01
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 20\,\text{g}$, length of the tube $h = 1.00\,\text{m}$, number of drops $n = 50$, speed at the end $v = 0.15\,\text{m/s}$, mass of the tube $m_\text{p} = 0.35\,\text{kg}$, specific heat capacity of copper $c = 385\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the rise in temperature $\Delta T$ of the tube in kelvins
**Principle:** Energy is conserved. In each drop, the part of the decrease in the potential energy of the magnet that does not remain as kinetic energy is converted through the eddy currents into internal energy of the tube. No heat is transferred to the surroundings, so all this energy raises the temperature of the tube, and the state of the copper does not change.
**Formula:** $Q = n \left(m g h - \dfrac{m v^2}{2}\right)$ and $Q = c m_\text{p} \Delta T$, so $\Delta T = \dfrac{n \left(m g h - \frac{m v^2}{2}\right)}{c m_\text{p}}$
**Conversions:** $m = 20\,\text{g} = 20 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.020\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $m g h - \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = 0.020\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.00\,\text{m} - \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.020\,\text{kg} \cdot (0.15\,\text{m/s})^2 = 0.19620\,\text{J} - 0.000225\,\text{J} = 0.195975\,\text{J}$, so $Q = 50 \cdot 0.195975\,\text{J} = 9.79875\,\text{J}$ and $\Delta T = \dfrac{9.79875\,\text{J}}{385\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.35\,\text{kg}} = 0.07272\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $0.073\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the mass of the tube $0.35\,\text{kg}$ and the mass of the magnet $20\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** The kinetic energy is only about $0.1\,\%$ of the potential energy, so the estimate $50 \cdot 0.2\,\text{J} = 10\,\text{J}$ is almost exact. Ten joules raise the temperature of a $0.35\,\text{kg}$ copper tube by less than a tenth of a kelvin, so the warming cannot be noticed by hand, although the energy has gone into the tube.

## fy7-6-L3-02
**Find:** why an intact aluminium plate stops quickly between the poles of a magnet but a comb-shaped plate swings for a long time
**Principle:** The magnetic flux through a moving plate changes as the plate enters the space between the poles and leaves it. The change induces eddy currents in the conducting plate, and by Lenz's law they oppose the motion. A current needs a closed loop.
**Model answer:** When an intact plate swings between the poles, the flux through the part of the plate between the poles changes, and large eddy currents appear in the plate, circulating in wide loops in the metal of the plate. By Lenz's law their field opposes the change, so the magnet brakes the plate whenever it moves between the poles, whether it is entering or leaving. The eddy currents convert the mechanical energy of the pendulum into internal energy of the plate, and the swings die away quickly. In the comb-shaped plate the slits cut the large current loops: the currents can circulate only inside the narrow teeth, so they and their braking force stay small. Less mechanical energy is converted into internal energy, and the plate swings for a long time. The same principle applies to the laminated iron core of a transformer.

## fy7-6-L3-03
**Given:** distance between the rails, that is, length of the rod in the field, $l = 0.40\,\text{m}$, resistance $R = 0.20\,\Omega$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.50\,\text{T}$, speed of the rod $v = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$

The powers are equal, as conservation of energy and Lenz's law require.

## fy7-6-L3-04
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 50\,\text{g}$, $F_\text{j} = k v$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, table of speed against time (example data)

## fy7-6-L3-05
**Given:** thickness of the sheet $d$ and power $P$: $(1.00\,\text{mm}, 1.21\,\text{W})$, $(0.80\,\text{mm}, 0.76\,\text{W})$, $(0.50\,\text{mm}, 0.31\,\text{W})$, $(0.25\,\text{mm}, 0.073\,\text{W})$; the volume of the core and the field are the same, the sheets are thin compared with the penetration depth of the field (example data)
**Find:** the power $P$ in watts when $d = 0.35\,\text{mm}$ (and also the dependence $P(d)$ and the comparison with a thicker sheet)
**Principle:** If the power is proportional to the square of the thickness, the ratio $P : d^2$ is a constant $k$, and the new power is $P = k d^2$. The physical reason: a changing flux induces eddy currents in the sheet, and by Lenz's law they oppose the change and convert electrical energy into internal energy. The thickness of the sheet limits the size of the current loop: in a thin sheet the area of the loop is small, so the change in flux and the induced voltage are small. When the resistance stays the same, the power grows as the square of the voltage, so a thin sheet reduces the loss a lot.
**Formula:** $P = k d^2$, so $k = \dfrac{P}{d^2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: $k$ is obtained in the unit $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{mm}^2}$.
**Substitution:** The ratios are $\dfrac{1.21}{1.00^2} = 1.21$, $\dfrac{0.76}{0.80^2} = 1.19$, $\dfrac{0.31}{0.50^2} = 1.24$ and $\dfrac{0.073}{0.25^2} = 1.17$ in the unit $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{mm}^2}$. The sum is $4.805\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{mm}^2}$, so $k = 1.201\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{mm}^2}$ and $P = 1.201\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{mm}^2} \cdot (0.35\,\text{mm})^2 = 0.147\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $0.15\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the measurement results. The ratio $P : d^2$ is constant, so $P$ is proportional to the square of the thickness. Part d): with a thickness of $1.0\,\text{mm}$ the loss is about $8.2$ times that of the $0.35\,\text{mm}$ sheets.
**Reasonableness:** The ratios are between $1.17\ldots1.24\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{mm}^2}$, at most about $3\,\%$ from the mean, so the quadratic dependence fits the data. Halving the thickness divides the power by four: $1.21\,\text{W} : 4 = 0.30\,\text{W}$, which corresponds to the measured value $0.31\,\text{W}$. The ratio of the thicknesses is $\dfrac{1.0\,\text{mm}}{0.35\,\text{mm}} = 2.86$, so the ratio of the losses is $2.86^2 = 8.2$; this is why the iron core is assembled from thin insulated sheets.

## fy7-6-L2-08
**Find:** what happens to an aluminium ring resting on top of a coil when the current of the coil is switched on and grows, and why.
**Principle:** An induced current arises in the ring whenever the magnetic flux through it changes. By Lenz's law the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change that produces the current. Aluminium is not a ferromagnetic material, so an unchanging field neither attracts nor repels the ring. The iron core of the coil strengthens the field, so the change in flux is large.
**Model answer:** The ring jumps upwards. When the switch is closed, the current of the coil grows from zero, so the magnetic field of the coil and the flux through the ring grow too. An induced current arises in the ring, and at the centre of the ring its field points against the field of the coil, so it opposes the growth of the flux. The ring is then like a small magnet whose side facing the coil is a pole of the same name as the upper end of the coil. Poles of the same name repel each other, and the light ring jumps up. We do not need to know which pole the upper end of the coil is: it is enough that the side of the ring facing the coil is always of the same name.

The repelling force lasts only as long as the current grows. When the current has settled to a constant value, the flux no longer changes, the induced current stops and the ring falls back on the coil. So the direct current of the battery does not prevent induction, because at the moment of switching on the current changes. If the induced current strengthened the growth of the flux, the ring and the coil would attract each other and energy would be created out of nothing, which is against the conservation of energy. The fact that aluminium conducts electricity well is true, but it only says that a current can flow, not the direction of the force.

## fy7-6-L2-09
**Given:** a graph of the braking force $F$ as a function of the speed $v$; speed $v = 0.25\,\text{m/s}$

**Principle:** The braking force of the eddy currents does work against the motion of the magnet. In a time $\Delta t$ the magnet moves a distance $v \Delta t$, and the work $F v \Delta t$ becomes internal energy of the tube, so the power is $P = F v$. The force is read from the graph.

## fy7-6-L3-06
**Given:** mass of the magnet $m = 45\,\text{g}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; a graph of the braking force $F$ as a function of the speed $v$
**Asked:** the constant speed $v$ at which the magnet falls, in metres per second
**Principle:** When the magnet falls at a constant speed, the sum of the forces on it is zero, so the braking force equals the weight: $F = mg$. The line in the graph passes through the origin, so $F$ is directly proportional to the speed: $F = kv$. The speed is the one at which the force on the line equals the weight.
**Reading the graph:** The line passes through the origin, so its slope is found from one point. At the speed $0.20\,\text{m/s}$ the force is $0.32\,\text{N}$, so $k = F : v = 0.32\,\text{N} : 0.20\,\text{m/s} = 1.6\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m}$. Another point gives the same: $0.48\,\text{N} : 0.30\,\text{m/s} = 1.6\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m}$.
**Formula:** $mg = kv$, so $v = \dfrac{mg}{k}$
**Conversions:** $m = 45\,\text{g} = 45 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.045\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $mg = 0.045\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 0.44145\,\text{N}$ and $v = \dfrac{0.44145\,\text{N}}{1.6\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m}} = 0.2759\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $0.28\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the mass $45\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check from the graph: the weight $0.44\,\text{N}$ lies on the line where the speed is a little below $0.30\,\text{m/s}$, as it should. A lighter $20\,\text{g}$ magnet would fall more slowly: $v = 0.1962\,\text{N} : 1.6\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m} = 0.12\,\text{m/s}$. A heavier magnet falls faster because it needs a larger braking force.
