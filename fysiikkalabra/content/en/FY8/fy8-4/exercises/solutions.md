## fy8-4-L1-01
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 532\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the momentum $p$ of a photon in units of $10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** A photon has no mass, but it has a momentum that depends only on the wavelength. The light has one wavelength, so every photon has the same momentum.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $532\,\text{nm} = 532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. Unit: $\tfrac{\text{J}\,\text{s}}{\text{m}} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 1.2455 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $1.25 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** According to the theory section, the momentum of a photon of $500\,\text{nm}$ is $1.3 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. The wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$ is a little longer, so the momentum is a little smaller, as it should be. The value is very small because $h$ is very small.

## fy8-4-L1-02
**Given:** wavelength of the X-rays $\lambda = 0.0711\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the momentum $p$ of a photon in units of $10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The momentum of a photon depends only on its wavelength, so the same formula applies to X-rays as to visible light.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $0.0711\,\text{nm} = 0.0711 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{0.0711 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 9.3193 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $9.32 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the wavelength $0.0711\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength is about $7000$ times shorter than that of visible light, $500\,\text{nm}$, so the momentum is about $7000$ times greater: $7000 \cdot 1.3 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s} \approx 9 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.

## fy8-4-L1-03
**Given:** momentum of the photon $p = 3.60 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the wavelength $\lambda$ of the photon in nanometres
**Principle:** The momentum and the wavelength of a photon are inversely proportional, so the wavelength follows from the known momentum.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, from which $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$
**Conversions:** $\tfrac{\text{J}\,\text{s}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}} = \tfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}} = \text{m}$. Finally $1\,\text{m} = 10^9\,\text{nm}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{3.60 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}} = 1.8406 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m} = 184.06\,\text{nm}$
**Answer:** $184\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures as in the momentum $3.60 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The momentum is about three times that of a photon of visible light of $500\,\text{nm}$ ($1.3 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$), so the wavelength must be about a third: $500\,\text{nm} : 2.7 \approx 185\,\text{nm}$. The photon belongs to ultraviolet radiation.

## fy8-4-L1-04
**Given:** momentum of the photon $p = 2.00 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$; $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$
**Asked:** the energy $E$ of the photon in units of $10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Principle:** The momentum of a photon is its energy divided by the speed of light, because a photon always travels at the speed $c$ and has no mass.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{E}{c}$, from which $E = pc$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in SI units, and $\text{kg}\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = \text{kg}\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} = \text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $E = 2.00 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 5.996 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $6.00 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, three significant figures as in the momentum $2.00 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** In electronvolts the energy is $\tfrac{5.996 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV} \approx 3.7\,\text{eV}$. The wavelength of the photon is $\lambda = \tfrac{h}{p} = \tfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{2.00 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}} \approx 331\,\text{nm}$, so the photon is in the near ultraviolet, a little beyond the violet end of visible light ($400\,\text{nm}$). This is reasonable, because the momentum is about one and a half times that of a photon of $500\,\text{nm}$.

## fy8-4-L1-05
**Given:** power of the laser $P = 1.0\,\text{mW}$, energy of one photon $hf = 3.14 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the number of photons per second $\tfrac{N}{t}$ in units of $10^{15}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
**Principle:** The energy of light travels as photons. The power is the total energy of the photons emitted per second, so the number of photons per second is the power divided by the energy of one photon.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{N}{t} \cdot hf$, from which $\dfrac{N}{t} = \dfrac{P}{hf}$
**Conversions:** $1.0\,\text{mW} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}$
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{N}{t} = \dfrac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{J/s}}{3.14 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} = 3.185 \cdot 10^{15}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
**Answer:** $3.2 \cdot 10^{15}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, two significant figures as in the power $1.0\,\text{mW}$.
**Reasonableness:** The photon energy $3.14 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ is that of a photon of red light. One millijoule therefore takes thousands of trillions of photons, so ordinary light looks continuous. The number is of the same order of magnitude as the number of photons of a laser pointer mentioned in the example of the chapter.

## fy8-4-L2-01
**Given:** wavelength of the incident photon $\lambda = 0.0711\,\text{nm}$, of the scattered one $\lambda' = 0.0735\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ received by the electron in electronvolts
**Principle:** In the Compton effect, the photon collides with an almost free electron. Energy is conserved, so the electron receives the energy that the photon loses: $hf = hf' + E_\text{k}$. The energies of the photons are obtained from the wavelengths.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} + E_\text{k}$, from which $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'}$
**Conversions:** $0.0711\,\text{nm} = 0.0711 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $0.0735\,\text{nm} = 0.0735 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. Finally joules to electronvolts by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0711 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} - \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0735 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 2.7939 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} - 2.7027 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} = 9.123 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$ and in electronvolts $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{9.123 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV} = 569.5\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $5.7 \cdot 10^2\,\text{eV}$, two significant figures, because the difference of the wavelengths $0.0024\,\text{nm}$ is known only to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of the incident photon is $\tfrac{2.7939 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV} \approx 17.4\,\text{keV}$. The wavelength grows by about $3\,\%$, so the photon loses about $3\,\%$ of its energy, about $0.6\,\text{keV}$. The electron gains energy, so the result is positive.

## fy8-4-L2-02
**Given:** energy of the gamma photon $E = 662\,\text{keV}$; $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the momentum $p$ of the photon in units of $10^{-22}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The momentum of a photon is its energy divided by the speed of light. The energy is given in electronvolts, so it is first converted to joules.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{E}{c}$
**Conversions:** $662\,\text{keV} = 662 \cdot 10^3\,\text{eV} = 662 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 1.0605 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{J}$
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{1.0605 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{J}}{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}} = 3.5374 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $3.54 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the energy $662\,\text{keV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of the gamma photon is about $40$ times the energy $17.4\,\text{keV}$ of the X-ray photon, so the momentum is about $40$ times the momentum of the X-ray photon of level 1 task 2: $40 \cdot 9.3 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s} \approx 3.7 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.

## fy8-4-L2-03
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 1550\,\text{nm}$, the photon is reflected perpendicularly back; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the momentum $\Delta p$ received by the mirror from one photon in units of $10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Momentum is conserved. In reflection the momentum of the photon reverses, so its change is $p - (-p) = 2p$. The mirror receives a momentum of the same magnitude in the direction of arrival of the photon, because the total momentum is conserved. The wavelength of the reflected photon is the same, because a heavy mirror hardly receives any energy.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = p - (-p)$, where $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so $\Delta p = 2 \cdot \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $1550\,\text{nm} = 1550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $\Delta p = \dfrac{2 \cdot 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{1550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 8.550 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $8.55 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the wavelength $1550\,\text{nm}$ (the constant $2$ is exact).
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength is about three times that of green light, $532\,\text{nm}$, so the momentum of one photon is about a third of $1.25 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, that is, about $4 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, and the reflection doubles it.

## fy8-4-L2-04
**Given:** photons per second $\tfrac{N}{t} = 2.40 \cdot 10^4\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, wavelength $\lambda = 550\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$
**Asked:** the power $P$ of the light in units of $10^{-15}\,\text{W}$
**Principle:** The power is the total energy of the photons arriving per second. The energy of each photon is $hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, because the light has one wavelength.
**Formula:** $P = \dfrac{N}{t} \cdot hf$, where $hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so $P = \dfrac{N}{t} \cdot \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $550\,\text{nm} = 550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $hf = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3.6118 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ and $P = 2.40 \cdot 10^4\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot 3.6118 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 8.668 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $8.67 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{W}$, three significant figures as in the number of photons $2.40 \cdot 10^4$ and the wavelength $550\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The power is a millionth of a billionth of a watt, so such light is too weak to be seen by the eye. A check the other way round: $\tfrac{8.668 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{W}}{3.6118 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} = 2.40 \cdot 10^4\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$.

## fy8-4-L3-01
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 589\,\text{nm}$, mass of the atom $m = 3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, initial speed $v_0 = 3.00 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m/s}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the number $N$ of photons needed, in units of $10^4$
**Principle:** Momentum is conserved. When the atom absorbs a photon coming against it, the momentum of the atom decreases by the momentum $\tfrac{h}{\lambda}$ of the photon, so its speed decreases by $\Delta v = \tfrac{h}{\lambda m}$. The atom stops when $N$ photons have reduced its momentum $m v_0$ to zero. The re-emitted photons leave in random directions, so on average their effects cancel.
**Formula:** $m v_0 = N p$, where $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so $N = \dfrac{m v_0}{p}$
**Conversions:** $589\,\text{nm} = 589 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{589 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 1.1250 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ and $m v_0 = 3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m/s} = 1.146 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, so $N = \dfrac{1.146 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{1.1250 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}} = 1.0187 \cdot 10^4$
**Answer:** $1.02 \cdot 10^4$ photons, three significant figures as in the mass, the speed and the wavelength.
**Reasonableness:** One photon changes the speed of the atom by $\tfrac{1.1250 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}} \approx 0.029\,\text{m/s}$, so stopping $300\,\text{m/s}$ takes about $300 : 0.029 \approx 10\,000$ photons. An atom can absorb and emit photons millions of times per second, so the braking takes only about a millisecond.

## fy8-4-L3-02
**Given:** wavelength of the incident photon $\lambda = 0.0500\,\text{nm}$, kinetic energy of the electron $E_\text{k} = 1.00\,\text{keV}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the wavelength $\lambda'$ of the scattered photon in nanometres
**Principle:** In the Compton effect, energy is conserved: the energy of the incident photon is shared between the scattered photon and the electron. The wavelength of the scattered photon follows from its energy.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} + E_\text{k}$, from which $\lambda' = \dfrac{hc}{hc/\lambda - E_\text{k}}$
**Conversions:** $0.0500\,\text{nm} = 0.0500 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $1.00\,\text{keV} = 1.00 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0500 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3.9729 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$, so the energy of the scattered photon is $3.9729 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} - 0.1602 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} = 3.8127 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$ and $\lambda' = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{3.8127 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}} = 5.210 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{m} = 0.05210\,\text{nm}$
**Answer:** $0.0521\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures as in the wavelength $0.0500\,\text{nm}$ and the energy $1.00\,\text{keV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of the incident photon is about $24.8\,\text{keV}$, and the electron receives $1.00\,\text{keV}$ of it, about $4\,\%$. The wavelength of the photon therefore grows by about $4\,\%$: $0.0500\,\text{nm} \cdot 1.04 = 0.0520\,\text{nm}$. The wavelength grows, as it must in the Compton effect.

## fy8-4-L3-03
**Given:** transition $n = 3 \to n = 2$, $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$, mass of the hydrogen atom $m = 1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, the atom initially at rest; $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the recoil speed $v$ of the atom in metres per second
**Principle:** The energy of the photon is the difference of the energy levels. Momentum is conserved: the total momentum is initially zero, so after the photon has left, the momentum $mv$ of the atom is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the momentum $\tfrac{E}{c}$ of the photon. The kinetic energy received by the atom is so small that the energy of the photon is in practice the difference of the levels.
**Formula:** $E = E_3 - E_2$ and $mv = \dfrac{E}{c}$, from which $v = \dfrac{E}{mc}$
**Conversions:** $E_3 = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{9} = -1.511\,\text{eV}$ and $E_2 = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{4} = -3.400\,\text{eV}$, so $E = 1.889\,\text{eV} = 1.889 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 3.026 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{3.026 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}{1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}} = 0.6044\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $0.604\,\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the mass $1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$ and the energy $13.6\,\text{eV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The momentum of the photon is $\tfrac{3.026 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}} \approx 1.0 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, roughly equal to the mass of a hydrogen atom in kilograms, so the speed is less than $1\,\text{m/s}$. It is very small compared with the speeds of the thermal motion of atoms, which at room temperature are kilometres per second, so the recoil does not show in everyday observations. The photon is red light, $656\,\text{nm}$.
