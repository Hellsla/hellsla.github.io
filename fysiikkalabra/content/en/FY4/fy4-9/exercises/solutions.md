## fy4-9-L1-01
**Given:** mass of the puck $m = 0.17\,\text{kg}$, velocity $v = 30\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the momentum of the puck $p$ in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Momentum is the product of mass and velocity. It is a vector with the direction of the velocity; here its magnitude is asked.
**Formula:** $p = mv$
**Conversions:** The mass and the velocity are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $p = 0.17\,\text{kg} \cdot 30\,\text{m/s} = 5.1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $5.1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.17\,\text{kg}$ and the velocity $30\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate with round numbers: $0.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 30\,\text{m/s} = 6\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. A light puck has a small momentum even though its velocity is large: the momentum of a walking person is about $100\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.

## fy4-9-L1-02
**Given:** pushing force $F = 12\,\text{N}$, time of action $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the impulse of the pushing force $I$ in $\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Principle:** The impulse of a constant force is the product of the force and the time it acts. The impulse has the direction of the force, here the direction of the push.
**Formula:** $I = F\,\Delta t$
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the time in seconds, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $I = 12\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{s} = 6.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, two significant figures like the force $12\,\text{N}$ and the time $0.50\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Half of $12\,\text{N}$ is $6\,\text{N}$, so the product is correct. If the mass of the cart were $15\,\text{kg}$ and there were no friction, its velocity would increase by $6.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s} : 15\,\text{kg} = 0.40\,\text{m/s}$, which fits a short push.

## fy4-9-L1-03
**Given:** mass of the ball $m = 0.27\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 0$, final velocity $v = 18\,\text{m/s}$; the effect of the weight is neglected
**Find:** the impulse the hand gives the ball $I$ in $\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Principle:** During the spike the force of the hand is the net force on the ball, when the effect of the weight is neglected. By the impulse–momentum principle its impulse equals the change in the ball's momentum.
**Formula:** $I = \Delta p = mv - mv_0$, and $v_0 = 0$, so $I = mv$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $I = 0.27\,\text{kg} \cdot 18\,\text{m/s} = 4.86\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Answer:** $4.9\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.27\,\text{kg}$ and the velocity $18\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $0.3\,\text{kg} \cdot 20\,\text{m/s} = 6\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, and both numbers were rounded up, so the result $4.9$ is suitable. The impulse is in the direction the ball leaves, since the ball started from rest and moved off in that direction.

## fy4-9-L1-04
**Given:** mass of the skier $m = 70\,\text{kg}$, momentum $p = 420\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the velocity of the skier $v$ in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Momentum is the product of mass and velocity, so the velocity is obtained by dividing the momentum by the mass.
**Formula:** $p = mv$, so $v = \dfrac{p}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed; $\tfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{\text{kg}} = \text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{420\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{70\,\text{kg}} = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the mass $70\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** $70\,\text{kg} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m/s} = 420\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, so the calculation works backwards. The velocity is about $22\,\text{km/h}$, which fits a skier.

## fy4-9-L1-05
**Given:** impulse $I = 0.15\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, contact time $\Delta t = 0.0010\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the average force of the racket $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** Impulse is force times time. When the force changes during contact, the impulse divided by the contact time gives the average force.
**Formula:** $I = F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{I}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** The time is in seconds, so no conversions are needed; $\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{s}} = \text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{0.15\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{0.0010\,\text{s}} = 150\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $150\,\text{N}$, that is, $1.5 \cdot 10^2\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the impulse $0.15\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$ and the time $0.0010\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Dividing by a thousandth of a second multiplies the number by a thousand: $0.15 \cdot 1000 = 150$. The force is large compared with a light shuttlecock, but it acts for only a thousandth of a second, as in hits generally.

## fy4-9-L2-01
**Given:** mass of the car $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 54\,\text{km/h}$, final velocity $v = 0$, stopping time $\Delta t = 3.0\,\text{s}$; air resistance is neglected
**Find:** the average braking force $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{kN}$
**Principle:** The road is horizontal, so the weight of the car and the normal force of the road cancel each other, and the horizontal net force is the braking friction force of the road. By the impulse–momentum principle its impulse equals the change in the car's momentum. The positive direction is the direction of motion of the car.
**Formula:** $F\,\Delta t = mv - mv_0 = -mv_0$, so the magnitude of the force is $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{mv_0}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $v_0 = 54\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{54\,\text{m}}{3.6\,\text{s}} = 15\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{1200\,\text{kg} \cdot 15\,\text{m/s}}{3.0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{18\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{3.0\,\text{s}} = 6000\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the velocity $54\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $3.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The sign of the force is negative, that is, it points against the motion, as a braking force should. The force is about half of the weight of the car, $1200\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 12\,\text{kN}$, which corresponds to ordinary braking on a dry road.

## fy4-9-L2-02
**Given:** mass of the ball $m = 46\,\text{g}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 0$, final velocity $v = 70\,\text{m/s}$, contact time $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{ms}$; the effect of the weight is neglected
**Find:** the average force of the club $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{kN}$
**Principle:** During the hit the force of the club is the net force on the ball, when the effect of the weight is neglected. By the impulse–momentum principle the impulse of the force of the club equals the change in the ball's momentum.
**Formula:** $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = mv - mv_0 = mv$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{mv}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $m = 46\,\text{g} = 0.046\,\text{kg}$ and $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{ms} = 0.00050\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{0.046\,\text{kg} \cdot 70\,\text{m/s}}{0.00050\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{3.22\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{0.00050\,\text{s}} = 6440\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $6.4\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the mass $46\,\text{g}$, the velocity $70\,\text{m/s}$ and the time $0.50\,\text{ms}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the ball is only $0.046\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.45\,\text{N}$, so the force of the club is over $10\,000$ times larger and neglecting the weight was justified. A large force is possible because it acts for only half a millisecond.

## fy4-9-L2-03
**Given:** mass of the ball $m = 60\,\text{g}$, incoming velocity $14\,\text{m/s}$ towards the wall, outgoing velocity $11\,\text{m/s}$ away from the wall
**Find:** the magnitude of the impulse the wall gives the ball $|I|$ in $\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Principle:** The force of the wall changes the ball's momentum, and by the impulse–momentum principle the impulse equals the change in momentum. The ball rebounds in the opposite direction, so the velocities need signs. The positive direction is chosen towards the wall: $v_0 = +14\,\text{m/s}$ and $v = -11\,\text{m/s}$.
**Formula:** $I = \Delta p = mv - mv_0 = m(v - v_0)$
**Conversions:** $m = 60\,\text{g} = 0.060\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $I = 0.060\,\text{kg} \cdot (-11 - 14)\,\text{m/s} = 0.060\,\text{kg} \cdot (-25\,\text{m/s}) = -1.5\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$
**Answer:** $1.5\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, two significant figures like the mass $60\,\text{g}$ and the velocities; the impulse points away from the wall.
**Reasonableness:** The impulse is larger than the $0.060\,\text{kg} \cdot 14\,\text{m/s} = 0.84\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$ needed just to stop the ball, because the wall also accelerates the ball back. If the velocities were subtracted without signs, the result would be only $0.18\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, which is too small even to stop the ball.

## fy4-9-L2-04
**Given:** mass of the puck $m = 0.17\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$, average force of the stick $F = 120\,\text{N}$ in the direction of motion, contact time $\Delta t = 0.025\,\text{s}$; friction from the ice is neglected
**Find:** the velocity of the puck $v$ after the hit in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** When friction is neglected, the force of the stick is the horizontal net force on the puck. By the impulse–momentum principle its impulse equals the change in the puck's momentum. The force is in the direction of motion, so the momentum increases.
**Formula:** $F\,\Delta t = mv - mv_0$, so $v = v_0 + \dfrac{F\,\Delta t}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed; $\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{kg}} = \text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $v = 8.0\,\text{m/s} + \dfrac{120\,\text{N} \cdot 0.025\,\text{s}}{0.17\,\text{kg}} = 8.0\,\text{m/s} + 17.65\,\text{m/s} = 25.65\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $26\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the velocity $8.0\,\text{m/s}$, the mass $0.17\,\text{kg}$ and the time $0.025\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** An impulse of $3.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$ increases the velocity of a light puck a lot, by about $18\,\text{m/s}$. The final velocity $26\,\text{m/s}$, about $92\,\text{km/h}$, fits a hard hit.

## fy4-9-L3-01
**Given:** combined mass of the child and the sled $m = 24\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 1.0\,\text{m/s}$; from the graph the pushing force as a function of time; friction is neglected
**Find:** the velocity of the sled $v$ after the push in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** When friction is neglected, the pushing force is the horizontal net force on the sled; the weight and the normal force of the snow cancel each other. By the impulse–momentum principle the impulse of the pushing force equals the change in momentum. The force changes, so the impulse is read from the area of the graph.
**Reading the graph:** The force rises from zero to $60\,\text{N}$ by $0.20\,\text{s}$, stays at $60\,\text{N}$ until $0.80\,\text{s}$ and falls to zero at $1.00\,\text{s}$. Under the graph is a trapezoid with height $60\,\text{N}$ and parallel sides $1.00\,\text{s}$ (base) and $0.60\,\text{s}$ (top). The area is the height times the average of the sides.
**Formula:** $I = F_\text{max} \cdot \dfrac{t_\text{base} + t_\text{top}}{2}$ and $I = mv - mv_0$, so $v = v_0 + \dfrac{I}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $I = 60\,\text{N} \cdot \dfrac{(1.00 + 0.60)\,\text{s}}{2} = 48\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$ and $v = 1.0\,\text{m/s} + \dfrac{48\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{24\,\text{kg}} = 1.0\,\text{m/s} + 2.0\,\text{m/s} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the mass $24\,\text{kg}$ and the velocity $1.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** If the force had been $60\,\text{N}$ for the whole second, the impulse would be $60\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$; because of the rising and falling edges it is slightly smaller, $48\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$. An increase in velocity of $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ from a push of one second is believable. In reality the friction of the snow lowers the final velocity slightly.

## fy4-9-L3-02
**Given:** mass of the passenger $m = 75\,\text{kg}$, stopping time $\Delta t = 0.12\,\text{s}$, average force of the belt at most $F = 10\,\text{kN}$, final velocity $v = 0$
**Find:** the greatest initial velocity $v_0$ from which the passenger can be stopped, in $\text{km/h}$
**Principle:** In the horizontal direction only the force of the belt acts on the passenger, so it is the net force. By the impulse–momentum principle the impulse of the force of the belt equals the change in the passenger's momentum. When the force and the time are at their greatest, the momentum to be stopped, and so the initial velocity, is also at its greatest.
**Formula:** $F\,\Delta t = mv_0 - 0$, so $v_0 = \dfrac{F\,\Delta t}{m}$
**Conversions:** $F = 10\,\text{kN} = 10\,000\,\text{N}$; finally the answer is converted to $\text{km/h}$ by multiplying by $3.6$, since $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
**Substitution:** $v_0 = \dfrac{10\,000\,\text{N} \cdot 0.12\,\text{s}}{75\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{1200\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}}{75\,\text{kg}} = 16\,\text{m/s} = 16 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} = 57.6\,\text{km/h}$
**Answer:** $58\,\text{km/h}$, two significant figures like the mass $75\,\text{kg}$ and the time $0.12\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The velocity corresponds to an ordinary speed on a country road or a main city street, and seat belt tests use velocities of the same order of magnitude. The force of the belt, $10\,\text{kN}$, is about $14$ times the weight of the passenger, $75\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 740\,\text{N}$, which shows why a higher collision velocity would be dangerous.

## fy4-9-L3-03
**Given:** mass of each ball $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, incoming velocity $8.0\,\text{m/s}$, outgoing velocity of the rubber ball $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ back, the clay ball stays in place, contact time $\Delta t = 0.020\,\text{s}$ for both
**Find:** the difference $\Delta F$ between the average forces of the rubber ball and the clay ball on the wall, in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The force of the wall is the horizontal net force on the ball, and by the impulse–momentum principle its impulse is the change in the ball's momentum. By Newton's third law the ball pushes the wall with a force equal in magnitude to the force of the wall on the ball. The positive direction is chosen towards the wall: $v_0 = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$, for the clay ball $v = 0$ and for the rubber ball $v = -6.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Formula:** The magnitude of the force is $F = \dfrac{m\,|v - v_0|}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta F = \dfrac{m(v_0 + v_\text{r})}{\Delta t} - \dfrac{mv_0}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{m v_\text{r}}{\Delta t}$, where $v_\text{r} = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$ is the outgoing speed of the rubber ball
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\Delta F = \dfrac{0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m/s}}{0.020\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{1.2\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{0.020\,\text{s}} = 60\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $60\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.20\,\text{kg}$ and the velocity $6.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Calculated separately, the force of the clay ball is $\tfrac{0.20 \cdot 8.0}{0.020}\,\text{N} = 80\,\text{N}$ and that of the rubber ball $\tfrac{0.20 \cdot 14}{0.020}\,\text{N} = 140\,\text{N}$, and the difference is $60\,\text{N}$. The rebounding ball pushes the wall harder, because in addition to stopping the ball the wall must accelerate it back.
