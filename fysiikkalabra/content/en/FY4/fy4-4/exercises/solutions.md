## fy4-4-L1-01
**Given:** $m = 2.5\,\text{kg}$, net force $\Sigma F = 6.0\,\text{N}$ horizontally
**Find:** the acceleration $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** By Newton's second law the net force determines the acceleration: $\Sigma F = ma$. The net force is given directly, so the forces do not need to be added.
**Formula:** $\Sigma F = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{\Sigma F}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, and $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} = \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{6.0\,\text{N}}{2.5\,\text{kg}} = 2.4\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $2.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the direction of the net force, two significant figures like the data $6.0\,\text{N}$ and $2.5\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The net force has a little more than twice as many newtons as the mass has kilograms, so the acceleration is a little over $2\,\text{m/s}^2$. The speed of the toy car increases by $2.4\,\text{m/s}$ every second, which fits a brisk push.

The net force is what is left when the resistive forces are subtracted from the force of the road on the tyre.

## fy4-4-L1-04
**Given:** $m = 12\,\text{kg}$, pulling force $F = 38\,\text{N}$ horizontally, friction force $F_\mu = 20\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the acceleration $a$ of the box in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** By Newton's second law the acceleration is determined by the net force, not by the pulling force alone. In the vertical direction the box does not accelerate, so the vertical forces cancel and the net force is horizontal.
**Free-body diagram:** The box as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of the pull. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the floor points up from the bottom surface, equally long. The pulling force $\overline{F}$ points forward and the friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the floor points backward from the back corner of the bottom surface, shorter than the pulling force.
```voimakuvio

G 117,72
N 117,72
F 38 oikea
F_mu 20 vasen
a oikea
```
**Formula:** In the horizontal direction $F - F_\mu = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{F - F_\mu}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{38\,\text{N} - 20\,\text{N}}{12\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{18\,\text{N}}{12\,\text{kg}} = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the direction of the pull, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** Without friction the acceleration would be $38\,\text{N} : 12\,\text{kg} \approx 3.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. Friction takes more than half of the pulling force, so the acceleration is less than half of that, as it should be.

## fy4-4-L2-01
**Given:** $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 0$, final velocity $v = 90\,\text{km/h}$, time $\Delta t = 10\,\text{s}$, uniform acceleration
**Find:** the net force $\Sigma F$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The velocity of the car increases steadily, so its acceleration is constant and is obtained from the change in velocity. By Newton's second law the net force is the mass times the acceleration.
**Formula:** $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$ and $\Sigma F = ma$, so $\Sigma F = \dfrac{m(v - v_0)}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $90\,\text{km/h} = 90 : 3.6\,\text{m/s} = 25\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $\Sigma F = \dfrac{1200\,\text{kg}\cdot(25\,\text{m/s} - 0)}{10\,\text{s}} = 1200\,\text{kg}\cdot 2.5\,\text{m/s}^2 = 3000\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $3000\,\text{N}$ or $3.0\,\text{kN}$ in the direction of motion, two significant figures like the time $10\,\text{s}$ and the velocity $90\,\text{km/h}$.
**Reasonableness:** An acceleration of $2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ is a brisk acceleration for an ordinary passenger car. The net force is about a quarter of the weight of the car, $1200\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 12\,\text{kN}$, just as the acceleration is about a quarter of the free-fall acceleration.

## fy4-4-L3-02

## fy4-4-L2-06

**Principle:** With a constant force $a = F \cdot \dfrac{1}{m}$, so the acceleration is proportional to the reciprocal of the mass $\dfrac{1}{m}$. With the reciprocal, the curve $(m, a)$ becomes a straight line, and the slope of the line is the force $F$. The line passes through the origin, because with a very large mass the acceleration would be almost zero. The condition for the slope and the straight line to make sense is that only the mass changes: the pulling force, the surface and the friction must stay the same.
**Reading the graph:** The reciprocals are $5.0$, $3.3$, $2.5$, $2.0$ and $1.7\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}}$. The points $(1/m, a)$ lie on a straight line, and from the line we read the points $(0, 0)$ and $(5.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}}, 6.0\,\text{m/s}^2)$.

**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, and $\text{kg}\cdot\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = \text{N}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $F = k = \dfrac{6.0\,\text{m/s}^2 - 0\,\text{m/s}^2}{5.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}} - 0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{kg}}} = 1.2\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** a) the slope is the pulling force $F$; b) the pulling force, the track and the friction were kept constant, and only the mass changed. The magnitude of the force is $1.2\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the readings of the accelerations.
**Reasonableness:** At every point $ma$ is close to the same value: $0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 6.1\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1.22\,\text{N}$ and $0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 2.4\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1.20\,\text{N}$.
