Experimental work and its assessment

Why does a good experiment plan already tell, before any measurement, how reliable the result will be?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • plan an experiment in which the variables are controlled and the result is calculated from a graph
  • identify sources of error and a systematic error from a graph
  • assess the shortcomings of an investigation report and write a justified conclusion

Prior knowledge

  • A measurement result is given as a mean and an error estimate, and errors are gross, random or systematicchapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement
  • A straight line is fitted to the measurement points, and its slope is read from two points of the linechapter Graphing and fitting a straight line
  • An investigation has a research question, a hypothesis, an independent, a dependent and controlled variables, and a reportchapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting

1Think about it

A matriculation exam question reads: Plan an experiment to determine the spring constant of a spring. You have a spring, a stand, five $50\,\text{g}$ masses and a metre rule. Aino writes a one-line answer: “I hang a mass on the spring, measure the extension and calculate the spring constant as the quotient of the force and the extension.” The physics is correct, but the answer gets only a fraction of the points.

What is missing from Aino's answer so that someone else could carry out the experiment from it and trust the result?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Repetition

A group measures the time a trolley takes to run down an inclined track five times and calculates the mean.What does the mean of five repetitions improve compared with a single measurement?

  • Only random errors are sometimes too large and sometimes too small and partly cancel each other. A systematic error, for example a wrongly measured track length, shifts every result in the same direction, and the mean does not remove it. Review the kinds of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • The spread of the individual results does not decrease with repetition: the fifth measurement is as imprecise as the first. Repetition reduces the effect of random error on the mean. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. Repetitions make the spread visible, and in the mean the effect of random errors is damped. A systematic error is the same in every result, so it is found by comparing with a known value or with another measuring method. This is covered in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • The mean also has an uncertainty, although it is smaller than that of a single result, and the mean does not remove a systematic error. Review the mean and the error estimate in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Resolution and uncertainty

A phone stopwatch shows the time to a hundredth of a second. A student starts and stops the watch by hand as a ball passes marks on the floor.What is the most sensible estimate of the uncertainty of the time?

  • Correct. The uncertainty of a hand-timed interval is set by the reaction time, which varies from one measurement to the next by tenths of a second. That is why the measured time must be long, so that the relative uncertainty stays small. This is covered in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • A digital display only removes the error of reading a scale. The timing error comes from when the button is pressed. Review the sources of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • $0.01\,\text{s}$ is the resolution of the watch. On its own it does not give the uncertainty, because the watch is started and stopped by a person whose reaction time varies much more. Review resolution and uncertainty in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Half a scale division is the reading uncertainty only when reading is the only source of error. Here the measuring method, timing by hand, brings a much larger uncertainty. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

A fair test

A student investigates how the period of a pendulum depends on the length of the string. Which set-up is a fair test?

  • An experiment must not assume what has not yet been shown. If the bob changes, any effect of the mass gets mixed up with the effect of the length. Review controlled variables in the chapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting.

  • The starting angle is another variable. At small angles it hardly affects the period, but at large angles it does, so it is kept the same and small. Review the pendulum model and its conditions in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

  • When two variables change at the same time, the cause of the change in the period cannot be inferred. In an experiment one variable is changed at a time. Review variables in the chapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting.

  • Correct. The independent variable is the length, the dependent variable is the period, and everything else is controlled. Then the change in the period is caused by the length. This is covered in the chapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting.

The line does not pass through the origin

A student measures the mass of water for different volumes. She pours water into a beaker and weighs the beaker with the water. The $(V, m)$ graph is a straight line that cuts the vertical axis at $85\,\text{g}$, although the mass at zero volume should be zero.What can be concluded from this?

  • The points lie on a straight line, so the mass increases steadily with the volume and the model fits. The shift affects all points equally, so the slope, that is, the density, is still usable. Review the meaning of the constant term in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Forcing the line through the origin does not remove the error but changes the slope wrongly. The line is fitted to the points, and the constant term is interpreted. Review fitting a straight line in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. The beaker adds the same mass to every weighing, so the line has shifted but its slope has not changed. The density can therefore be obtained reliably from the slope. This is covered in the chapters Graphing and fitting a straight line and Density: $m = \rho V$.

  • A random error would scatter the points on both sides of the line. Here all the points have shifted in the same direction, which is the hallmark of a systematic error, and repetition does not remove it. Review the kinds of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Reading the slope

A straight line has been fitted to the measurement points, and the quantity sought is its slope. How is the slope determined most reliably?

  • Correct. The fitted line runs through the middle of the set of points, and a slope read from points of the line far apart is precise, because the changes $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are large. This is covered in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Then the result depends on only two measurements and their errors, and the fitted line is not used at all. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The quotient $y : x$ is the slope only if the line passes through the origin. If the line has a constant term, the result is wrong. Review the equation of a line $y = kx + b$ in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Between adjacent points the change is small, so the random error of either point changes the result a lot. The line uses all the points. Review reading the slope in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

Conclusion

The group's hypothesis was that the free-fall acceleration is $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The measurement gave the result $g = (9.7 \pm 0.3)\,\text{m/s}^2$. Which conclusion is correct?

  • Correct. The difference $0.1\,\text{m/s}^2$ is smaller than the uncertainty $0.3\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the measurement is consistent with the hypothesis. This is covered in the chapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting.

  • Uncertainty belongs to every measurement, and it does not prevent a conclusion. It tells how strong the conclusion is. Review this in the chapters Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting and Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Every measurement result has an uncertainty, so exactly the same value cannot be expected. The result is compared with the hypothesis taking the uncertainty into account. Review this in the chapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting.

  • A measurement cannot prove a hypothesis: other values also fit within the uncertainty interval, and the interval is only an estimate. The result supports the hypothesis. Review how to word a conclusion in the chapter Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting.

3Investigate

The group's spring measurement

Made-up example data

A group checked whether the spring constant $25\,\text{N/m}$ stated by the spring's manufacturer is correct. The spring was hung from a stand, and a vertical ruler was fixed next to it. With no masses, the lower end of the spring was at the $1.0\,\text{cm}$ mark of the ruler. The group hung masses on the spring, read the position of the lower end from the ruler and recorded the reading as the extension $x$. From each row they calculated the spring constant $k = F : x$.

$m\ (\text{g})$ $F = mg\ (\text{N})$ $x\ (\text{cm})$ $k = F : x\ (\text{N/m})$
$50$ $0.491$ $2.99$ $16.4$
$100$ $0.981$ $4.90$ $20.0$
$150$ $1.472$ $6.90$ $21.3$
$200$ $1.962$ $6.85$ $28.6$
$250$ $2.453$ $10.79$ $22.7$

The group's conclusion: “The mean of the five results is $21.8\,\text{N/m}$. The manufacturer's $25\,\text{N/m}$ is therefore wrong.”

Assess the group's conclusion. Find two different errors in the data, say how each one is recognised, and suggest how the spring constant should be determined.

Model answer

Gross error. On the $200\,\text{g}$ row the reading $6.85\,\text{cm}$ is smaller than on the $150\,\text{g}$ row, although a heavier mass stretches the spring more. The result $28.6\,\text{N/m}$ clearly differs from the others. The reading was probably $8.85\,\text{cm}$, which would fit between the rows. The reading is checked and measured again; it is not deleted merely because it deviates, and the reason is recorded.

Systematic error. The extension is the difference between the reading and the initial reading, but the group did not subtract the initial reading $1.0\,\text{cm}$. Every extension is therefore $1.0\,\text{cm}$ too large and every $k$ too small. The error can be recognised from the way $k$ grows with the mass ($16.4 \to 22.7\,\text{N/m}$): the same $1.0\,\text{cm}$ is a larger part of the reading when the extension is small.

Conclusion. The mean $21.8\,\text{N/m}$ contains both errors, so the manufacturer's value cannot be rejected on its basis. The spring constant is better determined by drawing an $(F, x)$ graph: the slope of the line is $\tfrac{1}{k}$, and the error in the initial reading shifts the line but does not change the slope. From these measurements the slope gives $k \approx 25\,\text{N/m}$ when the faulty row is left out with a stated reason. In addition, each reading is measured three times, so that a deviation stands out at once.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A plan answers six questions

    In the matriculation exam, a “plan an experiment” task is marked on whether someone else could carry out the experiment from your answer. A good plan says what is changed, what is measured and with which instrument, what is kept constant, how many measurements and repetitions are made, how the result is calculated from a graph and which sources of error are the largest and how they are reduced. In an experiment one variable is changed at a time, because otherwise the cause of a change in the result cannot be inferred.

    Error, uncertainty and resolution

    The measurement error is the unknown difference between the result and the true value. The measurement uncertainty is an estimate of how large the error can be. The resolution is the smallest step the instrument shows, and on its own it does not give the uncertainty: because of the reaction time, the uncertainty of a hand-timed interval is of the order of tenths of a second, even though the watch shows hundredths.

    A random error shows as the spread of repeated results, and in the mean its effect is damped. A systematic error shifts all results in the same direction, and repetition does not remove it. It is revealed by comparing with a known value or from a graph: the line does not pass through the origin, although according to the model it should.

    Processing a measurement result

    The best estimate of repeated measurements is the mean, and in this course the estimate of the spread is half the range:

    $$\begin{aligned} \bar{x} &= \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} \\ \Delta x &= \frac{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}}{2} \end{aligned}$$

    where $n$ is the number of measurements, $x_\text{max}$ the largest and $x_\text{min}$ the smallest result. The result is given in the form $\bar{x} \pm \Delta x$, and the error estimate is rounded to one significant figure (two if the first digit is 1). The relative uncertainty

    $$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{\bar{x}}$$

    tells whether the uncertainty is small compared with the size of the result. The measured range is chosen so large that $\varepsilon$ stays small: the time is measured, for example, from ten swings.

    The result from the slope of a graph

    When according to the model the quantities depend linearly on each other, a straight line is fitted to the measurement points

    $$y = kx + b$$

    where $k = \tfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ is the slope, read from two points of the line far apart, and $b$ is the point where the line cuts the vertical axis. The model holds only in the measured range, and the line is extended outside it only with good reason. When the quantity sought comes from the slope, all the points affect the result and the random error of a single point less so.

    If according to the model the line should pass through the origin but $b$ clearly differs from zero, the measurement probably has a systematic error that is the same at every point.     Common mistakes

    • The plan says “measure and calculate”, but it does not say what is kept constant, what is measured with and how the result is obtained from a graph.
    • Two variables change at the same time, so the cause of the change in the result cannot be inferred.
    • The resolution of the instrument is given as the uncertainty, although the measuring method, such as the reaction time, brings a larger uncertainty.
    • Repetition is claimed to remove all errors. It only reduces the effect of random error on the mean.
    • The slope is calculated from two adjacent measurement points and not from the fitted line.
    • A deviating result is quietly removed. It is checked, and leaving it out is justified and recorded.
    • In the conclusion the hypothesis is “proved”. A measurement can only support a hypothesis within its uncertainty.
    • “Human error” is written as a source of error without saying what the error is, in which direction it takes the result and how it is reduced.

    5Examples

    Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

    Choosing a measuring instrument

    The diameter of a copper wire is about $0.5\,\text{mm}$. With which measuring method is the relative uncertainty of the diameter smallest?

    • The reading uncertainty is about $0.5\,\text{mm}$, as large as the whole diameter: the relative uncertainty is about $100\,\%$. The scale division of the instrument must be much smaller than the quantity measured.

    • The uncertainty is at least of the order of the resolution, about $0.05\,\text{mm}$, or $10\,\%$ of the diameter. A single measurement also does not reveal whether the wire is equally thick everywhere.

    • Correct. The reading uncertainty is about $0.01\,\text{mm}$, or about $2\,\%$ of the diameter, and five measuring points show whether the thickness varies. An instrument is chosen so that its resolution and uncertainty are small compared with the quantity measured.

    • This is a good method if no more precise instrument is available: $20$ diameters make about $10\,\text{mm}$, and the reading uncertainty $0.5\,\text{mm}$ is about $5\,\%$ of that. A micrometer screw gauge still reaches a clearly smaller relative uncertainty.

    Find the flaw in the plan

    A student plans an experiment to investigate how the bounce height of a rubber ball depends on the drop height. Which item of the plan is inadequate?

    • Correct, this is the flaw. The bounce height varies from one drop to the next, and the spread shows only through repetition. The mean of three or more repetitions damps the random error, and the spread gives an error estimate.

    • This item is fine: five evenly spaced values over a wide range are enough to show whether the dependence is linear.

    • This item is fine: the ball, the surface and the temperature affect the bounce, so they are controlled variables.

    • This item is fine: the video freezes the highest point of the ball, which is hard to see by eye. The camera must be at the height of the ball so that the reading is not distorted.

    Which graph?

    Different masses $m$ are hung on a spring, and the period $T$ of each oscillation is measured. The period of a mass on a spring is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$.From which graph is the spring constant $k$ obtained using the slope of a straight line?

    • $T$ is proportional to the square root of the mass, so the $(m, T)$ points lie on a curve and not on a straight line. The formula becomes the equation of a line when it is squared. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

    • Correct. Squaring gives $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\, m$, which is a straight line when $T^2$ is on the vertical axis and $m$ on the horizontal axis. From the slope $a$ one gets $k = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{a}$. Review the period in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

    • Swapping the axes does not straighten the graph: $m$ is proportional to the square of the period, so the points lie on a parabola. Moreover, the factor $2\pi k$ does not follow from the formula. Review this in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

    • The square root goes in the wrong direction: $\sqrt{T}$ is proportional to the fourth root of the mass. A straight line is obtained when the period is squared. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

    Zero error

    In ice water a thermometer shows $1.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ instead of $0.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the error is the same over the whole measuring range. The thermometer is used to read the temperature of water before and after heating, and the specific heat capacity is calculated from $c = \tfrac{Q}{m\,\Delta T}$.How does the error of the thermometer affect the result $c$?

    • A reading that is too high does not make the warming smaller: the initial and final readings shift by the same amount, and the difference stays the same.

    • Correct. A zero error shifts both readings by the same amount, so the temperature change $\Delta T$ is correct. The error needs to be corrected only if the temperature itself is needed, for example an absolute temperature.

    • A systematic error of known size can be corrected by subtracting it from the readings, and in a difference it cancels by itself.

    • Both readings are $1.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ too high, so their difference $\Delta T$ is correct. The formula needs only the change in temperature.

    Repeated timing

    A student measures the time a trolley takes on an inclined track five times: $5.62\,\text{s}$, $5.81\,\text{s}$, $5.55\,\text{s}$, $5.74\,\text{s}$ and $5.68\,\text{s}$. Calculate the relative uncertainty of the time when the error estimate is half the range. Give the answer as a percentage.

    %
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    First calculate the mean time.

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The error estimate is $\Delta t = \tfrac{t_\text{max} - t_\text{min}}{2}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    $\varepsilon = \tfrac{\Delta t}{\bar{t}} \cdot 100\,\%$, where $\Delta t = 0.13\,\text{s}$.

    Show the solution

    Given

    five run times $5.62\,\text{s}$, $5.81\,\text{s}$, $5.55\,\text{s}$, $5.74\,\text{s}$ and $5.68\,\text{s}$

    Find

    the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the time as a percentage

    Principle

    The best estimate of repeated results is the mean, and in this course the spread is estimated as half the range. The relative uncertainty tells how large the error estimate is compared with the result.

    Formula

    $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{t_1 + \dots + t_5}{5}$
    $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{t_\text{max} - t_\text{min}}{2}$
    $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\bar{t}} \cdot 100\,\%$

    Conversions

    None needed, all times are in seconds.

    Substitution

    the times add up to $28.40\,\text{s}$, so

    $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{28.40\,\text{s}}{5}$
    $= 5.68\,\text{s}$
    $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{5.81\,\text{s} - 5.55\,\text{s}}{2}$
    $= 0.13\,\text{s}$
    $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.13\,\text{s}}{5.68\,\text{s}} \cdot 100\,\%$
    $= 2.29\,\%$

    Answer

    $\varepsilon \approx 2.3\,\%$

    Two significant figures as in the error estimate $0.13\,\text{s}$. The result is $t = (5.68 \pm 0.13)\,\text{s}$.

    Reasonableness

    The times vary by about a tenth of a second on both sides of the mean, which fits timing by hand. An error of a tenth in a time of about six seconds is a few per cent.

    Spring constant from a graph

    The straight line fitted to an $(m, T^2)$ graph drawn from the oscillation of a mass on a spring passes through the points $(0.10\,\text{kg}$; $0.20\,\text{s}^2)$ and $(0.50\,\text{kg}$; $0.84\,\text{s}^2)$. Determine the spring constant. Give the answer in $\text{N/m}$.

    N/m
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What does the slope of the line mean when the formula for the period is written in the form $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\, m$?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    First calculate the slope $a = \tfrac{\Delta (T^2)}{\Delta m}$ from two points of the line.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    $a = 1.6\,\text{s}^2/\text{kg}$, so $k = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{a}$.

    Show the solution

    Given

    the points $(0.10\,\text{kg}$; $0.20\,\text{s}^2)$ and $(0.50\,\text{kg}$; $0.84\,\text{s}^2)$ of the fitted line

    Find

    the spring constant $k$ in $\text{N/m}$

    Principle

    The period of a mass on a spring is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$. Squaring the equation gives $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\, m$: the $(m, T^2)$ graph is a straight line whose slope is $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$. The slope is read from two points of the line far apart, so all measurement points affect the result. The line does not pass exactly through the origin, because the spring's own mass also oscillates, but that does not change the slope.

    Formula

    $a = \dfrac{\Delta (T^2)}{\Delta m}$ and $a = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$, from which $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{a}$

    Conversions

    None needed: the mass is in kilograms and $T^2$ in $\text{s}^2$, so $k$ comes out in $\text{kg/s}^2 = \text{N/m}$.

    Substitution

    $a$$= \dfrac{0.84\,\text{s}^2 - 0.20\,\text{s}^2}{0.50\,\text{kg} - 0.10\,\text{kg}}$
    $= \dfrac{0.64\,\text{s}^2}{0.40\,\text{kg}}$
    $= 1.6\,\text{s}^2/\text{kg}$
    $k$$= \dfrac{4\pi^2}{1.6\,\text{s}^2/\text{kg}}$
    $= 24.67\,\text{N/m}$

    Answer

    $k \approx 25\,\text{N/m}$

    Two significant figures, because the differences $0.64\,\text{s}^2$ and $0.40\,\text{kg}$ are known to two figures.

    Reasonableness

    Units:

    $\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2/\text{kg}}$$= \text{kg/s}^2$
    $= \text{N/m}$

    Check: with a $0.50\,\text{kg}$ mass

    $T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{0.50\,\text{kg} : 24.7\,\text{N/m}}$
    $\approx 0.89\,\text{s}$

    $T^2 \approx 0.80\,\text{s}^2$, which is close to the value on the line; the small difference is the effect of the spring's own mass.

    The zero of pressure

    The pressure of the air in a closed glass bottle was measured in a water bath at different temperatures. The points $(20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $108.7\,\text{kPa})$ and $(80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $130.8\,\text{kPa})$ were read from the line fitted to the data. Use the line to determine the temperature at which the pressure would be zero. Give the answer in degrees Celsius.

    °C
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Write the equation of the line in the form $p = p_0 + k\theta$ and calculate the slope $k$ from two points of the line.

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Solve for the constant term $p_0$ from either point: $p_0 = p - k\theta$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    The pressure is zero when $p_0 + k\theta_0 = 0$, that is, $\theta_0 = -\tfrac{p_0}{k}$.

    Show the solution

    Given

    the points $(20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $108.7\,\text{kPa})$ and $(80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $130.8\,\text{kPa})$ of the line; the volume of the bottle does not change

    Find

    the temperature $\theta_0$ at which the pressure would be zero, in degrees Celsius

    Principle

    In a rigid closed container, the pressure of a dilute gas is a linear function of temperature over the measuring range, $p = p_0 + k\theta$. The line is extended, or extrapolated, outside the measuring range to zero pressure. The extension is long, so the result is only an estimate: a real gas would condense before reaching zero pressure.

    Formula

    $k$$= \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\theta_2 - \theta_1}$

    $p_0 = p_1 - k\theta_1$ and $p_0 + k\theta_0 = 0$, from which $\theta_0 = -\dfrac{p_0}{k}$

    Conversions

    None needed: a temperature change is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, and the temperature asked for is in degrees Celsius.

    Substitution

    $k$$= \dfrac{130.8\,\text{kPa} - 108.7\,\text{kPa}}{80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
    $= \dfrac{22.1\,\text{kPa}}{60.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
    $= 0.3683\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}$
    $p_0$$= 108.7\,\text{kPa} - 0.3683\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C} \cdot 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
    $= 101.33\,\text{kPa}$
    $\theta_0$$= -\dfrac{101.33\,\text{kPa}}{0.3683\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}}$
    $= -275.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

    Answer

    $\theta_0 \approx -275\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

    Three significant figures, because the pressure difference $22.1\,\text{kPa}$ is known to three figures; because of the long extrapolation the uncertainty is still several degrees.

    Reasonableness

    The result is close to absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Check:

    $101.33\,\text{kPa} + 0.3683\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C} \cdot (-275.1\,{}^\circ\text{C})$
    $\approx 0\,\text{kPa}$

    A long enough measurement

    A student measures her walking speed in the school corridor. She walks at about $1.4\,\text{m/s}$, and the uncertainty of a hand-timed interval is $0.2\,\text{s}$. The relative uncertainty of the time must be at most $2\,\%$.

    How long must the measured distance be at least? Give the answer in metres.

    m
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The uncertainty of the timing stays the same whether you time a short or a long interval. How long must the time be so that $0.2\,\text{s}$ is at most $2\,\%$ of it?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    $t_\text{min} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}}$, where $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 0.02$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    The distance comes from the model of uniform motion $s = vt$.

    Show the solution

    Given

    speed $v = 1.4\,\text{m/s}$, uncertainty of the time $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$, largest allowed relative uncertainty $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 2\,\%$

    Find

    the shortest measured distance $s_\text{min}$ in metres

    Principle

    The uncertainty of timing by hand comes from the reaction time, and it is almost the same in a short and a long measurement. The relative uncertainty $\tfrac{\Delta t}{t}$ therefore decreases as the measured time increases. The walker moves at an almost constant speed, so the model of uniform motion $s = vt$ gives the distance from the time.

    Formula

    $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{t}$
    $\le \varepsilon_\text{max}$

    so

    $t_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}}$
    $s_\text{min}$$= v\,t_\text{min}$
    $= \dfrac{v\,\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}}$

    Conversions

    $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 2\,\% = 0.02$; the other quantities are in base units.

    Substitution

    Model answer

    a)

    Find

    an experiment plan for comparing the grip of two shoes: the quantities measured and the instruments, the measuring method, the things kept constant and the number of measurements

    Principle

    Grip means static friction, which keeps the shoe from starting to slide. The largest value of static friction is $\mu_0 N$, so it depends both on the surfaces and on how hard the shoe presses on the floor. The shoes have different weights, so the coefficient of static friction $\mu_0$, which describes only the surfaces, is suitable for the comparison. In a fair test only the shoe changes.

    Model answer

    Measured quantity. I compare the coefficients of static friction of the shoes against the floor board. The coefficient does not depend on the weight of the shoe, so shoes of different weights can be compared.

    Measurement (tilting method). I place the shoe on the board and put the same mass inside it, for example $1\,\text{kg}$, so that the sole presses against the board as in a game. I raise one end of the board slowly and steadily until the shoe starts to slide, and read the angle $\alpha_0$ of the board with the inclinometer at exactly that moment. If the angle becomes so large that the measurement fails, I use the pulling method: the board horizontal, the shoe is pulled horizontally with the spring balance, and I read the largest reading just before it starts to move. In that case the mass of the shoe and the mass inside it is weighed on the kitchen scale.

    Kept constant: the same board and the same spot on it, a clean and dry surface (I wipe off dust before every measurement), the same mass inside the shoe, the same position of the shoe and equally slow tilting or pulling. The room temperature and humidity stay the same when the measurements are made on the same day.

    Repetitions. I measure each shoe five times in turn, so that any change in the surface affects both equally. I record every reading in a table.

    b)

    Find

    how the coefficient of static friction is calculated from the measurements and how the difference between the shoes is shown

    Principle

    At the limiting angle, static friction is at its largest and equal to the component of the weight along the slope: $\mu_0\, mg\cos\alpha_0 = mg\sin\alpha_0$. In the pulling method on a horizontal board $F = \mu_0 mg$. The variation of the repetitions tells how large a difference can arise from chance alone.

    Model answer

    In the tilting method the mass cancels, and each measurement gives $\mu_0 = \tan\alpha_0$. In the pulling method $\mu_0 = \tfrac{F}{mg}$, where $F$ is the largest reading of the spring balance and $m$ the mass of the shoe and the mass inside it. For each shoe I calculate the mean of the five results and the error estimate as half the range, for example $\mu_0 = 0.62 \pm 0.03$.

    The difference is probably real if the intervals $\bar{\mu}_0 \pm \Delta\mu_0$ of the shoes do not overlap. If the intervals overlap, the difference is not larger than the variation of the measurements and cannot be shown with these measurements. That still does not prove that the shoes grip equally well.

    c)

    Find

    two significant sources of error and ways to reduce their effect

    Principle

    A source of error is best named so that it shows what happens and in which direction the result changes. The effect of a random error is reduced by repetitions, a systematic error by changing the measuring method.

    Model answer

    Tilting too fast or a jerky pull. If the board is raised quickly, the angle has time to grow before I read it, and the result is too large. A jerk in the pulling method raises the reading of the spring balance for a moment. Remedy: I tilt or pull slowly and steadily and repeat the measurement; the measurement can also be filmed with a phone, so that the moment of the reading can be seen from the video.

    Changes in the surface. Dust, sand or moisture on the board or the sole changes the friction, and wear of the sole during the measurements can change it gradually. Remedy: I wipe the board and the soles before every measurement and measure the shoes in turn, so that a change affects both in the same way.

    The zero of the spring balance is also checked before the measurement, with the balance in its measuring position.

    • Only the pulling force is chosen as the measure of grip, although the shoes have different weights. A heavier shoe needs a larger force even if its sole does not grip better.
    • The plan does not say what is kept constant, or the shoes are measured on different boards or on different days.
    • Each shoe is measured once, so the variation cannot be seen and the difference cannot be shown.

    Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

    Model answer

    a)

    Find

    an experiment plan for determining the specific heat capacity of rapeseed oil: the quantities measured, the measuring method and the things kept constant

    Principle

    The resistor converts electrical energy into internal energy at the power $P$, and this energy is transferred as heat to the oil, so in the time $t$ the energy $Pt$ is transferred to the oil when the losses are small. The heat $Q = cm\,\Delta T$ holds when the oil does not change phase. When the mass of the oil, the power and the change in temperature as a function of time are measured, $c$ can be solved.

    Model answer

    Measurements. I weigh the empty vacuum flask and then the flask with the oil, so that the mass of the oil is obtained from the difference, for example $m = 200.0\,\text{g}$. I record the power of the resistor, $50\,\text{W}$. I place the resistor and the thermometer probe in the oil and close the lid. I record the initial temperature, switch on the resistor and start the watch at the same moment. I read the temperature every $30\,\text{s}$ and stir all the time, so that the temperature is the same everywhere. I record the times and temperatures in a table.

    Kept constant: the same flask and lid, the same way of stirring, an initial temperature close to room temperature and a temperature rise of at most about $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so that the losses stay small. The resistor is completely inside the liquid.

    Comparison. I make the same measurement with the same mass of water. Then the teacher's claim can be tested directly, and the known specific heat capacity of water shows how much the container and the losses affect the result.

    b)

    Find

    how the specific heat capacity is obtained from the $(t, T)$ graph

    Principle

    When the losses are small, $P\,\Delta t = cm\,\Delta T$, so the temperature rises steadily and the $(t, T)$ graph is a straight line whose slope is $k = \tfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta t} = \tfrac{P}{cm}$.

    Model answer

    I draw the $(t, T)$ graph: time on the horizontal axis and temperature on the vertical axis. At the beginning the points lie on a line, and I fit a straight line to them. I read the slope from two points of the line far apart. Then $c = \tfrac{P}{mk}$. For example, a slope of $0.125\,\text{K/s}$ would give $c = \tfrac{50\,\text{W}}{0.200\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.125\,\text{K/s}} = 2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$.

    I use the initial part of the graph, because when the oil becomes warmer than its surroundings, more and more heat is transferred to the surroundings and the graph curves and becomes less steep. Using the slope is better than a single initial and final reading, because all the points affect the result.

    c)

    Given

    power $P = 50\,\text{W}$, mass $m = 200\,\text{g}$, temperature change $\Delta T = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 20\,\text{K}$, specific heat capacity $c = 2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$; no heat is lost

    Find

    the heating time $t$ in $\text{s}$

    Principle

    When no heat is lost, the energy $Pt$ given by the resistor goes entirely into heating the oil. The oil does not boil or change phase, so the heat is $Q = cm\,\Delta T$.

    Formula

    $Pt = cm\,\Delta T$, from which $t = \dfrac{cm\,\Delta T}{P}$

    Conversions

    $m$$= 200\,\text{g}$
    $= 200 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
    $= 0.200\,\text{kg}$
    $c$$= 2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$
    $= 2.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$
    $= 2000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$

    A temperature change is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins.

    Substitution

    $t$$= \dfrac{2000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \cdot 0.200\,\text{kg} \cdot 20\,\text{K}}{50\,\text{W}}$
    $= \dfrac{8000\,\text{J}}{50\,\text{J/s}}$
    $= 160\,\text{s}$

    Answer

    $t \approx 160\,\text{s}$

    Or about $2.7\,\text{min}$. Two significant figures as in the specific heat capacity $2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and the power $50\,\text{W}$.

    Reasonableness

    d)

    Find

    whether the specific heat capacity comes out too large or too small because of heat losses, and how the effect is reduced

    Principle

    The calculation assumes that all the energy $P\,\Delta t$ heats the oil. The losses are a systematic error, which always takes the result in the same direction.

    Model answer

    Part of the energy from the resistor heats the vacuum flask and the surrounding air, so the temperature of the oil rises more slowly than without losses. The slope $k$ is therefore too small, and $c = \tfrac{P}{mk}$ comes out too large. The error is systematic, so repeating the measurement does not remove it.

    The effect is reduced by keeping the lid closed and the container insulated, by measuring over a short temperature range and by using only the initial part of the graph, as in part b. An alternative is to start a few degrees below room temperature and fit the line to the whole measurement: then the oil gains roughly as much energy from the surroundings at the beginning as it loses at the end. In addition, the same measurement can be made with water: since the specific heat capacity of water is known, one sees how much the container and the losses change the result, and the result for the oil can be corrected.

    Common mistakes

    • The temperature is measured only at the beginning and at the end, so no graph can be drawn and it cannot be seen where the losses start to grow.

    • In part c the mass is left in grams or the kilojoules are not converted to joules, so the time comes out a thousand times too large or too small.
    • In part d it is stated that the losses “distort the result” without giving the direction. The losses reduce the slope, so $c$ comes out too large.

    Also accepted

    • In part b, $c$ can also be calculated from two points of the line on the graph directly with the formula $c = \tfrac{P\,\Delta t}{m\,\Delta T}$, which is the same thing as $c = \tfrac{P}{mk}$.
    • In part a, instead of the measurement with water, the heat capacity of the container can be measured separately, or it can be argued that the effect of the vacuum flask is small.

    Made-up example data · The report and its measurement results are an invented example.

    From a student pair's report:

    Research question: How large is the free-fall acceleration?

    Hypothesis: The free-fall acceleration is $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

    Method: We dropped a tennis ball from the school balcony. The height was measured with a tape measure, and it was $3.0\,\text{m}$. One of us dropped the ball, and the other timed it with a phone stopwatch. We repeated this three times.

    Results: $t_1 = 0.82\,\text{s}$, $t_2 = 0.75\,\text{s}$ and $t_3 = 0.79\,\text{s}$. The mean is $\bar{t} = 0.7867\,\text{s}$. With the formula $g = \tfrac{2h}{t^2}$ we got $g = 9.6955\,\text{m/s}^2$.

    Model answer

    a)

    Find

    the shortcomings of the report and why each weakens the result or the conclusion

    Principle

    A report is good if someone else can repeat the experiment and assess how reliable the result is. A result includes its uncertainty, and a conclusion is only as strong as the data.

    Model answer

    No uncertainty and too many digits. The result $9.6955\,\text{m/s}^2$ claims a precision of five digits, although the times vary by $0.07\,\text{s}$ and the result is uncertain already in the first decimal place. Without an uncertainty the result cannot be compared with the hypothesis.

    Timing by hand. The reaction time, about $0.1\,\text{s}$, is about $13\,\%$ of the fall time $0.8\,\text{s}$, and in the formula for $g$ the time is squared. The person dropping and the person timing are different people, so the watch does not start exactly at the moment of release. Three repetitions from one height are not enough to show the spread and do not reveal a systematic error.

    Air resistance. The explanation “the difference is due to air resistance” is unjustified. The difference from the table value is about $0.1\,\text{m/s}^2$, which is much smaller than the timing uncertainty (part b). The measurement therefore cannot show the effect of air resistance, and the timing error could just as well change the result in either direction.

    Proving. A measurement cannot prove a hypothesis. It can only support it: the table value is within the uncertainty interval.

    Description of the method. The report does not say from where to where the height was measured (from the bottom of the ball to the floor) or to what precision, so the experiment cannot be repeated exactly. The research question is also loose: it does not state the method or what the experiment is meant to test.

    b)

    Given

    drop height $h = 3.0\,\text{m}$, fall times $0.82\,\text{s}$, $0.75\,\text{s}$ and $0.79\,\text{s}$; the ball starts from rest

    Find

    the free-fall acceleration $g$ and its uncertainty $\Delta g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

    Principle

    The ball starts from rest, and over a short drop the air resistance is small, so the motion is uniformly accelerated: $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$. The best estimate is obtained from the mean of the times. In the extreme-value method $g$ is calculated with the shortest and the longest time; the shortest time gives the largest $g$.

    Formula

    $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, from which $g = \dfrac{2h}{t^2}$, and

    $\Delta g$$= \dfrac{g_\text{max} - g_\text{min}}{2}$

    where

    $g_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{2h}{t_\text{min}^2}$
    $g_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{2h}{t_\text{max}^2}$

    Conversions

    None needed, the height is in metres and the times in seconds.

    Substitution

    $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{0.82\,\text{s} + 0.75\,\text{s} + 0.79\,\text{s}}{3}$
    $= 0.7867\,\text{s}$
    $g$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{(0.7867\,\text{s})^2}$
    $= 9.695\,\text{m/s}^2$
    $g_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{(0.75\,\text{s})^2}$
    $= 10.67\,\text{m/s}^2$
    $g_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{(0.82\,\text{s})^2}$
    $= 8.923\,\text{m/s}^2$
    $\Delta g$$= \dfrac{10.67\,\text{m/s}^2 - 8.923\,\text{m/s}^2}{2}$
    $= 0.872\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Answer

    $g = (9.7 \pm 0.9)\,\text{m/s}^2$

    The error estimate is rounded to one significant figure and the result to the same decimal place.

    Reasonableness

    The table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is within the interval $8.8\text{–}10.6\,\text{m/s}^2$. The relative uncertainty is about $9\,\%$, which fits the fact that the variation of the time, $\pm 0.035\,\text{s}$, is about $4.5\,\%$ of the time and squaring doubles it.

    Find

    an improved method and how the result is calculated

    Principle

    The largest source of error is timing by hand. It disappears when the time is measured by a device. Several heights and a graph make the result independent of any single measurement.

    Model answer

    I film the drop with a phone in front of a vertical tape measure, with the camera at the height of the middle of the fall, so that perspective does not distort the readings. From the video I read the position of the ball frame by frame; at a frame rate of $60\,\text{frames/s}$ the interval between frames is about $0.017\,\text{s}$, which is much less than the reaction time. Alternatively I use two light gates.

    I drop the ball from, for example, five heights $0.5\text{–}3.0\,\text{m}$, three times from each. I draw the $(t^2, h)$ graph: according to the model $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ it is a straight line through the origin whose slope is $\tfrac{g}{2}$, so $g$ is twice the slope. From the video one can also calculate the velocity and draw a $(t, v)$ graph, whose slope is $g$.     d)

    Principle

    A constant error that is the same at every point shifts the line but does not change its slope. In a row-by-row calculation the same error goes straight into every result.

    Slope. Since every length lacks the same $2.5\,\text{cm}$, the whole line shifts, but its steepness does not change. The $g$ of part b is therefore not distorted, and the error shows as the constant term.

    Row-by-row calculation. If $g = \tfrac{4\pi^2 l}{T^2}$ were calculated from each row with the measured length, every result would be too small, because $l$ is too small.     Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    mass of the stone $m = 187.4\,\text{g}$, water level readings $V_1 = 120\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ and $V_2 = 190\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$

    Find

    the density $\rho$ of the stone in $\text{kg/m}^3$

    Principle

    A submerged stone displaces its own volume of water, so the volume of the stone is the rise of the water level. Density is mass divided by volume.

    Formula

    $V$$= V_2 - V_1$
    $\rho$$= \dfrac{m}{V}$
    $= \dfrac{m}{V_2 - V_1}$

    Conversions

    $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l} = 1\,\text{cm}^3$ and $1\,\text{g/cm}^3 = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

    Substitution

    $V$$= 190\,\text{cm}^3 - 120\,\text{cm}^3$
    $= 70\,\text{cm}^3$
    $\rho$$= \dfrac{187.4\,\text{g}}{70\,\text{cm}^3}$
    $= 2.677\,\text{g/cm}^3$
    $= 2.677 \cdot 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$
    $= 2677\,\text{kg/m}^3$

    Answer

    $\rho \approx 2680\,\text{kg/m}^3$

    Three significant figures, because the volume is the difference of two three-digit readings; part b shows that even the third digit is uncertain.

    Reasonableness

    The density is about $2.7$ times that of water, which is typical of ordinary rock types: the stone sinks, and a palm-sized stone weighs hundreds of grams.

    b)

    Given

    $m = (187.4 \pm 0.1)\,\text{g}$; readings $120\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ and $190\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$, each with an uncertainty of half the scale division $2\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$, that is, $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$

    Find

    the uncertainty $\Delta\rho$ of the density in $\text{kg/m}^3$

    Principle

    The volume is the difference of two readings, and each reading can be wrong by at most the estimated uncertainty, so the uncertainty of the difference is the sum of the uncertainties. In the extreme-value method the largest density is obtained with the largest mass and the smallest volume, the smallest the other way round.

    Formula

    $\Delta V$$= \Delta V_1 + \Delta V_2$
    $\rho_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{m + \Delta m}{V - \Delta V}$
    $\rho_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{m - \Delta m}{V + \Delta V}$
    $\Delta\rho$$= \dfrac{\rho_\text{max} - \rho_\text{min}}{2}$

    Conversions

    $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l} = 1\,\text{cm}^3$ and $1\,\text{g/cm}^3 = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

    Substitution

    $\Delta V$$= 1\,\text{cm}^3 + 1\,\text{cm}^3$
    $= 2\,\text{cm}^3$
    $\rho_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{187.5\,\text{g}}{68\,\text{cm}^3}$
    $= 2.757\,\text{g/cm}^3$
    $\rho_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{187.3\,\text{g}}{72\,\text{cm}^3}$
    $= 2.601\,\text{g/cm}^3$
    $\Delta\rho$$= \dfrac{2.757\,\text{g/cm}^3 - 2.601\,\text{g/cm}^3}{2}$
    $= 0.078\,\text{g/cm}^3$
    $= 78\,\text{kg/m}^3$

    Answer

    $\Delta\rho \approx 80\,\text{kg/m}^3$

    One significant figure, so $\rho = (2680 \pm 80)\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

    Reasonableness

    The relative uncertainty $80 : 2680 \approx 3\,\%$ is almost the same as the relative uncertainty of the volume, $2 : 70 \approx 2.9\,\%$, because the uncertainty of the mass is very small.

    c)

    Find

    whether the stone can be identified as granite or basalt by its density

    Principle

    The result is compared with the ranges in the table, taking the uncertainty into account. If the uncertainty interval falls only in the range of one rock type, the result supports it.

    Model answer

    The density of the stone is probably between $2600\text{–}2760\,\text{kg/m}^3$. The interval lies entirely in the typical range of granite, $2600\text{–}2800\,\text{kg/m}^3$, and the lower limit of basalt, $2800\,\text{kg/m}^3$, is outside the interval. The result therefore supports granite, and basalt is unlikely.

    Density alone does not prove the rock type, however: the table values are typical, not absolute limits, and many other rock types also have a density of $2.6\text{–}2.8\,\text{g/cm}^3$. Certainty comes only when the density is compared with, for example, the colour, grains and hardness of the stone.

    d)

    Find

    which measurement limits the precision more and how the result is made more precise

    Principle

    The relative uncertainties tell which measurement affects the result most. It pays to improve the precision of the measurement whose relative uncertainty is largest.

    Model answer

    The relative uncertainty of the mass is $0.1\,\text{g} : 187.4\,\text{g} \approx 0.05\,\%$ and that of the volume $2\,\text{cm}^3 : 70\,\text{cm}^3 \approx 3\,\%$. The volume therefore limits the result about 50 times more than the mass, and a more precise balance would not help.

    The uncertainty of the volume is reduced with a narrower measuring cylinder whose scale division is, for example, $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ or smaller, if the stone fits in it. Another way is an overflow can: the stone is lowered into a full can, the overflowing water is collected and weighed, and $V = \tfrac{m_\text{water}}{\rho_\text{water}}$. The uncertainty of the weighing is only $0.1\,\text{g}$, but the water left in the spout and on the walls of the can brings a larger uncertainty, about $0.5\,\text{cm}^3$, so the method is more precise than the measuring cylinder only when done carefully. Air bubbles, which would otherwise increase the volume, are removed from the surface of the stone.

    Common mistakes

    • The final reading $190\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ is taken as the volume of the stone instead of the rise of the water level.
    • Only the $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ of one reading is taken as the uncertainty of the volume, although the volume is the difference of two readings.
    • In the extreme values the largest mass is combined with the largest volume, so the range of the density comes out too small.
    • Units are converted wrongly: $1\,\text{g/cm}^3$ is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, not $1\,\text{kg/m}^3$ or $100\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

    Model answer

    With constant deceleration the braking distance is $s = \tfrac{v^2}{2a}$, that is, the distance is directly proportional to the square of the speed. When the speed doubles, the distance quadruples: in the table the distance at $10\,\text{km/h}$ is $1.0\,\text{m}$ and at $20\,\text{km/h}$ it is $3.8\,\text{m}$, which is close to four times as much.

    The assumption can be tested by drawing the $(v^2, s)$ graph. If the assumption holds, the points lie on a straight line through the origin whose slope is $\tfrac{1}{2a}$. The $(v, s)$ graph is not suitable for the test, because its points would lie on a parabola, which is hard to tell apart from other curves.

    b)

    Find

    a graph that tests the assumption and the deceleration determined from it

    Graph

    The speeds are first converted to $\text{m/s}$ by dividing by $3.6$ and then squared: $10\,\text{km/h} = 2.78\,\text{m/s}$, so $v^2 = 7.7\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, and similarly $17.4$, $30.9$, $48.2$ and $69.4\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$. On the horizontal axis is $v^2\ (\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2)$ and on the vertical axis $s\ (\text{m})$.

    Model answer

    The points are close to a straight line through the origin, so the assumption of constant deceleration is supported. The line is drawn through the origin, because at zero speed the braking distance is zero. The distant point $(60\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$; $7.2\,\text{m})$ is read from the line, so the slope is $k = \tfrac{7.2\,\text{m}}{60\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 0.12\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$. Since $s = \tfrac{1}{2a}\, v^2$, the slope is $k = \tfrac{1}{2a}$ and the deceleration is

    $a = \tfrac{1}{2k} = \tfrac{1}{2 \cdot 0.12\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}} = 4.17\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$,

    two significant figures as in the braking distances and the slope.

    c)

    Given

    deceleration $a = 4.17\,\text{m/s}^2$ (part b), a horizontal road, the wheels do not lock, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Find

    the smallest coefficient of static friction $\mu_0$ between the tyres and the asphalt

    Principle

    When air resistance is left out, the bicycle and the cyclist are slowed horizontally only by the friction force of the road on the tyres. The tyres do not slip on the asphalt, so this is static friction, which can be at most $\mu_0 N$. The road is horizontal, so vertically $N = mg$. By Newton's second law the friction force must be $ma$.

    Formula

    $ma = F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$, from which the mass cancels and $\mu_0 \ge \dfrac{a}{g}$

    Conversions

    None needed, the accelerations are in $\text{m/s}^2$.

    Substitution

    $\mu_0$$\ge \dfrac{4.17\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
    $= 0.425$

    Answer

    $\mu_0 \ge 0.42$

    Two significant figures as in the deceleration $4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. A coefficient of friction has no unit.

    Reasonableness

    The coefficient of static friction between dry asphalt and rubber is typically clearly larger, about $0.7\text{–}0.9$, so the braking is possible without the wheels locking. A deceleration of $4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ is a little under half the free-fall acceleration, which fits the braking of a bicycle.

    d)

    Given

    the app's speed is $5\,\%$ higher than the true speed in every run: $v_\text{app} = 1.05\, v$; the deceleration of part b is $4.17\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Find

    by how many per cent and in which direction the deceleration of part b differs from the true value, and whether the conclusion changes

    Principle

    The error is systematic: every speed has been multiplied by the same number. In the graph every $v^2$ has therefore been multiplied by $1.05^2$, and the slope of the line $k = \tfrac{s}{v^2}$ is too small in the same proportion. The deceleration is $a = \tfrac{1}{2k}$, so it is too large in the same proportion.

    Formula

    $v_\text{app}^2 = 1.05^2\, v^2$, so $k_\text{app} = \dfrac{k}{1.05^2}$ and $a_\text{app} = 1.05^2\, a$; the deviation is

    $\dfrac{a_\text{app} - a}{a}$$= 1.05^2 - 1$

    Conversions

    The percentage as a decimal number: $5\,\% = 0.05$.

    Substitution

    $1.05^2 - 1$$= 1.1025 - 1$
    $= 0.1025$
    $= 10.25\,\%$

    and the true deceleration

    $a$$= \dfrac{4.17\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.1025}$
    $= 3.78\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Answer

    The deceleration of part b is about $10\,\%$ too large; the true deceleration is about $3.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The result is given as an approximate value, because the deceleration $4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ is known to two figures. The conclusion about constant deceleration does not change: every $v^2$ changes in the same proportion, so the points still lie on a straight line through the origin; only its slope changes.

    Reasonableness

    The speed is squared in the formula, so a small relative error doubles: $2 \cdot 5\,\% = 10\,\%$. The smallest coefficient of friction of part c also decreases in the same proportion, to about $0.39$.

    Common mistakes

    • The graph drawn is $(v, s)$, and a straight line is fitted to the curved set of points.
    • The speeds are squared in $\text{km/h}$, so the deceleration comes out in the wrong unit and with the wrong size; the conversion is $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
    • The slope itself or $\tfrac{1}{k}$ is taken as the deceleration; the two of the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is forgotten.
    • In part c kinetic friction is used, although the wheels do not slip, or an exact value is given as the answer instead of a lower limit.
    • In part d the deviation is given as $5\,\%$, forgetting that the speed is squared in the formula.

    Also accepted

    • In part b, the slope can be determined from a calculator fit through the origin ($k \approx 0.121\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$, $a \approx 4.15\,\text{m/s}^2$) or from another distant point of the line. Decelerations of $4.0\text{–}4.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ are accepted, as are coefficients of $0.41\text{–}0.45$ in part c.
    • In part d the answer $10.25\,\%$ or “about $10\,\%$”.

    3. Calculation task

    The mass of a metal cube is $63.5\,\text{g}$, and its uncertainty is very small. The edge of the cube was measured with vernier callipers as $a = (2.00 \pm 0.02)\,\text{cm}$. Estimate the uncertainty $\Delta\rho$ of the density by calculating the density with the extreme values of the edge. Give the answer in $\text{g/cm}^3$.

    g/cm³
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The volume of a cube is $V = a^3$, so $\rho = \tfrac{m}{a^3}$.

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Calculate $\rho$ with the edges $1.98\,\text{cm}$ and $2.02\,\text{cm}$. Which one gives the larger density?

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    $\Delta\rho = \tfrac{\rho_\text{max} - \rho_\text{min}}{2}$.

    Show the solution

    Given

    $m = 63.5\,\text{g}$, edge $a = (2.00 \pm 0.02)\,\text{cm}$

    Find

    the uncertainty $\Delta\rho$ of the density in $\text{g/cm}^3$

    Principle

    The volume of a cube is $a^3$, so the density is $\rho = \tfrac{m}{a^3}$. The uncertainty is estimated with the extreme-value method: the density is calculated with the smallest and the largest value of the edge, and the uncertainty is half the difference. The smaller edge gives the larger density.

    Formula

    $\rho$$= \dfrac{m}{a^3}$
    $\Delta\rho$$= \dfrac{\rho_\text{max} - \rho_\text{min}}{2}$

    Conversions

    None needed when the mass is in grams and the edge in centimetres: the density comes out in $\text{g/cm}^3$.

    Substitution

    $\rho_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{63.5\,\text{g}}{(1.98\,\text{cm})^3}$
    $= 8.18\,\text{g/cm}^3$
    $\rho_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{63.5\,\text{g}}{(2.02\,\text{cm})^3}$
    $= 7.70\,\text{g/cm}^3$
    $\Delta\rho$$= \dfrac{8.18\,\text{g/cm}^3 - 7.70\,\text{g/cm}^3}{2}$
    $= 0.24\,\text{g/cm}^3$

    Answer

    $\Delta\rho \approx 0.2\,\text{g/cm}^3$

    One significant figure, as is usual for an error estimate. With the edge $2.00\,\text{cm}$ one gets $\rho = 7.94\,\text{g/cm}^3$, so $\rho = (7.9 \pm 0.2)\,\text{g/cm}^3$.

    Reasonableness

    The relative uncertainty of the edge is $1\,\%$, and cubing triples it to about $3\,\%$: $0.03 \cdot 7.94\,\text{g/cm}^3 \approx 0.24\,\text{g/cm}^3$. A density of $7.9\,\text{g/cm}^3$ fits iron.

    4. Explanation task: Justify

    Explain why the spring constant is best determined from the slope of an $(F, x)$ graph rather than by calculating a separate $k = F : x$ from each measurement and taking their mean.

    Model answer

    When $k$ is calculated separately from each measurement, the error of each reading goes straight into the result. If the extension has been read $1\,\text{cm}$ too large every time, every $k$ is too small, and the mean does not correct it. In a graph the same error shifts the whole line, but the slope does not change, so $k$ comes out right, and the error shows as the constant term. The line is fitted among all the points, so the random error of a single point has less effect. In addition, the graph shows at once whether the points lie on a line, that is, whether the model fits, and whether a deviating point that needs checking stands out.
