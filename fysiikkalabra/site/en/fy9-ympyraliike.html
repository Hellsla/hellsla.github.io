Equilibrium, circular motion and gravitation

Why can a satellite dish be aimed once and left in place, although the television satellite orbits the Earth at a speed of about $3\,\text{km/s}$?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve equilibrium, circular-motion and satellite problems with a free-body diagram and Newton's second law
  • draw and interpret the graph and the fit of a centripetal-force measurement
  • evaluate claims and measurements about moments, centripetal force and weightlessness with justification

1Think about it

A satellite dish is fixed to the gable wall of a detached house and points low in the southern sky towards a television satellite. The installer aimed the dish once, and it has not needed turning since. Yet the satellite orbits the Earth above the equator at a speed of about $3\,\text{km/s}$. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the situation and the numbers are given, but the law is not.

Why does the satellite seem to stay still as seen from the dish? What decides how far from the Earth it must orbit?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting-level check 1: the lever arm

A wheel nut is being undone with a spanner. The hand pulls the handle $0.30\,\text{m}$ from the centre of the nut, but the force is at a slant to the handle.Besides the size of the force, what is needed to calculate the moment of the force?

  • A long spanner does not increase the force of the hand. It lengthens the lever arm when the grip is further from the axis, and the moment is the force times the lever arm. Review in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force.

  • Correct. The lever arm is $r = d\sin\alpha$, so a slanting pull gives a smaller moment than an equally large perpendicular pull. If needed, review in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force.

  • The distance to the point of application is enough only when the force is perpendicular to the handle. The moment of a slanting force is set by the lever arm, which is shorter than $0.30\,\text{m}$. Review in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force.

Starting-level check 2: two supports

A uniform plank rests on two trestles. Trestle 1 is $0.5\,\text{m}$ and trestle 2 is $1.5\,\text{m}$ from the middle of the plank.Which trestle carries a larger share of the weight of the plank?

  • A longer distance does not mean a larger force. The moment condition about the centre of gravity is $N_1 \cdot 0.5\,\text{m} = N_2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{m}$, so $N_2$ is smaller. Review in the chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments.

  • Each support carries half of the weight only when the supports are equally far from the centre of gravity. Here the distances are different, so the normal forces are too. Review in the chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments.

  • Correct. From the moment condition about the centre of gravity $N_1 \cdot 0.5\,\text{m} = N_2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{m}$, so trestle 1 carries three times as much as trestle 2, that is, $75\,\%$ of the weight. If needed, review in the chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments.

Starting-level check 3: two points on the same record

The record on a record player turns steadily. There are two stickers on the record: A is $5\,\text{cm}$ and B $15\,\text{cm}$ from the centre.How do the periods and the speeds of the stickers compare?

  • Turning steadily means that the speed does not change with time. At different radii the speeds are still different, because in one revolution B travels a longer distance in the same time. Review in the chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed.

  • The record is rigid, so every point on it makes a revolution in the same time. In the same time B travels a circumference three times as long, so its speed is three times as large. Review in the chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed.

  • Correct. The record is rigid, so the period $T$ is the same, and the speed $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ is directly proportional to the radius. If needed, review in the chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed.

Starting-level check 4: a car on a bend

A car drives round a bend of a level road at a constant speed. Air resistance and rolling resistance are not taken into account.What horizontal force acts on the car?

  • The speed does not change and there are no resistive forces, so no net force along the tangent is needed. Changing direction needs a force pointing to the centre of the bend. Review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

  • Correct. Friction is the only horizontal force, so it is the net force: $F_\mu = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. On a slippery road static friction is not enough, and the car goes straighter than the road curves. If needed, review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

  • If the horizontal forces cancelled each other, the car would carry straight on. There is no centrifugal force: nothing pulls the car outwards. Review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

Starting-level check 5: gravitation further away

The weight of a space probe on the surface of the Earth is $800\,\text{N}$. The probe rises to a height of $6371\,\text{km}$, where its distance from the centre of the Earth is two Earth radii.How large is the gravitational force of the Earth on the probe then?

  • Gravitation does not stop at the edge of the atmosphere. It weakens with the square of the distance but never becomes zero. Review in the chapter The gravitational force between bodies.

  • Correct. $F = \gamma\tfrac{Mm}{r^2}$, so at twice the distance the force is $\tfrac14 \cdot 800\,\text{N} = 200\,\text{N}$. If needed, review in the chapter The gravitational force between bodies.

  • The gravitational force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, not to the distance. Twice the distance gives a quarter of the force. Review in the chapter The gravitational force between bodies.

Starting-level check 6: weightlessness

A space station orbits the Earth at a height of about $400\,\text{km}$, and an astronaut floats inside it. Which explanation is physically correct?

  • If the net force were zero, the station would carry straight on and not orbit the Earth. The gravitational force is the net force on the station. Review in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • Correct. Gravitation acts all the time and gives the station and the astronaut the same acceleration towards the Earth. That is why the floor does not press on the astronaut, although their weight is almost $90\,\%$ of its value at the surface. If needed, review in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • $400\,\text{km}$ is little compared with the radius of the Earth, $6371\,\text{km}$, so gravitation there is almost as strong as at the surface. Without it the station would not stay in its orbit. Review in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

The satellite is in uniform circular motion: the gravitation of the Earth is the only force, and it turns the direction of the velocity without changing the speed. In one revolution the satellite travels the circumference $2\pi r$ at the constant speed $v$ (chapters Moving in a circle: period and speed and Satellites and planets in circular orbits).

On the horizontal axis the GPS point is a little to the right of the reading $25$, at about $26.6$, and on the vertical axis a little below $4$, at about $3.87$. So the radius is about $26\,600\,\text{km}$ and the orbital speed about $3.87\,\text{km/s}$.

The real period of a GPS satellite is $11\,\text{h}\;58\,\text{min}$, about half a day, so the reading is right. The result lies between the ISS's roughly one and a half hours and the geostationary satellite's roughly one day, as the radii suggest it should: further away the orbit is longer and the speed smaller, so the period grows quickly.

4Theory

Key idea

The problems of this lesson start in the same way as mechanics problems usually do: choose the body, draw the free-body diagram and write Newton's second law. The situation tells what the acceleration is. In equilibrium the body at rest neither starts to move nor starts to turn, so both the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments are zero. In uniform circular motion the acceleration points to the centre of the path, so the sum of the forces points there too. Only gravitation acts on a satellite, so the gravitational force is itself the net force.

The opening question is solved in the same way. Only the gravitational force of the Earth acts on the satellite, and it gives the satellite its centripetal acceleration. The further away the satellite orbits, the longer its period. The geostationary orbit lies above the equator at exactly the distance at which the period is the same as the Earth's rotation period, $23\,\text{h}\;56\,\text{min}$. From the period the radius of the orbit comes out as $r \approx 42\,200\,\text{km}$, about $35\,800\,\text{km}$ above the Earth's surface, and the orbital speed as $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T} \approx 3.07\,\text{km/s}$. The satellite and the dish go round the Earth's axis in the same time and in the same direction, so as seen from the dish the satellite stays still.

Equilibrium, circular motion and gravitation

Moment and equilibrium

moment of a force
$$M = Fr$$
$$\Sigma\overline{F} = \overline{0}$$
$$\Sigma M = 0$$

where $M$ is the moment of the force ($\text{Nm}$), $F$ the size of the force ($\text{N}$) and $r$ the lever arm of the force ($\text{m}$), that is, the perpendicular distance of the axis of rotation from the line of action of the force. If the force makes the angle $\alpha$ with the line from the axis to the point of application and the point of application is at the distance $d$, the lever arm is $r = d\sin\alpha$. An anticlockwise moment is positive. For a body at rest the moment condition holds about any point, so it pays to choose the point of application of an unknown force as the pivot point. The weight acts at the centre of gravity.

Uniform circular motion

orbital speed
$$v = \omega r$$
centripetal acceleration
$$a_\text{n} = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$

where $v$ is the orbital speed ($\text{m/s}$), $r$ the radius of the path ($\text{m}$), $\omega = \tfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$ the angular velocity ($\text{rad/s}$), $T$ the period ($\text{s}$) and $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$ the rotation frequency ($\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$), so $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T} = 2\pi r f$, and $a_\text{n}$ the centripetal acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). In uniform circular motion the speed is constant. The velocity is along the tangent of the path, and the centripetal acceleration points to the centre of the path.

By Newton's second law the net force $\Sigma F$ ($\text{N}$) on the body gives it the centripetal acceleration, so the net force points to the centre. The centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ also holds at an instant when the speed changes: at the highest and the lowest point of the path there is no force along the tangent. In the equation of motion the forces pointing to the centre are positive, and perpendicular to the plane of the path the sum of the forces is zero.

Derive it yourself. A body of mass $m$ is in uniform circular motion at the speed $v$ on a path of radius $r$. Derive the net force $\Sigma F$ on the body from Newton's second law and the centripetal acceleration.

Show the derivation

Newton's second law $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$ also holds in circular motion. In uniform circular motion the acceleration is the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$, which points to the centre of the path. So the net force points to the centre, and its magnitude is

$$\Sigma F = ma_\text{n} = m\frac{v^2}{r}$$

Derive it yourself. A car of mass $m$ drives at the speed $v$ in a curve of a horizontal road with radius $r$. The friction of the road gives the car its centripetal force. Derive how large a friction force is needed and the greatest speed at which the car still stays on the road when the coefficient of static friction is $\mu_0$.

Show the derivation

In the curve the forces on the car are the weight $G = mg$, the normal force $N$ of the road and the friction force $F_\mu$ of the road (figure). There is no vertical acceleration, so $N = mg$. The only horizontal force is friction, so it is the net force. In uniform circular motion the net force is $\Sigma F = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$, so

$$F_\mu = m\frac{v^2}{r}$$

The tyres do not slide sideways, so the friction is static friction. It is at most $\mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$. The car stays on the road when

$$m\frac{v^2}{r} \le \mu_0 mg \quad\Rightarrow\quad v \le \sqrt{\mu_0 g r}$$

The mass cancels, so the greatest speed does not depend on the mass of the car.

Gravitation and satellites

Newton's law of gravitation
$$F_\text{g} = \gamma\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

where $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$ is the gravitational constant, $F_\text{g}$ the magnitude of the gravitational force ($\text{N}$), $m_1$ and $m_2$ the masses of the bodies ($\text{kg}$) and $r$ the distance between the centres ($\text{m}$). The law holds for point-like and spherically symmetric bodies. The gravitational field strength $g$ ($\text{N/kg}$, that is $\text{m/s}^2$) of a central body of mass $M$ ($\text{kg}$) is the gravitational force per unit mass of a body; at the Earth's surface it is the acceleration due to gravity.

Derive it yourself. A body of mass $m$ is at the distance $r$ from the centre of a central body of mass $M$. Derive the gravitational field strength $g$, that is the acceleration due to gravity, at this distance.

Show the derivation

By Newton's law of gravitation the gravitational force on the body is

$$F_\text{g} = \gamma\frac{Mm}{r^2}$$

This force is the weight $G = mg$ of the body when the rotation of the central body is ignored. Set the forces equal:

$$mg = \gamma\frac{Mm}{r^2}$$

The mass $m$ of the body cancels:

$$g = \gamma\frac{M}{r^2}$$

The field strength does not depend on the mass of the body, so all bodies fall with the same acceleration. Air resistance is neglected. At the Earth's surface $r = R$, and further away $g$ decreases with the square of the distance.

A satellite orbits a central body on a circular orbit of radius $r$ at the orbital speed $v$ ($\text{m/s}$). The only force on it is the gravitational force, which gives it the centripetal acceleration. For an Earth satellite $r = R + h$, where $R = 6371\,\text{km}$ and $h$ is the height above the surface, and the period is $T = \tfrac{2\pi r}{v}$.

Derive it yourself. A satellite of mass $m$ orbits a central body of mass $M$ on a circular orbit of radius $r$. Derive the orbital speed $v$ of the satellite.

Show the derivation

The only force on the satellite is the gravitational force, so it is the net force and points to the centre of the orbit. By Newton's second law it gives the satellite the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$. Writing the gravitational force by Newton's law of gravitation gives

$$\gamma\frac{Mm}{r^2} = m\frac{v^2}{r}$$

The mass $m$ of the satellite cancels. Multiply the equation by the radius $r$:

$$v^2 = \frac{\gamma M}{r}$$

The orbital speed is positive, so

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{r}}$$

The orbital speed does not depend on the mass of the satellite, and the further out the orbit, the smaller it is. The result holds when the central body is much more massive than the satellite, so that the central body stays in place.

Common mistakes

  • The distance in place of the lever arm. The moment of a slanting force is $Fd\sin\alpha$, not $Fd$.
  • The support further away carries more. It is the other way round: the support closer to the centre of gravity carries more.
  • A centrifugal force in the free-body diagram. There is no outward force. The centripetal force is the sum of the real forces, and it is not drawn as an arrow of its own.
  • Diameter and revolutions per minute. The radius is substituted into the formula, and $\tfrac{1}{\text{min}}$ is converted: $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$.
  • The height in place of the radius. The radius of the orbit is measured from the centre of the Earth: $r = R + h$.
  • Weightlessness is the absence of gravity. On a space station gravitation acts, but the station and the astronaut fall freely together, so the normal force is zero.

5Examples

Find the error: the lid of a chest

The lid of a wooden chest turns about hinges at its back edge, and the depth of the lid from the hinges to the front edge is $0.50\,\text{m}$. The lid is lifted at the front edge with a force of $30\,\text{N}$ at an angle of $40^\circ$ to the lid. A student calculated the moment of the force about the hinges like this: $M = Fd = 30\,\text{N} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} = 15\,\text{Nm}$.What is wrong with the solution?

  • The angle is between the force and the lid. The moment is given by the component perpendicular to the lid, $F\sin 40^\circ$. The component along the lid, $F\cos 40^\circ$, passes through the hinge and does not turn the lid. Review in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force.

  • The weight of the lid has a moment of its own, but the question asks for the moment of the lifting force. The error is in the lever arm of the force. Review in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force.

  • Correct. The force is not perpendicular to the lid, so the lever arm is shorter than $0.50\,\text{m}$: $r = 0.50\,\text{m} \cdot \sin 40^\circ \approx 0.32\,\text{m}$ and $M = 30\,\text{N} \cdot 0.32\,\text{m} \approx 9.6\,\text{Nm}$.

  • The unit of moment is $\text{Nm}$. In work the distance is along the force, in a moment the lever arm is perpendicular to the force, so the units are kept apart. Review in the chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force.

Read the graph: centripetal acceleration

The graph shows the centripetal acceleration as a function of the speed for two radii of the path, $50\,\text{m}$ and $100\,\text{m}$.Which claim is true according to the graph?

  • The curves move apart as the speed grows: at the speed $30\,\text{m/s}$ the accelerations are $18\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $9\,\text{m/s}^2$. Review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

  • Correct. At the radius $50\,\text{m}$ the acceleration is about $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ at the speed $10\,\text{m/s}$ and about $8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ at the speed $20\,\text{m/s}$, as $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ predicts.

  • In the graph the curve for the radius $100\,\text{m}$ is lower: twice the radius halves the acceleration, because $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$. Review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

  • A curve through the origin is not a straight line: the curves get steeper as the speed grows. Direct proportionality would show as a straight line. The acceleration is proportional to the square of the speed. Review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

Claim: a painter on a plank

A painter walks on a plank resting on two trestles, from above the left trestle towards the right trestle. The plank stays at rest.Which claim about the normal forces of the trestles is true while the painter walks?

  • The sum of the weights, and so the sum of the normal forces, stays the same, but the shares change. The trestle closer to the painter carries more: the moment condition decides the shares. Review in the chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments.

  • Then the sum of the normal forces would grow although the weights stay the same, and the sum of the forces would not be zero. When the right normal force grows, the left one must decrease by the same amount. Review in the chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments.

  • Correct. For translation $N_1 + N_2 = G_\text{plank} + G_\text{painter}$ stays the same. The moment condition about the left trestle shows that $N_2$ grows as the lever arm of the painter's weight grows.

  • The sum of the forces is zero all the time, so the sum of the normal forces is always equal to the sum of the weights. The position of the painter changes only the shares. Review in the chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments.

Which free-body diagram: a satellite

A satellite orbits the Earth in a circular orbit. Which description in words of the free-body diagram of the satellite is correct?

  • Correct. Gravitation is the only force and so the net force. It points to the centre and gives the centripetal acceleration $\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. The velocity is marked as an arrow of its own beside the body, not as a force.

  • No force is needed to keep the speed. In a circular orbit gravitation is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does not change the speed but turns the direction of the velocity. Review in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • Weightlessness means that the normal force is zero, not that gravitation is missing. Without a force the satellite would carry straight on. Review in the chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits.

  • There is no centrifugal force, and it is not drawn. If the forces cancelled each other, the satellite would carry straight on along the tangent of the orbit. Review in the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle.

The moment on a pedal

A cyclist stands on a pedal and presses it straight down with a force of $520\,\text{N}$. The length of the crank from the bottom-bracket axle to the pedal axle is $0.170\,\text{m}$, and the crank points forwards $35^\circ$ above the horizontal.

How large is the moment of the force about the bottom-bracket axle?

Nm
Hint 1: a guiding question

The lever arm of the force is the perpendicular distance of the bottom-bracket axle from the line of action of the force. Draw the crank and the vertical line through the pedal.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The distance of the vertical line of action from the axle is the horizontal reach of the crank: $r = d\cos 35^\circ$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$M = 520\,\text{N} \cdot 0.170\,\text{m} \cdot \cos 35^\circ$

Show the solution

Given

force $F = 520\,\text{N}$ straight down, length of the crank $d = 0.170\,\text{m}$, crank $35^\circ$ above the horizontal

Find

the moment $M$ of the force about the bottom-bracket axle

Principle

The crank can only turn about the bottom-bracket axle, so the turning effect of the force is given by the moment $M = Fr$. The lever arm $r$ is the perpendicular distance of the axle from the vertical line of action of the force. The angle between the force and the extension of the crank is $\alpha = 90^\circ + 35^\circ = 125^\circ$, so $r = d\sin 125^\circ = d\cos 35^\circ$ (chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force).

Formula

$M = Fr$ and $r = d\cos 35^\circ$, so $M = Fd\cos 35^\circ$

Conversions

None needed: the force is in newtons and the length in metres, so the moment comes out in newton-metres.

Substitution

$M$$= 520\,\text{N} \cdot 0.170\,\text{m} \cdot \cos 35^\circ$
$= 88.4\,\text{Nm} \cdot 0.8192$
$= 72.41\,\text{Nm}$

Answer

$M \approx 72\,\text{Nm}$

Two significant figures like the angle $35^\circ$.

Reasonableness

When the crank is horizontal, the lever arm is the whole $0.170\,\text{m}$ and the moment is largest, $88\,\text{Nm}$. When the crank is vertical, the lever arm is zero. The result lies between these, and at the angle $35^\circ$ the lever arm is still over $80\,\%$ of the length of the crank.

A snow shovel held level

A snow shovel lifts $4.0\,\text{kg}$ of snow, and the shovel is held still and level for a moment. The back hand is at the end of the handle, and the front hand supports the handle $0.45\,\text{m}$ from the back hand. The centre of gravity of the snow is $1.30\,\text{m}$ from the back hand. The mass of the shovel is $1.5\,\text{kg}$, and its centre of gravity is $0.90\,\text{m}$ from the back hand. The hands act on the shovel vertically.

How large a force does the front hand use to support the shovel?

N
Hint 1: a guiding question

Choose the back hand as the pivot point. Then the unknown force of the back hand does not appear in the moment condition.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The force of the front hand turns the shovel one way, the weights of the snow and the shovel the other way:

$F_\text{f} \cdot 0.45\,\text{m}$$= m_\text{snow} g \cdot 1.30\,\text{m} + m_\text{shovel} g \cdot 0.90\,\text{m}$

$F_\text{f} \cdot 0.45\,\text{m}$$= (4.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.30\,\text{m} + 1.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.90\,\text{m}) \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

and solve it for $F_\text{f}$.

Show the solution

Given

snow $m_\text{snow} = 4.0\,\text{kg}$ at the distance $r_\text{snow} = 1.30\,\text{m}$, shovel $m_\text{shovel} = 1.5\,\text{kg}$ at the distance $r_\text{shovel} = 0.90\,\text{m}$ and the front hand at the distance $r_\text{f} = 0.45\,\text{m}$, all measured from the back hand; the shovel level and at rest; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the force $F_\text{f}$ of the front hand

Principle

The shovel is at rest, so it is in equilibrium: the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments are zero. The back hand is chosen as the pivot point, so the unknown force of the back hand does not appear in the moment condition. The forces are vertical and the handle horizontal, so the lever arms are the distances from the back hand (chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments).

Formula

$F_\text{f}\, r_\text{f} - m_\text{snow} g\, r_\text{snow} - m_\text{shovel} g\, r_\text{shovel}$
$= 0$

so

Conversions

None needed: the masses are in kilograms and the distances in metres.

Substitution

The sum of the moments of the weights about the back hand is

Answer

$F_\text{f} \approx 140\,\text{N}$

Two significant figures like the mass $4.0\,\text{kg}$ and the distance $0.45\,\text{m}$.

A phone on a roundabout

A phone lies on a non-slip mat on the dashboard of a car. The coefficient of static friction between the phone and the mat is $0.40$. The car drives round a level roundabout at a constant speed, and the phone moves in a circle of radius $15\,\text{m}$.

What is the greatest speed at which the car can drive without the phone starting to slide? Answer in kilometres per hour.

km/h
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which force keeps the phone moving in a circle with the car? In which direction does it point?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Static friction is the only horizontal force on the phone: $F_{\mu 0} = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$, and it can be at most $\mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$v = \sqrt{\mu_0 g r}$, and finally $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.

Show the solution

Given

coefficient of static friction $\mu_0 = 0.40$, radius of the path $r = 15\,\text{m}$, level path and constant speed, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the greatest speed $v$ at which the phone does not slide, in kilometres per hour

Principle

The phone moves with the car in uniform circular motion, so the sum of the forces on it points to the centre of the path. Vertically the weight and the normal force cancel each other, $N = mg$. Horizontally the only force is the static friction of the mat, so it is the centripetal force. Static friction can be at most $\mu_0 N$, and at the greatest speed it is exactly this large (chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle).

Formula

Newton's second law $\Sigma F = ma$ with $a = a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ in uniform circular motion: $\mu_0 mg = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so the mass cancels and $v = \sqrt{\mu_0 g r}$

Conversions

The speed comes out in $\text{m/s}$ and is converted by substituting $1\,\text{m} = 0.001\,\text{km}$ and $1\,\text{s} = \tfrac{1}{3600}\,\text{h}$, that is, $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.

Substitution

$v$$= \sqrt{0.40 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 15\,\text{m}}$
$= \sqrt{58.86\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
$= 7.672\,\text{m/s}$
$= 7.672 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h}$
$= 27.6\,\text{km/h}$

Answer

$v \approx 28\,\text{km/h}$

Two significant figures like the coefficient of friction $0.40$ and the radius $15\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

Roundabouts are usually driven at $20\text{–}30\,\text{km/h}$, so the phone only just stays on the mat. The centripetal acceleration is then $\mu_0 g \approx 3.9\,\text{m/s}^2$. The mass cancelled, so a heavy and a light phone start to slide at the same speed.

Model answer

a)

Given

mass of the trailer $m_\text{t} = 165\,\text{kg}$ and centre of gravity at the distance $r_\text{t} = 0.58\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle; mass of the boat and the motor $m_\text{b} = 430\,\text{kg}$ and centre of gravity at the distance $r_\text{b} = 0.12\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle; coupling at the distance $r_\text{k} = 3.25\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the vertical force $F$ of the coupling on the drawbar and the nose weight $F : g$

Principle

Choose the trailer and the boat together as the body. In the free-body diagram the forces on them are the weight of the trailer $\overline{G}_\text{t}$ and the weight of the boat $\overline{G}_\text{b}$ down from the centres of gravity, the normal force of the ground $\overline{N}$ up at the wheels and the force of the coupling $\overline{F}$ up at the end of the drawbar. At rest the horizontal force of the coupling is zero. The trailer is at rest, so it is in equilibrium: the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments are zero. The axle is chosen as the pivot point, so the lever arm of the unknown normal force is zero. The forces are vertical and the distances horizontal, so the distances are the lever arms of the forces (chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments).

Formula

$F r_\text{k} - m_\text{t} g\, r_\text{t} - m_\text{b} g\, r_\text{b} = 0$, so

$F$$= \dfrac{(m_\text{t} r_\text{t} + m_\text{b} r_\text{b})\,g}{r_\text{k}}$

and the nose weight is

$m_\text{n}$$= \dfrac{F}{g}$
$= \dfrac{m_\text{t} r_\text{t} + m_\text{b} r_\text{b}}{r_\text{k}}$

Conversions

None needed: the masses are in kilograms and the distances in metres.

Substitution

The sum of the moments of the weights about the axle is

$(165\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.58\,\text{m} + 430\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.12\,\text{m}) \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 147.3\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 1445\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}$

so

$F$$= \dfrac{1445\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}}{3.25\,\text{m}}$
$= 444.6\,\text{N}$

and the nose weight is

$\dfrac{444.6\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$$= 45.3\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$F \approx 440\,\text{N}$

And the nose weight about $45\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures like the distances of the centres of gravity $0.58\,\text{m}$ and $0.12\,\text{m}$. The nose weight is below the lower limit of the instructions, $50\,\text{kg}$.

Reasonableness

The common centre of gravity is

$\tfrac{147.3\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{595\,\text{kg}}$$\approx 0.25\,\text{m}$

in front of the axle, that is, about $\tfrac{0.25}{3.25} \approx 8\,\%$ of the distance of the coupling. So the coupling carries about $8\,\%$ of the total mass $595\,\text{kg}$, about $45\,\text{kg}$, as found.

b)

Given

from part a $F = 444.6\,\text{N}$; masses $m_\text{t} = 165\,\text{kg}$ and $m_\text{b} = 430\,\text{kg}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the normal force $N$ of the ground on the wheels

Principle

The trailer is at rest, so the sum of the vertical forces is zero: the upward normal force and the force of the coupling together carry the weights of the trailer and the boat (chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments).

Formula

$N + F - (m_\text{t} + m_\text{b})g = 0$, so $N = (m_\text{t} + m_\text{b})g - F$

Conversions

$N$$= 595\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 444.6\,\text{N}$
$= 5837\,\text{N} - 444.6\,\text{N}$
$= 5392\,\text{N}$

$N \approx 5.39\,\text{kN}$

Three significant figures, because most of it comes from the weight, whose masses are given to three figures.

Reasonableness

The wheels carry $\tfrac{5392}{5837} \approx 92\,\%$ of the weight, because the common centre of gravity is close to the axle. The normal force corresponds to a mass of about $550\,\text{kg}$, and the masses total $595\,\text{kg}$: the coupling and the wheels together carry the whole weight.

c)

Given

target nose weight $m_\text{n} = 60\,\text{kg}$; the masses and distances of part a; the boat moved forwards by $\Delta x$

Find

the move $\Delta x$

Principle

The moment condition about the axle still holds. The move changes only the lever arm of the weight of the boat, from $0.12\,\text{m}$ to $0.12\,\text{m} + \Delta x$. The nose weight $60\,\text{kg}$ corresponds to the force $F = 60\,\text{kg} \cdot g$ (chapter Moment: the turning effect of a force).

Formula

$m_\text{n} g\, r_\text{k}$$= m_\text{t} g\, r_\text{t} + m_\text{b} g\,(r_\text{b} + \Delta x)$

so $g$ cancels and

$\Delta x$$= \dfrac{m_\text{n} r_\text{k} - m_\text{t} r_\text{t} - m_\text{b} r_\text{b}}{m_\text{b}}$

Conversions

The result is converted to centimetres: $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.

Substitution

$m_\text{b} \Delta x$$= 195\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} - 95.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} - 51.6\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}$
$= 47.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}$

so

$\Delta x$$= \dfrac{47.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{430\,\text{kg}}$
$= 0.1109\,\text{m}$
$= 0.1109 \cdot 100\,\text{cm}$
$= 11.1\,\text{cm}$

The boat is moved about $11\,\text{cm}$ forwards, two significant figures like the distances of the centres of gravity.

Reasonableness

When $430\,\text{kg}$ moves $0.111\,\text{m}$, the moment grows by $47.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}$, and divided by the lever arm of the coupling, $3.25\,\text{m}$, it adds $\tfrac{47.7}{3.25}\,\text{kg} \approx 15\,\text{kg}$ to the nose weight: $45\,\text{kg} + 15\,\text{kg} = 60\,\text{kg}$. A small move is enough, because the boat is heavy.

d)

Find

how $30\,\text{kg}$ loaded behind the axle changes the nose weight, and why too small a nose weight is dangerous

Principle

A weight behind the axle turns the trailer about the axle in the opposite direction to the weights in front of the axle, so it reduces the moment the coupling has to provide (chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments).

Model answer

The moment of the load about the axle is $30\,\text{kg} \cdot g \cdot 1.40\,\text{m}$, and it reduces the moment of the coupling force. The nose weight decreases by $\tfrac{30\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.40\,\text{m}}{3.25\,\text{m}} \approx 13\,\text{kg}$, from the $45\,\text{kg}$ of part a to about $32\,\text{kg}$. If there were even more load at the back, the common centre of gravity of the trailer and the load would move behind the axle. Then the nose weight would be negative: the coupling would have to pull the drawbar down, and the trailer would lift the rear of the car. The normal force on the rear wheels, and so the upper limit of their static friction, would decrease, and the grip in braking and on bends weakens. A small positive nose weight keeps the centre of gravity of the trailer in front of the axle. That is why a heavy load is placed at the axle or a little in front of it.

Common mistakes

  • Moments are taken about the coupling, but the normal force $N$ is not included, or both $N$ and its lever arm about the axle are written into the moment condition.
  • Distances are measured from the wrong place: the distances of the centres of gravity are from the axle, and the lever arm of the coupling force is $3.25\,\text{m}$, not $3.25\,\text{m} - 0.58\,\text{m}$.
  • The nose weight is given with newtons and mass mixed up: $445\,\text{N}$ is not $445\,\text{kg}$.
  • In part d the weight loaded behind the axle is added to the nose weight, although its moment is in the opposite direction.

Also accepted

  • In part a via the common centre of gravity: $x_\text{cg} = \tfrac{m_\text{t} x_\text{t} + m_\text{b} x_\text{b}}{m_\text{t} + m_\text{b}} \approx 0.248\,\text{m}$ and $F = (m_\text{t} + m_\text{b})g \cdot \tfrac{x_\text{cg}}{3.25\,\text{m}}$, the same result.

a)

Find

whether the claim that there is no gravity at a height of $400\,\text{km}$ is true

Principle

The gravitational field strength $g = \gamma\tfrac{M}{r^2}$ decreases with the square of the distance but never becomes zero. Weightlessness is free fall: only gravitation acts on the body, and the normal force is zero (chapter Satellites and planets in circular orbits).

Model answer

The claim is wrong. At a height of $400\,\text{km}$ the distance from the centre of the Earth is $6771\,\text{km}$, only about $6\,\%$ more than at the surface. The field strength there is $\gamma\tfrac{M}{r^2} = \tfrac{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}}{(6.771 \cdot 10^{6})^2}\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 8.7\,\text{m/s}^2$, almost $90\,\%$ of its value at the surface. It is gravitation that keeps the station in its orbit: it is the only force on the station and turns the direction of its velocity all the time. Without gravitation the station would carry straight on out of its orbit. Astronauts float because the station and the astronaut fall freely together: both get the same acceleration towards the Earth. So the floor does not press on the astronaut, the normal force is zero, and a scale would read zero, although the weight acts all the time.

Principle

An astronaut moving with the ring is in uniform circular motion, so the sum of the forces on them points to the centre of the ring: $\Sigma F = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. The orbital speed is $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ (chapters Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle and Moving in a circle: period and speed).

Model answer

Inside the station only the normal force of the floor acts on the astronaut, when the gravitation of the Earth is ignored because it acts on the station in the same way. The astronaut moves in a circle with the ring, so the net force on them must point to the centre of the ring. That is why the outer wall, the floor, pushes on the soles of their feet towards the centre with the force $N = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. On the Earth's surface the same push on the soles feels like supporting one's weight, so the astronaut feels heavy, and “down” is away from the centre of the ring, towards the outer wall. There is no outward centrifugal force: without the floor the astronaut would carry straight on. The floor pushes with as large a force as the Earth's surface when $\tfrac{v^2}{r} = g$. With $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ we get $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{r}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{110\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}} \approx 21\,\text{s}$, so the ring turns about three times a minute, and the orbital speed of the floor is about $33\,\text{m/s}$.

Model answer

When the astronaut lets go, no forces act on the key any more, because the gravitation of the Earth is ignored. So, seen from outside, the key carries straight on along the tangent of the path with the velocity it had at the moment of release. The floor, however, curves beneath the path of the key, so the straight path hits the floor after a moment. To the astronaut the key seems to fall “down” to the floor just as on the Earth. The key does not accelerate outwards, and no force pushes it: the floor comes to meet it. Because the hand is closer to the centre than the floor, the orbital speed of the hand is a little smaller than that of the floor, so the key hits the floor a little behind the point right below the feet, against the direction of rotation.

Model answer

a)

Given

knife edge at the $40.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; weight $m_\text{p} = 200\,\text{g}$ at the $10.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; stone at the $82.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; metre stick $m_\text{m} = 120\,\text{g}$, uniform, centre of gravity at the $50.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; reading of the scale $113\,\text{g}$

Find

the mass $m_\text{s}$ of the stone and a comparison with the reading of the scale

Principle

The stick is at rest, so the sum of the moments about the edge is zero. The edge is not at the centre of gravity of the stick, so the weight of the stick also turns the stick: its lever arm is $10.0\,\text{cm}$, and it turns in the same direction as the stone. The forces are vertical and the stick horizontal, so the lever arms are the distances from the edge (chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments).

Formula

$m_\text{p} g\, d_\text{p} - m_\text{s} g\, d_\text{s} - m_\text{m} g\, d_\text{m}$$= 0$
$m_\text{p} g\, d_\text{p} - m_\text{s} g\, d_\text{s} - m_\text{m} g\, d_\text{m}$
$= 0$

so $g$ cancels and

$m_\text{s}$$= \dfrac{m_\text{p} d_\text{p} - m_\text{m} d_\text{m}}{d_\text{s}}$

where $d_\text{p}$, $d_\text{s}$ and $d_\text{m}$ are the distances of the weight, the stone and the centre of gravity of the stick from the edge

Conversions

The lever arms from the edge:

$d_\text{p}$$= 40.0\,\text{cm} - 10.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 30.0\,\text{cm}$
$d_\text{s}$$= 82.0\,\text{cm} - 40.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 42.0\,\text{cm}$
$d_\text{m}$$= 50.0\,\text{cm} - 40.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 10.0\,\text{cm}$

No other conversions are needed: the centimetres cancel, and the mass comes out in grams.

Substitution

$m_\text{s} \cdot 42.0\,\text{cm}$$= 200\,\text{g} \cdot 30.0\,\text{cm} - 120\,\text{g} \cdot 10.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 6000\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm} - 1200\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}$
$= 4800\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}$

so

$m_\text{s}$$= \dfrac{4800\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}}{42.0\,\text{cm}}$
$= 114.3\,\text{g}$

Answer

$m_\text{s} \approx 114\,\text{g}$

Three significant figures like the measured positions. The result differs from the reading of the scale, $113\,\text{g}$, by about $1\,\%$.

Reasonableness

Reading the balance point to $\pm 0.5\,\text{cm}$ changes the result by about $\pm 1.5\,\text{g}$, so the difference from the reading of the scale is within the accuracy of the measurement. The $142.9\,\text{g}$ of the report is $26\,\%$ too large, because the moment of the stick's own weight, $1200\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}$, was left out.

Principle

The moment condition includes every force that has a lever arm about the chosen point. A systematic error shifts the result in one direction, a random error scatters the results in both directions (chapters Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments and Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

The conclusion of the report is wrong. The biggest shortcoming is in the calculation model: the knife edge was not at the centre of gravity of the stick, so the stick's own weight acted with the lever arm $10.0\,\text{cm}$ and turned the stick in the same direction as the stone. a plan for an improved experiment with the same equipment

Principle

A reliable result is obtained when the model includes all the forces, there are several measurements and the result is determined from a graph by fitting. A quantity that is kept constant is recorded (chapters Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments and Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting).

Model answer

The simplest improvement is to place the knife edge at the centre of gravity of the stick, at the $50.0\,\text{cm}$ mark, so that the lever arm of the stick's weight is zero. First check that the empty stick stays horizontal. The support and the string of the stone are kept the same throughout the experiment. The weight is hung in turn at, for example, five different distances from the edge, and each time the balance point of the stone is found and its distance $d_\text{s}$ from the edge is read. Each balance is repeated three times, and the mean of the results is calculated. From the moment condition $m_\text{p} d_\text{p} = m_\text{s} d_\text{s}$, so the graph of the moment of the weight $m_\text{p} d_\text{p}$ as a function of the lever arm of the stone $d_\text{s}$ is a straight line through the origin whose slope is the mass of the stone. If the edge is left somewhere other than the centre of gravity, the line gets a constant term, which is the moment of the stick's weight, and the slope is still the mass of the stone. a)

Given

Principle

Choose the bottom of the loop as the zero level. The mechanical energy of the car at the top of the first hill is $mgh$. Formula

Conversions

mass $m = 75\,\text{kg}$, horizontal distance of the centre of gravity from the axle $r_\text{g} = 0.40\,\text{m}$, horizontal distance of the handles from the axle $r_\text{h} = 1.25\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the vertical lifting force $F$

Principle

The barrow is at rest, so it is in equilibrium. The legs no longer touch the ground, so the forces on the barrow are the weight at the centre of gravity, the normal force of the wheel at the axle and the lifting force at the handles. The wheel axle is chosen as the pivot point, so the lever arm of the unknown normal force is zero. The forces are vertical, so the lever arms are the horizontal distances from the axle (chapter Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments).

Formula

$F\, r_\text{h} - mg\, r_\text{g} = 0$, so

$F$$= \dfrac{mg\, r_\text{g}}{r_\text{h}}$

Conversions

None needed: the mass is in kilograms and the distances in metres.

Substitution

$F$$= \dfrac{75\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.40\,\text{m}}{1.25\,\text{m}}$
$= \dfrac{294.3\,\text{Nm}}{1.25\,\text{m}}$
$= 235.4\,\text{N}$

Answer

$F \approx 240\,\text{N}$

Two significant figures like the mass $75\,\text{kg}$ and the distance $0.40\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

The weight is $736\,\text{N}$, and the lifting force is $\tfrac{0.40}{1.25} = 32\,\%$ of it, as the moment condition requires. The wheel carries the remaining $500\,\text{N}$. That is why it pays to load the barrow close to the wheel.

4. Explanation task

Two satellites orbit the Earth in circular orbits at the same height. The mass of one is $100\,\text{kg}$ and that of the other $3000\,\text{kg}$. Use Newton's second law and the law of gravitation to explain why their orbital speeds are equal, although a $30$ times larger gravitational force acts on the heavier one. Also say how the orbital speed changes if the satellite is moved further from the Earth.

Model answer

Choose the satellite as the body. Only the gravitational force of the Earth, $F = \gamma\tfrac{Mm}{r^2}$, acts on it, and it points to the centre of the Earth. So it is the net force, and in uniform circular motion Newton's second law gives $\gamma\tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. A $30$ times larger force acts on the heavier satellite, but turning it into a circular orbit also needs a $30$ times larger force, because the force needed, $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$, is proportional to the mass. The mass $m$ cancels from the equation, and the orbital speed is $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$, which depends only on the mass of the Earth and the radius of the orbit. If the satellite is moved further away, $r$ grows and the orbital speed decreases: for example, at four times the radius the orbital speed is half. The result holds when the satellite is much lighter than the Earth and the orbit is a circle.
