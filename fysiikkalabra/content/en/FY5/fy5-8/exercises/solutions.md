## fy5-8-L1-01
**Given:** spring constant $k = 250\,\text{N/m}$, extension $x = 0.12\,\text{m}$, handle at rest
**Find:** the magnitude $F$ of the spring force in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The force of the spring is a harmonic force: its magnitude is directly proportional to the extension, and it points towards the equilibrium position, here along the spring back towards the machine. For the magnitude the absolute value of the expression is enough.
**Formula:** $F = -kx$, so the magnitude of the force is $|F| = kx$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $|F| = 250\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot 0.12\,\text{m} = 30\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $30\,\text{N}$ towards the machine, two significant figures like the extension $0.12\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** $30\,\text{N}$ corresponds to the weight of about $3\,\text{kg}$, which is a light but noticeable resistance in a gym machine. The unit $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot\text{m} = \text{N}$ is the unit of force.

## fy5-8-L1-02
**Given:** stretching force $F_\text{v} = 450\,\text{N}$, extension $x = 0.050\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the spring constant $k$ in $\text{N/m}$
**Principle:** The force stretching a spring is directly proportional to the extension when the spring is not stretched too much. The constant of proportionality is the spring constant, which tells how many newtons are needed per metre of extension.
**Formula:** $F_\text{v} = kx$, so $k = \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{450\,\text{N}}{0.050\,\text{m}} = 9000\,\text{N/m}$
**Answer:** $9000\,\text{N/m}$ or $9.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{N/m}$, two significant figures like the extension $0.050\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** A trampoline spring must be stiff, because a jumper landing stretches many springs at the same time with forces of hundreds of newtons. A centimetre of extension needs $90\,\text{N}$, which feels like a stiff spring.

## fy5-8-L1-03
**Given:** spring constant $k = 180\,\text{N/m}$, extension $x = 0.15\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the elastic potential energy $E_\text{p}$ in $\text{J}$
**Principle:** When a spring is stretched, the stretching force does work, which is stored in the spring as potential energy. The force grows steadily from zero to $kx$, so the energy is the area of the triangle of the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph.
**Formula:** $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 180\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.15\,\text{m})^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 180\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot 0.0225\,\text{m}^2 = 2.025\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the extension $0.15\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** At the end of the extension the force is $180\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot 0.15\,\text{m} = 27\,\text{N}$. The area of the triangle gives the same result: $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 27\,\text{N}\cdot 0.15\,\text{m} \approx 2.0\,\text{J}$. The energy corresponds to lifting an object of about $2\,\text{kg}$ by $10\,\text{cm}$.

## fy5-8-L2-01
**Given:** extension $x = 8.0\,\text{cm}$, stretching force $F_\text{v} = 6.0\,\text{N}$ at the end of the extension
**Find:** the elastic potential energy $E_\text{p}$ in $\text{J}$
**Principle:** The reading of the force meter is the stretching force. From it and the extension we first get the spring constant. The elastic potential energy is the work of the stretching force, the area of the triangle of the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph.
**Formula:** $F_\text{v} = kx$, so $k = \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$, and $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$
**Conversions:** $x = 8.0\,\text{cm} = 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.080\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{6.0\,\text{N}}{0.080\,\text{m}} = 75\,\text{N/m}$ and $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 75\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.080\,\text{m})^2 = 0.24\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $0.24\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the force $6.0\,\text{N}$ and the extension $8.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The area of the triangle gives the same directly: $\tfrac{1}{2}F_\text{v}x = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 6.0\,\text{N}\cdot 0.080\,\text{m} = 0.24\,\text{J}$. Without the half the result would be $0.48\,\text{J}$, but the force was not $6.0\,\text{N}$ the whole way; it grew from zero.

## fy5-8-L2-02
**Given:** compression $x = 6.0\,\text{cm}$, elastic potential energy $E_\text{p} = 0.90\,\text{J}$
**Find:** the spring constant $k$ in $\text{N/m}$
**Principle:** The elastic potential energy of a compressed spring depends on the square of the compression in the same way as that of a stretched spring on the extension. When the energy and the compression are known, the spring constant is solved from the expression for the energy.
**Formula:** $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$, so $k = \dfrac{2E_\text{p}}{x^2}$
**Conversions:** $x = 6.0\,\text{cm} = 6.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.060\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{2\cdot 0.90\,\text{J}}{(0.060\,\text{m})^2} = \dfrac{1.80\,\text{J}}{0.0036\,\text{m}^2} = 500\,\text{N/m}$
**Answer:** $500\,\text{N/m}$ or $5.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{N/m}$, two significant figures like the energy and the compression.
**Reasonableness:** Check: $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 500\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.060\,\text{m})^2 = 0.90\,\text{J}$. A fully compressed spring pushes with a force of $500\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot 0.060\,\text{m} = 30\,\text{N}$, which suits a toy gun that a child can cock. The unit $\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{m}^2} = \tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{m}^2} = \text{N/m}$.

## fy5-8-L2-03
**Given:** spring constant $k = 150\,\text{N/m}$, initial extension $x_1 = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, final extension $x_2 = 9.0\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** the increase $\Delta E_\text{p}$ in the elastic potential energy in $\text{J}$
**Principle:** The elastic potential energy depends on the extension from the rest length, not on the change in the extension. The increase is the difference between the energies of the final and initial states. In the graph it is the difference between the areas of two triangles.
**Formula:** $\Delta E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx_2^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}kx_1^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}k\left(x_2^2 - x_1^2\right)$
**Conversions:** $x_1 = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 0.030\,\text{m}$ and $x_2 = 9.0\,\text{cm} = 0.090\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $\Delta E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 150\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot\left((0.090\,\text{m})^2 - (0.030\,\text{m})^2\right) = 75\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot 0.0072\,\text{m}^2 = 0.54\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $0.54\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the extensions.
**Reasonableness:** At the start the energy is $0.0675\,\text{J}$ and at the end $0.6075\,\text{J}$, nine times as much, because the extension became three times as large. If $\tfrac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$ had been calculated, the result would be only $0.27\,\text{J}$, which is too little: the last centimetres are stretched with an already large force.

## fy5-8-L3-01
**Given:** mass of the passengers $\Delta m = 300\,\text{kg}$, extra compression of the springs $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, four identical springs; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the spring constant $k$ of one spring in $\text{kN/m}$
**Principle:** The springs are already compressed by the weight of the car itself, but for a harmonic force the force is directly proportional to the compression, so the change in the force is proportional to the change in the compression: $F_2 - F_1 = k(x_2 - x_1) = k\,\Delta x$. The car is at rest before and after, so the increase in the forces of the springs cancels the weight of the passengers. The load is shared equally, so one spring carries a quarter of it.
**Formula:** $4k\,\Delta x = \Delta m\, g$, so $k = \dfrac{\Delta m\, g}{4\,\Delta x}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{kN/m} = 1000\,\text{N/m}$
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{300\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{4\cdot 0.030\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{2943\,\text{N}}{0.12\,\text{m}} = 24\,525\,\text{N/m} = 24.525\,\text{kN/m}$
**Answer:** $25\,\text{kN/m}$, two significant figures like the compression $3.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** A spring constant of tens of kilonewtons per metre is typical for a car's suspension. If the whole $1200\,\text{kg}$ car loaded the same springs, they would be compressed about $12\,\text{cm}$, which is a believable suspension travel. The mass of the car itself was not needed, because only the changes in the force and the compression are involved.

## fy5-8-L3-02
**Given:** spring constant $k = 200\,\text{N/m}$, compression $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, mass of the toy $m = 50\,\text{g}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the height of rise $h$ from the starting point in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** Air resistance and the mass of the spring are neglected, so mechanical energy is conserved. At the starting point the toy is at rest, and the energy is the elastic potential energy of the compressed spring. At the highest point the toy is momentarily at rest, so all the energy is gravitational potential energy, taking the zero level at the starting point.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = mgh$, so $h = \dfrac{kx^2}{2mg}$
**Conversions:** $x = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 0.030\,\text{m}$ and $m = 50\,\text{g} = 50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.050\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $h = \dfrac{200\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.030\,\text{m})^2}{2\cdot 0.050\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{0.18\,\text{J}}{0.981\,\text{N}} = 0.1835\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $0.18\,\text{m}$ or about $18\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the compression $3.0\,\text{cm}$ and the mass $50\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of the spring is $0.090\,\text{J}$, and lifting a $50\,\text{g}$ toy $18\,\text{cm}$ needs $0.050\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 0.1835\,\text{m} \approx 0.090\,\text{J}$. A jump of a few tens of centimetres is typical for such a toy; in reality some of the energy goes into the oscillation of the spring and the toy and into air resistance, so the toy rises a little less.

## fy5-8-L1-06
**Find:** when the proportionality $F_\text{v} = kx$ describes a spring well.
**Principle:** The harmonic force is a model that holds when the spring is not stretched too much. Then the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph is a line through the origin, and its slope is the spring constant. At too large an extension the material of the spring begins to stretch in a different way or stays permanently deformed, and the graph bends away from the line.
**Model answer:** The expression describes a spring well only when the spring is not stretched too far, because otherwise the force no longer grows steadily with the extension. The position of the spring does not matter: the spring constant of a vertical spring is measured exactly by using the weight of a mass. The expression holds equally well for a stretched and a compressed spring, and the spring constant is constant only in the working range of the spring.

## fy5-8-L1-07
**Find:** how the elastic potential energy of a spring changes when the extension is doubled.
**Principle:** The elastic potential energy of a spring is the area of the triangle of the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph, $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. The extension is raised to the second power in the formula, so the energy is proportional to the square of the extension.
**Model answer:** The energy becomes four times as large, because it is proportional to the second power of the extension: $\tfrac{1}{2}k(2x)^2 = 4\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. In the graph the base and the height of the triangle both double, so its area becomes four times as large. The force doubles, but the stretching is also done over a distance twice as long, and so the energy grows more than the force. It does not stay the same, because the formula depends on the extension, and it does not grow only by the square root, because the extension is raised to the power two and not one half.

## fy5-8-L2-07

## fy5-8-L3-04
**Given:** extensions $x$ and forces $F_\text{v}$ from the table, start of the measurement $(0;\ 0)$, step $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 0.030\,\text{m}$
**Find:** a) whether $\tfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$ is constant, b) the work $W$ done by the stretching force over the extension $12\,\text{cm}$ in $\text{J}$, c) a comparison with the formula $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$
**Principle:** According to the harmonic force $F_\text{v} = kx$ the quotient $\tfrac{F_\text{v}}{x} = k$ would be constant. The work done by the force stretching the band is the area between the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph and the $x$ axis, and it can be calculated also when the graph is not a line. A rubber band does not return all of the work (hysteresis), so the area is an upper limit for the recoverable energy of the band. The area is divided into trapezoids.
**Reading the graph:** The quotients $\tfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$ at the extensions $3.0$, $6.0$, $9.0$ and $12.0\,\text{cm}$ are $50\,\text{N/m}$, $37\,\text{N/m}$, $31\,\text{N/m}$ and $33\,\text{N/m}$. They are not constant: the largest and the smallest differ by more than $40\,\%$, which is much more than measurement uncertainty would explain. A rubber band does not follow the harmonic force, and it has no single spring constant.
**Formula:** $W_i = \Delta x\cdot\dfrac{F_{i} + F_{i+1}}{2}$ and $W = \sum W_i$
**Conversions:** $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $W_1 = 0.030\,\text{m}\cdot\tfrac{0 + 1.5}{2}\,\text{N} = 0.0225\,\text{J}$, and all the trapezoids are
```latex
\begin{aligned}
W_2 &= 0.030\,\text{m}\cdot\tfrac{1.5 + 2.2}{2}\,\text{N} = 0.0555\,\text{J} \\
W_3 &= 0.030\,\text{m}\cdot\tfrac{2.2 + 2.8}{2}\,\text{N} = 0.0750\,\text{J} \\
W_4 &= 0.030\,\text{m}\cdot\tfrac{2.8 + 4.0}{2}\,\text{N} = 0.1020\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
```
The sum is
```latex
\begin{aligned}
W &= (0.0225 + 0.0555 \\
&\quad + 0.0750 + 0.1020)\,\text{J} \\
&= 0.255\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
```
In part c) the spring constant of the first point is $k = 50\,\text{N/m}$, and the formula gives
```latex
\begin{aligned}
E_\text{p} &= \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 50\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.12\,\text{m})^2 \\
```

about half of the final force $4.0\,\text{N}$, as the area under the graph should be.

## fy5-8-L3-05
**Given:** draw distance $x$ and pull force $F_\text{v}$ from the table (last point $0.50\,\text{m}$, $151\,\text{N}$), mass of the arrow $m = 25\,\text{g}$, launch speed $v = 47.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** a) the spring constant $k$ and the stored energy $E_\text{p}$, b) the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ of the arrow and c) the efficiency $\eta = \tfrac{E_\text{k}}{E_\text{p}}$ as a percentage
**Principle:** A crossbow follows the harmonic force over the measured range, so its spring constant is the slope of the fitted line of the $(x, F_\text{v})$ graph and the stored energy is the area of the triangle $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. The arrow starts from rest, so its kinetic energy at launch is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. If energy were conserved, it should equal the stored energy; the difference is a loss.
**Reading the graph:** The points are close to a line through the origin that passes through the point $(0.50\,\text{m};\ 151\,\text{N})$. The quotients $\tfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$ are $295$, $307$, $303$ and $302\,\text{N/m}$, so they are approximately constant and the harmonic force is acceptable. The slope is read from two points of the fitted line: the origin and the point $(0.50\,\text{m};\ 151\,\text{N})$.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$, $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $\eta = \dfrac{E_\text{k}}{E_\text{p}}$
**Conversions:** $x = 50\,\text{cm} = 50 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.50\,\text{m}$ and $m = 25\,\text{g} = 25 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.025\,\text{kg}$

```latex
\begin{aligned}
E_\text{p} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 302\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\cdot(0.50\,\text{m})^2 \\
&= 37.75\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
```
The kinetic energy of the arrow and the efficiency are
```latex
\begin{aligned}
E_\text{k} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 0.025\,\text{kg}\cdot(47.0\,\text{m/s})^2 \\
&= 27.61\,\text{J} \\
\eta &= \frac{27.61\,\text{J}}{37.75\,\text{J}} = 0.731
\end{aligned}
```
**Answer:** $73\,\%$ of the stored energy changed into the kinetic energy of the arrow, two significant figures like the mass of the arrow $25\,\text{g}$. The rest, about $27\,\%$ or about $10\,\text{J}$, probably went into the kinetic energy of the spring and the string, vibration, sound and heat: the spring and the string move themselves and finally stop. The answer is based on the assumption that the graph is a line over the whole draw distance.
**Reasonableness:** The efficiency is below $100\,\%$, as it must be, and an efficiency above $70\,\%$ is usual for real crossbows. The speed is checked the other way round: if all of the $37.75\,\text{J}$ went to the arrow, its speed would be

a little more than the measured $47.0\,\text{m/s}$.

## fy5-8-L2-08
**Find:** which conclusion about the spring constant of the spring is justified, based on the example data of the table and the reading uncertainty $\pm 0.05\,\text{N}$ of the force meter.
**Principle:** The stretching force is $F_\text{v} = kx$, so the points $(x, F_\text{v})$ must lie on a line through the origin whose slope is the spring constant $k$. For each row we can calculate the ratio $\tfrac{F_\text{v}}{x}$ and its uncertainty $\tfrac{0.05\,\text{N}}{x}$. If one value of $k$ fits into the uncertainty intervals of all the rows, the differences between the rows are explained by the measurement uncertainty.
**Model answer:** The extensions are converted to metres, for example $x = 4.0\,\text{cm} = 0.040\,\text{m}$. The ratio of the row $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the uncertainty of the ratio are

$$\begin{aligned}
&\frac{F_\text{v}}{x} = \frac{1.02\,\text{N}}{0.040\,\text{m}} \approx 25.5\,\text{N/m} \\
&\Delta\!\left(\frac{F_\text{v}}{x}\right) = \frac{0.05\,\text{N}}{0.040\,\text{m}} \approx 1.3\,\text{N/m}
\end{aligned}$$

In the same way the ratios of the other rows, as the extension increases, are $24.5\,\text{N/m}$ ($\pm 2.5\,\text{N/m}$), $24.5\,\text{N/m}$ ($\pm 0.8\,\text{N/m}$), $25.0\,\text{N/m}$ ($\pm 0.6\,\text{N/m}$) and $25.1\,\text{N/m}$ ($\pm 0.5\,\text{N/m}$). The value $25\,\text{N/m}$ is within the uncertainty interval of every row, so within the measurement accuracy the points lie on the same line through the origin. From the points $(0;\ 0)$ and $(0.100\,\text{m};\ 2.50\,\text{N})$ of the fitted line we get

$k = \dfrac{2.50\,\text{N}}{0.100\,\text{m}}$

$k = 25\,\text{N/m}$

The justified conclusion is therefore that the spring constant is about $25\,\text{N/m}$, because the ratio of every row differs from it by less than the measurement uncertainty allows. The spring constant does not increase with the extension: the force grows clearly more than the reading uncertainty, but the ratio stays the same. At the extension $4.0\,\text{cm}$ the ratio is the largest, but its difference $0.5\,\text{N/m}$ is compared with the uncertainty of the ratio $1.3\,\text{N/m}$, not with the uncertainty of the force $0.05\,\text{N}$; the difference fits within the uncertainty, so the data does not show that the spring is stiffer there. The value $0.25$ is the slope in the unit $\text{N/cm}$, not $\text{N/m}$.
