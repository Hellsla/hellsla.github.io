## fy6-12-L1-01
**Given:** charge of the proton $q = e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, voltage between the plates $U = 240\,\text{V}$
**Find:** the work $W$ done by the electric field on the proton in joules
**Principle:** When a charge moves through a potential difference $U$, the electric field does the work $qU$. The proton is positive and travels from the positive plate to the negative one, that is, toward a lower potential, so the work is positive.
**Formula:** $W = qU$, where $q = e$, so $W = eU$
**Conversions:** Not needed: $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $W = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 240\,\text{V} = 3.845 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $3.84 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$, three significant figures like the voltage $240\,\text{V}$.
**Reasonableness:** In electronvolts the work is $240\,\text{eV}$, and $240 \cdot 1.6 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$. The energy of one particle is a very small number of joules, as it should be.

## fy6-12-L1-02
**Given:** charge $q = +3.0\,\text{nC}$, potential of the point $V = 120\,\text{V}$
**Find:** the potential energy $E_\text{p}$ in microjoules
**Principle:** Potential is potential energy per unit charge, so the potential energy of a charge is the charge times the potential of the point. The charge is positive and the potential is positive, so the potential energy is positive.
**Formula:** $E_\text{p} = qV$
**Conversions:** $q = 3.0\,\text{nC} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$ and finally $1\,\text{J} = 10^{6}\,\mu\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $E_\text{p} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 120\,\text{V} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{J} = 0.36\,\mu\text{J}$
**Answer:** $0.36\,\mu\text{J}$, two significant figures like the charge $3.0\,\text{nC}$.
**Reasonableness:** The estimate $3 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 100\,\text{V} = 3 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{J}$ is close to the result. A double charge would give a double potential energy at the same point, as can be seen from the formula.

## fy6-12-L1-03
**Given:** kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = 5.0\,\text{keV}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the kinetic energy in joules
**Principle:** An electronvolt is the energy that a charge of the magnitude of the elementary charge gets when it travels through a potential difference of $1\,\text{V}$, that is, $1\,\text{eV} = e \cdot 1\,\text{V}$. The energy in joules is obtained by multiplying the number of electronvolts by this.
**Formula:** $E_\text{k} = N \cdot 1\,\text{eV}$, where $N$ is the number of electronvolts and $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Conversions:** $5.0\,\text{keV} = 5.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{eV} = 5000\,\text{eV}$
**Substitution:** $E_\text{k} = 5000 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 8.01 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $8.0 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the energy $5.0\,\text{keV}$.
**Reasonableness:** A thousand electronvolts is about $1.6 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$, and five times that is $8 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$. The number of joules is small, because it is the energy of a single electron.

## fy6-12-L1-04
**Given:** $q = 4.0\,\mu\text{C}$, $V_A = 60\,\text{V}$, $V_B = 15\,\text{V}$
**Find:** the work $W_{AB}$ done by the electric field in millijoules
**Principle:** The electric force is conservative, so its work depends only on the potentials of the initial and final points: the work is the charge times the potential difference. A positive charge moves to a lower potential, so the work is positive.
**Formula:** $W_{AB} = q(V_A - V_B)$
**Conversions:** $q = 4.0\,\mu\text{C} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{C}$ and finally $1\,\text{J} = 1000\,\text{mJ}$.
**Substitution:** $W_{AB} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{C} \cdot (60\,\text{V} - 15\,\text{V}) = 1.8 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{J} = 0.18\,\text{mJ}$
**Answer:** $0.18\,\text{mJ}$, two significant figures like the charge $4.0\,\mu\text{C}$.
**Reasonableness:** The potential difference is $45\,\text{V}$, and $4 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{C} \cdot 50\,\text{V} = 2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{J}$ is close to the result. The work is positive, because the potential energy of the charge decreases when it moves to a lower potential.

## fy6-12-L1-05
**Given:** the potential $V$ between the plates from the graph; the proton moves from $x = 4.0\,\text{cm}$ to $x = 1.0\,\text{cm}$; only the electric force does work
**Find:** the increase of the kinetic energy $\Delta E_\text{k}$ in electronvolts
**Principle:** When only the electric force does work, the kinetic energy increases by as much as the potential energy decreases. The charge of the proton is $e$, so every volt by which the potential falls gives it one electronvolt of kinetic energy.
**Reading the graph:** At $x = 4.0\,\text{cm}$ the line is on the grid line of $200\,\text{V}$ and at $x = 1.0\,\text{cm}$ on the grid line of $50\,\text{V}$. So the potential falls in the displacement.
**Formula:** $\Delta E_\text{k} = -\Delta E_\text{p} = e(V_1 - V_2)$, where $V_1 = V(4.0\,\text{cm})$ and $V_2 = V(1.0\,\text{cm})$
**Conversions:** Not needed: when the charge is $e$ and the potential difference is in volts, the energy is obtained directly in electronvolts.
**Substitution:** $\Delta E_\text{k} = e \cdot (200\,\text{V} - 50\,\text{V}) = 150\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $150\,\text{eV}$, two significant figures, because the potentials are read from the $50\,\text{V}$ grid lines and the positions are given to two significant figures.
**Reasonableness:** The slope of the graph is $50\,\text{V}$ per centimetre, and over a distance of $3.0\,\text{cm}$ the potential changes by $150\,\text{V}$. The proton moves toward the negative plate, that is, in the direction of the field, so its kinetic energy increases, as it should for a positive charge.

## fy6-12-L2-01
**Given:** field strength $E = 2.5\,\text{kV/m}$, displacement in the direction of the field $s = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, charge of the proton $e$
**Find:** the change of the potential energy $\Delta E_\text{p}$ in electronvolts with its sign
**Principle:** In a uniform field the potential changes steadily, and in the direction of the field it decreases by $Es$. The potential energy of the proton is $eV$, so it decreases by as many electronvolts as the potential decreases in volts.
**Formula:** $\Delta V = -Es$ and $\Delta E_\text{p} = e\Delta V$, so $\Delta E_\text{p} = -eEs$
**Conversions:** $E = 2.5\,\text{kV/m} = 2.5 \cdot 1000\,\text{V/m} = 2500\,\text{V/m}$ and $s = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $\Delta V = -2500\,\text{V/m} \cdot 0.030\,\text{m} = -75\,\text{V}$ and $\Delta E_\text{p} = e \cdot (-75\,\text{V}) = -75\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $-75\,\text{eV}$, two significant figures like the field strength and the distance.
**Reasonableness:** A positive charge moves by itself in the direction of the field, and then its potential energy decreases, so the sign is right. In joules the change is $-75 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \approx -1.2 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$, which corresponds to the work $eEs$ done by the field.

## fy6-12-L2-02
**Given:** plate separation $d = 2.0\,\text{cm}$, voltage $U = 300\,\text{V}$, distance of point $P$ from the negative plate $x = 0.80\,\text{cm}$; the negative plate is the zero point; charge of the electron $-e$
**Find:** the potential energy $E_\text{p}$ of the electron at point $P$ in electronvolts
**Principle:** In a uniform field the potential grows steadily from the negative plate to the positive one, $V = Ex$, where $E = U : d$. The charge of the electron is negative, so its potential energy $E_\text{p} = -eV$ is negative at a point whose potential is positive.
**Formula:** $V_P = Ex = \dfrac{U}{d}\,x$ and $E_\text{p} = -eV_P$, so $E_\text{p} = -e\,\dfrac{Ux}{d}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the centimetres cancel in the ratio $x : d$, and with the charge $e$ the potential in volts gives the energy in electronvolts.
**Substitution:** $V_P = 300\,\text{V} \cdot \dfrac{0.80\,\text{cm}}{2.0\,\text{cm}} = 120\,\text{V}$ and $E_\text{p} = -e \cdot 120\,\text{V} = -120\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $-120\,\text{eV}$ or $-1.2 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{eV}$, two significant figures like the distances.
**Reasonableness:** Point $P$ is $40\,\%$ of the way from the negative plate, so its potential is $40\,\%$ of the voltage $300\,\text{V}$. The electron tends toward the positive plate, where its potential energy would be even lower, $-300\,\text{eV}$.

## fy6-12-L2-03
**Given:** charge $q = 2e$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, mass $m = 6.645 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, potential difference $U = 2.0\,\text{kV}$, initial speed zero
**Find:** the speed $v$ in metres per second
**Principle:** Only the electric force does work, so potential energy changes into kinetic energy: the field does the work $qU$, and a particle that starts from rest gets a kinetic energy of the same size. The charge of the alpha particle is double, so it gets double the energy compared with a proton.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = qU = 2eU$, from which $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{4eU}{m}}$
**Conversions:** $U = 2.0\,\text{kV} = 2.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{V} = 2000\,\text{V}$
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{4 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 2000\,\text{V}}{6.645 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}} = 4.392 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $4.4 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the voltage $2.0\,\text{kV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The kinetic energy is $4000\,\text{eV} \approx 6.4 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$, and $v^2 \approx 2 \cdot 6 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J} : (7 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}) \approx 2 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so $v \approx 4 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$. The speed is much smaller than the speed of light, so the expression $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ is suitable.

## fy6-12-L2-04
**Given:** final speed $v = 1.0 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s}$, initial speed zero, $m = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the accelerating voltage $U$ in volts
**Principle:** Only the electric force does work, so the kinetic energy gained by the electron equals the work $eU$ done by the field. When the kinetic energy is known from the speed, the voltage is obtained by solving the energy equation.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = eU$, from which $U = \dfrac{mv^2}{2e}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: $1\,\text{kg} \cdot 1\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = 1\,\text{J}$ and $1\,\text{J} : 1\,\text{C} = 1\,\text{V}$.
**Substitution:** $mv^2 = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg} \cdot (1.0 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s})^2 = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$ and $U = \dfrac{9.109 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}}{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 284.3\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $2.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{V}$ or about $280\,\text{V}$, two significant figures like the speed.
**Reasonableness:** The kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9.1 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg} \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \approx 4.6 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J} \approx 280\,\text{eV}$, so about $280\,\text{V}$ is needed. Even a voltage of a few hundred volts gives a light electron a great speed.

## fy6-12-L3-01
**Given:** $v_A = 2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$, $V_A = 40\,\text{V}$, $V_B = 160\,\text{V}$, $m = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, charge of the electron $q = -e$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the speed $v_B$ in metres per second
**Principle:** Only the electric force does work, so the sum of the kinetic energy and the potential energy is conserved. The potential energy of the electron is $-eV$, so it decreases when the electron moves to a higher potential, and the kinetic energy increases by the same amount.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_A^2 - eV_A = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_B^2 - eV_B$, from which $E_{\text{k},B} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_A^2 + e(V_B - V_A)$ and $v_B = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_{\text{k},B}}{m}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: all quantities are in base units.
**Substitution:** $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_A^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg} \cdot (2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s})^2 = 1.822 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J}$, $e(V_B - V_A) = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 120\,\text{V} = 1.922 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$, $E_{\text{k},B} = 1.822 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{J} + 1.922 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J} = 2.105 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$ and $v_B = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 2.105 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}}{9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}}} = 6.80 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $6.8 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the initial speed and the potentials.
**Reasonableness:** At point $A$ the kinetic energy of the electron is only about $11\,\text{eV}$, and it gains another $120\,\text{eV}$, so the kinetic energy grows to about $12$ times and the speed to about $\sqrt{12} \approx 3.5$ times: $3.5 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s} = 7 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$. The electron speeds up, because a negative charge accelerates toward a higher potential.

## fy6-12-L3-02
**Given:** mass of the electron $m_\text{e} = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, mass of the proton $m_\text{p} = 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$; each starts from rest and travels through the same potential difference $U$, and the magnitude of each charge is $e$
**Find:** the ratio of the speeds of the electron and the proton $v_\text{e} : v_\text{p}$ when they travel from rest through the same potential difference $U$
**Principle:** The magnitude of each charge is $e$, so the field does the same work $eU$ on each and they get the same kinetic energy, although they move in opposite directions. The same kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ gives a smaller mass a greater speed.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2}m_\text{e}v_\text{e}^2 = eU = \tfrac{1}{2}m_\text{p}v_\text{p}^2$, so $\dfrac{v_\text{e}}{v_\text{p}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{m_\text{p}}{m_\text{e}}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the units of the masses cancel, and the voltage is not needed.
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{v_\text{e}}{v_\text{p}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}{9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{1837} = 42.86$
**Answer:** The speed of the electron is about $43$ times as great; two significant figures are enough for a ratio, and the masses are given to four figures.
**Reasonableness:** The mass of the proton is about $1800$ times as great, and $\sqrt{1800} \approx 42$. The result does not depend on the voltage, as long as the speeds stay much smaller than the speed of light. So the same energy gives a speed that is inversely proportional to the square root of the mass.

## fy6-12-L3-03
**Given:** plate separation $d = 2.0\,\text{cm}$, voltage $U = 600\,\text{V}$, initial speed $v_0 = 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$, $m = 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the distance of the turning point from the positive plate in millimetres
**Principle:** The proton moves toward a higher potential, so its potential energy increases and its kinetic energy decreases by the same amount. It stops at the point where the whole initial kinetic energy has changed into potential energy. In a uniform field the potential grows steadily, $V = Ex = \dfrac{U}{d}\,x$ measured from the negative plate.
**Formula:** $E_{\text{k},0} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2$ and $E_{\text{k},0} = eV = e\,\dfrac{U}{d}\,x$, from which $x = \dfrac{E_{\text{k},0}\,d}{eU}$; the distance from the positive plate is $d - x$
**Conversions:** $d = 2.0\,\text{cm} = 0.020\,\text{m}$ and finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $E_{\text{k},0} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg} \cdot (3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s})^2 = 7.529 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$, $x = \dfrac{7.529 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J} \cdot 0.020\,\text{m}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 600\,\text{V}} = 0.01566\,\text{m}$ and $d - x = 0.020\,\text{m} - 0.01566\,\text{m} = 0.00434\,\text{m} = 4.34\,\text{mm}$
**Answer:** $4.3\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures like the initial data.
**Reasonableness:** At the start the kinetic energy is about $470\,\text{eV}$, so the proton can climb $470\,\text{V}$ of potential. That is less than $600\,\text{V}$, so the proton turns back before the positive plate: $470 : 600 \approx 0.78$ of the distance, or about $16\,\text{mm}$ from the negative plate and about $4\,\text{mm}$ from the positive one.
