Fusion history: an interactive timeline

Why has the road from the first idea of fusion energy to a fusion power plant taken more than a hundred years?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explore a historical timeline by era, theme and search and collect evidence from it.
  • identify patterns in fusion history, such as secrecy and openness, claims and checks, and slow steps.
  • evaluate how the Cold War slowed and pushed fusion research, using events with their years.

Prior knowledge

  • Secrecy, Huemul, ZETA and the Geneva conference of 1958chapter Secret beginnings: fusion research to the 1950s
  • The T-3 results and the Culham measurement of 1969chapter Science across the Iron Curtain
  • The big machines and the Geneva summit of 1985chapter Big machines and world politics: 1970s to 1985

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 1.

1Think about it

In 1920 Arthur Eddington told an audience in Cardiff that the stars shine by turning hydrogen into helium, and that people might one day control this energy. In this chapter you have followed the story through secret laboratories, false claims, a measurement in Moscow and a summit in Geneva. Now you will see the whole road at once, from the first ideas of modern physics to the machines being built today.

What patterns appear when you look at a hundred years of fusion history at once?

Think about it: what do you remember?

Without looking back, write down three events from fusion history that you remember from this chapter, each with its year (a guess is fine) and one word about why it mattered. Then compare with a partner: did you choose the same events?

    3Investigate

    How to use the timeline

    The timeline below tells the whole story of this course in about a hundred events, from the discovery of X-rays in 1895 to the construction of ITER today. Every event is a card. A closed card shows its year, place and title; open it to read a short text, the people involved and the sources it was checked against.

    • Eras. The buttons above the timeline choose one of four eras: The birth of modern physics (1895–1938), The nuclear age and secrecy (1938–1958), The tokamak race (1958–1985) and From Geneva to ITER (1985–2026). All eras shows everything.
    • Themes. Each event has one theme, shown by a colour and a shape on the year strip: scientific discovery (circle), machines and experiments (square), politics and society (diamond) and people (triangle). The Theme menu shows one theme only.
    • Search. The search box finds a word in the titles, texts, names, places and countries, for example Culham, Sakharov or Geneva. The line under the year strip says how many events are shown.
    • People and the map. Some cards have a short biography behind Who was …?. When an event is about a machine, Show on the map opens it in the unit Global Fusion Explorer, the world map of fusion facilities.

    The events were checked on 1 November 2026. Facts that change, such as ITER's schedule, carry their date. The timeline is a selection, not a complete list of fusion history.

    Model answer

    Huemul (1951): Perón announced that Richter had produced controlled fusion; Balseiro's team inspected the laboratory in 1952 and found no nuclear reactions. ZETA (1958): British scientists suggested that its neutrons came from fusion; Basil Rose's measurements showed that the neutrons flew mostly in one direction, so they came from accelerated ions, and the claim was withdrawn. Cold fusion (1989): two chemists announced fusion at room temperature; laboratories around the world could not repeat it. T-3 (1968): Soviet scientists reported a temperature of about ten million degrees; the British Culham team measured it with a laser in 1969 and confirmed it. The first three claims were made public before others could check them and failed the check, while T-3 was doubted but passed an independent check.

          4Theory

          What the timeline shows

          From open science to secrecy, and back. Until 1938 fusion was open science about the stars. From the discovery of fission to 1958, the nuclear age, politics is the most common theme: bombs, secret programmes and false claims. In 1958 the files were opened at Geneva, and from 1985 the story becomes one of cooperation, which led to ITER.

          A wider world. In the 1950s almost all events take place in the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union, with Argentina's false claim and the conference in Switzerland. Since 2015 the events come from seven countries in Europe, North America and Asia (China, France, Germany, Japan, South Korea, the United Kingdom and the United States). Fusion research has spread from a few rival powers to a worldwide network. (The map unit shows many more facilities than the timeline.)

          Claims and checks. The Huemul claim (1951), ZETA's neutrons (1958) and cold fusion (1989) were announced before others could check them, and all failed when others checked them. Only ZETA's team withdrew its claim; Huemul was exposed by an inspection, and other laboratories could not repeat cold fusion. The T-3 result (1968) went the other way: it was doubted, then confirmed by an independent measurement in 1969. In every case, checking by others decided.

          Slow steps. The first tokamak, T-1, started in 1958. Almost forty years later, in 1997, JET produced $16\ \text{MW}$ of fusion power, still with less fusion power out than heating power in. Each step needed a bigger and more expensive machine, so progress came in decades, not years. From Eddington's idea in 1920 to ITER's planned deuterium–tritium operation in 2039 (the plan as of November 2026) is about 120 years.

          Common misconception

          "History is a straight line of progress." The timeline shows dead ends (Huemul, ZETA's claim, cold fusion), designs that were put aside and came back (the stellarator, set aside after 1969 and revived in Wendelstein 7-X), and long pauses while big machines were built. A second mistake is to treat a planned date as an event: ITER's dates after 2026 are plans of the ITER Organization, not results.

            A student writes in a project: "According to the timeline, ITER will produce fusion energy with deuterium and tritium in 2039." What is the problem with this sentence?

            • The timeline has many ITER events, including the 2024 schedule. The problem is that the sentence turns a plan into a fact.

            • Right. 2039 is the start of deuterium–tritium operation in ITER's 2024 baseline, a plan of the ITER Organization. A careful sentence says: "As of November 2026, ITER plans to start deuterium–tritium operation in 2039."

            4. Explanation task: The chapter's big question

            Answer the big question of this chapter in five or six sentences: How did the Cold War influence scientific research? Use at least three events from the timeline, with their years, and say both how the Cold War slowed or distorted fusion research and how it pushed it forward.

            Sentence starters

            • At first, the Cold War …
            • For example, in …
            • On the other hand, rivalry also …
            • So, the Cold War both … and …

            Model answer

            The Cold War first made fusion research secret. Around 1950–1951 Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States all started classified programmes, because fusion was linked to the hydrogen bomb and to neutrons for bomb fuel, so scientists could not share or check results. At the same time, rivalry brought money: Perón's false Huemul claim in 1951 helped persuade the United States to fund Spitzer's stellarator. When the plasma problems turned out to be hard for everyone, the files were opened at Geneva in 1958. In 1969 British scientists crossed the Iron Curtain to verify the Soviet T-3 result, which shows that evidence could matter more than politics. Finally, in 1985 Reagan and Gorbachev called for cooperation on fusion, which led to ITER. So the Cold War both slowed fusion with secrecy and pushed it forward with funding and, in the end, with cooperation.

              Summary

              • The timeline has four eras: modern physics (to 1938), the nuclear age and secrecy (1938–1958), the tokamak race (1958–1985) and cooperation from Geneva to ITER (1985–2026).
              • Politics dominates the secret years; machines dominate the years of cooperation.
              • Fusion research spread from three rival powers in the 1950s to a worldwide network.
              • Claims that others could not check (Huemul, ZETA, cold fusion) failed; a doubted result that was independently checked (T-3) changed the field.
              • Progress came in steps of decades, because each step needed a bigger machine; as of November 2026, ITER plans deuterium–tritium operation in 2039.
