Mass defect and nuclear binding energy

Why is a helium-4 atom lighter than two hydrogen atoms and two neutrons put together, even though it consists of exactly those particles?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the mass defect and the binding energy of a nucleus from atomic masses.
  • explain the shape of the binding energy curve and why fusion and fission release energy.
  • calculate the reaction energy of a nuclear reaction from the masses of the starting materials and the particles formed.

Prior knowledge

  • A nucleus consists of protons and neutrons, and in a decay equation the mass number and the charge number are conservedchapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay
  • Energy can be given in electronvolts: $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Atomic mass unit

    The masses of atoms and nuclei are given in atomic mass units. The atomic mass unit $\text{u}$ is defined so that the mass of a carbon-12 atom is exactly $12\,\text{u}$, which gives $1\,\text{u} = 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$. The masses of the proton and the neutron are each about $1\,\text{u}$, so the mass of an atom in atomic mass units is close to its mass number $A$ but not exactly equal to it. The values in the table are masses of atoms: they include the electrons too. The mass of a hydrogen atom ${}^{1}\text{H}$ is the mass of a proton plus the mass of an electron.

    Particle Symbol Mass ($\text{u}$)
    hydrogen-1 ${}^{1}\text{H}$ $1.007825$
    neutron $\text{n}$ $1.008665$
    hydrogen-2 ${}^{2}\text{H}$ $2.014102$
    helium-4 ${}^{4}\text{He}$ $4.002603$
    carbon-12 ${}^{12}\text{C}$ $12.000000$
    oxygen-16 ${}^{16}\text{O}$ $15.994915$
    iron-56 ${}^{56}\text{Fe}$ $55.934936$
    uranium-238 ${}^{238}\text{U}$ $238.050787$

    Source: the atomic mass table AME2020 (Atomic Mass Evaluation 2020, IAEA Atomic Mass Data Center), rounded to six decimals. This many decimals are needed for nuclear masses because the important differences are small.

    Mass defect

    A helium-4 atom contains two protons, two neutrons and two electrons. The same particles are in two hydrogen atoms and two neutrons, whose total mass is $2 \cdot 1.007825\,\text{u} + 2 \cdot 1.008665\,\text{u} = 4.032980\,\text{u}$. The mass of the helium-4 atom, however, is only $4.002603\,\text{u}$. When the nucleons are bound into a nucleus, their mass is smaller than when they are apart. The difference $4.032980\,\text{u} - 4.002603\,\text{u} = 0.030377\,\text{u}$ is the mass defect $\Delta m$ of the nucleus, about $0.76\,\%$ of the mass of the atom. Hydrogen atoms are used instead of separate protons so that the masses of the electrons cancel: there are two electrons on both sides.

    Mass and energy

    The mass defect is not matter that has vanished. According to Einstein's theory of relativity, mass and energy are equivalent: a change $\Delta E$ in the energy of an object changes its mass by $\tfrac{\Delta E}{c^2}$, where $c$ is the speed of light. When protons and neutrons bind into a nucleus, the strong interaction pulls them together and energy is released, for example as gamma photons. The energy, and therefore also the mass, of the resulting nucleus is smaller than that of the separate nucleons. The same energy is needed if the nucleus is broken back into separate nucleons. This energy is the binding energy $E_\text{B}$ of the nucleus.

    Why a change of mass is not noticed in everyday life

    The equivalence of mass and energy applies to every change of energy, but $c^2$ is so large that in ordinary phenomena the change of mass is too small to measure. A mass of one gram corresponds to the energy

    $$\begin{aligned} E &= 1.00 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \\ &\quad \cdot (2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &\approx 9.0 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{J}. \end{aligned}$$

    When carbon burns, the mass decreases by only about one ten-billionth. In nuclei the binding energy is so large that the mass defect is about $0.8\,\%$ of the mass of the atom for helium-4 and uranium-238 alike. This is why nuclear physics calculates energy directly from the difference of masses.

    Binding energy

    Mass defect and binding energy

    A nucleus ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ has $Z$ protons and $N = A - Z$ neutrons. The mass defect is calculated from atomic masses:

    $$\Delta m = Z\,m_\text{H} + N\,m_\text{n} - m_\text{atom}$$

    where $m_\text{H}$ is the mass of a hydrogen atom ${}^{1}\text{H}$, $m_\text{n}$ the mass of a neutron and $m_\text{atom}$ the mass of the atom ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$. The binding energy of a nucleus is the energy corresponding to the mass defect

    $$E_\text{B} = \Delta m\,c^2$$

    where $E_\text{B}$ is the binding energy and $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ the speed of light. The binding energy of the electrons themselves in the atom is not taken into account, because it is at most hundreds of kiloelectronvolts, and even for the heaviest atoms less than a thousandth of the binding energy of the nucleus.

    The atomic mass unit as energy

    The energies of nuclei are given in megaelectronvolts: $1\,\text{MeV} = 10^{6}\,\text{eV}$ and $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602177 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$. Let us calculate the energy that corresponds to a mass of $1\,\text{u}$. Because the mass defect is a small difference, six significant figures are used here for the constants:

    $$\begin{aligned} E &= 1\,\text{u} \cdot c^2 \\ &= 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg} \\ &\quad \cdot (2.99792 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &= 1.49241 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{J} \\ &= \frac{1.49241 \cdot 10^{-10}}{1.602177 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV} \\ &= 9.3149 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{eV} \\ &\approx 931.5\,\text{MeV} \end{aligned}$$

    So $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$. When the mass defect is in atomic mass units, this is put in place of the unit, and $c^2$ cancels: for example $0.001000\,\text{u} \cdot c^2 = 0.001000 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 0.9315\,\text{MeV}$. With four-figure constants $1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, $2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ and $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ the result would be $931.9\,\text{MeV}$, so four-figure constants already change the fourth figure.

    Binding energy per nucleon

    The strength of the binding of nuclei is compared with the binding energy per nucleon

    $$\frac{E_\text{B}}{A}$$

    where $A$ is the mass number, that is, the number of nucleons. The graph shows it calculated from atomic masses for $32$ nuclei. For light nuclei the value rises steeply, because each nucleon has more and more neighbours to which the strong interaction binds it. Helium-4 is exceptionally tightly bound. The value is highest, about $8.8\,\text{MeV}$, for iron-56 and nickel-62. For heavier nuclei it falls gently, because the strong interaction acts only between nearest neighbours, but the electric repulsion of the protons reaches across the whole nucleus.

    The graph shows when a nuclear reaction releases energy: when the nuclei formed are more tightly bound than the original ones, the binding energy increases and the difference is released. The joining of light nuclei, fusion, and the splitting of a heavy nucleus, fission, move nucleons towards the peak of the graph. They are discussed in the chapter Fission, fusion and nuclear power.

    Reaction energy

    In a nuclear reaction nuclei change: a nucleus decays, or another nucleus or particle hits a nucleus. In a reaction the mass number and the charge number are conserved (chapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay), but mass is not. The reaction energy is

    $$Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$$

    where $m_\text{initial}$ is the sum of the masses of the starting materials and $m_\text{final}$ the sum of the masses of the particles formed. When $Q > 0$, energy is released as the kinetic energy of the particles formed and as gamma radiation; when $Q < 0$, the reaction needs energy. The formula counts the masses of all the particles on both sides. The atomic masses in tables also include the electrons of the atoms. In alpha decay and most nuclear reactions there are equally many electrons on both sides, so atomic masses can be put into the formula directly. For example, in the alpha decay of uranium-238, ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U} \to {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, the mass decreases by $0.004583\,\text{u}$, so $Q = 0.004583 \cdot 931.5\,\text{MeV} \approx 4.27\,\text{MeV}$.

    Atomic masses in beta decay

    In beta decay the charge number of the nucleus changes, so the initial and daughter atoms have different numbers of electrons. If nuclear masses are used, the mass of the electron or positron formed is included. With the masses $M$ of neutral atoms the bookkeeping depends on the type of decay. In beta minus decay the daughter nucleus has one more proton, so the daughter atom has one more electron than the initial atom. This extra electron matches the mass of the beta particle formed, so the mass of the electron is not added separately:

    $$Q(\beta^-) = [M(\text{X}) - M(\text{Y})]\,c^2$$

    where $M(\text{X})$ is the mass of the initial atom and $M(\text{Y})$ the mass of the daughter atom. In beta plus decay the daughter atom has one electron fewer than the initial atom, and in addition a positron is formed, whose mass is the same as that of an electron. That is why the mass of two electrons is subtracted:

    $$Q(\beta^+) = [M(\text{X}) - M(\text{Y}) - 2m_e]\,c^2$$

    where $m_e = 0.000549\,\text{u}$ is the mass of the electron. The model ignores the binding energies of the electrons of the atom, which are negligibly small at this precision.

    Common misconception

    Many think that binding energy is energy stored in the nucleus, which is released when the nucleus is broken into pieces. The opposite is true. The binding energy was released when the nucleus formed, and the same energy is needed to break the nucleus into separate nucleons. This is why the nucleus is lighter than its parts. A large binding energy per nucleon means a tightly bound, stable nucleus, not a store of energy. In a nuclear reaction energy is released when the total binding energy of the nuclei formed is greater than that of the original ones.

    Another mistake is to think that mass is conserved in a nuclear reaction as in chemistry. In a nuclear reaction the mass number, that is, the number of nucleons, and the charge number are conserved, but the mass changes by the amount of the energy released, $\Delta m = \tfrac{Q}{c^2}$.

    Summary

    • Atomic masses are given in atomic mass units: $1\,\text{u}$ is $\tfrac{1}{12}$ of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, that is, $1\,\text{u} = 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$.
    • A nucleus is lighter than its separate nucleons. The mass defect is $\Delta m = Z\,m_\text{H} + N\,m_\text{n} - m_\text{atom}$.
    • The binding energy $E_\text{B} = \Delta m\,c^2$ is needed to break a nucleus into nucleons, and it is released when a nucleus forms. Putting in $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ gives the energy in megaelectronvolts.
    • The binding energy per nucleon is highest, about $8.8\,\text{MeV}$, for iron and nickel; fusion and fission release energy because they move nucleons into more tightly bound nuclei.
    • The reaction energy is $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$: mass is not conserved in a nuclear reaction, although the mass number and the charge number are. With atomic masses, in beta minus decay $Q(\beta^-) = [M(\text{X}) - M(\text{Y})]\,c^2$, and in beta plus decay $2m_e$ is also subtracted.

      Example 1: Binding energy of helium-4

      Calculate the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon of the helium-4 nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. Use the atomic masses $m({}^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\,\text{u}$ and $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\,\text{u}$ (AME2020) and $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$.

      Step 1/7

      Known and asked. The atomic number is $Z = 2$ and the mass number $A = 4$, so the number of neutrons is $N = A - Z = 2$. The atomic masses are $m_\text{atom} = 4.002603\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\,\text{u}$ and $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\,\text{u}$. The binding energy $E_\text{B}$ and the binding energy per nucleon $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ are asked in megaelectronvolts.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The nucleus is lighter than its separate nucleons, and the mass defect $\Delta m$ corresponds to the binding energy $E_\text{B} = \Delta m\,c^2$. We calculate with atomic masses: two hydrogen atoms contain two electrons, just like the helium-4 atom, so the masses of the electrons cancel.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The mass defect, the binding energy and its share per nucleon:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta m &= Z\,m_\text{H} + N\,m_\text{n} - m_\text{atom} \\ E_\text{B} &= \Delta m\,c^2 \\ \frac{E_\text{B}}{A} &= \frac{\Delta m\,c^2}{A} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The mass is not converted to kilograms: $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ is put in place of the unit of the mass defect, so $c^2$ cancels and the energy is obtained directly in megaelectronvolts.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First the mass defect, then the energy:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta m &= Z\,m_\text{H} + N\,m_\text{n} - m_\text{atom} \\ &= 2 \cdot 1.007825\,\text{u} \\ &\quad + 2 \cdot 1.008665\,\text{u} \\ &\quad - 4.002603\,\text{u} \\ &= 0.030377\,\text{u} \\[6pt] E_\text{B} &= \Delta m\,c^2 = 0.030377\,\text{u} \cdot c^2 \\ &= 0.030377 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 \\ &= 28.296\,\text{MeV} \\[6pt] \frac{E_\text{B}}{A} &= \frac{28.296\,\text{MeV}}{4} \\ &= 7.074\,\text{MeV} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The binding energy of the helium-4 nucleus is $28.30\,\text{MeV}$ and the binding energy per nucleon $7.074\,\text{MeV}$, four significant figures like the conversion factor $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. With rounded numbers $\Delta m \approx 0.03\,\text{u}$ and $0.03 \cdot 900\,\text{MeV} = 27\,\text{MeV}$, close to the result. The mass defect is about $0.8\,\%$ of the mass of the atom, as it usually is for nuclei, and the value $7.07\,\text{MeV}$ per nucleon is the helium-4 point of the graph, clearly higher than its neighbours.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      A student calculates the binding energy of the carbon-12 nucleus.She correctly gets the mass defect $\Delta m = 0.098940\,\text{u}$ and writes

      $$\begin{aligned} &E_\text{B} = \Delta m\,c^2 \\ &= 0.098940 \cdot 931.5\,\text{MeV} \\ &\quad \cdot (2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &\approx 8.3 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{MeV} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • One can calculate with kilograms and joules, but it is not required here: with the conversion factor $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ the energy is obtained directly in megaelectronvolts. The error is that $c^2$ has been included twice.

      • Dividing would give the binding energy per nucleon, which is a different quantity. What was asked was the whole binding energy $E_\text{B}$. The error is that $c^2$ has been included twice, although it is already in the conversion factor $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$.

      • By definition the mass of the carbon-12 atom is exactly $12\,\text{u}$, but six hydrogen atoms and six neutrons together weigh $12.098940\,\text{u}$. So the mass defect $0.098940\,\text{u}$ is correct. The error is that $c^2$ has been included twice.

      • Correct. When $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ is put in place of the unit $\text{u}$, $c^2$ cancels: $0.098940 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 92.16\,\text{MeV}$. The unit also reveals the error: the unit of the student's result would be $\text{MeV} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}$, not a unit of energy.

      Principle

        10²⁰ kg
        Answer

        $4.9 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        $L = 1.0 \cdot 10^{28}\,\text{W}$ (example data), $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\,\text{u}$, $m({}^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603\,\text{u}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $t = 1\,\text{yr} = 365.25 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$, mass of the star $M = 2.0 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg}$, share of hydrogen $70\,\%$

        Asked

        a) the decrease in mass in a year $\Delta m_\text{yr}$, b) the share that turns into energy $f$, c) the amount of hydrogen turned into helium in a year $m_\text{hydrogen}$ in the unit $10^{20}\,\text{kg}$, d) the share of hydrogen used up in a billion years

        Principle

        Power is energy per unit time, so the energy radiated in a year is $E = L\,t$. By the equivalence of mass and energy, the mass of a radiating star decreases by $\Delta m_\text{yr} = \tfrac{E}{c^2}$. The same decrease in mass arises in reactions where the mass of four hydrogen atoms is greater than the mass of one helium atom, so the amount of hydrogen used up is obtained from the decrease in mass and the share that turns into energy.

        Formula

        a)

        $\Delta m_\text{yr}$$= \dfrac{L\,t}{c^2}$

        b)

        $f$$= \dfrac{4\,m_\text{H} - m({}^{4}\text{He})}{4\,m_\text{H}}$

        c)

        $m_\text{hydrogen}$$= \dfrac{\Delta m_\text{yr}}{f}$

        d) share $= \dfrac{10^{9} \cdot m_\text{hydrogen}}{0.70\,M}$

        Unit conversions

        $t$$= 365.25 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
        $= 3.15576 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$
        $1\,\text{W} \cdot 1\,\text{s}$$= 1\,\text{J}$
        $= 1\,\text{kg} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}$

        The share $f$ is a ratio, so the atomic mass unit cancels.

        Substitution

        a)

        $\Delta m_\text{yr}$$= \dfrac{1.0 \cdot 10^{28}\,\text{W} \cdot 3.15576 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}}{(2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2}$
        $= \dfrac{3.15576 \cdot 10^{35}\,\text{J}}{8.988 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
        $= 3.511 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}$

        b) $4\,m_\text{H} = 4.031300\,\text{u}$ and the decrease in mass is $4.031300\,\text{u} - 4.002603\,\text{u} = 0.028697\,\text{u}$, so

        $f$$= \dfrac{0.028697\,\text{u}}{4.031300\,\text{u}}$
        $= 0.007119$
        $= 0.712\,\%$

        c)

        $m_\text{hydrogen}$$= \dfrac{3.511 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}}{0.007119}$
        $= 4.932 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$

        d) There is $0.70 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg} = 1.4 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg}$ of hydrogen, and in a billion years $10^{9} \cdot 4.932 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg} = 4.9 \cdot 10^{29}\,\text{kg}$ is used up, so the share is

        $\dfrac{4.9 \cdot 10^{29}\,\text{kg}}{1.4 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg}}$$= 0.035$
        $= 3.5\,\%$

        Answer

        $4.9 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$

        Two significant figures like the power $1.0 \cdot 10^{28}\,\text{W}$. The mass decreases by $3.5 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}$ in a year, and only $0.71\,\%$ of the mass of the hydrogen atoms turns into energy.

        Reasonableness

        The power of the star is about $26$ Suns ($3.83 \cdot 10^{26}\,\text{W}$), and the Sun turns about $6 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{kg}$ of hydrogen into helium per second, that is, about $1.9 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{kg}$ per year; multiplying this by $26$ gives about $5 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$, which agrees with the result. We assume that all the hydrogen is available (in reality only the core fuses, so the share of the hydrogen of the core is greater). In a billion years only $3.5\,\%$ of the supply is used up, so the power does not change much because of the decrease in hydrogen, and the assumption of constant power is reasonable over this time span. The decrease in mass because of radiation, $3.5 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}$, is only $1.8 \cdot 10^{-13}$ of the mass of the star.

      Optional 4. Explanation task: Explain and compare

      When carbon burns, $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2$, energy of $393.5\,\text{kJ}$ is released for every $44.0\,\text{g}$ of starting materials.

      Does the mass change in burning? Why is it said in chemistry that mass is conserved, whereas in nuclear reactions the energy is calculated from the change of mass?

      Model answer

      The mass changes, because the equivalence of mass and energy applies to every change of energy. The mass of the combustion products is smaller by $\Delta m = \tfrac{E}{c^2} = \tfrac{393.5 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{J}}{(2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2} \approx 4.4 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{kg}$, which is about $10^{-10}$, a ten-billionth of the mass of the starting materials. Such a small change cannot be weighed, so in chemistry mass is conserved in practice. In nuclear reactions the relative change of mass is hundreds of thousands or millions of times greater: about $2 \cdot 10^{-5}$ in the alpha decay of uranium-238 and about $10^{-3}$ of the mass of the starting materials in the fission of uranium-235. This is why the energy of a nuclear reaction is calculated from the difference of masses.
