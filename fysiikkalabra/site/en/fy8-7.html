Lasers and quantum structures

Why does the light of a laser pointer cross a dark classroom as a narrow beam and show on the wall as a small dot, while the light of a torch spreads into a large, dim patch?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain the roles of stimulated emission, population inversion and mirrors in a laser.
  • calculate the laser wavelength and the photons per second from the laser levels and power.
  • describe how the size of a quantum dot determines the colour of its light, and name applications of quantum structures.

How the calculation works

The pumping rate is $W = \tfrac{P_\text{p}}{1\,\text{W} \cdot \tau}$, where $\tau = 1.0\,\text{ms}$. In the steady state, atoms are pumped to the upper level as fast as they leave it. With three levels, before the threshold

$$N_2 = \frac{W\tau}{1 + W\tau},$$

and with two levels $N_2 = \tfrac{W\tau}{1 + 2W\tau}$, which always stays below $50\,\%$. When the laser works, $N_2$ stays at the threshold value $55\,\%$, and the energy pumped beyond it goes into the beam: $P_\text{out} = 100\,\text{mW} \cdot (W\tau N_1 - N_2)$, when $N_1$ and $N_2$ are shares.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Atom and photon: three events

    In the chapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model, an atom absorbs a photon whose energy is the difference of two levels, or emits one when it drops to a lower level. An excited atom usually emits its photon by itself, at a random moment and in a random direction. This is spontaneous emission. The light of a lamp and of an LED is produced this way, so the photons of different atoms leave in different directions and out of step.

    In 1917 Albert Einstein concluded that there is also a third event. When a photon whose energy is exactly the difference of the levels $E_2 - E_1$ hits an excited atom, it can make the atom drop to the lower level at once. This is stimulated emission. The incoming photon is not absorbed; a second photon is created beside it with the same energy, that is the same wavelength, the same direction of travel and the same phase. One photon becomes two identical ones, two become four, and so on. The laser is based on this amplification of light: the word comes from the English light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation.

    Population inversion

    A photon that makes an excited atom emit can be absorbed when it hits an atom in the ground state. Which happens more often depends on which level has more atoms. Normally almost all atoms are in the ground state, so a photon is much more likely to be absorbed than multiplied, and light becomes weaker in matter. Light is amplified only if the upper level has more atoms than the lower one, $N_2 > N_1$. This state is called population inversion. Heating does not produce it: even in warm matter the lower level always has more atoms than the upper one.

    Inversion is made by pumping, that is by bringing energy to the atoms with light, an electric discharge or an electric current. Two levels are not enough, because the photons of the pump light cause stimulated emission in atoms of the upper level just as likely as they excite atoms of the ground state: at most half of the atoms can be brought to the upper level. That is why a laser medium has at least three levels. In ruby, the green light of the pump lamp lifts atoms to a short-lived level $E_3$, from which they move quickly and without light to the level $E_2$. The level $E_2$ is long-lived: an atom stays in it for a few milliseconds on average, hundreds of thousands of times longer than in an ordinary excited state ($10^{-8}\,\text{s}$). So more atoms have time to collect on the level $E_2$ than are left in the ground state.

    Mirrors make light into a beam

    The laser medium is between two mirrors. One mirror reflects all the light, the other for example $99\,\%$, and lets the rest through. A photon travelling along the axis between the mirrors passes through the medium hundreds of times and creates stimulated copies of itself on the way. Photons leaving sideways exit the medium and do not have time to multiply. So only light along the axis is amplified, and a beam comes out through the partly transmitting mirror. A laser starts to work only when the pumping is so strong that the amplification makes up for the light lost through the mirror and elsewhere. This limit of the pumping is called the threshold.

    Laser light is monochromatic because all the photons are produced in the same transition. It is directional because only light along the axis is amplified, and the beam spreads only a little. It is also coherent: the waves are in step, because each stimulated photon copies the phase of the incoming photon. The light of a torch contains many wavelengths, and it leaves in different directions and out of step. A laser diode works in the same way: an electric current pumps a semiconductor, and the cleaved ends of the crystal act as mirrors.

    Quantum structures: size determines the energy levels

    The energy gap of a semiconductor is the smallest energy that its electron can get from light or give up as light, and it determines the colour of an LED (chapter Light from an LED and measuring the Planck constant). In a large crystal the energy gap depends only on the material. In the chapter The wave-mechanical model of the atom and orbitals, the states of an electron were compared with the standing waves of a string: the allowed vibrations of a shorter string are higher and farther apart. The same happens when electrons are confined in a structure only a few nanometres in size. The allowed energies move apart, and the energy gap grows as the structure gets smaller. Such structures are called quantum structures. A thin layer is a quantum well, and a nanocrystal confined in every direction is a quantum dot.

    So the colour of a quantum dot can be chosen by its size, even though the material is the same. A quantum dot made of cadmium selenide with a diameter of $2\,\text{nm}$ emits cyan light, and a dot with a diameter of $6\,\text{nm}$ emits red light. The energy gap of a large cadmium selenide crystal is $1.74\,\text{eV}$, which corresponds to dark red light at the edge of the visible range, about $713\,\text{nm}$. Making the dot smaller thus moves the light towards shorter wavelengths. The Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2023 was awarded for the discovery and production of quantum dots.

    Applications of quantum structures

    In a quantum dot display, blue backlight hits quantum dots, which absorb it and emit pure green or red light in the same way as in the fluorescence of the chapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms. Most laser diodes are quantum well lasers, in which the thickness of the well selects the wavelength. Quantum dot layers are being studied for solar cells, and by the size of the dots they can be tuned to absorb the wanted part of the spectrum of the Sun.

    The basic unit of a quantum computer is the qubit: a quantum system that has two states and can also be in a combination of them, a superposition. Qubits are made, for example, of electrons confined in the quantum dots of a semiconductor and of superconducting circuits. Such a circuit behaves like an artificial atom with its own quantised energy levels. In Finland, superconducting quantum computers are being built by VTT and IQM. The states of a qubit are sensitive, so the circuits are cooled close to absolute zero and shielded from disturbances of the environment.

    Laser photons

    All the photons of a laser are produced in the same transition, so the energy and wavelength of each photon are

    $$\begin{aligned} hf &= E_2 - E_1 \\ \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_2 - E_1} \end{aligned}$$

    where $E_2$ is the energy of the upper and $E_1$ of the lower laser level, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ is the Planck constant, $f$ the frequency of the light, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ the speed of light and $\lambda$ the wavelength.

    Because the photons are identical, the power of a laser is the number of photons leaving per second multiplied by the energy of one photon:

    $$P = \frac{N}{t} \cdot hf$$

    where $P$ is the power of the laser and $N$ the number of photons leaving in the time $t$. The number of photons per second is thus $\tfrac{N}{t} = \tfrac{P}{hf}$. Energies are often calculated in electronvolts and converted to joules by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

    With the same power, a laser of a longer wavelength emits more photons, because one photon carries less energy. In the graph, a red laser pointer of $1.0\,\text{mW}$ emits about $3.3 \cdot 10^{15}$ photons per second and a violet one of equal power about $2.0 \cdot 10^{15}$.

    Common misconception

    Many think that laser light is dangerous because its photons would be especially energetic. But the energy of a photon depends only on the wavelength: the photon energy $1.91\,\text{eV}$ of a red laser pointer is the same as that of any light of $650\,\text{nm}$, and the photons of violet light of $405\,\text{nm}$ are more energetic, whether they come from a laser or from a lamp. The danger of a laser is that the whole power travels in a narrow beam, which the lens of the eye focuses on the retina into a very small spot.

    Another mistake is to think that in stimulated emission the atom absorbs a photon and emits two. The incoming photon is not absorbed but continues on its way. The energy of the new photon comes from the excited atom, which drops from the level $E_2$ to the level $E_1$, so energy is conserved.

    Summary

    • An excited atom can emit a photon by itself (spontaneous emission) or under the influence of an incoming photon (stimulated emission). Stimulated emission produces a photon with the energy, direction and phase of the incoming photon.
    • Light is amplified only when the upper laser level has more atoms than the lower one, $N_2 > N_1$ (population inversion). It is obtained by pumping a medium that has at least three levels and a long-lived upper laser level.
    • Between the mirrors, light along the axis passes through the medium many times and is amplified; the laser works when the pumping exceeds the threshold. Laser light is monochromatic, directional and coherent.
    • The energy of the photons of a laser is $hf = E_2 - E_1$, and the power is $P = \tfrac{N}{t} \cdot hf$.
    • In quantum structures the electrons are confined in a space of nanometre size, and the energy gap grows as the structure gets smaller: a smaller quantum dot emits a shorter wavelength. Applications are quantum dot displays, quantum well lasers, research on solar cells and the qubits of quantum computers.
    Optional Tunnelling

    Tunnelling

    According to classical physics, a ball whose energy is not enough to get over a hill rolls back. In quantum physics, the probability of detecting an electron does not end at the edge of a barrier but decreases quickly inside the barrier. If the barrier is very thin, the probability behind the barrier remains small but not zero: the electron can be found behind the barrier even though its energy is smaller than the energy needed to cross the barrier. This is called tunnelling. The probability decreases extremely steeply as the barrier gets thicker and higher, so tunnelling is significant only for barriers of the order of nanometres.

    In a scanning tunnelling microscope, a sharp metal tip is brought about a nanometre from a surface. The current of electrons tunnelling between the tip and the surface changes strongly with the distance, so the tip can be used to image single atoms. A bit is written into a memory cell of flash memory by moving electrons through a thin insulating layer by tunnelling. In superconducting qubits the current passes through a thin insulating layer by tunnelling; the Nobel Prize in Physics 2025 was awarded for the discovery of quantum tunnelling and the quantisation of energy in such circuits. Alpha decay, discussed in the chapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay, is also tunnelling.

      Problem

      The energy difference of the levels of the laser transition of a helium–neon laser is $1.96\,\text{eV}$, and the power of the laser is $1.0\,\text{mW}$. How many photons does the laser emit per second?

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. The energy difference of the levels is $E_2 - E_1 = 1.96\,\text{eV}$ and the power is $P = 1.0\,\text{mW}$. We are asked for the number of photons leaving per second, $\tfrac{N}{t}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. All the photons of a laser are produced in the same transition, so the energy of each is $hf = E_2 - E_1$. The power is the number of photons per second multiplied by the energy of one photon.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. We substitute the difference of the levels for the energy of the photon and solve the number of photons per second:

      $$\begin{aligned} P &= \frac{N}{t} \cdot (E_2 - E_1) \\ \frac{N}{t} &= \frac{P}{E_2 - E_1} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The power is

      $P$$= 1.0\,\text{mW}$
      $= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W}$

      and the energy difference is converted to joules by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$:

      $E_2 - E_1$$= 1.96 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
      $= 3.1399 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} \frac{N}{t} &= \frac{P}{E_2 - E_1} \\ &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W}}{3.1399 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \\ &= 3.1848 \cdot 10^{15}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The laser emits about $3.2 \cdot 10^{15}$ photons per second. The result is given with two significant figures, because the power $1.0\,\text{mW}$ is given with two.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The unit is correct, because

      $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{J}}$$= \tfrac{\text{J/s}}{\text{J}}$
      $= \tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$

      The energy difference corresponds to the wavelength

      $\lambda$$= \tfrac{hc}{E_2 - E_1}$
      $\approx 633\,\text{nm}$

      the familiar red light of a helium–neon laser. According to the graph, a laser of $1.0\,\text{mW}$ emits about $3.3 \cdot 10^{15}$ photons per second at the wavelength $650\,\text{nm}$, so a slightly smaller number at a slightly shorter wavelength is credible.

      Optional Find the error
      A student calculates how many photons per second a green laser pointer with the power $5.0\,\text{mW}$ and the wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$ emits:

      $$\begin{aligned} \frac{N}{t} &= \frac{P \lambda}{hc} \\ &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W} \cdot 532}{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}} \\ &\approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{25}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • The power is substituted in watts, because $h$ has the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$ and $1\,\text{W} = 1\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}$. The conversion $5.0\,\text{mW} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W}$ is correct.

      • Correct. $\lambda = 532\,\text{nm} = 532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$, which gives $\tfrac{N}{t} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$. The error is revealed by a unit check: $\tfrac{\text{W} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{J}\,\text{m}} = \tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$ only if the wavelength is in metres.

      At least how much heat is produced in the fibre per second? Give the answer in watts.

      W
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      First calculate how much pump power is needed at least. When each pump photon produces one laser photon, there are equally many photons per second.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      With the same number of photons per second, the power is proportional to the energy of a photon $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$: $P_\text{p} = P_\text{L} \cdot \tfrac{\lambda_\text{L}}{\lambda_\text{p}}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $P_\text{p}$$= 1000\,\text{W} \cdot \tfrac{1070\,\text{nm}}{976\,\text{nm}}$

      Show the solution

      Given

      power of the beam $P_\text{L} = 1.0\,\text{kW}$, wavelength of the laser light $\lambda_\text{L} = 1070\,\text{nm}$, wavelength of the pump light $\lambda_\text{p} = 976\,\text{nm}$; one pump photon produces at most one laser photon

      Asked

      the heat produced in the fibre per second, that is the heating power $P_\text{heat}$ in watts, the smallest possible value

      Principle

      Energy is conserved: the power of the pump light divides into the beam and heat. The least heat is produced when each pump photon produces a laser photon. Then there are equally many photons per second, and since $P = \tfrac{N}{t} \cdot \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, the powers are in the same ratio as the energies of the photons.

      Formula

      $\tfrac{N}{t}$$= \tfrac{P_\text{L} \lambda_\text{L}}{hc}$
      $P_\text{p}$$= \tfrac{N}{t} \cdot \tfrac{hc}{\lambda_\text{p}}$
      $= P_\text{L} \cdot \tfrac{\lambda_\text{L}}{\lambda_\text{p}}$

      so

      $$P_\text{heat} = P_\text{p} - P_\text{L} = P_\text{L} \left(\frac{\lambda_\text{L}}{\lambda_\text{p}} - 1\right)$$

      Conversions

      $P_\text{L}$$= 1.0\,\text{kW}$
      $= 1.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{W}$
      $= 1000\,\text{W}$

      1. Stimulated emission

      A photon whose energy is exactly the difference of the levels of the atom hits an excited atom, and stimulated emission takes place. Which statement is true?

      • Correct. The incoming photon continues on its way, and the atom emits an identical photon beside it. That is why laser light is amplified and is coherent.

      • Energy is conserved: the atom gives up only the difference of the levels $E_2 - E_1$. Two photons leave, and the energy of each is $E_2 - E_1$.

      • In spontaneous emission the direction is random, but a stimulated photon travels in the direction of the incoming photon. That is exactly why only light along the axis is amplified between the mirrors.

      • The incoming photon is not absorbed. The atom loses only the energy $E_2 - E_1$, and that is enough for one new photon; one of the leaving photons is the incoming photon.

      3. The colour of a quantum dot

      Two quantum dots are made of the same material, but one glows blue and the other red. Explain in three or four sentences what causes the difference and which of the dots is smaller.

      Model answer

      In a quantum dot, the electrons are confined in a space a few nanometres in size. In the same way as the standing waves of a shorter string are higher, in a smaller dot the allowed energies of an electron are farther apart and the energy gap is larger. The energy of the photon emitted by the dot equals the energy gap, and the energy of a photon of blue light is greater than that of red light. So the dot that glows blue is smaller.

          number of chromium ions $N = 1.0 \cdot 10^{19}$, wavelength of the pump light $\lambda = 550\,\text{nm}$, one pump photon lifts one ion to the upper laser level, the laser transition ends in the ground state, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the smallest absorbed pump energy $E$ in joules

          Principle

          In a population inversion the upper laser level has more ions than the lower. Because the lower laser level is the ground state, more than half of the ions have to be lifted to the upper level, that is at least $\tfrac{N}{2}$, and each needs one pump photon. By assumption the ions do not decay in the middle of the pumping.

          Formula

          $E$$= \dfrac{N}{2} \cdot \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

          Conversions

          $\lambda$$= 550\,\text{nm}$
          $= 550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $E$$= \dfrac{1.0 \cdot 10^{19}}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
          $= 5.0 \cdot 10^{18} \cdot 3.6118 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
          $= 1.8059\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $1.8\,\text{J}$

          Two significant figures like the number of ions $1.0 \cdot 10^{19}$; the inversion requires slightly more than this.

          Reasonableness

          The estimate is a lower limit: in reality the ions decay already during the pumping and only a small part of the light of a flash lamp is absorbed, so the lamp has to produce much more energy. That is why a three-level ruby laser needs a powerful flash lamp. One pump photon, $3.6 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ or $2.25\,\text{eV}$, is green light, as it should be.

        2. In a green laser pointer, the light of an infrared laser diode ($808\,\text{nm}$, power $1.0\,\text{W}$) pumps a crystal that emits laser light with the wavelength $1064\,\text{nm}$. A second crystal always combines two $1064\,\text{nm}$ photons into one $532\,\text{nm}$ photon.

          At most how many green photons per second can the laser pointer emit, when each pump photon produces at most one $1064\,\text{nm}$ photon ($h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$)?

          photons
          Answer

          $2.0 \cdot 10^{18}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          wavelength of the pump light $\lambda_\text{p} = 808\,\text{nm}$ and power $P = 1.0\,\text{W}$; one pump photon produces at most one photon of $1064\,\text{nm}$; two photons of $1064\,\text{nm}$ combine into one photon of $532\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the greatest number of green photons per second $\tfrac{N_\text{g}}{t}$

          Principle

          First we calculate the number of pump photons per second from the power and the energy of a photon. At most as many infrared photons can be produced, and two infrared photons make one green one, so there are at most half as many green photons as pump photons. Energy is conserved: the energy of two photons of $1064\,\text{nm}$ equals that of one photon of $532\,\text{nm}$.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{N_\text{p}}{t}$$= \dfrac{P\lambda_\text{p}}{hc}$
          $\dfrac{N_\text{g}}{t}$$= \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot \dfrac{N_\text{p}}{t}$

          from which

          $\dfrac{N_\text{g}}{t}$$= \dfrac{P\lambda_\text{p}}{2hc}$

          Conversions

          $\lambda_\text{p}$$= 808\,\text{nm}$
          $= 808 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$

          the power is already in watts.

          Substitution

          $\dfrac{N_\text{g}}{t}$$= \dfrac{1.0\,\text{W} \cdot 808 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{2 \cdot 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}$
          $= \dfrac{4.0675 \cdot 10^{18}}{2}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $= 2.0338 \cdot 10^{18}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$

          Answer

          $2.0 \cdot 10^{18}$

          Green photons per second, two significant figures like the power $1.0\,\text{W}$.

          Reasonableness

          The total power of the green photons would be at most

          $2.0 \cdot 10^{18}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot \tfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
          $\approx 0.76\,\text{W}$

          less than the pump power $1.0\,\text{W}$, as conservation of energy requires. The green power of a real laser pointer is only milliwatts, because the conversions are much weaker than this ideal case.

        3. The energy difference of the two states of a superconducting qubit corresponds to a photon with the frequency $5.0\,\text{GHz}$.

          How many times greater is the energy of a photon of red light ($650\,\text{nm}$) than the energy difference of the states of the qubit ($c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$)?

          Answer

          $9.2 \cdot 10^{4}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          frequency of the photon corresponding to the energy difference of the states of the qubit $f_\text{q} = 5.0\,\text{GHz}$, wavelength of the red light $\lambda = 650\,\text{nm}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the ratio of the energies $\tfrac{E_\text{light}}{E_\text{q}}$

          Principle

          The energy of both photons is $hf$, so the ratio of the energies is the ratio of the frequencies, and the Planck constant cancels. The frequency of the red light is obtained from the wavelength, $f = \tfrac{c}{\lambda}$.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{E_\text{light}}{E_\text{q}}$$= \dfrac{hc / \lambda}{h f_\text{q}}$

          from which

          $\dfrac{E_\text{light}}{E_\text{q}}$$= \dfrac{c}{\lambda f_\text{q}}$

          Conversions

          $f_\text{q}$$= 5.0\,\text{GHz}$
          $= 5.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}$
          $\lambda$$= 650\,\text{nm}$
          $= 650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $\dfrac{E_\text{light}}{E_\text{q}}$$= \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}}$
          $= \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^{8}}{3.25 \cdot 10^{3}}$
          $= 9.2246 \cdot 10^{4}$

          Answer

          about $9.2 \cdot 10^{4}$, that is about $92\,000$ times greater, two significant figures like the frequency $5.0\,\text{GHz}$.

          Reasonableness

          The frequency of red light is about $4.6 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, and

          $\tfrac{4.6 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}}{5.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}}$$\approx 9 \cdot 10^{4}$

          The energy difference of the qubit is only about $2 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{eV}$, so even a small thermal motion or disturbance can change its state; that is why qubits are cooled close to absolute zero. Unit: $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{m} \cdot \text{Hz}} = 1$.
