Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change

Why is the mean temperature of the Earth about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and not below freezing, even though the Earth radiates as much energy into space as it absorbs from the Sun?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • recognise the ways heat is transferred: conduction, convection and thermal radiation.
  • calculate the thermal power conducted through a building part.
  • use energy balance to explain the greenhouse effect and climate change.

Prior knowledge

  • Heat and work change internal energy, $\Delta U = Q + W$, and energy is conservedchapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy
  • Heat is the transfer of energy from hotter to colder because of a temperature differencechapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy

1Think about it

The Moon is on average as far from the Sun as the Earth, and the same amount of sunlight hits each square metre of its surface. However, the Moon has no atmosphere, and the mean temperature of its surface is well below freezing. The same difference can be seen on a small scale on Earth: on a clear winter night the frost gets harsher quickly, but on a cloudy night the temperature falls only a little.

The atmosphere does not produce energy itself. How can it still keep the ground warmer than it would be without it?

Optional Review from the previous lesson
In chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy, the change in internal energy was written as $\Delta U = Q + W$. Every minute, $600\,\text{J}$ of heat transfers from the stove to the water in a pan, and every minute $600\,\text{J}$ of heat transfers from the water to the surroundings. No work is done. What happens to the internal energy and the temperature of the water?

  • The heat leaving would decrease the internal energy only if it were not replaced. Here as much comes into the water as leaves it, so $\Delta U = 0$.

  • Only the net transfer changes the internal energy. $600\,\text{J}$ comes into the water and $600\,\text{J}$ leaves it every minute, so $Q = 600\,\text{J} - 600\,\text{J} = 0$ and $\Delta U = 0$.

  • Correct. In a minute $Q = +600\,\text{J} - 600\,\text{J} = 0$ and $W = 0$, so $\Delta U = Q + W = 0$. When the energy coming in and the energy going out are equal, the temperature does not change.

  • The first law is enough: $\Delta U = Q + W$, and here $Q = 0$ and $W = 0$ every minute. You do not need the temperature to decide whether the internal energy changes.

W/m²

Show the solution

Given

with the ground in balance, $P_\text{sun} = 240\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ is absorbed from the Sun and the surface radiates $P_\text{rad} = 390\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$; the surface gives out energy only as thermal radiation

Find

the power radiated down by the atmosphere $P_\text{down}$ in $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$

Principle

The temperature of the surface stays constant ($+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), so energy neither accumulates in the surface nor decreases: the power coming into the surface is equal to the power leaving it. There are two inputs, the Sun and the thermal radiation radiated down by the atmosphere, and only one output, the radiation of the surface itself.

Formula

In balance the power coming in is equal to the power leaving:

$$P_\text{sun} + P_\text{down} = P_\text{rad}$$

$$P_\text{down} = P_\text{rad} - P_\text{sun}$$

Conversions

No conversions are needed, because all the powers are given in the same unit $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$.

Substitution

$P_\text{down}$$= 390\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} - 240\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$
$= 150\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$

Answer

$150\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$

Three significant figures. The example values $390$ and $240\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ are given as whole numbers, so the difference has the same precision in W/m²; no data limits it any further.

Reasonableness

Check the balance: $240 + 150 = 390\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ comes in and $390\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ goes out. The result is positive and smaller than the radiation of the surface, because part of the power leaving is already covered by the Sun. Without an atmosphere $P_\text{down} = 0$ and the surface would radiate only $240\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$, which corresponds to the colder situation.

Given

row "just after" of the table: power leaving $236.4\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ and excess $3.6\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$; absorbed power $240.0\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$; in the model the power leaving increases by $k = 1.2\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$ for every kelvin of warming

Find

the total warming $\Delta T$ in kelvins when the power leaving is again equal to the power absorbed

Principle

The concentration stays constant, so the absorbed power stays the same $240.0\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$. The Earth warms while the excess is positive, and as it warms it radiates more. Balance is reached when the warming has increased the power leaving by exactly the excess.

Formula

The power leaving must increase by the excess $\Delta P$, and the increase is $k\,\Delta T$:

$$k\,\Delta T = \Delta P \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta T = \frac{\Delta P}{k}$$

Conversions

No conversions are needed, because the excess and the coefficient are given in the units $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ and $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$ and the square metres cancel.

Substitution

$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{3.6\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}}{1.2\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}}$
$= 3.0\,\text{K}$

Answer

$3.0\,\text{K}$

Two significant figures. The limiting data are the excess $240.0 - 236.4 = 3.6\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ (the difference has one decimal and two figures) and the coefficient $1.2\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$ (two figures).

Reasonableness

Check the balance: the power leaving is $236.4 + 1.2 \cdot 3.0 = 240.0\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$, equal to the power absorbed. The result fits the table: at two kelvins the excess is still $1.2\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$ and after the third kelvin it is zero. The warming therefore continues for a long time after the concentration has stopped increasing, but it stops at a new balance.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Because of a temperature difference, energy transfers by itself, as a net amount, from a warmer object to a colder one. Energy that transfers in this way is called heat, and it transfers in three ways. In conduction, energy passes through matter from one particle to the next, as faster vibrating particles nudge their slower neighbours. Metals conduct heat well, because in them free electrons also carry energy, and they carry most of it; air and wool conduct it poorly. In convection, a warm liquid or gas flows and carries energy with it: warm air rises above a radiator, because it has expanded and is lighter than the surrounding air.

    In thermal radiation, an object emits electromagnetic radiation, which needs no medium. This is how the Sun's energy reaches the Earth through empty space, and how the glow of a fireplace warms your face. All objects radiate, and the hotter an object is, the more radiation it emits and the shorter the wavelengths of that radiation are. Thermal radiation therefore leaves both objects, so the colder object also radiates towards the warmer one. The warmer one radiates more, however, so the net transfer of energy is to the colder object.

    Heat losses of a building

    A large part of the heating energy of a house is used because heat is conducted out through the walls, roof, windows and floor. The heat insulation of a building part is described by its U-value, or thermal transmittance, which tells you how many watts of thermal power pass through one square metre when the difference between the indoor and outdoor temperature is one kelvin. The smaller the U-value, the better the structure insulates. The U-value of a modern wall is about $0.17\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$, but even a good window has about $1.0\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$, so a small window lets through as much heat as many times its area of wall. Good insulation saves energy and reduces the emissions from heating.

    Energy balance

    The temperature of an object stays constant when the power coming into it is equal to the power leaving it. This is called energy balance. The Earth receives energy as radiation from the Sun, and about $30\,\%$ of it is reflected straight back into space by clouds, ice and light surfaces. The rest is absorbed, and the Earth radiates the same power away as long-wavelength thermal radiation. Without an atmosphere, the balance would settle at a mean temperature of about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

    Greenhouse effect

    Water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere let sunlight mostly through, although about a fifth of it is absorbed in the atmosphere. They absorb the thermal radiation emitted by the ground and radiate part of it back down. The ground therefore receives more energy than from the Sun alone, and the balance settles at about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. This is called the greenhouse effect, and it answers the question at the start.

    Climate change

    Human activity, mainly the use of fossil fuels, has raised the carbon dioxide concentration of the atmosphere from about 280 parts per million to over 420 parts per million. When greenhouse gases are added, for a while less energy escapes into space than arrives from the Sun. Today the difference is about $1\,\text{W}$ for every square metre of the Earth. The extra energy warms the oceans, the land and the air until a new balance settles at a higher temperature. This is called climate change. A power difference that looks small is huge over the whole area of the Earth, and most of it is stored in the oceans, whose large heat capacity slows the warming down but also keeps it going for a long time. Energy choices such as insulation, heat pumps and zero-emission power plants are therefore also ethical choices that affect future generations.

    Thermal power and U-value

    The thermal power transferred through a building part is

    $$P = U A \Delta T$$

    where $P$ is the thermal power (W), $U$ is the U-value of the building part (W/(m²·K)), $A$ is the area (m²) and $\Delta T$ is the difference between the indoor and outdoor temperature (K). The formula holds when the temperatures on both sides of the building part stay constant. In energy balance the power coming into an object and the power leaving it are equal, and the temperature stays constant.

    Common misconception

    Many people mix up the greenhouse effect and ozone depletion, although they are different things: ozone depletion lets more ultraviolet radiation through, but it hardly warms the Earth. Another mistake is to think that the greenhouse effect is entirely harmful. The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth habitable, and the problem is its strengthening because of human emissions. A third mistake is to believe that a woollen sweater produces heat. It only slows down the conduction and convection of heat away from the body, just as the insulation in a wall slows down the escape of heat from a house.

    Summary

    • Heat transfers by conduction, convection and thermal radiation, and because of a temperature difference the net transfer of energy is from the warmer object to the colder one. All objects emit thermal radiation, the colder one too.
    • The thermal power transferred through a building part is $P = UA\Delta T$. A small U-value means good heat insulation.
    • In energy balance the power coming in and the power going out are equal, and the temperature stays constant.
    • Greenhouse gases absorb the thermal radiation of the ground and radiate part of it back, which raises the equilibrium temperature of the ground from about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
    • An increase in greenhouse gases breaks the balance for a while, and the Earth warms until a new balance settles at a higher temperature.

      Example 1: Heat loss of a house

      The walls of a detached house have an area of 120 m² and a U-value of $0.17\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$, and the windows have an area of 20.0 m² and a U-value of $1.0\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$. It is $21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ indoors and $-15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ outdoors. How large is the thermal power conducted through the walls and windows, and how much energy does it transfer in one day?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Walls: $A_\text{s} = 120\,\text{m}^2$, $U_\text{s} = 0.17\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$. Windows: $A_\text{i} = 20.0\,\text{m}^2$, $U_\text{i} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$. Temperatures $T_\text{in} = 21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $T_\text{out} = -15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. You are asked for the thermal power $P$ and the energy $E$ in one day.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Heat is conducted through a structure from warm to cold, and when the temperatures stay constant, the power is proportional to the area and the temperature difference: $P = UA\Delta T$. The walls and windows conduct heat in parallel, so their powers are added. The power is constant, so the energy is the power multiplied by the time.

      Rearranged formula. First solve the power and then the energy:

      $$\begin{aligned} P &= (U_\text{s} A_\text{s} + U_\text{i} A_\text{i})\,\Delta T \\[6pt] E &= Pt \end{aligned}$$$$\begin{aligned} P &= (U_\text{s} A_\text{s} \\ &\quad + U_\text{i} A_\text{i})\,\Delta T \\[6pt] E &= Pt \end{aligned}$$

      Unit conversions. The temperature difference is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius:

      $\Delta T$$= 21\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-15\,{}^\circ\text{C})$

      Result. The power conducted through the walls and windows is $P \approx 1.5\,\text{kW}$, and in one day it transfers the energy $E \approx 35\,\text{kWh}$. The result is given to two significant figures. The limiting data are the U-values $0.17$ and $1.0$ and the temperature difference $36\,\text{K}$ (a difference of whole degrees), all with two figures; the areas $120\,\text{m}^2$ and $20.0\,\text{m}^2$ are more precise.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The area of the windows is only a sixth of the area of the walls, but the same amount of heat goes through them ($20.0\,\text{W/K}$ and $20.4\,\text{W/K}$), because their U-value is almost six times as large. With rounded numbers, $40\,\text{W/K} \cdot 36\,\text{K} \approx 1.4\,\text{kW}$ and $1.4\,\text{kW} \cdot 24\,\text{h} \approx 34\,\text{kWh}$. The heating of the house replaces this power, and the roof, floor and ventilation add to the need.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      The area of a window is 1.5 m² and its U-value is $1.0\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$. It is $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ indoors and $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ outdoors.       Conduction
      Energy is transferred through the interactions of the particles of matter without any bulk motion of the matter.
      Convection

      Energy is transferred as electromagnetic radiation and needs no medium.
      Thermal insulator
      A material in which energy is transferred slowly by conduction.
      Energy balance
      The energy of a system stays constant when as much energy enters it as leaves it.

      The Earth's temperature stays steady when the power received equals the power radiated to space.

      The atmosphere absorbs part of the Earth's thermal radiation and raises the mean surface temperature.

      The long-term warming of the Earth changes the energy balance, glaciers, oceans and other parts of the climate system.

        A detached house is heated by an air-to-water heat pump whose coefficient of performance is on average 2.8 over the heating season. The coefficient of performance tells how many kilowatt-hours of heat the pump delivers to the house for each kilowatt-hour of electricity it uses.

        More insulation wool is blown into the roof, and its U-value decreases from $0.35\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$ to $0.10\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$. The area of the roof is $100\,\text{m}^2$. The heating season lasts $5800\,\text{h}$, and the difference between the indoor and outdoor temperature is on average $19\,\text{K}$ during it.

        How many kilowatt-hours of electricity does the extra insulation save over the heating season?

        A neighbour thinks insulation does not matter to the owner of a heat pump, because the pump gets its heat free from the outdoor air. Justify why insulation is still worthwhile.

        kWh
        Answer

        $980\,\text{kWh}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        coefficient of performance $\varepsilon = 2.8$, $U_1 = 0.35\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$, $U_2 = 0.10\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$, $A = 100\,\text{m}^2$, $\Delta T = 19\,\text{K}$, $t = 5800\,\text{h}$

        Find

        the electrical energy $W$ saved by the extra insulation, in kilowatt-hours, and a justification of why insulation is worthwhile with a heat pump

        Principle

        Insulation reduces the heat conducted through the roof, and the heat pump has to deliver less heat indoors. The coefficient of performance tells you that the pump delivers heat $\varepsilon$ times the electrical energy it uses, so the electricity is the heat divided by $\varepsilon$.

        Formula

        $Q = (U_1 - U_2)A\Delta T\,t$ and $W = \dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon}$, so

        $W$$= \dfrac{(U_1 - U_2)A\Delta T\,t}{\varepsilon}$

        Conversions

        The time is already in hours and the result is wanted in kilowatt-hours, so the power $\text{W}$ is converted to kilowatts by dividing by a thousand: $\text{Wh} / 1000 = \text{kWh}$.

        Substitution

        $Q$$= (0.35 - 0.10)\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 100\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 19\,\text{K} \cdot 5800\,\text{h}$
        $= 475\,\text{W} \cdot 5800\,\text{h}$
        $= 2\,755\,000\,\text{Wh}$
        $= 2755\,\text{kWh}$
        $W$$= \dfrac{2755\,\text{kWh}}{2.8}$
        $\approx 983.9\,\text{kWh}$

        Answer

        $980\,\text{kWh}$

        Two significant figures. The limiting data are the coefficient of performance $2.8$, the difference of the U-values $0.35 - 0.10 = 0.25$ and the temperature difference $19\,\text{K}$, all with two figures; the area $100\,\text{m}^2$ and the time $5800\,\text{h}$ are values with at least two figures. The calculator result $983.9\,\text{kWh}$ is rounded. The pump does not create heat but moves it using electricity, and the more heat the house needs, the more electricity is used. That is why insulation is worthwhile: a smaller heating need means a smaller electricity bill.

        Reasonableness

        Back-calculation: $983.9 \cdot 2.8 \approx 2755\,\text{kWh}$. The electricity saving is smaller than the heat saving of $2755\,\text{kWh}$, because the pump delivers $2.8$ kWh of heat from one kilowatt-hour of electricity; in addition to the electricity, heat comes from the outdoor air. With direct electric heating ($\varepsilon = 1$) the saving would be the whole $2755\,\text{kWh}$. In frost the coefficient of performance decreases and the backup resistance heater starts, so the saving is then greatest.

      5. Explanation task: Explain and transfer

      Your friend claims: "The Earth radiates as much energy into space as it absorbs from the Sun, so extra carbon dioxide cannot change its temperature. Besides, the greenhouse effect is entirely harmful." Use energy balance and thermal radiation to explain what is right and what is wrong in the claim.

      Model answer

      The first part of the claim is true in a state of balance: when the Earth radiates into space the same power as it absorbs from the Sun, its temperature stays constant. The balance is not permanent, however. When carbon dioxide is added, the atmosphere absorbs more of the thermal radiation of the ground and radiates part of it back down, and for a while less power leaves into space than the Earth absorbs. The extra power warms the oceans, the land and the air. A warmer Earth radiates more, so the warming continues until the power leaving is again equal to the power absorbed. A new balance therefore settles at a higher temperature. The greenhouse effect is not entirely harmful either: the natural greenhouse effect raises the mean temperature of the ground from about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The problem is its strengthening because of human emissions.
