Energy production, the environment and climate change

Why does the way we produce electricity decide how much carbon dioxide ends up in the atmosphere, even though the electricity from a socket at home is always the same?

In this lesson you learn

  • You can explain the greenhouse effect and why burning fossil fuels strengthens it.
  • You can calculate an emission from the emission factor and the energy, and compare ways of producing energy.
  • You can justify a choice using several criteria for comparison.

Prior knowledge

  • When a fuel burns, chemical energy is released, and energy is measured in joules and kilowatt-hourschapter Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm
  • Ways of producing energy differ in power, availability and environmental effectschapter Ways of producing energy and how they compare

1Think about it

Before industrialisation the atmosphere contained about $280\,\text{ppm}$ of carbon dioxide, that is, $280$ molecules per million molecules of the atmosphere. Now the concentration is about $420\,\text{ppm}$ (NOAA measurements, 2023), and the rise is largely caused by burning fossil fuels for electricity, heat and transport. Even so, carbon dioxide is less than half a part per thousand of the atmosphere.

How can such a small part of the atmosphere change the temperature of the whole Earth, and why does it matter how electricity is produced?

Optional Review: burning a fuel
Petrol burns in a car engine. Which description is physically correct?

  • Matter does not turn into energy. In burning, the atoms are rearranged, and the carbon ends up in carbon dioxide molecules. Both energy and matter are conserved: chemical energy changes into heat and kinetic energy, but the carbon atoms do not disappear.

  • Energy does not appear out of nothing. The energy the engine gets comes from the chemical energy of the fuel, and part of it becomes waste heat (chapter Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm).

  • Correct. When a fuel burns, chemical energy is released (chapter Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm), and the carbon atoms of the fuel combine with oxygen: $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$. That is why every amount of energy obtained by burning a carbon-containing fuel brings carbon dioxide with it.

  • Petrol is a hydrocarbon, and its carbon ends up as carbon dioxide when it burns. Water vapour comes from the hydrogen, but carbon dioxide is the most important greenhouse gas in exhaust gas.

Where do the emissions of wind power come from?

In the table the emission factor of wind power is $11\text{–}12\,\text{g/kWh}$. A wind turbine does not burn anything.

Where do the life-cycle emissions of wind power mainly come from?

  • The warming of the rotor and generator is waste heat (chapter Ways of producing energy and how they compare), and it is small. Waste heat is not a carbon dioxide emission; the emissions come from making the materials and from transport.

  • Correct. Life-cycle emissions include all stages: making steel, concrete and copper, transport and construction, and maintenance and dismantling. They are divided by the energy produced over the whole lifetime of the turbine, which is why the factor is small.

  • A turning rotor does not release carbon dioxide from the air. The air passes the turbine and its composition does not change. The emissions come from making and building the turbine.

  • The figure is not a measurement error. It is the median of the life-cycle emissions from several studies, and it is small but not zero.

What does the emission factor not tell you?

The emission factors of nuclear power and wind power are almost equally small in the table, about $12\,\text{g/kWh}$. Even so, both are much debated.

What else should you know before you can say which is the better choice?

  • Correct. The emission factor is one measure, and it tells only about greenhouse gases. For nuclear power it is small, but radioactive waste and safety are other criteria. For wind power, weather-dependent production and land use matter. A sustainable choice weighs all of them.

  • The difference of $1\,\text{g/kWh}$ is small compared with the uncertainty of the figures, because they are medians of several studies. When the climate effect is practically the same, other criteria decide, such as waste, safety, land use and dependence on the weather.

  • Price is an important criterion, but not the only one. The cheapest way may produce radioactive waste, take a lot of land or depend on the weather. A sustainable energy economy weighs the environment, the economy and society together.

  • A more accurate measurement might change the factors slightly, but it would still tell only about greenhouse gases. Waste, safety, land use and dependence on the weather do not show in the emission factor at all, so they are assessed separately.

4Theory summary

Key idea

The Sun warms the ground by radiating energy to it, as visible light and near-infrared radiation. The atmosphere lets most of this radiation through. The warm ground sends energy back into space as thermal radiation, and the greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb it. The most important ones are water vapour, carbon dioxide $\text{CO}_2$ and methane $\text{CH}_4$. They radiate the energy out again in all directions, so part of it returns to the ground.

So greenhouse gases let sunlight through but slow down the escape of thermal radiation. The ground stays warmer than it would be without them: the greenhouse effect raises the mean temperature of the Earth from about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. This natural effect keeps the Earth habitable, because without it the water in the oceans would be frozen.

The problem is that the effect is getting stronger. When people add carbon dioxide and methane to the atmosphere, thermal radiation escapes more slowly and the mean temperature of the Earth rises. This long-term change is called climate change. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has grown from about $280\,\text{ppm}$ (before industrialisation) to about $420\,\text{ppm}$ (NOAA measurements, 2023). Even so, carbon dioxide is less than half a part per thousand of the air. A small share is enough, because nitrogen and oxygen, the main gases of the air, hardly absorb thermal radiation: it is the few greenhouse gas molecules that decide how easily thermal radiation escapes.

The greenhouse effect is treated with calculations on the FY3 course in the chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change. Here it is enough to understand what the gases do to radiation and where the extra gases come from.

Fuels link energy and climate

A fossil fuel, such as coal, oil or natural gas, is chemical energy from ancient living things: coal formed mostly from plants, oil and natural gas from marine plankton. When such a carbon-containing fuel burns completely, its carbon combines with oxygen: $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$. The chemical energy is released as heat (chapter Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm), and the carbon dioxide leaves with the flue gases into the atmosphere. The more energy we get by burning carbon-containing fuels, the more carbon dioxide is released. Not every fuel contains carbon: when hydrogen burns, only water is formed.

The carbon of a fossil fuel has been underground for millions of years, so burning it adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. The carbon of biomass, for example wood, was bound into plants only during the last few decades. Even so, burning biomass is not emission-free: the carbon dioxide from burning stays in the atmosphere until new growth binds it back over decades, and growing, harvesting and transport cause their own emissions.

Ways of producing energy are compared by life-cycle emissions

Emissions come from more than just burning. A power plant must be built from steel, concrete and copper, the fuel must be mined or collected and transported, and in the end the plant is dismantled. That is why ways of producing energy are compared by life-cycle emissions: the emissions of all stages are added together and divided by the energy produced over the whole life cycle.

For comparison, methane and other greenhouse gases are converted into carbon dioxide equivalents ($\text{CO}_2$-eq.), that is, the amount of carbon dioxide that has the same warming effect.

Emissions are not the only criterion

Besides the effect on the climate, ways of producing energy are weighed by land use, transport, waste and safety:

  • Coal and natural gas: the largest emissions. The fuels are mined and transported by ship, train or pipeline, and the flue gases also contain other pollutants.
  • Biomass: takes up forest and field area. Biomass is bulky and its energy content per unit mass is small, so it is not worth transporting far.
  • Solar and wind power: need a lot of area per unit of energy produced, and wind power affects the landscape and birds. Production depends on the weather, so regulating power or storage is needed.
  • Hydropower: dams and reservoirs change the river environment and block the passage of fish, but production can be regulated.
  • Nuclear power: needs little area, and only a small mass of fuel, so there is little transport. Spent fuel is radioactive waste that must be isolated for a long time, and the consequences of an accident could be serious, although the risk is small.

A sustainable energy economy produces and uses energy so that meeting today's needs does not take away the possibilities of future generations. There is no best power plant: the choice weighs the climate effect, land use, waste, safety, price and whether electricity is always available (chapter Ways of producing energy and how they compare).

Carbon dioxide emission

The emission is obtained by multiplying the energy used or produced by the emission factor:

$$m = e \cdot E$$

where $m$ is the emission ($\text{g}$ or $\text{kg}$ of carbon dioxide equivalent), $e$ is the emission factor ($\text{g/kWh}$) and $E$ is the energy ($\text{kWh}$).

The emission factor tells how many grams of carbon dioxide equivalent are produced per kilowatt-hour. The units carry through the calculation: when $\text{g/kWh}$ is multiplied by kilowatt-hours, grams are left. The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$. The emission of a fuel is calculated in the same way when the factor is, for example, $\text{g/MJ}$ and the energy is in megajoules.

Way of producing energy $e\ (\text{g/kWh})$
Coal 820
Natural gas 490
Biomass 230
Solar power 41–48
Hydropower 24
Nuclear power 12
Wind power 11–12

The source of the table is the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2014), Working Group III, Annex III: medians of life-cycle emissions, rounded, in the unit grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilowatt-hour. The lower limit for solar power is for rooftop panels and the upper limit for utility-scale plants; the lower limit for wind power is onshore and the upper limit offshore. The figure for biomass applies to biomass grown for energy. It includes the emissions of growing, harvesting, transport and the power plant, but the carbon dioxide from burning is counted as zero, because new growth binds it back. So the figure does not show that the binding takes decades.

In the figure all the bars are drawn to the same scale. The dark bars are fossil fuels and the light bars are other ways of producing energy. The emission factor of coal is about $70$ times that of nuclear and wind power ($820 : 12 \approx 68$). The bars of nuclear and wind power are narrow but not zero: their life cycles also include building and transport.

Common misconception

"Wind, solar and nuclear power are emission-free." They are low-emission, not emission-free: building the plants, making the fuel and transport cause emissions, about $12\,\text{g/kWh}$ for wind and nuclear power. For coal the figure is about $820\,\text{g/kWh}$.

"The greenhouse effect is a harmful phenomenon." The natural greenhouse effect is necessary, because without it the mean temperature of the Earth would be about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The problem is the strengthening of the effect when carbon dioxide and methane are added to the atmosphere.

Summary

  • Greenhouse gases let most of the Sun's radiation through but slow down the escape of thermal radiation from the ground. The natural greenhouse effect raises the mean temperature from about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, strengthens the greenhouse effect and leads to climate change.
  • The emission is the emission factor times the energy: $m = e \cdot E$. Ways of producing energy are compared by life-cycle emission factors, which range from about $12\,\text{g/kWh}$ (nuclear and wind power) to about $820\,\text{g/kWh}$ (coal).
  • The emission factor does not tell everything: land use, transport, waste, safety and the availability of electricity are weighed separately when looking for a sustainable energy economy.

    Optional Find the error
    A student calculates how much carbon dioxide equivalent is produced when $3600\,\text{kWh}$ of electricity is produced with wind power ($e = 12\,\text{g/kWh}$).The student writes

    $$\begin{aligned} m &= E : e \\ &= 3600\,\text{kWh} : 12\,\text{g/kWh} \\ &= 300\,\text{kg} \end{aligned}$$

    Where is the error?

    • Wind power is not emission-free: its life-cycle emission factor is $11\text{–}12\,\text{g/kWh}$, because the turbine is made, transported and built. The principle of the calculation ($m = e \cdot E$) is still correct; the error is in the calculation itself.

    • Correct. The unit reveals the error: $\text{kWh} : \text{g/kWh}$ does not give a mass but $\text{kWh}^2/\text{g}$. In the multiplication $\text{g/kWh} \cdot \text{kWh}$ the kilowatt-hours cancel and grams are left. The result $43\,\text{kg}$ is also reasonable: the same electricity from gas would give about $41$ times more.

    • The unit is not the only error. The division gives the wrong size and the wrong unit ($\text{kWh}^2/\text{g}$). With multiplication the result is $43\,\text{kg}$, not $300\,\text{kg}$.

    • The emission factor is given exactly in the unit $\text{g/kWh}$, so the energy in kilowatt-hours can be used directly. Converting to megajoules would need $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$ and would make the calculation needlessly complicated.

    Where should the wood chips come from?

    An example municipality builds a heating plant that burns forest wood chips. The chips could be brought by lorry either from the municipality's own forests about $50\,\text{km}$ away or from another region about $400\,\text{km}$ away.     Which source is better for the climate, and which criterion decides?

      Optional 3. Explain

      Explain why the natural greenhouse effect is useful but its strengthening is a problem. Also say why burning fossil fuels strengthens the effect.

      Model answer

      The Sun warms the ground with radiation that the greenhouse gases of the atmosphere let mostly through. The warm ground sends out energy as thermal radiation, which the greenhouse gases absorb and partly radiate back down. That is why the ground is at about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and not at about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the effect keeps the Earth habitable. When fossil fuels burn, their carbon combines with oxygen and is released as carbon dioxide. When there is more of the gas, thermal radiation escapes more slowly than before, so the effect gets stronger and the mean temperature rises: that is climate change.

          the field area needed is $50$ times the area of the solar park ($100\,\text{km}^2$ and $2.0\,\text{km}^2$), two significant figures like the yields $0.50\,\text{GWh/ha}$ and $0.010\,\text{GWh/ha}$.

          Reasonableness

          $100\,\text{km}^2$ is a square with a side of $10\,\text{km}$. The ratio of the areas $100 : 2.0 = 50$ is the same as the ratio of the yields $0.50 : 0.010 = 50$, as it should be, because the energy is the same.

          Conclusion

          Biomass electricity needs about $50$ times the area of a solar park, a square of field with a side of ten kilometres, which could otherwise grow food or forest. The emission factor tells only the greenhouse gases per kilowatt-hour; it does not tell how much land a way of producing energy takes from food production, nature and the landscape. That is why land use must be weighed separately.

        3. Example situation: a city bus uses $30$ litres of diesel per $100\,\text{km}$ and has $25$ passengers. The energy content of diesel is $36\,\text{MJ/}\textsf{l}$ and the emission factor of burning is $74\,\text{g/MJ}$. A petrol car driven by one person uses $6.0$ litres per $100\,\text{km}$ ($32\,\text{MJ/}\textsf{l}$ and $69\,\text{g/MJ}$).

          How many times as large is the emission from burning of the car per passenger compared with that of the bus?

          kertaa
          Answer

          4.1 kertaa

          Show the solution

          Given

          bus $30\,\textsf{l}$ per hundred kilometres, $36\,\text{MJ/}\textsf{l}$, $74\,\text{g/MJ}$, $25$ passengers; car $6.0\,\textsf{l}$ per hundred kilometres, $32\,\text{MJ/}\textsf{l}$, $69\,\text{g/MJ}$, one passenger

          Find

          ratio of the emission of the car to the emission of the bus per passenger

          Principle

          Calculate the emission of each vehicle per hundred kilometres as the factor times the energy and divide the emission of the bus among the passengers. Then compare the emissions of one person by division.

          Formula

          $m_\text{bus} = e_\text{b} \cdot V_\text{b} \cdot h_\text{b} : N$, $m_\text{car} = e_\text{c} \cdot V_\text{c} \cdot h_\text{c}$ and the ratio is $m_\text{car} : m_\text{bus}$, where $N$ is the number of passengers on the bus, $e$ the emission factor, $V$ the amount of fuel and $h$ the energy content per litre

          Conversions

          Not needed: the litres and megajoules cancel, and the emissions come out in grams.

          Substitution

          $m_\text{bus}$$= 74\,\text{g/MJ} \cdot 30\,\textsf{l} \cdot 36\,\text{MJ/}\textsf{l} : 25$
          $= 79\,920\,\text{g} : 25$
          $= 3196.8\,\text{g}$
          $m_\text{car}$$= 69\,\text{g/MJ} \cdot 6.0\,\textsf{l} \cdot 32\,\text{MJ/}\textsf{l}$
          $= 13\,248\,\text{g}$

          and $13\,248\,\text{g} : 3196.8\,\text{g} = 4.14$

          Answer

          $4.1$

          Times, two significant figures like the consumptions $6.0\,\textsf{l}$ and $30\,\textsf{l}$.

          Reasonableness

          The bus uses five times as much fuel as the car, but its load is shared among $25$ passengers, so the emission per passenger is smaller. With rounded numbers $13\,000 : 3200 \approx 4$.
