## fy9-sailymislait-s1

### a)
**Given:** difference in height $h = 36.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; no resistive forces assumed
**Find:** the final speed $v$ without resistive forces
**Principle:** The forces on the rider are the weight and the force of the cable. The force of the cable is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work. When there are no resistive forces, only the weight does work and mechanical energy is conserved (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*). The zero level is chosen at the end of the line.
**Formula:** $mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so the mass cancels and $v = \sqrt{2gh}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 36.0\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{706.3\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 26.58\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 26.6\,\text{m/s}$, or about $96\,\text{km/h}$, three significant figures like the height $36.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The speed is the same as that of a body that has fallen freely through $36\,\text{m}$, as it should be on a frictionless path. Almost $100\,\text{km/h}$ would be too much for a zip line, so the resistive forces matter a lot.

### b)
**Given:** $m = 82.0\,\text{kg}$, $h = 36.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, final speed $v = 15.0\,\text{m/s}$, length of the cable $s = 410\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the work done by the resistive forces $W_\text{other}$ and the average magnitude $F$ of the resistive forces along the cable
**Principle:** Air resistance and the wheel resistance are forces other than the weight. By the work–energy principle their work equals the change in mechanical energy (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*). The resistive forces point against the motion, so the work is negative, and its magnitude is the average force times the distance travelled. The zero level is at the end of the line.
**Formula:** $mgh + W_\text{other} = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so $W_\text{other} = \tfrac12 mv^2 - mgh$ and $F = \dfrac{|W_\text{other}|}{s}$
**Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{J} = 0.001\,\text{kJ}$. The other quantities are in base units.
**Substitution:** $\tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 82.0\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(15.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 = 9225\,\text{J}$ and $mgh = 82.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 36.0\,\text{m} = 28\,959\,\text{J}$, so $W_\text{other} = 9225\,\text{J} - 28\,959\,\text{J} = -19\,734\,\text{J} = -19.7\,\text{kJ}$. The force is $F = \dfrac{19\,734\,\text{J}}{410\,\text{m}} = 48.13\,\text{N}$.
**Answer:** The resistive forces do the work $W_\text{other} \approx -19.7\,\text{kJ}$, and their average magnitude is $F \approx 48.1\,\text{N}$, three significant figures like the given data.
**Reasonableness:** The resistive forces take about $68\,\%$ of the potential energy, which fits a high speed and a rider in a wide posture. The average force is only about $6\,\%$ of the weight $804\,\text{N}$, but it acts over a long distance.

### c)
**Given:** $m = 82.0\,\text{kg}$, speed before the brake $v = 15.0\,\text{m/s}$, final speed $0$, braking time $\Delta t = 1.6\,\text{s}$, cable horizontal
**Find:** the average horizontal force $F_\text{avg}$ of the brake on the trolley and its comparison with the weight; the system is the rider, the harness and the trolley together ($82.0\,\text{kg}$)
**Principle:** On the horizontal cable the horizontal momentum of the system (rider, harness and trolley) changes only because of the force of the brake, when air resistance is neglected here as small. By the impulse–momentum principle the impulse of the force equals the change in momentum (chapter *Momentum and impulse*).
**Formula:** $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = |\Delta p| = mv$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{mv}{\Delta t}$; weight $G = mg$ and the ratio $\dfrac{F_\text{avg}}{G}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{82.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 15.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}{1.6\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{1230\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}}{1.6\,\text{s}} = 768.8\,\text{N}$. The weight is $mg = 82.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 804.4\,\text{N}$, and the ratio is $768.8\,\text{N} : 804.4\,\text{N} \approx 0.96$.
**Answer:** $F_\text{avg} \approx 770\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the braking time $1.6\,\text{s}$. The force is almost as large as the combined weight of the rider, the harness and the trolley.
**Reasonableness:** The deceleration is $15.0\,\text{m/s} : 1.6\,\text{s} \approx 9.4\,\text{m/s}^2$, about the same as the acceleration of free fall. It feels like a strong but short jerk, and the harness spreads the force over the whole body.

### d)
**Find:** whether the attendant's claim and its reasoning are correct
**Principle:** Without resistive forces mechanical energy is conserved and the mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. The work $W_\text{other}$ done by the resistive forces reduces the mechanical energy (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*).
**Model answer:** The reasoning is wrong. Gravity does pull a light rider less strongly, but setting a lighter rider in motion takes proportionally less energy: without resistive forces the mass cancels out of the energy equation, and everyone would arrive at the speed $\sqrt{2gh} \approx 26.6\,\text{m/s}$. The observation may still be true because of the resistive forces. Air resistance depends on the size and posture of the rider but is not proportional to the mass. Of the mechanical energy of a light rider, which is proportional to the mass, air resistance therefore takes a larger share, and the rider arrives more slowly. So the cause is air resistance, not weaker gravity.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a, $v = 2gh$ is written and the square root forgotten, which also makes the unit wrong, $\text{m}^2\text{/s}^2$.
- In part b, the work is given as positive or calculated from the potential energy $mgh$ without the kinetic energy.
- In part b, the force is calculated from the difference in height $36.0\,\text{m}$ and not from the distance travelled $410\,\text{m}$; work is force times the distance along the motion.
- In part c, the force is taken to be $mv$ without the time, or the result is divided by the braking time twice.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part c via the deceleration: $a = \dfrac{15.0\,\text{m/s}}{1.6\,\text{s}}$ and $F = ma$, the same result.

## fy9-sailymislait-s2

### a)
**Given:** $v_0 = 2.40\,\text{m/s}$, speed before the impact $v_1 = 0.62\,\text{m/s}$, distance $s = 27.0\,\text{m}$, $m = 19.0\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the coefficient of friction $\mu$
**Principle:** The ice is horizontal, so the stone does not accelerate vertically, and the normal force equals the weight: $N = mg$. Kinetic friction $F_\mu = \mu N = \mu mg$ is constant and points against the motion, so its work is $-\mu mgs$. The potential energy does not change, so by the work–energy principle the change in kinetic energy is the work done by friction (chapters *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle* and *Weight, friction and the inclined plane*).
**Formula:** $\tfrac12 mv_0^2 - \mu mgs = \tfrac12 mv_1^2$, so the mass cancels and $\mu = \dfrac{v_0^2 - v_1^2}{2gs}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\mu = \dfrac{\left(2.40\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 - \left(0.62\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 27.0\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{5.376\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}{529.7\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 0.01015$
**Answer:** $\mu \approx 0.010$, two significant figures like the speed $0.62\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The coefficient of friction is very small, as it should be for a stone sliding on smooth ice: friction is only about one per cent of the stone's weight. The work done by friction is $-0.01015 \cdot 19.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 27.0\,\text{m} \approx -51\,\text{J}$, and the kinetic energy decreased by exactly that much: $\tfrac12 \cdot 19.0\,\text{kg} \cdot (5.76 - 0.384)\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} \approx 51\,\text{J}$.

### b)
**Given:** mass of each stone $m = 19.0\,\text{kg}$; before, $v_1 = 0.62\,\text{m/s}$ and the other stone at rest; after, the velocity of the thrown stone $v_1' = 0.05\,\text{m/s}$ in the same direction
**Find:** the velocity $v_2'$ of the struck stone and whether the collision is elastic
**Principle:** The forces between the stones are internal. The collision lasts only an instant, and friction is small, so the impulse of the external forces is negligible and the total momentum is conserved (chapter *Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions*). The collision is elastic if kinetic energy is also conserved. The direction of the throw is positive.
**Formula:** $mv_1 = mv_1' + mv_2'$, so the mass cancels and $v_2' = v_1 - v_1'$; kinetic energies $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 mv_1^2$ and $E_\text{k}' = \tfrac12 mv_1'^2 + \tfrac12 mv_2'^2$; share $\dfrac{E_\text{k}'}{E_\text{k}}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v_2' = 0.62\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 0.05\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 0.57\,\text{m/s}$. Kinetic energies: $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 \cdot 19.0\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(0.62\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 = 3.652\,\text{J}$ and $E_\text{k}' = \tfrac12 \cdot 19.0\,\text{kg} \cdot \left[\left(0.05\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 + \left(0.57\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2\right] = 3.110\,\text{J}$, so $E_\text{k}' : E_\text{k} = 0.852$.
**Answer:** $v_2' \approx 0.57\,\text{m/s}$ in the direction of the throw. The result is a difference, so it is precise to a hundredth of a metre per second: the $0.05\,\text{m/s}$ read from the video is known only to about $0.01\,\text{m/s}$, and that limits the result. About $85\,\%$ of the kinetic energy is conserved, so the collision is not perfectly elastic but close to it.
**Reasonableness:** In a perfectly elastic collision stones of equal mass would exchange velocities: the thrown stone would stop, and the other would move on at $0.62\,\text{m/s}$. Granite is hard, so an almost elastic collision is to be expected, and about $0.5\,\text{J}$ is transformed into sound and internal energy of the stones.

### c)
**Given:** $v_2' = 0.57\,\text{m/s}$, $\mu = 0.01015$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, final speed $0$
**Find:** the sliding distance $s_2$ of the struck stone
**Principle:** The same work–energy principle as in part a: the work $-\mu mgs_2$ done by friction transforms all the kinetic energy into internal energy. The ice is the same, so the coefficient of friction is the same.
**Formula:** $\tfrac12 mv_2'^2 - \mu mgs_2 = 0$, so $s_2 = \dfrac{v_2'^2}{2\mu g}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $s_2 = \dfrac{\left(0.57\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2}{2 \cdot 0.01015 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}} = \dfrac{0.3249\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}{0.1991\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}} = 1.632\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $s_2 \approx 1.6\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the speed $0.57\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** With the same friction the sliding distance is proportional to the square of the speed. The thrown stone would have stopped from $2.40\,\text{m/s}$ over the distance $\dfrac{(2.40\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 0.01015 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 28.9\,\text{m}$, and $(0.57 : 2.40)^2 \cdot 28.9\,\text{m} \approx 1.6\,\text{m}$ agrees. The distance is short, because the stone has only about $6\,\%$ of the original kinetic energy left.

### d)
**Find:** why a swept stone slides further, even though the brooms do not touch the stone
**Principle:** By the work–energy principle friction does the negative work $-\mu mgs$, and the sliding distance is $s = \dfrac{v^2}{2\mu g}$ (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*).
**Model answer:** At the moment of release the stone has a certain kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$, and as it slides this is transformed, through the negative work done by friction, into internal energy of the stone and the ice. Each metre takes the amount $\mu mg$ of kinetic energy. When sweeping reduces the coefficient of friction, less kinetic energy is used per metre, so the same kinetic energy lasts for a longer distance: the distance is inversely proportional to the coefficient of friction. So sweeping does not add energy to the stone, and the stone does not need to touch the broom: sweeping only changes the ice in front of the stone so that the work done by friction per metre is smaller.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a, potential energy is included in the work–energy principle even though the ice is horizontal, or the speeds are subtracted before squaring: $(2.40 - 0.62)^2$ is not the same as $2.40^2 - 0.62^2$.
- In part b, kinetic energy is assumed to be conserved and the speed calculated from it; conservation of momentum is enough, and the kinetic energy is checked only afterwards.
- In part c, the distance and speeds of the throw are used in a direct proportion, that is, the distance is assumed to be proportional to the speed and not to its square.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a with kinematics: in uniformly decelerated motion $a = \dfrac{v_0^2 - v_1^2}{2s}$ and $\mu = a : g$, the same result.

### c)
**Given:** the graph of part b and the points read from its line
**Find:** the reaction time $t_\text{r}$ and the deceleration $a$
**Principle:** In the linearised model $\dfrac{s_\text{p}}{v_0} = t_\text{r} + \dfrac{1}{2a}\,v_0$ the intercept is the reaction time and the slope is $k = \dfrac{1}{2a}$. The slope is read from two points of the line far apart, not from measured points (chapter *Graphing and fitting a straight line*).
**Reading the graph:** From the line we read $(10\,\text{m/s};\ 1.66\,\text{s})$ and $(25\,\text{m/s};\ 2.86\,\text{s})$. The extension of the line crosses the vertical axis at about $0.86\,\text{s}$.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{\Delta (s_\text{p}/v_0)}{\Delta v_0}$, $t_\text{r} = y_1 - k v_1$ from a point of the line and $a = \dfrac{1}{2k}$, where $y = s_\text{p}/v_0$ and $(v_1;\ y_1)$ and $(v_2;\ y_2)$ are points of the line; $k = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{v_2 - v_1}$
**Conversions:** The speeds are already in the unit $\text{m/s}$, so the unit of the slope is $\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{m/s}} = \tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$ and that of the deceleration $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{2.86\,\text{s} - 1.66\,\text{s}}{25\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}} = \dfrac{1.20\,\text{s}}{15\,\text{m/s}} = 0.0800\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$, $t_\text{r} = 1.66\,\text{s} - 0.0800\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}} \cdot 10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 0.86\,\text{s}$ and $a = \dfrac{1}{2 \cdot 0.0800\,\text{s}^2\text{/m}} = 6.25\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** The reaction time is $t_\text{r} \approx 0.86\,\text{s}$ and the deceleration $a \approx 6.3\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures, because the values are read from the graph to about $0.02\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** A reaction time of less than a second is typical for an alert driver who is waiting for a signal. The deceleration is smaller than the roughly $9\,\text{m/s}^2$ of dry asphalt, as it should be on a wet road. A calculator fit gives $t_\text{r} = 0.864\,\text{s}$ and $a = 6.26\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the values read from the graph are the same.

### d)
**Given:** from part c $t_\text{r} = 0.86\,\text{s}$ and $a = 6.25\,\text{m/s}^2$; $v_0 = 100\,\text{km/h}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the coefficient of friction $\mu$ and the stopping distance $s_\text{p}$ from $100\,\text{km/h}$, and the reliability of the estimate
**Principle:** On a horizontal road the normal force equals the weight, and when braking as hard as the grip allows, friction $\mu mg$ is the only horizontal force. By Newton's second law $\mu mg = ma$ (chapters *Weight, friction and the inclined plane* and *Road safety: braking, seat belts and airbags*). The stopping distance is calculated with the same model that the straight line confirmed.
**Formula:** $\mu = \dfrac{a}{g}$ and $s_\text{p} = v_0 t_\text{r} + \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$
**Conversions:** $100\,\text{km/h} = 100 \cdot \dfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 27.78\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $\mu = \dfrac{6.25\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = 0.637$ and $s_\text{p} = 27.78\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 0.86\,\text{s} + \dfrac{\left(27.78\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2}{2 \cdot 6.25\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}} = 23.9\,\text{m} + 61.7\,\text{m} = 85.6\,\text{m}$

At a high speed on a wet road, however, the grip of the tyres can weaken, even to the point of aquaplaning, and in ordinary traffic the driver is not waiting for a signal, so the real distance may be longer.
**Reasonableness:** A coefficient of friction of $0.64$ is plausible for wet asphalt, smaller than the roughly $0.9$ of dry asphalt. ### a)
**Find:** whether claim 1 is correct, and what the power of a wind turbine depends on
**Principle:** The power of the wind is the kinetic energy of the air flowing through the rotor per second, and the turbine converts the share $\eta$ of it into electricity: $P = \tfrac12 \eta \rho A v^3$ (chapter *Hydro and wind power as energy converters*).
**Model answer:** The claim is wrong, because it assumes the power is proportional to the wind speed. The power depends on the speed in two ways. Each kilogram of air has the kinetic energy $\tfrac12 v^2$, which grows with the square of the speed. In addition, the mass of air $\rho A v$ flows through the rotor every second, and it grows with the speed. That is why the power is proportional to the cube of the speed. When the speed increases from $6\,\text{m/s}$ to $9\,\text{m/s}$, the power increases by a factor of $\dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = \left(\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^3 = \left(\tfrac{9}{6}\right)^3 = 3.375$, that is, by about $240\,\%$ and not by $50\,\%$. This holds when the efficiency stays roughly the same and the turbine is not yet running at its rated power; above it the blades are turned and the power is limited.

### b)
**Find:** whether claim 2 is correct, and what the energy chain of a hydropower plant is
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: it changes from one form to another but is not created from nothing (chapter *Forms of energy and conservation of energy*). A hydropower plant converts the potential energy $mgh$ of the water into electricity, and the efficiency is at most $100\,\%$ (chapters *Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy* and *Hydro and wind power as energy converters*).
**Model answer:** The claim is wrong. The plant does not produce energy from nothing but converts it. The water behind the dam has potential energy compared with the tailwater: one cubic metre of water loses about $1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 9.0\,\text{m} \approx 88\,\text{kJ}$ in a fall of $9.0\,\text{m}$. In the penstock the potential energy is transformed into kinetic energy, the flowing water turns the turbine, and the turbine turns the generator, which converts the energy of motion into electricity. The energy of the water comes from the Sun: solar radiation evaporates water, which rises into the air and falls as rain at a higher level in the catchment area. So the same water does not circulate through the plant by itself; the Sun “lifts” it again every time. Not all the energy the water loses is converted into electricity: friction in the penstock and the turbine and the heating of the generator transform part of it into internal energy, and the water discharged into the tailwater still has kinetic energy. That is why the efficiency is below $100\,\%$, here $90\,\%$.

### c)
**Find:** whether claim 3 is correct, and what the present electric power of the plant is
**Principle:** The electric power of a hydropower plant is $P = \eta \rho Q g h$: the mass of water $\rho Q$ flowing per second loses the potential energy $\rho Q g h$, of which the share $\eta$ is converted into electricity (chapter *Hydro and wind power as energy converters*).
**Model answer:** The present electric power is $P = 0.90 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 120\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 9.0\,\text{m} \approx 9.54 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W} \approx 9.5\,\text{MW}$. The power is directly proportional both to the flow rate $Q$ and to the head $h$. Raising the dam from $9\,\text{m}$ to $12\,\text{m}$ increases the head by a factor of $\dfrac{P'}{P} = \dfrac{h'}{h} = \tfrac{12}{9} = \tfrac43$, that is, by a third, and the power in the same ratio to about $12.7\,\text{MW}$. A flow rate a third larger would increase the power by exactly the same amount. So the claim is correct, provided the efficiency stays the same and raising the dam does not change the flow rate of the river. In practice the flow rate depends on the rainfall and the catchment area, so it cannot be increased, but the height can.

## fy9-sailymislait-s5

### a)
**Given:** $m_\text{A} = 0.520\,\text{kg}$, $m_\text{B} = 0.480\,\text{kg}$; in test 2 $v_\text{A} = 0.622\,\text{m/s}$ and the common velocity $v' = 0.303\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the relative change in the total momentum in per cent
**Principle:** Before the collision only trolley A moves, so the total momentum is its momentum. Afterwards the trolleys move together, so the momentum is the total mass times the common velocity (chapter *Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions*). The relative change is the change divided by the original value.
**Formula:** $p = m_\text{A} v_\text{A}$, $p' = (m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})\,v'$ and the change $\dfrac{\Delta p}{p} = \dfrac{p' - p}{p} = \dfrac{(m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})\,v' - m_\text{A} v_\text{A}}{m_\text{A} v_\text{A}}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units. The ratio is turned into per cent by multiplying by $100\,\%$.
**Substitution:** $p = 0.520\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.622\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 0.3234\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ and $p' = 1.000\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.303\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 0.3030\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$, so $\dfrac{0.3030 - 0.3234}{0.3234} = -0.0632 = -6.3\,\%$
**Answer:** The total momentum decreased by about $6.3\,\%$, two significant figures, because the difference of two nearly equal numbers is less precise than the numbers themselves.
**Reasonableness:** If momentum were conserved exactly, the common velocity would be $0.3234\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} : 1.000\,\text{kg} \approx 0.323\,\text{m/s}$; the measured $0.303\,\text{m/s}$ is only $0.02\,\text{m/s}$ smaller. In the other tests the change is $-11.9\,\%$, $-3.7\,\%$ and $-2.9\,\%$.

### b)
**Find:** whether the group's conclusion is justified, and what the difference between the measurements is due to
**Principle:** The total momentum is conserved when the impulse of the external forces is negligible (chapter *Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions*). In an inelastic collision it is kinetic energy that is transformed, not momentum. A systematic error shifts all the results in the same direction (chapter *Measuring and the reliability of a measurement*).
**Model answer:** The conclusion is not justified. What is turned into heat and sound in an inelastic collision is kinetic energy: here too about half of the kinetic energy disappeared in test 2. Momentum is not a form of energy, and it cannot be turned into heat. The difference is most likely due to the way of measuring. The common velocity was measured only $0.40$–$0.80\,\text{m}$ after the point of collision, and on an ordinary track rolling resistance slows the trolleys down all the time. Rolling resistance is an external force, so its impulse reduces the momentum while the trolleys travel to the measuring range. This is supported by the difference being systematic, since the momentum decreased in every test, and by the relative difference being largest in the slowest test ($-12\,\%$) and smallest in the fastest ($-3\,\%$). Slow trolleys spend longer on the way, and a resistive force of constant magnitude reduces a small velocity relatively more. The deceleration need not be large: in test 2 the difference corresponds to a deceleration of only about $0.01\,\text{m/s}^2$. So the data do not show that momentum is not conserved.

### c)
**Find:** an improved experimental setup for testing conservation of momentum
**Principle:** A conservation law can be tested only when the effect of external forces between the measurements is small and the uncertainty of the measurements is known (chapters *Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions* and *Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting*).
**Model answer:** The velocities are measured as close to the collision as possible: for example light gates just before and just after the point of collision, or video analysis is used to draw a $(t, v)$ graph of each trolley and the velocities at the moment of collision are read from the extensions of the lines. The effect of rolling resistance is reduced by using an air track or by tilting the track slightly so that a trolley pushed on its own moves at a constant speed. The masses of the trolleys are weighed on a balance together with their hook-and-loop strips. Each test is repeated several times, and the uncertainty of the velocity and the mass is used to estimate how large a difference between the momenta can arise from measurement uncertainty alone. In addition, the masses and velocities are varied: if momentum is conserved in all tests within the uncertainty, the result supports the conservation law. From the same measurements the kinetic energy before and after is calculated, and the transformed share is compared with the theoretical value $\dfrac{m_\text{B}}{m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}} = 48\,\%$, so that the report distinguishes momentum from energy.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a, the momentum after the collision is calculated with the mass of trolley A only, even though the trolleys move together.
- In part b, the conclusion is accepted because “something is lost in an inelastic collision”; what is lost is kinetic energy, not momentum.
- In part b, the difference is explained by random error, even though all the results deviate in the same direction.
- In part c, only repeats are suggested; repeats reduce random error but do not remove systematic error.

## fy9-sailymislait-s6

### a)
**Given:** drop height $h = 0.60\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the ram just before the impact
**Principle:** The ram falls freely, and air resistance is small for such a short drop, so only the weight does work and mechanical energy is conserved (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*). The zero level is the top of the pile.
**Formula:** $Mgh = \tfrac12 Mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{11.77\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 3.431\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 3.4\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the height $0.60\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** A drop of half a metre gives a speed of a few metres per second, as for example in a jump from a chair to the floor. The kinetic energy of the ram is $Mgh = 3500\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m} \approx 20.6\,\text{kJ}$.

### b)
**Given:** $M = 3500\,\text{kg}$, $m = 1100\,\text{kg}$, $v = 3.431\,\text{m/s}$, pile at rest; the ram does not bounce
**Find:** the common velocity $v'$ and the kinetic energy $E_\text{loss}$ transformed into other forms of energy
**Principle:** The collision lasts a very short time, so the impulse of the force from the ground and of the weight during it is small compared with the impulse of the forces between the bodies. That is why the total momentum is conserved (chapter *Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions*). The ram and the pile move together, so the collision is perfectly inelastic, and kinetic energy is transformed into internal energy and sound. Downwards is the positive direction.
**Formula:** $Mv = (M + m)\,v'$, so $v' = \dfrac{Mv}{M + m}$; $E_\text{loss} = \tfrac12 Mv^2 - \tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v' = \dfrac{3500\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.431\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}{4600\,\text{kg}} = 2.611\,\text{m/s}$; $\tfrac12 Mv^2 = 20\,601\,\text{J}$ and $\tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 4600\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(2.611\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 = 15\,675\,\text{J}$, so $E_\text{loss} = 20\,601\,\text{J} - 15\,675\,\text{J} = 4926\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $v' \approx 2.6\,\text{m/s}$ and $E_\text{loss} \approx 4.9\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the drop height.
**Reasonableness:** In a perfectly inelastic collision with a body at rest the share of kinetic energy transformed is $\dfrac{m}{M + m} = \dfrac{1100}{4600} \approx 24\,\%$, and $4926 : 20\,601 \approx 0.24$ agrees. That is why the ram is made much heavier than the pile: a larger share of the energy stays in the motion that pushes the pile into the ground.

### c)
**Given:** $M + m = 4600\,\text{kg}$, $v' = 2.611\,\text{m/s}$, kinetic energy after the collision $15\,675\,\text{J}$, depth of sinking $s = 18\,\text{mm}$, final speed $0$
**Find:** the average force $F$ of the ground on the pile
**Principle:** After the collision the ram and the pile move together through the distance $s$ downwards and stop. The work done by the weight is taken into account as the decrease in potential energy $(M + m)\,gs$. The force of the ground points against the motion, so its work is $-Fs$. By the work–energy principle the initial kinetic and potential energy plus the work done by the ground's force equal the final energy (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*). The zero level is the final position of the pile head.
**Formula:** $\tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2 + (M + m)\,gs - Fs = 0$, so $F = \dfrac{\tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2}{s} + (M + m)\,g$
**Conversions:** $18\,\text{mm} = 18 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m} = 0.018\,\text{m}$ and finally $1\,\text{N} = 0.001\,\text{kN}$
**Substitution:** $F = \dfrac{15\,675\,\text{J}}{0.018\,\text{m}} + 4600\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 870\,800\,\text{N} + 45\,100\,\text{N} = 915\,900\,\text{N} \approx 916\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $F \approx 0.92\,\text{MN}$, two significant figures like the depth $18\,\text{mm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The force is about $20$ times the weight of the ram and the pile, $45\,\text{kN}$, because the kinetic energy is stopped over a very short distance. The weight affects the result by only about $5\,\%$, so forgetting it would be a small but real mistake.

### d)
**Given:** $E_\text{loss} = 4926\,\text{J}$ per blow, share $k = 40\,\%$, number of blows $N = 50$, warming mass $m_\text{t} = 30\,\text{kg}$, $c = 460\,\text{J/(kg} \cdot \text{K)}$
**Find:** the rise in temperature $\Delta T$ of the top of the pile and the effect of the assumptions
**Principle:** The kinetic energy transformed in the collision does not disappear: part of it becomes internal energy of the steel at the top of the pile. When the steel gives off no heat to the surroundings and its state does not change, the increase in internal energy raises the temperature, and $\Delta U = cm\Delta T$ holds (chapter *Heating and cooling of matter*).
**Formula:** $\Delta U = N k E_\text{loss} = c\,m_\text{t}\,\Delta T$, so $\Delta T = \dfrac{N k E_\text{loss}}{c\,m_\text{t}}$, where $N$ is the number of blows and $k$ is the share that becomes internal energy of the pile top
**Conversions:** $40\,\% = 0.40$; the other quantities are in base units. A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius.
**Substitution:** $\Delta T = \dfrac{50 \cdot 0.40 \cdot 4926\,\text{J}}{460\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 30\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{98\,520\,\text{J}}{13\,800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}} = 7.14\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $\Delta T \approx 7\,\text{K}$, one significant figure, because the share $40\,\%$ and the warming mass $30\,\text{kg}$ are only estimates. The real warming is smaller: the steel conducts heat to the lower parts of the pile and gives it off to the air between the blows, and part of the energy is transformed into sound and internal energy of the ram.
**Reasonableness:** One blow warms the top by only about $0.14\,\text{K}$, but there are many blows. A warming of a few degrees fits the fact that the head of the pile feels warm but not hot after a series of blows.
