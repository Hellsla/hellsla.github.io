Mechanical energy and momentum

Why do two railway wagons that couple together move on more slowly than before coupling, even though nothing brakes them?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve energy, impulse and collision problems by choosing the right conservation law
  • draw and interpret graphs of braking and collision data
  • assess claims and measurements about the conservation of momentum and energy with justification

Prior knowledge

  • Kinetic energy is $\tfrac12 mv^2$ and potential energy $mgh$chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy
  • The work done by forces other than the weight changes the mechanical energychapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle
  • The impulse of the net force equals the change in momentumchapter Momentum and impulse
  • The total momentum is conserved when the impulse of the external forces is smallchapter Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions
  • The stopping distance is the reaction distance plus the braking distancechapter Road safety: braking, seat belts and airbags

1Think about it

In a railway yard a shunting locomotive pushes a $32\,\text{t}$ goods wagon into motion and brakes. The wagon rolls freely at $1.6\,\text{m/s}$ and collides with an identical wagon standing still. The coupling locks, and the wagons move on together. Nobody brakes, and the rolling resistance of the rails is very small during the collision. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the law is not.

How fast do the wagons move on together, and do they have as much kinetic energy together as the rolling wagon had before the collision?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting point 1: the speed doubles

A car accelerates from $40\,\text{km/h}$ to $80\,\text{km/h}$. By what factor does the kinetic energy of the car increase?

  • Correct. $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so the factor is $\left(\tfrac{80}{40}\right)^2 = 4$. If the average deceleration stays the same, the braking distance also becomes four times as long. Review this if needed in the chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy.

  • The mass stays the same, but the kinetic energy also depends on the speed, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 mv^2$, and even on its square. Review this in the chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy.

  • Kinetic energy is not proportional to the speed but to its square, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 mv^2$. Twice the speed gives a factor of $2^2 = 4$. Review this in the chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy.

Starting point 2: a steep and a gentle slide

Two frictionless slides start at the same height and end at the same level. One is steep and short, the other gentle and long. A child starts from rest on each.On which slide is the child's speed greater at the bottom?

  • A longer time does not give a greater speed, because the acceleration is smaller on the gentle slide. Without friction the mechanical energy is conserved, and the speed depends only on the difference in height. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • On the steep slide the acceleration is greater, but on the gentle one it acts over a longer distance. Energy decides: without friction $mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ depends only on the height. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • Correct. The normal force does no work, so the mechanical energy is conserved and $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ on both slides. Review this if needed in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

Starting point 3: friction on the way down

A sledge slides down a hill from rest, and at the foot of the hill its speed is less than $\sqrt{2gh}$.Which statement about friction and energy is correct?

  • Correct. By the work–energy principle $mgh + W_\text{other} = \tfrac12 mv^2$, and the work done by friction $W_\text{other}$ is negative. The total energy is conserved when the internal energy of the snow, the runners and the air is included. Review this if needed in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • The total energy is always conserved. Friction transforms mechanical energy into internal energy: the snow, the runners and the air warm up slightly. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • The change in potential energy depends only on the difference in height, $mgh$, and friction does not change it. Friction does negative work, which reduces the kinetic energy. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

Starting point 4: mat or floor

A gymnast jumps down from an apparatus, once onto a thick mat and once onto a hard floor. Both times she hits the surface at the same speed and stops.Why does the mat protect her?

  • The change in momentum is the same both times: the momentum changes from $mv$ to zero. The mat makes the stopping time longer, so the same change is produced by a smaller average force. Review this in the chapter Momentum and impulse.

  • Stopping always requires an impulse of magnitude $|\Delta p| = mv$. The mat transforms the kinetic energy into internal energy, but the protective effect comes from the longer stopping time and therefore the smaller force. Review this in the chapter Momentum and impulse.

  • Correct. By the impulse–momentum principle $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = |\Delta p|$, and $|\Delta p|$ is the same. A longer $\Delta t$ means a smaller average force. Review this if needed in the chapter Momentum and impulse.

Starting point 5: a body check on the ice

Two ice hockey players collide and stick together. The friction of the ice is negligible during the collision. What is conserved in the collision?

  • Kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision. When the players stick together, the collision is perfectly inelastic, and part of the kinetic energy is transformed into internal energy. Review this in the chapter Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions.

  • Correct. The impulse of the external forces is small, so momentum is conserved, but in a perfectly inelastic collision the largest possible share of the kinetic energy is transformed into other forms of energy. Review this if needed in the chapter Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions.

  • Exactly the other way round. The forces between the players are internal and opposite, so the total momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy is transformed into internal energy. Review this in the chapter Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions.

Starting point 6: the power of a wind turbine

The wind speed increases from $5\,\text{m/s}$ to $10\,\text{m/s}$, and the efficiency of the turbine stays the same. The turbine is not yet running at its rated power.By what factor does its power increase?

  • The kinetic energy per kilogram becomes four times as large, but twice the amount of air also flows through every second. That is why $P = \tfrac12\eta\rho Av^3$, and the factor is $2^3 = 8$. Review this in the chapter Hydro and wind power as energy converters.

  • Correct. $P = \tfrac12\eta\rho Av^3$, so the factor is $2^3 = 8$. Above the rated power the power is limited, and the formula no longer holds. Review this if needed in the chapter Hydro and wind power as energy converters.

  • At twice the speed, twice the amount of air flows through the rotor every second, and each kilogram has four times the kinetic energy. The power increases by a factor of $2 \cdot 4 = 8$. Review this in the chapter Hydro and wind power as energy converters.

Calculate the kinetic energy before the collision, when only glider A moves, and after it, when both gliders move together.

Show the solution

Given

$m_\text{A} = 0.300\,\text{kg}$, $m_\text{B} = 0.200\,\text{kg}$; in the hook-and-loop collision $v_\text{A} = 0.480\,\text{m/s}$, B at rest, and afterwards the common velocity $v' = 0.284\,\text{m/s}$

Find

the share of the kinetic energy transformed into other forms of energy, in per cent

Principle

The gliders stick together, so the collision is perfectly inelastic. Momentum is conserved, because on the air track the external forces cancel each other, but kinetic energy is transformed into internal energy of the strip and the gliders and into sound. The transformed share is the decrease in kinetic energy divided by the original kinetic energy.

Formula

$E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 m_\text{A} v_\text{A}^2$, $E_\text{k}' = \tfrac12 (m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})\,v'^2$ and the share $\dfrac{E_\text{k} - E_\text{k}'}{E_\text{k}}$

Conversions

All quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed. The ratio is turned into per cent by multiplying by $100\,\%$.

Substitution

$E_\text{k}$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 0.300\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(0.480\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2$
$= 0.03456\,\text{J}$
$E_\text{k}'$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 0.500\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(0.284\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2$
$= 0.02016\,\text{J}$

The share is

$\dfrac{0.03456\,\text{J} - 0.02016\,\text{J}}{0.03456\,\text{J}}$
$= 0.4166$
$= 41.7\,\%$

Answer

about $42\,\%$, two significant figures, because the difference of two nearly equal energies magnifies the uncertainty in the third digit of the velocities.

Reasonableness

In a perfectly inelastic collision with a body at rest the share of kinetic energy transformed is

$\dfrac{m_\text{B}}{m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}}$$= \dfrac{0.200}{0.500}$
$= 40\,\%$

so the result is as expected. Momentum was conserved: $0.144\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ before and $0.142\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ after. With the spring buffer only about $2\,\%$ of the kinetic energy was transformed (almost elastic), with the rubber buffer about $25\,\%$, and momentum was conserved in all three to within about $2\,\%$.

4Theory

Key idea

A large share of the mechanics problems in the exam can be solved with two conservation laws. Energy is always conserved: mechanical energy is transformed into internal energy when friction, air resistance or the deformation of a body does work, but the total energy stays the same. Momentum is conserved when the sum of the external forces on the system is zero or their impulse during the collision is negligible.

A solution starts with choosing the law to suit the situation. When the question asks for a speed or a height at different points and the work done by the forces is known, use the work–energy principle. When bodies collide or push each other, use conservation of momentum. When the question asks for a force and the time is known, use the impulse–momentum principle. Many problems combine them: first energy, then a collision, then energy again.

This also answers the opening question. In the coupling the forces between the wagons are internal, so the momentum $32\,000\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.6\,\text{m/s} = 51\,200\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ is conserved. It is shared by twice the mass, so the speed is halved, $0.80\,\text{m/s}$. The kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$ then decreases from $41\,\text{kJ}$ to $20\,\text{kJ}$: half of it is transformed into internal energy of the buffers and couplings and into sound.

Mechanical energy and momentum

Energy

$$\begin{aligned} E_\text{k} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2 \\ E_\text{p} &= mgh \end{aligned}$$

where $m$ is the mass ($\text{kg}$), $v$ the speed ($\text{m/s}$), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $h$ the height above the chosen zero level ($\text{m}$). $E_\text{p} = mgh$ holds near the Earth's surface. The work–energy principle is

$$E_{\text{k},1} + E_{\text{p},1} + W_\text{other} = E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2}$$

where $W_\text{other}$ is the work done by forces other than the weight ($\text{J}$). The work done by a constant force is $W = F_\parallel\,s$, where $F_\parallel$ is the component of the force along the motion; the work done by friction and air resistance is negative. When $W_\text{other} = 0$, mechanical energy is conserved.

Momentum and impulse

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{p} &= m\overline{v} \\ \Sigma\overline{F}\,\Delta t &= \Delta\overline{p} \end{aligned}$$

where $\overline{p}$ is the momentum ($\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$) and $\Sigma\overline{F}$ a constant net force acting for the time interval $\Delta t$. For a varying force the average force is $F_\text{avg} = |\Delta p| : \Delta t$. In one dimension a positive direction is chosen, and velocities pointing in the opposite direction are negative. When the impulse of the external forces is negligible,

$$m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2'$$

In an elastic collision kinetic energy is also conserved. In a perfectly inelastic collision the bodies move on with a common velocity, $v_1' = v_2' = v'$, and the largest share of kinetic energy is transformed into internal energy.

Applications

On a horizontal road the stopping distance of a car is the reaction distance plus the braking distance:

$$\begin{aligned} s_\text{p} &= v_0\,t_\text{r} + \frac{v_0^2}{2a} \\ P_\text{hydro} &= \eta\rho Qgh \\ P_\text{wind} &= \tfrac12\eta\rho Av^3 \end{aligned}$$

where $t_\text{r}$ is the reaction time ($\text{s}$) and $a$ the deceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$), assumed constant; at the limit of locking the wheels $a = \mu g$. In the electric power of the power plants $\eta$ is the efficiency and $\rho$ the density of water or air; for a hydropower plant $Q$ is the flow rate ($\text{m}^3\text{/s}$) and $h$ the head ($\text{m}$), for a wind turbine $A$ is the area swept by the rotor ($\text{m}^2$) and $v$ the wind speed. The expression for the wind power holds when the efficiency stays constant and the turbine runs below its rated power.

Common mistakes

  • Kinetic energy and momentum mixed up. In a collision momentum is conserved, kinetic energy only in an elastic collision. Momentum is not turned into heat: what is turned into heat is energy.
  • Signs forgotten. A ball that bounces back changes its momentum more than one that stops: for a ball bouncing back at the same speed $\Delta p = -mv - mv = -2mv$.
  • Friction disappears from the work–energy principle. The work done by friction is negative, and it transforms mechanical energy into internal energy; it neither creates nor destroys energy.
  • Kilometres per hour substituted directly. $72\,\text{km/h} = 72 \cdot \tfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20\,\text{m/s}$, before the speed is squared.
  • The square of the speed forgotten. Twice the speed makes the kinetic energy and the braking distance four times as large, and the wind power eight times as large.
  • Conservation of momentum without its condition. Say why the external forces do not change the momentum during the collision.

  • Momentum, impulse and average force: Momentum and impulse. Collisions: Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: a ball bounces off a wall

A student calculated the change in momentum of a $0.20\,\text{kg}$ ball that hits a wall at $5.0\,\text{m/s}$ and bounces straight back at $4.0\,\text{m/s}$: “$\Delta p = 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 4.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 5.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = -0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • Correct. When the direction towards the wall is positive, $\Delta p = 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot (-4.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}) - 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 5.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = -1.8\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$. The impulse the wall exerts on the ball is equal to it.

  • The momentum $p = mv$ changes when the velocity changes, and a mere change of direction also changes the velocity. The mistake is in the sign: the velocity of the ball bouncing back is $-4.0\,\text{m/s}$.

  • The change in momentum needs the mass, and its unit is $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$. The magnitude of the change in velocity is indeed $9.0\,\text{m/s}$ when the signs are taken into account, so $|\Delta p| = 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.0\,\text{m/s}$.

Read the graph: a hard and a soft buffer

In the graph the same $1.0\,\text{kg}$ trolley stops from the same speed, once against a hard and once against a soft buffer. Read the largest forces and the durations of the collisions from the graph.Which statement is correct?

  • Correct. From the graph you read about $80\,\text{N}$ and $0.04\,\text{s}$, and $20\,\text{N}$ and $0.16\,\text{s}$. The trolley stops from the same speed, so $|\Delta p| = mv$ and the impulses are the same. The soft buffer spreads the same impulse over four times the time, so the force is a quarter.

  • You read the largest forces correctly ($80\,\text{N}$ and $20\,\text{N}$), but the impulse is the area between the curve and the time axis, not the largest force. The hard collision lasts only about a quarter of the time, so the areas are equal: the change in the trolley's momentum is the same. Review this in the chapter Momentum and impulse.

  • You read the durations correctly ($0.16\,\text{s}$ and $0.04\,\text{s}$), but a longer time does not increase the impulse when the force is smaller at the same time. The areas are equal: both times the impulse is the same as the change in the trolley's momentum. Review this in the chapter Momentum and impulse.

Justified claim: skaters push off

Two skaters stand at rest on the ice and push each other. The adult's mass is $80\,\text{kg}$ and the child's $40\,\text{kg}$.Which statement is correct just after the push?

  • Momentum is a vector. The skaters move in opposite directions, and their momenta cancel each other, so the total momentum stays zero. Only the kinetic energy increases, and it comes from the energy of the muscles.

  • The forces are equal and act for the same time, so the momenta are equal. The lighter one moves faster and covers a longer distance during the push, so more work is done on it: the child's kinetic energy is twice as large.

  • Correct. The total momentum is zero and stays zero, so the momenta are equal and opposite: $80v_\text{a} = 40v_\text{c}$. With the same momentum the kinetic energy $\tfrac{p^2}{2m}$ of the lighter one is twice as large.

Justified claim: a wet road

The speed of a car is $80\,\text{km/h}$, and the driver's reaction time is the same as on a dry road. On the wet road the deceleration is half of that on the dry road.Which statement about the stopping distance is correct?

  • The braking distance depends on the square of the speed, but it is inversely proportional to the deceleration: half the deceleration gives twice the braking distance. The reaction distance stays the same.

  • Only the braking distance $\tfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$ doubles. The reaction distance $v_0 t_\text{r}$ does not depend on the road, so the stopping distance increases by less than a factor of two.

  • Correct. $s_\text{p} = v_0 t_\text{r} + \tfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$. When $a$ is halved, the second term doubles, but the reaction distance stays the same. For example, $t_\text{r} = 1.0\,\text{s}$ and $a = 8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ give $22\,\text{m} + 31\,\text{m} = 53\,\text{m}$, and on the wet road $22\,\text{m} + 62\,\text{m} = 84\,\text{m}$.

A cyclist going downhill

A cyclist starts from the top of a hill at $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ and freewheels $22\,\text{m}$ lower down without pedalling. The cyclist and the bicycle have a total mass of $85\,\text{kg}$. Air resistance and rolling resistance together do $-11\,\text{kJ}$ of work during the descent.

What is the speed at the bottom of the hill?

m/s
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which forces do work? The work done by the weight is the change in potential energy; the work done by the other forces is calculated separately.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The work–energy principle: $\tfrac12 mv_1^2 + mgh + W_\text{other} = \tfrac12 mv_2^2$, when the zero level is at the bottom of the hill.

$v_2$$= \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2gh + \dfrac{2W_\text{other}}{m}}$

Show the solution

Given

$v_1 = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$, difference in height $h = 22\,\text{m}$, $m = 85\,\text{kg}$, work done by the resistive forces $W_\text{other} = -11\,\text{kJ}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the speed $v_2$ at the bottom of the hill

Principle

The weight does work, which is taken into account as the change in potential energy. The normal force is perpendicular to the motion and does no work. Air resistance and rolling resistance are other forces whose work is known, so the work–energy principle is used (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The zero level is chosen at the bottom of the hill.

Formula

$\tfrac12 mv_1^2 + mgh + W_\text{other} = \tfrac12 mv_2^2$, so

$v_2$$= \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2gh + \dfrac{2W_\text{other}}{m}}$

Conversions

$-11\,\text{kJ}$$= -11 \cdot 1000\,\text{J}$
$= -11\,000\,\text{J}$

Substitution

$v_2^2$$= \left(3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 + 2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 22\,\text{m} + \dfrac{2 \cdot (-11\,000\,\text{J})}{85\,\text{kg}}$
$= 9.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} + 431.6\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} - 258.8\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}$
$= 181.8\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}$

so

$v_2$$= \sqrt{181.8}\,\text{m/s}$
$= 13.48\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v_2 \approx 13\,\text{m/s}$

Or about $49\,\text{km/h}$, two significant figures like the height $22\,\text{m}$ and the work $11\,\text{kJ}$.

Reasonableness

Without the resistive forces the speed would be $\sqrt{440.6}\,\text{m/s} \approx 21\,\text{m/s}$. Here the resistive forces take more than half of the potential energy $mgh \approx 18.3\,\text{kJ}$ released in the descent, which fits a high speed, since air resistance grows quickly.

A small hydropower plant

$45\,\text{m}^3$ of water flows through the turbines of a small river hydropower plant every second, and the head is $8.5\,\text{m}$. The efficiency of the plant is $88\,\%$.

What is the electric power of the plant? Answer in megawatts.

MW
Hint 1: a guiding question

How much potential energy does the water flowing through the turbines lose every second?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The mass of water flowing in one second is $\rho Q$, and its potential energy is $\rho Q g h$ every second.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$P$$= \eta \rho Q g h$
$= 0.88 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 45\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 8.5\,\text{m}$
Show the solution

Given

flow rate $Q = 45\,\text{m}^3\text{/s}$, head $h = 8.5\,\text{m}$, efficiency $\eta = 88\,\%$, density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the electric power $P$ in megawatts

Principle

Over the head the water loses potential energy, and the plant converts the share $\eta$ of it into electricity. Power is energy per second: the mass flowing in one second is $\rho Q$ (chapter Hydro and wind power as energy converters). The kinetic energy of the water in the headwater and the tailwater is assumed to be small.

Formula

$P = \eta \rho Q g h$

Conversions

$88\,\% = 0.88$ and finally $1\,\text{W} = 10^{-6}\,\text{MW}$. The other quantities are in base units.

Substitution

$P$$= 0.88 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 45\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 8.5\,\text{m}$
$= 3\,302\,000\,\text{W}$
$= 3.302\,\text{MW}$

Answer

$P \approx 3.3\,\text{MW}$

Two significant figures like the flow rate $45\,\text{m}^3\text{/s}$ and the head $8.5\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

One cubic metre of water loses

$1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 8.5\,\text{m}$
$\approx 83\,\text{kJ}$

in the fall. With $45$ cubic metres flowing every second, the power of the water is about $3.8\,\text{MW}$, and $88\,\%$ of it is $3.3\,\text{MW}$. A few megawatts is a typical power for a small hydropower plant.

Find

Find

Principle

Model answer

The reasoning is wrong. Of the mechanical energy of a light rider, which is proportional to the mass, air resistance therefore takes a larger share, and the rider arrives more slowly. So the cause is air resistance, not weaker gravity.

Model answer

Each metre takes the amount $\mu mg$ of kinetic energy. So sweeping does not add energy to the stone, and the stone does not need to touch the broom: sweeping only changes the ice in front of the stone so that the work done by friction per metre is smaller.

  • In part a with kinematics: in uniformly decelerated motion $a = \dfrac{v_0^2 - v_1^2}{2s}$ and $\mu = a : g$, the same result.

The claim is wrong, because it assumes the power is proportional to the wind speed. The power depends on the speed in two ways. Principle

Model answer

The claim is wrong. The plant does not produce energy from nothing but converts it. The energy of the water comes from the Sun: solar radiation evaporates water, which rises into the air and falls as rain at a higher level in the catchment area. So the same water does not circulate through the plant by itself; the Sun “lifts” it again every time. Not all the energy the water loses is converted into electricity: friction in the penstock and the turbine and the heating of the generator transform part of it into internal energy, and the water discharged into the tailwater still has kinetic energy. That is why the efficiency is below $100\,\%$, here $90\,\%$.

The velocities are measured as close to the collision as possible: for example light gates just before and just after the point of collision, or video analysis is used to draw a $(t, v)$ graph of each trolley and the velocities at the moment of collision are read from the extensions of the lines. The effect of rolling resistance is reduced by using an air track or by tilting the track slightly so that a trolley pushed on its own moves at a constant speed. The masses of the trolleys are weighed on a balance together with their hook-and-loop strips. Each test is repeated several times, and the uncertainty of the velocity and the mass is used to estimate how large a difference between the momenta can arise from measurement uncertainty alone. In addition, the masses and velocities are varied: if momentum is conserved in all tests within the uncertainty, the result supports the conservation law. From the same measurements the kinetic energy before and after is calculated, and the transformed share is compared with the theoretical value $\dfrac{m_\text{B}}{m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}} = 48\,\%$, so that the report distinguishes momentum from energy.

Made-up example data · The numbers of the pile driver are an invented example. The specific heat capacity of steel is a table value.

On a building site steel piles are driven into the ground with a pile driver. The machine lifts a ram above the top of the pile and lets it fall freely. The ram hits the head of the pile and does not bounce, but the pile and the ram move down together until the ground stops them.

  • mass of the ram $3500\,\text{kg}$, mass of the pile $1100\,\text{kg}$
  • drop height $0.60\,\text{m}$
  • the pile sinks $18\,\text{mm}$ deeper with one blow
  • mass of the steel at the top of the pile that warms up in the blows about $30\,\text{kg}$; specific heat capacity of steel $460\,\text{J/(kg} \cdot \text{K)}$

  2. b) What is the common velocity of the ram and the pile just after the collision? How much kinetic energy is transformed into other forms of energy in the collision? 4 p
  3. c) What average force does the ground exert on the pile as the pile sinks deeper? 4 p

Skills from the chapters: Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions, Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle, Heating and cooling of matter: $Q = cm\Delta T$, Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy

Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Given

drop height $h = 0.60\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the speed $v$ of the ram just before the impact

Principle

The ram falls freely, and air resistance is small for such a short drop, so only the weight does work and mechanical energy is conserved (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The zero level is the top of the pile.

Formula

$Mgh = \tfrac12 Mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$v$$= \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}}$
$= \sqrt{11.77\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
$= 3.431\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v \approx 3.4\,\text{m/s}$

Two significant figures like the height $0.60\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

A drop of half a metre gives a speed of a few metres per second, as for example in a jump from a chair to the floor. The kinetic energy of the ram is

$Mgh$$= 3500\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.60\,\text{m}$
$\approx 20.6\,\text{kJ}$

b)

Given

$M = 3500\,\text{kg}$, $m = 1100\,\text{kg}$, $v = 3.431\,\text{m/s}$, pile at rest; the ram does not bounce

Find

the common velocity $v'$ and the kinetic energy $E_\text{loss}$ transformed into other forms of energy

Principle

The collision lasts a very short time, so the impulse of the force from the ground and of the weight during it is small compared with the impulse of the forces between the bodies. That is why the total momentum is conserved (chapter Conservation of momentum and one-dimensional collisions). The ram and the pile move together, so the collision is perfectly inelastic, and kinetic energy is transformed into internal energy and sound. Downwards is the positive direction.

Formula

$Mv = (M + m)\,v'$, so $v' = \dfrac{Mv}{M + m}$; $E_\text{loss} = \tfrac12 Mv^2 - \tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$v'$$= \dfrac{3500\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.431\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}{4600\,\text{kg}}$
$= 2.611\,\text{m/s}$
$\tfrac12 Mv^2$
$= 20\,601\,\text{J}$
$\tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2$
$= \tfrac12 \cdot 4600\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(2.611\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2$
$= 15\,675\,\text{J}$

so

$E_\text{loss}$$= 20\,601\,\text{J} - 15\,675\,\text{J}$
$= 4926\,\text{J}$

Answer

$v' \approx 2.6\,\text{m/s}$

And $E_\text{loss} \approx 4.9\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the drop height.

Reasonableness

In a perfectly inelastic collision with a body at rest the share of kinetic energy transformed is

$\dfrac{m}{M + m}$$= \dfrac{1100}{4600}$
$\approx 24\,\%$

and $4926 : 20\,601 \approx 0.24$ agrees. That is why the ram is made much heavier than the pile: a larger share of the energy stays in the motion that pushes the pile into the ground.

c)

Given

$M + m = 4600\,\text{kg}$, $v' = 2.611\,\text{m/s}$, kinetic energy after the collision $15\,675\,\text{J}$, depth of sinking $s = 18\,\text{mm}$, final speed $0$

Find

the average force $F$ of the ground on the pile

Principle

After the collision the ram and the pile move together through the distance $s$ downwards and stop. The work done by the weight is taken into account as the decrease in potential energy $(M + m)\,gs$. The force of the ground points against the motion, so its work is $-Fs$. By the work–energy principle the initial kinetic and potential energy plus the work done by the ground's force equal the final energy (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The zero level is the final position of the pile head.

Formula

$\tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2 + (M + m)\,gs - Fs = 0$, so

$F$$= \dfrac{\tfrac12 (M + m)\,v'^2}{s} + (M + m)\,g$

Conversions

$18\,\text{mm}$$= 18 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
$= 0.018\,\text{m}$

and finally $1\,\text{N} = 0.001\,\text{kN}$

Substitution

$F$$= \dfrac{15\,675\,\text{J}}{0.018\,\text{m}} + 4600\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$= 870\,800\,\text{N} + 45\,100\,\text{N}$
$= 915\,900\,\text{N}$
$\approx 916\,\text{kN}$

Answer

$F \approx 0.92\,\text{MN}$

Two significant figures like the depth $18\,\text{mm}$.

Reasonableness

The force is about $20$ times the weight of the ram and the pile, $45\,\text{kN}$, because the kinetic energy is stopped over a very short distance. The weight affects the result by only about $5\,\%$, so forgetting it would be a small but real mistake.

d)

Given

$E_\text{loss} = 4926\,\text{J}$ per blow, share $40\,\%$, number of blows $50$, warming mass $m_\text{t} = 30\,\text{kg}$, $c = 460\,\text{J/(kg} \cdot \text{K)}$

Find

the rise in temperature $\Delta T$ of the top of the pile and the effect of the assumptions

Principle

The kinetic energy transformed in the collision does not disappear: part of it becomes internal energy of the steel at the top of the pile. When the steel gives off no heat to the surroundings and its state does not change, the increase in internal energy raises the temperature, and $\Delta U = cm\Delta T$ holds (chapter Heating and cooling of matter).

Formula

$\Delta U$$= 50 \cdot 0.40 \cdot E_\text{loss}$
$= c\,m_\text{t}\,\Delta T$

so

$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{50 \cdot 0.40 \cdot E_\text{loss}}{c\,m_\text{t}}$

Conversions

$40\,\% = 0.40$; the other quantities are in base units. A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius.

Substitution

$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{50 \cdot 0.40 \cdot 4926\,\text{J}}{460\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 30\,\text{kg}}$
$= \dfrac{98\,520\,\text{J}}{13\,800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}}$
$= 7.14\,\text{K}$

Answer

$\Delta T \approx 7\,\text{K}$

One significant figure, because the share $40\,\%$ and the warming mass $30\,\text{kg}$ are only estimates. The real warming is smaller: the steel conducts heat to the lower parts of the pile and gives it off to the air between the blows, and part of the energy is transformed into sound and internal energy of the ram.

Reasonableness

One blow warms the top by only about $0.14\,\text{K}$, but there are many blows. A warming of a few degrees fits the fact that the head of the pile feels warm but not hot after a series of blows.

Common mistakes

  • In part b, the common velocity is calculated from conservation of kinetic energy; in a perfectly inelastic collision kinetic energy is not conserved, only momentum.
  • In part c, the original kinetic energy of the ram $20.6\,\text{kJ}$ is put into the work–energy principle, even though part of it was already transformed into internal energy in the collision.
  • In part c, the millimetres of the sinking are substituted without converting them.
  • In part d, the increase in internal energy is calculated from one blow, or the whole kinetic energy $20.6\,\text{kJ}$ is assumed to be transformed into internal energy of the steel.

Also accepted

  • In part c with the kinetic energy alone, $F = \dfrac{15\,675\,\text{J}}{0.018\,\text{m}} \approx 0.87\,\text{MN}$, if the solution states that the contribution of the weight is left out as small.

What percentage of the mechanical energy was transformed into internal energy during the slide?

%
Hint 1: a guiding question

What is the child's mechanical energy at the top and at the bottom, when the zero level is at the bottom of the slide?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Show the solution

Given

$m = 25\,\text{kg}$, difference in height $h = 3.2\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, final speed $v = 5.4\,\text{m/s}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the share of the mechanical energy transformed into internal energy, in per cent

Principle

The total energy is conserved. Friction and air resistance do negative work, so the mechanical energy decreases by exactly the amount transformed into internal energy (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The zero level is chosen at the bottom of the slide.

Formula

$E_\text{mech,1} = mgh$, $E_\text{mech,2} = \tfrac12 mv^2$ and the share $\dfrac{mgh - \tfrac12 mv^2}{mgh}$

Conversions

All quantities are in base units. The ratio is turned into per cent by multiplying by $100\,\%$.

Substitution

$mgh$$= 25\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 3.2\,\text{m}$
$= 784.8\,\text{J}$
$\tfrac12 mv^2$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 25\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(5.4\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2$
$= 364.5\,\text{J}$

The share is

$\dfrac{784.8\,\text{J} - 364.5\,\text{J}}{784.8\,\text{J}}$
$= 0.5356$
$= 53.6\,\%$

Answer

about $54\,\%$, two significant figures like the height $3.2\,\text{m}$ and the speed $5.4\,\text{m/s}$.

Reasonableness

Without friction the speed would be $\sqrt{2gh} \approx 7.9\,\text{m/s}$; the measured $5.4\,\text{m/s}$ is smaller, as it should be with friction. A plastic slide and clothes rub a lot, so about half of the energy can be transformed into internal energy.

4. Explanation task

Two identical cars collide head-on, each at $50\,\text{km/h}$. In a second test an identical car hits a stationary concrete wall at the same speed.

Which is more dangerous for the driver? Justify your answer with momentum, impulse and energy.

Model answer

The situations are equally dangerous for the driver. In the head-on collision the momenta of the cars are equal and opposite, so the total momentum is zero. It is conserved in the collision, so the cars stop where they are, just as if each had driven into a wall. In both tests the driver's momentum changes from $mv$ to zero, so the impulse on the driver is the same. Both cars crumple in the same way, so the stopping time and therefore the average force $F_\text{avg} = mv : \Delta t$ are the same. The energy also adds up: in the head-on collision the kinetic energy of two cars is transformed into internal energy, but it is absorbed by two fronts, each taking its own car's $\tfrac12 mv^2$. More dangerous would be a collision with a heavier or faster car, or a collision in which your own front crumples over a shorter distance, because that shortens the stopping time.
