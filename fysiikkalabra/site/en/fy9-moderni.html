Quantum phenomena, the nucleus and radiation

Why do the bright red grow lights of a greenhouse not damage the DNA of the plants or of the gardener, even though their power is kilowatts, while even weak X-rays can?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve problems about photons, atoms, nuclei and radiation by choosing a suitable model
  • draw and interpret graphs of measurement data on the photoelectric effect and radioactive decay
  • assess claims and measurements about radiation and nuclear energy with justification

Prior knowledge

  • The energy of one photon is $E = hf$, and it gives its energy to one electronchapter The photoelectric effect and the photon
  • The energy of the photon of a spectral line is the difference of two energy stateschapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms
  • Each half-life halves the number of undecayed nucleichapter The decay law and half-life

1Think about it

In winter the tomatoes of a greenhouse grow under LED grow lights. The total power of the lights is several kilowatts, and most of the light is red, with a wavelength of about $660\,\text{nm}$. The radiation power of a dentist's X-ray unit is much smaller, but the energy of its photons is about $60\,\text{keV}$. The ionisation energy of a water molecule is $12.6\,\text{eV}$. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the formula is not.

Why does a large light power not ionise, while a small X-ray power does? How large is the energy of one red photon in electronvolts?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting level 1: brighter red light

A zinc plate is lit with red light, and no electrons leave the plate. The intensity of the light is made ten times larger, but the colour stays the same.What happens?

  • Brighter light brings more photons, but the energy $hf$ of each photon stays the same. One electron gets the energy of one photon, so if $hf < W$, no electrons leave. Review in the chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon.

  • The wave model predicted a delay, but none is observed in experiments. An electron does not collect energy from several photons, so at too low a frequency electrons never leave. Review in the chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon.

  • Correct. The intensity tells the number of photons, and the frequency sets the energy of one photon. The energy of a red photon is smaller than the work function of zinc, about $4.3\,\text{eV}$. Review if needed in the chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon.

Starting level 2: a photon in hydrogen gas

Photons with energy $11.0\,\text{eV}$ pass through hydrogen atoms in the ground state. The levels of hydrogen are $E_1 = -13.6\,\text{eV}$, $E_2 = -3.40\,\text{eV}$ and $E_3 = -1.51\,\text{eV}$. What happens to the photons?

  • Ionisation from the ground state needs $13.6\,\text{eV}$, so $11.0\,\text{eV}$ is not enough to free the electron. Nor does the photon match any difference of levels. Review in the chapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model.

  • The transition $1 \to 2$ needs exactly $10.2\,\text{eV}$. When the electron stays in the atom, the extra energy has nowhere to go, so the photon is not absorbed. Review in the chapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model.

  • Correct. The differences from the ground state are $10.2\,\text{eV}$ and $12.1\,\text{eV}$, and ionisation needs $13.6\,\text{eV}$. A photon of $11.0\,\text{eV}$ matches none of them, so it passes through. Review if needed in the chapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model.

Starting level 3: a red and a blue LED

A red and a blue LED are connected in turn to an adjustable voltage supply, and the voltage is raised from zero.Which one starts to conduct and shine at a lower voltage?

  • Correct. At the threshold voltage $eU_\text{k} \approx hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so the LED with the longer wavelength needs a lower voltage: red about $1.9\,\text{V}$, blue about $2.7\,\text{V}$. Review if needed in the chapter Light from an LED and measuring the Planck constant.

  • The same voltage supply does not mean the same threshold. Each electron must get an energy $eU$ that is enough for the energy $hf$ of one photon, so the threshold depends on the colour of the light. Review in the chapter Light from an LED and measuring the Planck constant.

  • A short wavelength means a large photon energy $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. The electron must get at least this energy from the voltage supply, so the blue LED needs a higher voltage. Review in the chapter Light from an LED and measuring the Planck constant.

Starting level 4: iodine-131 three weeks later

The half-life of iodine-131 is $8.0$ days. A sample contains $N_0$ iodine-131 nuclei at first. What fraction of the nuclei is still undecayed after $24$ days?

  • Correct. $N = N_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{24/8.0} = N_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{3} = \tfrac18 N_0$. Review if needed in the chapter The decay law and half-life.

  • Half-lives multiply the amount by $\tfrac12$; they do not divide it by their number. After three half-lives $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac18$ is left. Review in the chapter The decay law and half-life.

  • Decay is not steady. Each half-life halves the amount that is left, so the sample never disappears completely. Review in the chapter The decay law and half-life.

Starting level 5: the decay of carbon-14

Carbon-14 ($Z = 6$) decays by beta minus decay. Which nucleus is formed? The atomic number of nitrogen is $7$ and that of boron $5$.

  • The mass number of the electron is zero, so the mass number $14$ is conserved. The charge number is conserved: $6 = 7 + (-1)$, so nitrogen-14 is formed. Review in the chapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay.

  • In beta minus decay a neutron of the nucleus turns into a proton, and an electron leaves the nucleus. The charge of the nucleus increases by one, so $Z$ is $7$. Review in the chapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay.

  • Correct. ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}$: the mass number $14$ and the charge number $6$ are conserved. Review if needed in the chapter The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay.

Starting level 6: radon in indoor air

The alpha radiation of radon and its decay products is stopped by a sheet of paper.Why is radon still the largest source of radiation dose for people in Finland?

  • Alpha radiation does not pass through the dead surface layer of the skin. The danger is that the decay products of radon are breathed into the lungs, where alpha radiation hits the cells. Review in the chapter Ionising radiation and the body.

  • Correct. In the lungs alpha radiation gives up its energy over a distance of a few cells, and its weighting factor is $20$. Review if needed in the chapter Radiation dose and radiation safety in Finland.

  • The radon dose does not come from radiation passing through the walls. Radon is a gas that is breathed in, and its decay products stay in the lungs. Review in the chapter Radiation dose and radiation safety in Finland.

Hint 1: a guiding question

What part of the radiation of source C gets through $3.0\,\text{mm}$ of aluminium? What part through $10\,\text{mm}$ of lead?

Hint 2: what is the connection

First subtract the background of $20$ counts per minute. Behind the aluminium almost only gamma radiation is left, $583$ counts per minute.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$\tfrac{176}{583} = \left(\tfrac12\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$ with $d = 10\,\text{mm}$. Solve $d_{1/2}$ with a logarithm.

Show the solution

Given

counts per minute with no absorber and behind paper, $3.0\,\text{mm}$ of aluminium and $10\,\text{mm}$ of lead; background radiation $20$ counts per minute. The readings of source C $980$, $962$, $603$ and $196$; from the table the half-value thickness of lead $5.5\,\text{mm}$ for the gamma radiation of caesium-137

Find

the half-value thickness $d_{1/2}$ of lead for the gamma radiation of source C in $\text{mm}$

Principle

Alpha radiation is stopped by paper and beta radiation by a few millimetres of aluminium, but gamma radiation is only attenuated: each half-value thickness halves it (chapter Ionising radiation and the body). The reading of source A drops to the background level behind the paper, so it emits only alpha radiation. The reading of source B drops to the background level only behind the aluminium, so it emits beta radiation. The reading of source C decreases behind the aluminium but does not vanish, so it emits beta and gamma radiation. Behind the aluminium almost only gamma radiation is left, so it is compared with the reading behind the lead. The background is subtracted first.

Formula

$I = I_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{d/d_{1/2}}$, so

$\ln\tfrac{I_0}{I}$$= \tfrac{d}{d_{1/2}} \ln 2$
$d_{1/2}$$= \dfrac{d \ln 2}{\ln (I_0 : I)}$

Conversions

The counts are in the same unit, so their ratio is a pure number, and no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$I_0 = 603 - 20 = 583$ and $I = 196 - 20 = 176$ counts per minute, so

$d_{1/2}$$= \dfrac{10\,\text{mm} \cdot \ln 2}{\ln (583 : 176)}$
$= \dfrac{10\,\text{mm} \cdot 0.693}{1.198}$
$= 5.79\,\text{mm}$

Answer

$d_{1/2} \approx 5.8\,\text{mm}$

Two significant figures, because the lead thickness $10\,\text{mm}$ and the randomly varying counts do not give a better accuracy.

Reasonableness

Ten millimetres is just under two half-value thicknesses, and the net reading fell to about a third, as $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{1.7} \approx 0.31$ predicts. The result is a little larger than the $5.5\,\text{mm}$ of the table, because the aluminium also attenuates the gamma radiation by about $6\,\%$, so $583$ is a slightly too small estimate of the original gamma radiation. Correcting for this gives $5.5\,\text{mm}$, so source C may well be caesium-137.

4Theory

Key idea

What joins the phenomena of the module is quantised energy. Light exchanges energy with matter as photons whose energy is $E = hf$, and one photon gives its energy to one electron. The electrons of an atom and the nucleons of a nucleus can only be in certain energy states, so in transitions a photon of a definite energy is created or disappears. The differences between electron levels are electronvolts, the energies of nuclear levels and nuclear reactions millions of electronvolts. In the nucleus mass and energy are linked: $E = mc^2$.

In a modern physics problem in the exam it pays to ask first what one particle does: what is the energy of one photon, which transition or decay happens and which numbers are conserved. Only then do you calculate how many photons or nuclei there are, that is the power, the activity or the dose.

This answers the opening question. The energy of a red photon of a grow light ($660\,\text{nm}$) is $E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda} \approx 1.9\,\text{eV}$, which is much less than the ionisation energy of a water molecule, $12.6\,\text{eV}$. A larger power only brings more photons of the same kind, and not one of them frees an electron. The photon energy of a dental X-ray is about $60\,\text{keV}$, over $30\,000$ times as much, so a single photon can already ionise and damage DNA.

Photons, the nucleus and radiation

The photon and the atom

$$\begin{aligned} E &= hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \\ hf &= W + E_\text{k,max} \\ hf &= E_\text{u} - E_\text{l} \\ p &= \frac{h}{\lambda} \end{aligned}$$

where $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ is the Planck constant, $f$ the frequency, $\lambda$ the wavelength, $W$ the work function of the metal, $E_\text{k,max}$ the largest kinetic energy of an emitted electron, $E_\text{u}$ and $E_\text{l}$ the energies of the upper and the lower energy state and $p$ the momentum of the photon. Electrons are emitted only when $hf \geq W$. An atom absorbs only a photon whose energy is exactly the difference of levels, unless the photon ionises the atom. For hydrogen $E_n = -\tfrac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$. At the threshold voltage of an LED $eU_\text{k} \approx hf$. Conversions: $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ and $hc \approx 1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm}$.

The nucleus and decay

$$\begin{aligned} N &= N_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}} \\ A &= \lambda N,\quad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} \\ Q &= (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2 \end{aligned}$$

where $N$ is the number of undecayed nuclei at the time $t$, $T_{1/2}$ the half-life, $A$ the activity ($\text{Bq}$), $\lambda$ the decay constant and $Q$ the reaction energy. In a decay equation the mass number and the charge number are conserved: in alpha decay $Z$ decreases by two and $A$ by four, in beta minus decay $Z$ increases by one. The decay law describes a large set of nuclei. The binding energy is $E_\text{B} = \Delta m\,c^2$, and $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$. Fission and fusion release energy, because the binding energy per nucleon of the products is larger.

Radiation and dose

$$\begin{aligned} D &= \frac{E}{m},\quad H_\text{T} = w_\text{R} D_\text{T} \\ H &= \dot{H} t \\ \dot{H}_2 &= \dot{H}_1 \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 \\ \dot{H} &= \dot{H}_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{d/d_{1/2}} \end{aligned}$$

where $D$ is the absorbed dose ($\text{Gy}$), $E$ the absorbed energy, $m$ the mass of the tissue, $H_\text{T}$ the equivalent dose ($\text{Sv}$), $w_\text{R}$ the radiation weighting factor (alpha $20$, beta and gamma $1$), $\dot{H}$ the dose rate, $t$ the time, $r$ the distance and $d_{1/2}$ the half-value thickness. The inverse square law holds for a point source when the air does not attenuate the radiation. The half-value thickness applies to photon radiation, because alpha and beta radiation have a range. The recession speed of galaxies is $v = H_0 d$, and for a small redshift $v = cz$.

Common mistakes

  • Brighter light, more energetic photons. The intensity tells the number of photons; the frequency sets the energy of one photon.
  • The photon carries the energy of a level. The photon energy is the difference of levels $E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$, not $|E_n|$.
  • Electronvolts into a formula with $h$ in joules. Convert first: $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.
  • Two half-lives make everything decay. After two half-lives a quarter is left, and each half-life halves what is left.
  • Mixing up mass number and mass. The mass number is conserved, but the mass decreases when energy is released.
  • The background is not subtracted. The background radiation is subtracted from the counts before ratios are calculated.
  • Doubling the distance halves the dose rate. The dose rate of a point source falls to a quarter.

Find the mistake: the photons of a laser pointer

The power of a green laser pointer is $5.0\,\text{mW}$ and its wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$.A student calculated the photon energy $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda} = 2.33\,\text{eV}$ and wrote: “The number of photons leaving per second is $\tfrac{P}{E} = \tfrac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W}}{2.33\,\text{eV}} = 2.1 \cdot 10^{-3}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • Correct. $2.33\,\text{eV} = 2.33 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 3.73 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, so $\tfrac{N}{t} = \tfrac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{W}}{3.73 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$. Review if needed in the chapter Lasers and quantum structures.

  • The laser power $5.0\,\text{mW}$ is already the power of the light. The mistake is that the energy is in electronvolts: it must be converted into joules before dividing. Review in the chapter Lasers and quantum structures.

  • The expressions are the same, because $f = \tfrac{c}{\lambda}$, and the energy $2.33\,\text{eV}$ is right. The mistake is in the units: the ratio of watts to electronvolts is not a number per second. Review in the chapter Lasers and quantum structures.

Read the graph: binding energy per nucleon

The graph shows the binding energy per nucleon of nuclei as a function of the mass number. Which claim is correct?

  • In the graph $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ decreases from about $8.5\,\text{MeV}$ to about $7.6\,\text{MeV}$, so the product would be less tightly bound, and the reaction would need energy. Review in the chapter Mass defect and nuclear binding energy.

  • Correct. The binding energy per nucleon increases by about $0.9\,\text{MeV}$, so about $240 \cdot 0.9\,\text{MeV} \approx 220\,\text{MeV}$ is released, that is about $200\,\text{MeV}$. Review if needed in the chapter Fission, fusion and nuclear power.

  • The nuclei at the peak are more tightly bound than all the others. From them neither fusion nor fission can lead to a more tightly bound nucleus, so iron cannot be used as an energy source. Review in the chapter Mass defect and nuclear binding energy.

Justified claim: which decay is possible?

The mass of a free neutron is $939.6\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, that of a proton $938.3\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ and that of an electron $0.51\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$. Which decay can happen spontaneously?

  • Correct. Charge $0 = 1 - 1 + 0$, baryon number $1 = 1$, lepton number $0 = 1 - 1$, and $Q = (939.6 - 938.3 - 0.51)\,\text{MeV} \approx 0.8\,\text{MeV} > 0$. Review if needed in the chapter The Standard Model: elementary particles and interactions.

  • Charge is conserved, but lepton number is not: at the start it is $0$ and at the end $1$. That is why an antineutrino $\bar{\nu}$ is always included. Review in the chapter The Standard Model: elementary particles and interactions.

  • The numbers are conserved, but a free proton is lighter than a neutron and a positron together, so $Q < 0$. In a nucleus beta plus decay can happen when the energy of the nucleus is enough. Review in the chapter The Standard Model: elementary particles and interactions.

Justified claim: electrons one at a time

In a double-slit experiment the electron beam is so weak that there is only one electron in the apparatus at a time. Hits build up on the detector for a long time.Which claim is correct?

  • An electron is always detected whole at one point. The fringe pattern appears only as the distribution of many hits. Review in the chapter Light and electrons as waves and particles.

  • The fringes are not caused by collisions of electrons. They form even though the electrons arrive one at a time, because the probability of each electron's hit is described by a wave through both slits. Review in the chapter Light and electrons as waves and particles.

  • Correct. The position of a single hit cannot be predicted, but the wave gives the probability: hardly any electrons hit the dark fringes. Review if needed in the chapter Light and electrons as waves and particles.

A dental X-ray

In a dentist's X-ray tube electrons are accelerated from the cathode to the anode by a voltage of $65\,\text{kV}$. An electron braking in the anode can give all its kinetic energy to one photon.

How short is the shortest wavelength of the X-rays?

nm
Hint 1: a guiding question

How much kinetic energy does an electron get from a voltage $U$?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The electron gets the energy $eU$, and the largest photon energy is $hf = eU$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$\lambda_\text{min} = \tfrac{hc}{eU}$. Finally convert metres into nanometres.

Show the solution

Given

accelerating voltage $U = 65\,\text{kV}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

Find

the shortest wavelength $\lambda_\text{min}$ in $\text{nm}$

Principle

The electric field does the work $eU$ on the electron, so the electron arrives at the anode with kinetic energy $eU$. The largest photon energy arises when the electron gives all its kinetic energy to one photon. The largest energy corresponds to the shortest wavelength (chapter Radiation in medicine and technology).

Formula

$hf_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda_\text{min}}$
$= eU$

so $\lambda_\text{min} = \dfrac{hc}{eU}$

Conversions

$65\,\text{kV}$$= 65 \cdot 1000\,\text{V}$
$= 65\,000\,\text{V}$

and finally $1\,\text{m} = 10^{9}\,\text{nm}$

Substitution

$\lambda_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 65\,000\,\text{V}}$
$= 1.908 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{m}$
$= 0.01908\,\text{nm}$

Answer

$\lambda_\text{min} \approx 0.019\,\text{nm}$

Two significant figures like the voltage $65\,\text{kV}$.

Reasonableness

The estimate $hc \approx 1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm}$ gives $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 65\,000\,\text{eV} \approx 0.019\,\text{nm}$. The wavelength is tens of thousands of times shorter than that of visible light, as it should be for X-rays.

When the tree was felled, no new carbon-14 came in any more. What happened to the activity after that?

Show the solution

Given

the ratio of activities $\tfrac{A}{A_0} = 0.720$, half-life $T_{1/2} = 5700\,\text{a}$

Find

the age $t$ of the canoe in years

Principle

A living tree takes carbon from the air, so its share of carbon-14 stays the same. When the tree was felled, carbon-14 only decayed, and the activity decreases according to the decay law (chapter The decay law and half-life). The activity per gram does not depend on the size of the sample.

$\dfrac{A}{A_0}$$= \left(\dfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$

so

$\ln\dfrac{A_0}{A}$$= \dfrac{t}{T_{1/2}} \ln 2$
$t$$= T_{1/2} \cdot \dfrac{\ln (A_0 : A)}{\ln 2}$

Conversions

The age comes out in the same unit as the half-life, years, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$t$$= 5700\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{\ln (1 : 0.720)}{\ln 2}$
$= 5700\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{0.3285}{0.6931}$
$= 2701\,\text{a}$

$t \approx 2700\,\text{a}$

Two significant figures like the half-life $5700\,\text{a}$.

One half-life would leave $50\,\%$, so $72\,\%$ means less than half a half-life, and $2700\,\text{a} \approx 0.47\,T_{1/2}$. Check: $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{0.474} \approx 0.72$.

Fusion of two deuterium nuclei

In a fusion experiment two deuterium nuclei join: ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} \to {}^{3}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$. The atomic masses are ${}^{2}\text{H}$: $2.014102\,\text{u}$, ${}^{3}\text{He}$: $3.016029\,\text{u}$ and the neutron $1.008665\,\text{u}$. How much energy does one reaction release?

MeV
Hint 1: a guiding question

Where does the released energy come from? Compare the masses of the reactants and the products.

Hint 2: what is the connection

$Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$. Atomic masses can be used, because there are two electrons on both sides.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

Substitute the mass difference in atomic mass units and $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$.

Show the solution

Given

$m({}^{2}\text{H}) = 2.014102\,\text{u}$, $m({}^{3}\text{He}) = 3.016029\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\,\text{u}$, $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$

Find

the energy $Q$ released by one reaction in $\text{MeV}$

Principle

In a nuclear reaction the mass number and the charge number are conserved, but the mass is not: the products are lighter, and the energy corresponding to the mass difference is released as kinetic energy of the products (chapters Mass defect and nuclear binding energy and Fission, fusion and nuclear power). Atomic masses can be used, because there are two electrons on each side.

Formula

$Q$$= (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$
$= \left(2m({}^{2}\text{H}) - m({}^{3}\text{He}) - m_\text{n}\right)c^2$

Conversions

The mass difference is substituted in atomic mass units, and $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ is substituted for the unit, so that $c^2$ cancels.

Substitution

$\Delta m$$= 2 \cdot 2.014102\,\text{u} - 3.016029\,\text{u} - 1.008665\,\text{u}$
$= 0.003510\,\text{u}$

so

$Q$$= 0.003510 \cdot 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2$
$= 3.270\,\text{MeV}$

Answer

$Q \approx 3.27\,\text{MeV}$

Three significant figures, because the mass difference $0.003510\,\text{u}$ is known to four figures and the conversion factor $931.5$ to four.

Reasonableness

The mass decreases by only about $0.09\,\%$, which is typical of nuclear reactions. The result is smaller than the $17.6\,\text{MeV}$ of deuterium–tritium fusion, because helium-3 is much less tightly bound than helium-4.

Graph

One photon gives all its energy $hf$ to one electron. Principle

An alpha particle is heavy and doubly charged, so it ionises densely and loses its energy over a short distance: a few centimetres in air and less than a tenth of a millimetre in tissue. Model answer

Radon is the largest single source of radiation dose in Finland and, after smoking, the most common cause of lung cancer. All the points are used: $\ln(N - N_\text{bg})$ is plotted against time, and the slope of the line is $-\tfrac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}$, or the model $N = A\,e^{-Ct} + B$, in which the constant $B$ is the background, is fitted with a calculator. The measurement is repeated with a new sample and the results compared, so that the spread can be seen. The distance of the sample from the GM tube is kept the same throughout. The result is given with its uncertainty, for example $T_{1/2} = (152 \pm 4)\,\text{s}$.

Common mistakes

  • In part a, the background is not subtracted, or it is subtracted only from the first reading.
  • In part a, the half-life is read directly by linear interpolation, as if the decrease were steady.
  • In part b, the group's explanation about “older nuclei” is accepted, or only calculation errors are assessed without mentioning random variation.
  • In part c, only “a more accurate meter” is suggested without saying what changes in the measurement and why.

Also accepted

  • In part a, a fit of all the points with a calculator or from a graph: $T_{1/2} \approx 153\,\text{s}$.
  • In part a, some other pair of points far apart after subtracting the background, as long as the result is between $145$ and $157\,\text{s}$.

a)

Given

electric power $P_\text{e} = 1600\,\text{MW}$, thermal power $P_\text{th} = 4300\,\text{MW}$, energy per fission $E_\text{f} = 200\,\text{MeV}$

Find

the efficiency $\eta$ and the number of fissions per second $n$

Principle

The efficiency is the ratio of the useful power to the power used (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants). The thermal power of the reactor is the energy of one fission times the number of fissions per second (chapter Fission, fusion and nuclear power).

Formula

$\eta$$= \dfrac{P_\text{e}}{P_\text{th}}$

$P_\text{th} = nE_\text{f}$, so $n = \dfrac{P_\text{th}}{E_\text{f}}$

Conversions

$200\,\text{MeV}$$= 200 \cdot 10^{6} \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
$= 3.204 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{J}$

$4300\,\text{MW} = 4300 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}$

Substitution

$\eta$$= \dfrac{1600\,\text{MW}}{4300\,\text{MW}}$
$= 0.3721$
$= 37.21\,\%$
$n$$= \dfrac{4300 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}}{3.204 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{J}}$
$= 1.342 \cdot 10^{20}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$

Answer

The efficiency is $\eta \approx 37.2\,\%$, and about $1.34 \cdot 10^{20}$ fissions happen in the reactor per second, three significant figures like the powers.

Reasonableness

The efficiency of a thermal power plant with a steam turbine is typically $30$–$40\,\%$. There are enormous numbers of fissions, because one fission releases only about $3 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{J}$.

b)

Given

$n = 1.342 \cdot 10^{20}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$ (part a), $1\,\text{a} = 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$, mass of a uranium-235 atom $235\,\text{u}$, $1\,\text{u} = 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, $P_\text{th} = 4300\,\text{MW}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$

Find

the mass $m$ of uranium-235 that undergoes fission in a year and the mass $\Delta m$ converted into energy

Principle

Each fission uses up one uranium-235 nucleus. The released energy corresponds to the mass defect: the mass of the reaction products is smaller than that of the reactants (chapters Mass defect and nuclear binding energy and Fission, fusion and nuclear power).

Formula

$m = nt \cdot 235\,\text{u}$ and $E = \Delta m\,c^2$, so $\Delta m = \dfrac{P_\text{th}t}{c^2}$

Conversions

$235\,\text{u}$$= 235 \cdot 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$
$= 3.903 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$

Substitution

$nt$$= 1.342 \cdot 10^{20}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$
$= 4.236 \cdot 10^{27}$

so

$m$$= 4.236 \cdot 10^{27} \cdot 3.903 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$
$= 1653\,\text{kg}$
$\Delta m$$= \dfrac{4300 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} \cdot 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}}{(2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2}$

$= 1.51\,\text{kg}$

Answer

About $1650\,\text{kg}$ of uranium-235 undergoes fission in a year, and about $1.51\,\text{kg}$ of mass is converted into energy, three significant figures like the powers.

Reasonableness

Only $1.51 : 1653 \approx 0.09\,\%$ of the mass of the uranium is converted into energy, which is the same as $200\,\text{MeV}$ divided by the rest energy of a uranium atom, $235 \cdot 931.5\,\text{MeV}$. The rest of the mass remains as fission products and neutrons, because the number of nucleons is conserved.

c)

Given

$P_\text{th} = 4300\,\text{MW}$, $P_\text{e} = 1600\,\text{MW}$, specific heat capacity of seawater $c_\text{w} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, temperature rise $\Delta T = 11\,\text{K}$

the mass flow rate of seawater $\tfrac{m}{t}$

Principle

Energy is conserved: the part of the reactor's thermal power that does not become electricity goes to the seawater in the condenser (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants). The heat needed to warm the water is $Q = c_\text{w}m\Delta T$ (chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy).

Formula

$P_\text{th} - P_\text{e}$$= \dfrac{Q}{t}$

Conversions

$4300\,\text{MW} - 1600\,\text{MW}$

Substitution

About $5.9 \cdot 10^{4}\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$ of seawater must be pumped, two significant figures like the temperature rise $11\,\text{K}$.

Reasonableness

That is about $59\,\text{m}^3$ per second, the flow of a medium-sized river. The cooling water of a large nuclear power plant really is of this order, and in winter the heat shows as an open water area in front of the plant.

the mass $m_\text{c}$ of hard coal burned in a year

The coal power plant produces the same electrical energy, but only $40\,\%$ of the energy of the fuel becomes electricity (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants). The energy of the fuel is the heat of combustion times the mass (chapter Fuels and heat of combustion).

$1600\,\text{MW}$$= 1600 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}$
$24\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$$= 24 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

and finally $1\,\text{t} = 1000\,\text{kg}$

4. Explanation task

A nuclear power plant gets its energy from the fission of uranium-235, the Sun from the fusion of hydrogen into helium. Using the graph, explain why both the splitting of a heavy nucleus and the joining of light nuclei release energy, and why the mass decreases in both.

Model answer

The graph shows that the binding energy per nucleon is largest for medium-heavy nuclei, around the mass number $60$, and smaller for both light and heavy nuclei. When uranium-235 splits into two medium-heavy nuclei, the nucleons move from the right edge of the graph towards the peak, and the binding energy per nucleon increases from about $7.6\,\text{MeV}$ to about $8.5\,\text{MeV}$. When hydrogen fuses into helium, the nucleons move upwards from the left edge. In both, the products are more tightly bound than the reactants, so the total binding energy increases and the difference is released as kinetic energy and gamma radiation. A larger binding energy means a larger mass defect $\Delta m = \tfrac{E_\text{B}}{c^2}$: the total mass of the products is smaller than that of the reactants, and the released energy is $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$. The mass number and the charge number are conserved, but the mass is not.
