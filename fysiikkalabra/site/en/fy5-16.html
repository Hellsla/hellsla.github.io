Noise, acoustics and sound reproduction

Why can a concert of a few hours damage hearing permanently, even though the sound does not feel painful?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • estimate the harms of noise and calculate the reference time of a noise dose from a noise level.
  • compare ways of controlling noise at the source, along the path and at the ear.
  • explain reverberation, active noise cancelling, and the working principle of a loudspeaker and a microphone.

Prior knowledge

  • The sound intensity level $L$ is given in decibels, and it always rises by the same amount when the intensity is multiplied by the same factorchapter Intensity and the decibel scale
  • A wave is reflected from an obstacle according to the law of reflection and bends behind the edge of an obstaclechapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap
  • Waves that meet add together, and they can strengthen or weaken each otherchapter When waves combine: superposition and interference

1Think about it

Saturday's festival gig was great. In the front row, in front of the speaker towers, the bass could be felt in the chest, but the ears did not hurt. On the way home a friend's speech sounded muffled, and at night there was ringing in the ears. By morning the ringing was almost gone. The friend's father says that he wears hearing protectors all day in the noisy hall at his workplace, even though the hall is nowhere near as loud as the gig.

What decides how much noise the ear can take, and how can hearing be protected?

Recap: two sources

The left speaker tower of a concert stage produces a level of $90\,\text{dB}$ at the audience. The right tower, which is identical, produces an equal level at the same place.What is the level when both towers are playing?

  • The second tower brings more energy to the listener, so the intensity increases. The level rises, although only by about $3\,\text{dB}$, because the intensity doubles (chapter Intensity and the decibel scale).

  • Decibels do not add directly; the intensities add (chapter Intensity and the decibel scale). Two towers double the intensity, and that raises the level by only about $3\,\text{dB}$. A level of $180\,\text{dB}$ would correspond to an intensity about a billion times greater.

  • A rise of $6\,\text{dB}$ corresponds to four times the intensity, that is, four identical towers. Two towers double the intensity, which raises the level by about $3\,\text{dB}$.

  • Correct. Two equally strong sources double the intensity, and $10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg 2 \approx 3\,\text{dB}$ (chapter Intensity and the decibel scale). This is needed when estimating how much noise reaches the ear.

Prediction 1: level and time

The upper action value for noise at work corresponds to eight hours of exposure to a noise level of $85\,\text{dB}$.Roughly how long does it take at a noise level of $94\,\text{dB}$ for the ear to receive the same dose of sound energy?

  • Correct. $94\,\text{dB} - 85\,\text{dB} = 9\,\text{dB}$ is three steps of $3\,\text{dB}$, so the intensity is $2^3 = 8$ times as great. The same energy builds up in an eighth of the time: $8\,\text{h} : 8 = 1\,\text{h}$.

  • Hearing damage does not appear the moment a limit is crossed; what matters is the accumulated dose: level and time together. At $94\,\text{dB}$ the same dose as $8\,\text{h}$ at $85\,\text{dB}$ builds up in an hour. Only a very strong impulsive sound, such as a gunshot from close by, can damage the ear immediately.

  • The decibel number does not grow in the same proportion as the intensity: every $3\,\text{dB}$ doubles the intensity. A difference of $9\,\text{dB}$ is three doublings, that is, eight times the intensity, so the same dose is reached in $8\,\text{h} : 8 = 1\,\text{h}$.

  • The time shortens much more. A time of $4\,\text{h}$ corresponds to a level of $88\,\text{dB}$, where the intensity is doubled. At $94\,\text{dB}$ the intensity is eight times as great, so the same dose is reached in $1\,\text{h}$.

Prediction 2: how can sound silence sound?

Noise-cancelling headphones silence the hum of an aircraft engine even though they have no thick cups. The headphones have a microphone and a small loudspeaker.Which explanation is physically correct?

  • Correct. When the overpressure of the noise and the underpressure of the anti-noise arrive at the same place at the same time, they add up to almost zero: destructive interference (chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference). This works best for a low and steady sound, such as the hum of an engine.

  • The cups and pads mainly dampen high sounds, and a low hum cannot be dealt with without thick cups. The microphone listens to the noise, and the electronics produce a sound in the opposite phase to it, which cancels the hum by interference.

  • A masking sound does not reduce the energy reaching the ear but adds to it: the hum is still there, it just stands out less. Noise-cancelling headphones produce a sound that is in the opposite phase to the noise, so that the pressure variations cancel each other.

  • A sound vibrating in the same phase would strengthen the noise (constructive interference), and more energy would reach the ear. The ear is not protected from a loud sound. The anti-noise must be in the opposite phase so that the pressure variations cancel.

How many steps of $3\,\text{dB}$ are there from a level of $97\,\text{dB}$ to a level of $103\,\text{dB}$?

Each step halves the time: $30\,\text{min} \to 15\,\text{min} \to \ldots$

Show the solution

Given

the rule of the table: $t_0 = 8\,\text{h}$ at a level of $L_0 = 85\,\text{dB}$, and every rise of $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the time; level $L = 103\,\text{dB}$.

Find

the reference time $t_\text{ref}$ in minutes.

Principle

In the table the intensity doubles and the time halves with every step of $3\,\text{dB}$, so the product of intensity and time, that is, the energy reaching the ear, stays the same. The same rule continues for higher levels.

Formula

$t_\text{ref} = \dfrac{t_0}{2^{\,n}}$, where $n = \dfrac{L - L_0}{3\,\text{dB}}$

Conversions

The answer is asked in minutes:

$t_0$$= 8\,\text{h}$
$= 8 \cdot 60\,\text{min}$
$= 480\,\text{min}$

Substitution

$n$$= \dfrac{103\,\text{dB} - 85\,\text{dB}}{3\,\text{dB}}$
$= 6$
$t_\text{ref}$$= \dfrac{480\,\text{min}}{2^6}$
$= \dfrac{480\,\text{min}}{64}$
$= 7.5\,\text{min}$

Answer

$7.5\,\text{min}$

The rule is a rule of thumb, so two significant figures are enough.

Reasonableness

From the last row of the table ($97\,\text{dB}$, $30\,\text{min}$) two halvings lead to the result: $30\,\text{min}$, then $15\,\text{min}$ and finally $7.5\,\text{min}$. The intensity is $64$ times as great, and

$64 \cdot 7.5\,\text{min}$$= 480\,\text{min}$
$= 8\,\text{h}$

so the dose is the same.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Noise and hearing

    Noise is sound that is not wanted or that is harmful. The same sound can be music to one person and noise to another, but whether it harms hearing does not depend on taste: what matters is the level of the sound and how long one listens to it. Even moderate noise weakens concentration and sleep and increases stress, so noise affects well-being even when hearing is not damaged.

    Hearing damage arises in the cochlea of the inner ear. There the hair cells change the vibration of sound into nerve signals. A loud sound strains and damages the hair cells. The dulling of hearing and the ringing in the ears felt after a concert are signs of strain. They often pass, but repeated strain leads to permanent hearing loss, because human hair cells do not regenerate. Permanent ringing in the ears, or tinnitus, is a common result of noise. Sound causes pain only at a level of about $120\,\text{dB}$, so pain does not warn in time.

    Dose: level and time

    The dose that harms hearing builds up in the same way as energy: what matters is how much energy the sound brings to the ear during the whole exposure. In the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale we learned that a rise of $3\,\text{dB}$ in the intensity level corresponds to roughly a doubling of the intensity. When the intensity doubles, twice as much energy reaches the ear every second, so the same dose is reached in half the time. That is why a noise level only a few decibels higher can shorten many times over the time in which the same dose builds up.

    Noise at work in law

    In Finland, noise at work is regulated by Government Decree 85/2006 on the protection of workers from the risks arising from noise. The decree compares a worker's daily noise exposure, that is, the average noise level over an eight-hour working day, with action values. When the lower action value $80\,\text{dB(A)}$ is exceeded, the employer must make hearing protectors available to the workers and give them information and training about the dangers of noise. When the upper action value $85\,\text{dB(A)}$ is reached or exceeded, hearing protectors must be used, and the employer must reduce the noise according to a plan, a noise control programme. The exposure limit value is $87\,\text{dB(A)}$: the daily exposure at the ear, taking the attenuation of the hearing protectors into account, must not exceed it. The action values, in contrast, are compared with the exposure without hearing protectors. So the action values are not safe or permitted upper limits of exposure, but levels at which the employer's duties begin. The values are A-weighted: the meter weights frequencies roughly as the ear hears them. The decree also has its own C-weighted limits for peak values of sound, which apply to a gunshot or an impact, for example. The law does not set the same limits for the noise of concerts and headphones, but the ear cannot tell leisure from work.

    Reference time for noise exposure

    The upper action value corresponds to eight hours of exposure to a level of $85\,\text{dB(A)}$. The same energy dose can be received in a shorter time at a higher level, and every rise of $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the time. This rule of thumb follows from the fact that noise exposure is calculated as an average of energy. In this chapter the calculated time in which the energy dose of the upper action value builds up is called the reference time. The daily reference time is

    $$t_\text{ref} = \frac{t_0}{2^{\,n}}$$

    $$n = \frac{L - L_0}{3\,\text{dB}}$$

    where $L$ is the A-weighted noise level at the ear (in calculations simply dB), $L_0 = 85\,\text{dB}$ is the upper action value, $t_0 = 8\,\text{h}$ is the corresponding time, and $n$ tells how many steps of $3\,\text{dB}$ the level $L$ is above the action value. For example, at a level of $94\,\text{dB}$ we have $n = 3$, so the time is halved three times: $8\,\text{h} : 8 = 1\,\text{h}$. When $n$ is not a whole number, the power is calculated with a calculator.

    The reference time is not a permitted or safe time but a calculated reference value. Staying below the reference dose does not guarantee that an individual's hearing is safe, because people's hearing withstands noise in different ways, and it does not remove the employer's other duties: they begin already at the lower action value $80\,\text{dB(A)}$. Exposure should be kept clearly below the reference dose. The noise level at the ear is obtained with the rules of the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale: two equally strong sources raise the level by about $3\,\text{dB}$, and doubling the distance from a point source lowers the level in an open space by about $6\,\text{dB}$. A hearing protector lowers the level reaching the ear by its attenuation, when it is well fitted.

    Noise control

    Noise can be reduced in three places. At the source, a quieter machine is chosen, it is serviced, its vibration is isolated, or it is enclosed in a sound-absorbing casing. Along the path, the distance is increased or a barrier such as a noise wall is built. The barrier stops the direct sound, but sound bends over its edge because of diffraction (chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap), and the lower the sound, the more it bends. That is why a barrier dampens low sounds less well than high ones. Soft, porous surfaces dampen reflections along the path. At the ear, hearing protectors are used: ear defenders or earplugs, which typically attenuate by $20$–$30\,\text{dB}$.

    Control at the source is the most effective, because it protects everyone. A hearing protector protects only when it is on: at a level of $100\,\text{dB}$ the daily dose of the upper action value builds up in just $15\,\text{min}$, so taking the protector off for a moment cancels a large part of its benefit. At a concert, earplugs, distance from the loudspeakers and breaks in a quiet place are simple means, and the volume of headphones should be kept moderate.

    Room acoustics

    Reflection and absorption

    In a room, sound reaches the listener directly and reflected from the walls, ceiling and floor. Reflected sound travels a longer distance, so it arrives slightly later. Hard, smooth surfaces such as concrete, glass and tiles reflect most of the sound. Soft, porous materials such as mineral wool panels, textiles and people's clothes absorb sound: in them the vibration of the air changes into internal energy of the material. In a reverberant room the reflected sound fills the whole room, so the level falls less with distance from the source than outdoors.

    Reverberation

    When a sound source falls silent, reflections can still be heard for a moment. This dying sound is called reverberation. The reverberation time $T$ is the time in which the sound level falls by $60\,\text{dB}$ after the source has fallen silent; the intensity has then fallen to a millionth. Long reverberation muddles the syllables of speech together, and the room feels noisy, because people raise their voices over each other. In a classroom a reverberation time of about half a second is often aimed for, while in a concert hall music benefits from a time of about two seconds. Absorbing panels in the ceiling and on the walls shorten the reverberation time.

    Active noise cancelling

    Noise-cancelling headphones have a microphone that hears the noise of the environment. The electronics of the headphones form from it a signal that is in the opposite phase, and the loudspeaker of the headphones plays it. In the ear canal the pressure variations of the noise and of the anti-noise add together and largely cancel each other: this is destructive interference (chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference). The method works best for steady, low hum, such as the sound of an engine or ventilation. The period and the wavelength of a low sound are long, so the anti-noise easily stays in the opposite phase. At high frequencies even a small timing error spoils the cancellation, and they are dampened by the cups of the headphones. Noise-cancelling headphones allow music to be listened to at a lower volume, but they do not replace hearing protectors in loud noise.

    Loudspeaker and microphone

    A loudspeaker changes an electrical signal into sound. The alternating current from the amplifier flows in a coil that is attached to a cone and sits around the pole of a permanent magnet. The magnet exerts on the current-carrying coil a force whose direction changes with the direction of the current, so the coil and the cone vibrate at the same frequency as the current. The cone compresses and rarefies the air in front of it, and a sound wave is produced. Energy changes from electrical energy into the kinetic energy of the cone and further into sound. Most of the electrical energy, however, changes into internal energy of the coil: the efficiency of a loudspeaker is often only about $1\,\%$, so an electrical power of $50\,\text{W}$ produces a sound power of only about $0.5\,\text{W}$. The interplay of magnetism and electricity is studied in more detail on later courses.

    A microphone works the other way round. The pressure variations of the sound move a thin diaphragm. In a dynamic microphone the diaphragm moves a coil around the pole of a magnet, and an alternating voltage that varies in step with the sound is produced in the coil. Energy changes from sound into the kinetic energy of the diaphragm and from that into an electrical signal. A loudspeaker and a microphone are thus similar in construction, but energy changes in opposite directions in them.

    Common misconception

    “The ear gets used to noise.” Noise may stop feeling disturbing, but that is because the brain learns to filter out steady sound. The strain on the hair cells does not decrease with getting used to it; the damage accumulates unnoticed over the years. Hearing loss is often noticed only when it becomes difficult to distinguish speech in a hubbub, and by then the damage can no longer be repaired.

    Summary

    • Noise is unwanted or harmful sound. The dose that harms hearing depends on both level and time.
    • The action values for noise at work, $80\,\text{dB(A)}$ and $85\,\text{dB(A)}$, start duties of the employer; they are not safety limits. The limit value $87\,\text{dB(A)}$, with hearing protectors taken into account, must not be exceeded.
    • The upper action value corresponds to $8\,\text{h}$ at $85\,\text{dB(A)}$, and every rise of $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the reference time in which the same energy dose builds up: $t_\text{ref} = t_0 / 2^{\,n}$.
    • Noise is controlled at the source, along the path (distance, barrier, absorbing surfaces) and at the ear (hearing protectors for the whole exposure).
    • Hard surfaces reflect and soft ones absorb sound. The reverberation time is the time in which the level falls by $60\,\text{dB}$.
    • Anti-noise cancels low noise by destructive interference.
    • A loudspeaker changes an electrical signal into the motion of a cone and into sound, a microphone changes sound into the motion of a diaphragm and into an electrical signal.

      Example 1: A drummer's hearing protection

      A drummer practises with a band for $2.0\,\text{h}$ a day, and the noise level at the drummer's ear is $100\,\text{dB}$. By at least how many decibels must the hearing protectors attenuate, so that the noise exposure of the practice does not exceed the dose that corresponds to the upper action value $85\,\text{dB}$ for eight hours?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Noise level $L = 100\,\text{dB}$, exposure time $t = 2.0\,\text{h}$, reference dose $L_0 = 85\,\text{dB}$ for a time $t_0 = 8\,\text{h}$. We are asked for the minimum attenuation $\Delta L$ of the hearing protectors.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The dose depends on both the level and the time, and every rise of $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the reference time. We look for the reference level $L_\text{ref}$ at which the reference time is exactly $2.0\,\text{h}$. The protectors must lower the level reaching the ear to this value, so the attenuation is the difference of the levels.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. From the formula $t = \tfrac{t_0}{2^{\,n}}$ we get

      $$\begin{aligned} 2^{\,n} &= \frac{t_0}{t} \\ L_\text{ref} &= L_0 + n \cdot 3\,\text{dB} \\ \Delta L &= L - L_\text{ref} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Conversions. Not needed: both times are in hours, so the unit cancels, and the levels are in decibels.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution.

      $2^{\,n}$$= 8\,\text{h} : 2.0\,\text{h}$
      $= 4$

      so $n = 2$, because $2^2 = 4$. The reference level is

      $L_\text{ref}$$= 85\,\text{dB} + 2 \cdot 3\,\text{dB}$
      $= 91\,\text{dB}$

      and the attenuation is

      $\Delta L$$= 100\,\text{dB} - 91\,\text{dB}$
      $= 9\,\text{dB}$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The hearing protectors must attenuate by at least $9\,\text{dB}$. The levels are given as whole decibels, so the result is given as whole decibels.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Without protectors the reference time would be $8\,\text{h} : 2^5 = 15\,\text{min}$, so a two-hour practice would give eight times the dose. Eight times the intensity corresponds to three steps of $3\,\text{dB}$, that is, $9\,\text{dB}$, as we obtained. Ordinary protectors attenuate by $20$–$30\,\text{dB}$, so the calculated minimum is easy to meet, and a larger attenuation protects hearing better; musicians often use plugs that attenuate evenly so that playing sounds natural.

      Example 2: Find the error

      The level of a loudspeaker is $96\,\text{dB}$ at a distance of $2.0\,\text{m}$.A student calculates the level at $4.0\,\text{m}$ like this: “When the distance doubles, the intensity falls to a quarter, so the level is $96\,\text{dB} : 4 = 24\,\text{dB}$.” Where is the error?

      • The intensity of a point source is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, so it really does fall to a quarter. The error is that the decibel number was divided. Correct: $96\,\text{dB} - 6\,\text{dB} = 90\,\text{dB}$.

      • Correct. When the intensity is multiplied by some factor, the level changes by a constant amount: $10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg \tfrac14 \approx -6\,\text{dB}$. A result of $24\,\text{dB}$ would be quieter than a whisper, although the loudspeaker is only $4.0\,\text{m}$ away.

      • The difference is not in rounding. Decibels are not divided: a quarter of the intensity corresponds to a fall in level of $6\,\text{dB}$, so the level is $90\,\text{dB}$.

      • In a reverberant room the level does fall less, but the calculation is wrong already in an open space: a level of $96\,\text{dB}$ does not become $24\,\text{dB}$ but $90\,\text{dB}$.

        Answer

        the daily reference time $t_\text{ref}$ in hours

      1. Exercise 16-17

        A warehouse worker spends $4.0\,\text{h}$ of the working day at the loading dock, where the noise level is $88\,\text{dB}$, and the remaining $4.0\,\text{h}$ in the packing room, where the level is $82\,\text{dB}$.

        What percentage is the worker's daily dose of the dose that corresponds to the upper action value ($8\,\text{h}$ at a level of $85\,\text{dB}$)?

        %
        Answer

        $125\,\%$

        Show the solution

        Given

        $t_1 = 4.0\,\text{h}$ at a level $L_1 = 88\,\text{dB}$, $t_2 = 4.0\,\text{h}$ at a level $L_2 = 82\,\text{dB}$; reference $L_0 = 85\,\text{dB}$ and $t_0 = 8\,\text{h}$

        Find

        the day's dose as a percentage of the reference dose

        Principle

        The dose is the energy accumulated in the ear, so the shares of the different periods are added. The share of each period is the ratio of its duration to the reference time at that level.

        Formula

        $t_{\text{ref},i}$$= \dfrac{t_0}{2^{\,n_i}}$

        where $n_i = \dfrac{L_i - L_0}{3\,\text{dB}}$, and dose $= \left(\dfrac{t_1}{t_{\text{ref},1}} + \dfrac{t_2}{t_{\text{ref},2}}\right) \cdot 100\,\%$

        Conversions

        Not needed: the times are in hours, and the unit cancels.

        Substitution

        $n_1 = 1$, so

        $t_{\text{ref},1}$$= 8\,\text{h} : 2$
        $= 4.0\,\text{h}$
        $n_2$$= \dfrac{82\,\text{dB} - 85\,\text{dB}}{3\,\text{dB}}$
        $= -1$

        so

        $t_{\text{ref},2}$$= 8\,\text{h} \cdot 2$
        $= 16\,\text{h}$

        The dose

        $D$$= \dfrac{4.0\,\text{h}}{4.0\,\text{h}} + \dfrac{4.0\,\text{h}}{16\,\text{h}}$
        $= 1 + 0.25$
        $= 1.25$
        $= 125\,\%$

        Answer

        $125\,\%$

        The times are given to two figures, so the result is an estimate, about $1.3$ times the dose. The daily exposure exceeds the upper action value, so hearing protectors must be used and the noise must be reduced.

        The $4\,\text{h}$ at the loading dock alone fill the whole dose, so the result must be over $100\,\%$. The share of the packing room is small, because there the intensity is only a quarter of the intensity at the dock.

        $46\,\text{dB}$

      3. Exercise 16-19

        Noise-cancelling headphones dampen the low hum of an aircraft engine, whose frequency is about $100\,\text{Hz}$, well. The speech of passengers, whose frequencies are about $1000$–$3000\,\text{Hz}$, can be heard through clearly better. Explain the difference using wavelengths and phase, when the speed of sound in air is $343\,\text{m/s}$.

        Answer

        The wavelength of the hum is about $3.4\,\text{m}$ and its period $10\,\text{ms}$, so the anti-noise stays in the opposite phase at the ear; the wavelength of speech is only about $11$–$34\,\text{cm}$, and even a small difference in timing or position spoils the cancellation.

        Show the solution

        Find

        why anti-noise dampens a low hum better than speech

        Principle

        Anti-noise cancels noise by destructive interference only if it is in the opposite phase at the ear. The wavelength $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$ and the period $T = \dfrac{1}{f}$ tell how precisely the timing and the position must match.

        Model answer

        The wavelength of the hum is $\lambda = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{100\,\text{Hz}} \approx 3.4\,\text{m}$ and its period $10\,\text{ms}$. The whole ear canal and the inside of the cup are small compared with the wavelength, and the electronics have time to form the opposite signal, so the anti-noise stays in the opposite phase at the ear. The wavelengths of speech are $\dfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{3000\,\text{Hz}} \approx 11\,\text{cm}$ and $\dfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{1000\,\text{Hz}} \approx 34\,\text{cm}$, and the period is at most $1\,\text{ms}$. Even a small timing error or a difference in position of a few centimetres shifts the phase a lot, and then the waves no longer cancel each other. In addition, speech varies quickly, whereas a hum is steady. High sounds are dampened mainly by the cups of the headphones.

      2. Concept task: Reverberation

      4. Explanation task: Loudspeaker and microphone

      Explain how a loudspeaker changes the electrical signal of an amplifier into sound and how a microphone works the other way round. Say which forms of energy appear in each and where most of the electrical energy given to a loudspeaker ends up.

      Model answer

      In a loudspeaker the alternating current of the amplifier flows in a coil that is around the pole of a permanent magnet and attached to a cone. The magnet exerts on the coil a force whose direction changes with the current, so the cone vibrates at the frequency of the current and compresses and rarefies the air: sound is produced. Energy changes from electrical energy into kinetic energy and sound, but most of it, often about $99\,\%$, changes into internal energy of the coil. In a microphone the pressure variations of the sound move a diaphragm, and in a dynamic microphone the coil attached to the diaphragm moves around the pole of the magnet, so an alternating voltage that follows the sound is produced in the coil. Energy changes from sound into kinetic energy and into an electrical signal.
