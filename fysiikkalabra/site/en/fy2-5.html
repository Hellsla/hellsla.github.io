Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm

Why do a kilogram of petrol and a kilogram of air-dried firewood release different amounts of energy when they burn, even though both are just burning matter?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the energy released from a fuel using its heat of combustion and mass.
  • compare fuels and food by energy content and explain how food energy differs from the heat of combustion.
  • tell released energy apart from useful energy using efficiency.

Prior knowledge

  • Energy is neither created nor destroyed but transformed from one form to anotherchapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy
  • The unit of energy is the joule, and $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$chapter Energy transformations and the joule
  • Efficiency tells you what share of the energy taken in is usefulchapter Efficiency
  • Power is energy per unit of timechapter Power P = ΔE/Δt

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

At a petrol station a car is filled with petrol, and in a cottage fireplace firewood is burnt. On the side of a chocolate bar it says "energy $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams". In all three, energy is stored in the substance, and it is released when the substance burns or when the body uses it.

Is the same amount of energy released from a kilogram of different substances, and how can you calculate the energy released?

Optional Review: energy transformations
Firewood is burning in a fireplace and the room warms up. Which description of the energy transformation is correct?

  • Energy is not lost: the total energy is conserved (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy). Chemical energy is transformed into heat and light, and some of it is carried into the surroundings by the flue gases.

  • The wood does not move, so it has no kinetic energy. In burning, the chemical energy of the substance is transformed.

  • Correct. Chemical energy is stored in the structure of the wood, and when it burns it is transformed into heat and light (chapter Energy transformations and the joule). Today we calculate how much energy is released.

  • Energy does not appear out of nothing. The energy of the heat comes from the chemical energy of the wood, which is transformed into another form (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy).

Prediction 1: petrol and firewood

You burn a kilogram of petrol and a kilogram of air-dried firewood. Which one releases more energy, and how much more?

  • The difference is much smaller: the heats of combustion of the most common fuels are all of the same order of magnitude, about $10$–$50\,\text{MJ/kg}$. The ratio of petrol to wood is only $43 : 15 \approx 2.9$.

  • The length of burning does not tell you the amount of energy released. According to measurements, a kilogram of wood releases about $15\,\text{MJ}$ and a kilogram of petrol about $43\,\text{MJ}$, so wood is weaker here.

  • Correct. The heat of combustion of petrol is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$ and that of air-dried wood about $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$, and $43 : 15 \approx 2.9$. The values are rounded typical figures (sources below the table in the Formula section of the chapter).

  • Measurements disprove this: about $43\,\text{MJ}$ is released from petrol and about $15\,\text{MJ}$ from air-dried wood. The same mass does not mean the same energy, because substances contain different amounts of chemical energy for their mass.

Prediction 2: chocolate and firewood

The packaging of a chocolate bar says: energy $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams. A hundred grams of air-dried firewood is burnt. Which one contains more chemical energy?

  • Correct. The $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams of chocolate is $22\,\text{MJ/kg}$, and that is more than the $1500\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams, or $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$, of wood. Both numbers are energy per unit of mass, but the label gives the energy the body can use. If chocolate were burned, slightly more energy would be released, so chocolate wins the comparison in that case too.

  • In both, the energy is chemical, and its amount is measured in joules. When you take a hundred grams of each, the energies can be compared directly: $2200\,\text{kJ}$ in chocolate, $1500\,\text{kJ}$ in wood.

  • Food is also a store of chemical energy. A hundred grams of wood releases about $15\,\text{MJ/kg} \cdot 0.100\,\text{kg} = 1.5\,\text{MJ} = 1500\,\text{kJ}$ when it burns, which is less than the $2200\,\text{kJ}$ of the chocolate.

The heat of combustion $H$ tells you how much energy is released from one kilogram of fuel, and it is measured in $\text{MJ/kg}$.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    In this chapter a fuel means a chemical fuel: a substance whose chemical energy is released by burning it. The uranium in a nuclear power plant is also called a fuel, but nuclear fuel does not burn: its energy is nuclear energy (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy). Chemical energy is energy stored in the structure of the atoms and molecules of a substance. When a fuel burns, the structure changes and energy is released as heat and light. Energy does not appear out of nothing: chemical energy is transformed into another form. The same is true of food: your body slowly "burns" food and releases energy from it.

    Different fuels contain different amounts of chemical energy for their mass. The quantity that tells you this is the heat of combustion $H$. It is the energy released when one kilogram of fuel burns, and its unit is, for example, $\text{MJ/kg}$. The heat of combustion of petrol is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$, so about $43\,\text{MJ}$ is released from one kilogram (about $43 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}$). The heat of combustion of air-dried firewood (moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) is only about $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$, so to get the same energy you need almost three times the mass of wood.

    Because the heat of combustion is energy per unit of mass, the energy released is directly proportional to the mass of fuel burnt: twice as much fuel releases twice as much energy. This gives the law used to calculate the energy released.

    Heat of combustion and released energy

    The energy released when a fuel burns is

    $$E = Hm$$

    where $E$ is the energy released ($\text{J}$ or $\text{MJ}$), $H$ is the heat of combustion of the fuel ($\text{MJ/kg}$) and $m$ is the mass of fuel burnt ($\text{kg}$). The mass must be in kilograms, because the heat of combustion is energy per kilogram. For example, $3.0\,\text{kg}$ of petrol releases $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 3.0\,\text{kg} = 129\,\text{MJ} \approx 130\,\text{MJ}$, because the mass is known to two figures.

    The table gives the heats of combustion of common fuels. They are net heats of combustion, meaning that the heat released when water vapour in the flue gas condenses is not counted, and energy statistics use them. The values are rounded: the real value varies from batch to batch of fuel.

    Fuel $H\ (\text{MJ/kg})$
    natural gas 48
    petrol 43
    diesel 43
    coal 26
    wood pellets 17
    firewood (air-dried) 15
    peat (milled peat) 10

    Sources: firewood ($13\text{–}15\,\text{GJ/t}$, moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) and milled peat ($9\text{–}11\,\text{GJ/t}$, moisture $40\text{–}50\,\%$): Statistics Finland, Polttoaineluokitus, määritelmät (2017). Natural gas $48.0\,\text{MJ/kg}$: IPCC, 2006 Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories, volume 2, table of default heating values (2006). Typical values: coal (IPCC 2006: $25.8\,\text{MJ/kg}$), diesel (IPCC 2006: $43.0\,\text{MJ/kg}$), petrol ($43\,\text{MJ/kg}$; the IPCC 2006 value for pure petrol is $44.3\,\text{MJ/kg}$, but Finnish petrol contains ethanol, which has a lower heat of combustion) and wood pellets (published typical value). The values are rounded.

    Moisture and fuel quality

    Wet wood gives less energy per kilogram than dry wood, because part of its mass is water, which does not burn, and in addition part of the released energy is used to vaporise this water. This is why the firewood in the table is air-dried (moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) and the peat is milled peat with a moisture of $40\text{–}50\,\%$. Completely dry wood would have a heat of combustion of about $19\,\text{MJ/kg}$.

    The energy content of food and the heat of combustion

    A nutrition label gives energy per hundred grams, for example $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per $100\,\text{g}$. When you convert the energy to megajoules and the mass to kilograms, you get the energy content of the food per unit of mass, here $e = 22\,\text{MJ/kg}$. Both the energy content of food and the heat of combustion of a fuel can be given in the unit $\text{MJ/kg}$. The nutrition label describes the energy the body can use, whereas the heat of combustion of a fuel describes the energy released in burning. So they are not the same quantity: your body does not get all the energy from food that would be released if it burned, because, for example, part of the food is not absorbed from the intestine. The calculation is still the same as with the heat of combustion: the energy of food is $E = em$.

    Food $\text{kJ}$ / $100\,\text{g}$ $e$ ($\text{MJ/kg}$)
    potato, boiled 300 3
    rye bread 1000 10
    milk chocolate 2200 22
    butter 3000 30

    Typical nutritional values (reference: Fineli, THL), rounded and varying between products.

    Common misconception

    • Heat of combustion is not temperature. Many people think the heat of combustion tells you how hot the fuel burns. It is actually energy per unit of mass.
    • Energy is not created in burning. The chemical energy of the fuel is transformed into heat and light, but energy does not appear out of nothing.
    • Released energy is not useful energy. In a boiler, a fireplace or an engine, part of the energy is wasted, and the useful share is calculated with efficiency (chapter Efficiency).

    Burning carbon-containing fuels, such as wood, peat and fossil fuels, also produces carbon dioxide, which is covered in the chapter Energy production, the environment and climate change.

    Summary

    • The chemical energy of a fuel is released as heat and light when it burns. Energy is not created but transformed.
    • The heat of combustion $H$ is the energy released per kilogram of fuel, with the unit $\text{MJ/kg}$.
    • The energy released is $E = Hm$ when the mass $m$ is in kilograms.
    • The heat of combustion of petrol and diesel is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$, of coal $26$, of air-dried wood $15$ and of peat $10\,\text{MJ/kg}$.
    • The $\text{kJ}$ per $100\,\text{g}$ on a food package can be converted to $\text{MJ/kg}$ like a heat of combustion, but it describes the energy the body can use, not the energy released in burning.
    • Released energy is not the same as useful energy: useful energy is found with efficiency.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      In a fireplace, $6.0\,\text{kg}$ of air-dried firewood with a heat of combustion of $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ is burnt. How much energy is released from the wood? Give the answer in megajoules and in kilowatt-hours.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The mass of the firewood is $m = 6.0\,\text{kg}$ and from the table the heat of combustion is $H = 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. We want the energy released, $E$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The wood burns, so its chemical energy is transformed into heat. The heat of combustion tells you the energy per kilogram, so the energy released is found by multiplying the heat of combustion by the mass.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The quantity we want, $E$, is already alone on the left side of the equation:

      $$E = Hm$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The mass is already in kilograms, so you can calculate the megajoules directly. For kilowatt-hours we use $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$, from which $1\,\text{MJ} = 1\,\text{kWh} : 3.6$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} E &= Hm \\ &= 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{kg} \\ &= 90\,\text{MJ} \end{aligned}$$

      In kilowatt-hours

      $E$$= 90\,\text{MJ}$
      $= 90 \cdot (1\,\text{kWh} : 3.6)$
      $= 25\,\text{kWh}$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The wood releases $90\,\text{MJ}$ of energy, which is $25\,\text{kWh}$. The answer is given to two significant figures, because both the mass $6.0\,\text{kg}$ and the heat of combustion $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ are known to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. One kilogram of wood gives $15\,\text{MJ}$, which is about $4\,\text{kWh}$, so six kilograms give about six times as much: $6 \cdot 4\,\text{kWh} = 24\,\text{kWh}$, which matches the result. The energy equals that of a one-kilowatt ($1\,\text{kW}$) heater switched on for $25\,\text{h}$. However, you do not get all of it as heat for the room, because some of the heat leaves with the flue gases (chapter Efficiency).

      1. Concept

      What does the heat of combustion $H$ of a fuel tell you?

      • The burning rate is related to power (chapter Power P = ΔE/Δt), not to the heat of combustion. The heat of combustion tells you how much energy is released, not how fast.

      • The heat of combustion is not a temperature but energy per unit of mass. The temperature of the flame and the energy released are different things.

      • The total energy also depends on the mass. The heat of combustion is the energy per kilogram, and the total energy is $E = Hm$.

      • Correct. The unit $\text{MJ/kg}$ tells you the energy per unit of mass, and the total energy is found by multiplying it by the mass: $E = Hm$.

        1. Core

          At a petrol station, $2.0\,\text{kg}$ of petrol was bought for a chainsaw. The heat of combustion of petrol is $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          How much energy is released when the petrol burns? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          86 MJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          petrol, mass $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$ and heat of combustion $H = 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          the energy $E$ released in burning, in megajoules

          Principle

          The chemical energy of petrol is released when it burns. The heat of combustion gives the energy released per kilogram, so the energy is the product of the heat of combustion and the mass.

          Formula

          $E = Hm$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is already in kilograms and the answer is wanted in megajoules, like the heat of combustion.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{kg}$
          $= 86\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $86\,\text{MJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          One kilogram gives $43\,\text{MJ}$, so two kilograms give twice as much, about $2 \cdot 40\,\text{MJ} = 80\,\text{MJ}$. The result is of the right order of magnitude and positive.

        2. Core

          On a winter day, $2.0\,\text{kg}$ of natural gas is burnt in a gas boiler. Read the heat of combustion of natural gas from the bar chart in the figure.

          How much energy is released from the gas? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          96 MJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          natural gas, mass $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$; heat of combustion $H = 48\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ is read from the figure

          Find

          the energy $E$ released, in megajoules

          Principle

          When the gas burns, its chemical energy is transformed into heat. The energy released is proportional to the mass, so we use the definition of the heat of combustion.

          Reading the graph

          The top bar of the bar chart is natural gas, and the number $48$ is printed at its end. The unit on the horizontal axis is $\text{MJ/kg}$, so $H = 48\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$; the end of the bar is also a little before the grid line $50$.

          Formula

          $E = Hm$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the heat of combustion in megajoules per kilogram, so the energy comes out in megajoules.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 48\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{kg}$
          $= 96\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $96\,\text{MJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          The heat of combustion of gas is about $50\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, so two kilograms give about $100\,\text{MJ}$. The result is slightly smaller, because $48 < 50$.

        3. A coal-fired power plant burns $12\,000\,\text{kg}$ of coal per hour, with a heat of combustion of $26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          How much energy is released from the coal in an hour? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          310000 MJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          coal, mass $m = 12\,000\,\text{kg}$ and heat of combustion $H = 26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          the energy $E$ released in one hour, in megajoules

          Principle

          The chemical energy of coal is released when it burns, and the energy released is the product of the heat of combustion and the mass. The mass is already in kilograms, as the heat of combustion requires.

          Formula

          $E = Hm$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the answer is wanted in megajoules, like the heat of combustion.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 12\,000\,\text{kg}$
          $= 312\,000\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $310\,000\,\text{MJ}$

          Two significant figures like the heat of combustion $26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          Reasonableness

          Roughly

          $10\,000\,\text{kg} \cdot 30\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 300\,000\,\text{MJ}$

          The energy for one hour at a power plant is large, because about ten tonnes of fuel are used.

        5. The energy content of a hiker's nut mix is $24\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ (example value). A handful of nuts weighs $0.040\,\text{kg}$.

          How much energy do you get from the handful? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          0.96 MJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          nuts, energy content $e = 24\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ and mass $m = 0.040\,\text{kg}$

          Find

          the energy $E$ contained in the nuts, in megajoules

          Principle

          The energy of food is calculated in the same way as for a fuel: the energy content is the energy the body can use per kilogram, so the energy is the product of it and the mass. The energy content is not a heat of combustion, even though the unit is the same.

          Formula

          $E = em$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the answer is wanted in megajoules.

          Substitution

          $E$$= em$
          $= 24\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.040\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.96\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $0.96\,\text{MJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $0.040\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          An estimate with rounded numbers: $25\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.04\,\text{kg} = 1\,\text{MJ}$, so $0.96\,\text{MJ}$ is of the right order of magnitude. It is about $1000\,\text{kJ}$, the typical energy of a small snack.

        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          The nutritional label on a loaf of bread (example) says: energy $1200\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams. A slice of bread weighs $40\,\text{g}$.

          How much energy is in the slice? Give the answer in kilojoules.

          kJ
          Answer

          480 kJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          according to the label $1200\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams, mass of the slice $m = 40\,\text{g}$

          Find

          the energy $E$ of the slice, in kilojoules

          Principle

          The label gives the energy content of the food, that is the energy the body can use, per unit of mass. It is not a heat of combustion, but the calculation is done in the same way: the label is converted to megajoules per kilogram and multiplied by the mass.

          Formula

          $E = em$, where the energy content $e$ is the energy on the label divided by the mass on the label

          Conversions

          $1200\,\text{kJ}$$= 1200 \cdot (1 : 1000)\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 1.2\,\text{MJ}$
          $100\,\text{g}$$= 100 \cdot (1 : 1000)\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.100\,\text{kg}$

          and

          $40\,\text{g}$$= 40 \cdot (1 : 1000)\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.040\,\text{kg}$

          Substitution

          $e$$= 1.2\,\text{MJ} : 0.100\,\text{kg}$
          $= 12\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          and

          $E$$= 12\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.040\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.48\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 480\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $480\,\text{kJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $40\,\text{g}$.

          Reasonableness

          The slice is $40\,\%$ of a hundred grams, and $40\,\%$ of $1200\,\text{kJ}$ is $0.4 \cdot 1200\,\text{kJ} = 480\,\text{kJ}$. The result is smaller than $1200\,\text{kJ}$, as it should be.

        2. Core

          A diesel generator on a building site must release $1.8\,\text{GJ}$ of energy from fuel. The heat of combustion of diesel is $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          How many kilograms of diesel must be burnt? Give the answer in kilograms.

          kg
          Answer

          42 kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          the energy released must be $E = 1.8\,\text{GJ}$, heat of combustion of diesel $H = 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          the mass $m$ of diesel to be burnt, in kilograms

          Principle

          By the law $E = Hm$ the energy is the product of the heat of combustion and the mass. Now the energy is known and the mass is wanted, so the law is solved for the mass.

          Formula

          $E = Hm$, so $m = \dfrac{E}{H}$

          Conversions

          $1.8\,\text{GJ}$$= 1.8 \cdot 1000\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 1800\,\text{MJ}$

          Substitution

          $m$$= 1800\,\text{MJ} : 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 41.86\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $42\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures like the data $1.8\,\text{GJ}$ and $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          Reasonableness

          One kilogram gives about $43\,\text{MJ}$, so $42\,\text{kg}$ gives about $42 \cdot 43\,\text{MJ} \approx 1800\,\text{MJ}$, which is $1.8\,\text{GJ}$.

        3. In the pellet burner of a detached house, $40\,\text{kg}$ of wood pellets with a heat of combustion of $17\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ are burnt. The efficiency of the boiler is $85\,\%$.

          How much useful energy is obtained from the pellets to heat the house? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          580 MJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          wood pellets, mass $m = 40\,\text{kg}$ and heat of combustion $H = 17\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$; efficiency of the boiler $\eta = 85\,\% = 0.85$

          Find

          the useful energy $E_\text{useful}$ in megajoules

          Principle

          The pellets release the energy $E_\text{in} = Hm$. Efficiency is the share of the energy taken in that is obtained as useful energy (chapter Efficiency), so only part of the released energy ends up heating the house.

          Formula

          $E_\text{in} = Hm$ and

          $\eta$$= \dfrac{E_\text{useful}}{E_\text{in}}$

          so $E_\text{useful} = \eta\, E_\text{in}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and $85\,\% = 0.85$.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{in}$$= 17\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 40\,\text{kg}$
          $= 680\,\text{MJ}$

          and

          $E_\text{useful}$$= 0.85 \cdot 680\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 578\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $580\,\text{MJ}$

          Two significant figures like the data $40\,\text{kg}$ and $85\,\%$.

          Reasonableness

          The useful energy is smaller than the $680\,\text{MJ}$ released, because part of the heat leaves with the flue gases. Estimate: $0.85 \approx 0.9$ and $0.9 \cdot 680\,\text{MJ} \approx 610\,\text{MJ}$, so $578\,\text{MJ}$ is of the right order of magnitude.

        4. A passenger car needs $48\,\text{MJ}$ of useful energy to drive $100\,\text{km}$. The efficiency of the engine is $25\,\%$. The heat of combustion of petrol is $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          How many kilograms of petrol are used on a $100\,\text{km}$ journey? Give the answer in kilograms.

          kg
          Answer

          4.5 kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          useful energy $E_\text{useful} = 48\,\text{MJ}$, efficiency $\eta = 25\,\% = 0.25$, heat of combustion of petrol $H = 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          the mass $m$ of petrol, in kilograms

          Principle

          The engine transforms only part of the energy of the petrol into motion (chapter Efficiency). First we use the efficiency to find the energy needed from the petrol, and then the law $E = Hm$ to find the mass.

          Formula

          $\eta$$= \dfrac{E_\text{useful}}{E_\text{in}}$

          so

          $E_\text{in}$$= \dfrac{E_\text{useful}}{\eta}$

          $E_\text{in} = Hm$, so $m = \dfrac{E_\text{in}}{H}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: $25\,\% = 0.25$ and the energies are in megajoules.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{in}$$= 48\,\text{MJ} : 0.25$
          $= 192\,\text{MJ}$

          and

          $m$$= 192\,\text{MJ} : 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 4.465\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $4.5\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures like the data $48\,\text{MJ}$ and $25\,\%$.

          Reasonableness

          The density of petrol is about $0.74\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\textsf{l}}$, so $4.5\,\text{kg}$ is about $6\,\textsf{l}$, a typical consumption per hundred kilometres. The energy of the petrol, $192\,\text{MJ}$, is four times the useful energy, because the efficiency is one quarter.

        1. To heat a cottage, $120\,\text{MJ}$ of energy must be released from fuel. The energy can be obtained either from air-dried firewood ($15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$) or from light heating oil ($43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$). Calculate the mass of each fuel needed and say how many kilograms more wood than oil are needed. Also explain why more wood is needed. Give the answer in kilograms.

          kg
          Answer

          5.2 kg

          Show the solution

          Given

          the energy needed $E = 120\,\text{MJ}$, air-dried firewood $H_\text{wood} = 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ and heating oil $H_\text{oil} = 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          how many kilograms more wood than oil are needed, $\Delta m = m_\text{wood} - m_\text{oil}$

          Principle

          The same energy $E$ must be released from both fuels. By the law $E = Hm$ the mass is the energy divided by the heat of combustion, so a smaller heat of combustion needs a larger mass.

          Formula

          $E = Hm$, so $m = \dfrac{E}{H}$ for each fuel, and $\Delta m = m_\text{wood} - m_\text{oil}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the energy is in megajoules and the heats of combustion in megajoules per kilogram.

          Substitution

          $m_\text{wood}$$= 120\,\text{MJ} : 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 8.0\,\text{kg}$

          and

          $m_\text{oil}$$= 120\,\text{MJ} : 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 2.79\,\text{kg}$

          Difference:

          $\Delta m$$= 8.0\,\text{kg} - 2.79\,\text{kg}$
          $= 5.21\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $5.2\,\text{kg}$

          More wood is needed, two significant figures like the data $120\,\text{MJ}$ and $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. More wood is needed because one kilogram of wood contains less chemical energy than one kilogram of oil.

          Reasonableness

          The mass of wood is about $2.9$ times the mass of oil, since $43 : 15 = 2.87$. So $2.87 \cdot 2.79\,\text{kg} \approx 8.0\,\text{kg}$, and the difference is $2.87 - 1 = 1.87$ times the mass of oil: $1.87 \cdot 2.79\,\text{kg} \approx 5.2\,\text{kg}$, as it should be.

        2. A hiker carries food with an energy content of $18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ (example value). There is $2.4\,\text{kg}$ of food, and the hiker needs $12\,\text{MJ}$ of energy from food per day.

          How many daily rations does the food provide? Give the answer as a decimal number and justify your calculation.

          daily rations
          Answer

          3.6 daily rations

          Show the solution

          Given

          energy content of the food $e = 18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, mass $m = 2.4\,\text{kg}$, daily consumption $12\,\text{MJ}$

          Find

          the number of daily rations $N$ that the food provides (as a decimal number)

          Principle

          The total energy obtainable from the food is found by multiplying the energy content by the mass, as with a fuel by the law $E = Hm$. The number of rations is how many times the daily consumption fits into the total energy, so $N = E : \text{daily consumption}$.

          Formula

          $E = em$ and the number of days $N = \dfrac{E}{12\,\text{MJ}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the energy in megajoules.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 2.4\,\text{kg}$
          $= 43.2\,\text{MJ}$

          and

          $N$$= 43.2\,\text{MJ} : 12\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 3.6$

          Answer

          $3.6$

          Daily rations, two significant figures like the data $2.4\,\text{kg}$ and $18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          Reasonableness

          The need for three rations is $36\,\text{MJ}$ and for four $48\,\text{MJ}$. There is $43.2\,\text{MJ}$ of food, so the result lies between three and four: three full days and part of a fourth.

        3. A peat-fired power plant burns $36\,\text{t}$ of milled peat per hour, with a heat of combustion of $10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. The efficiency of the plant in electricity production is $35\,\%$.

          What is the electric power of the power plant? Give the answer in megawatts. Remember: $1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s}$ and one hour is $3600\,\text{s}$.

          MW
          Answer

          35 MW

          Show the solution

          Given

          peat $m = 36\,\text{t}$ per hour, $H = 10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, efficiency of electricity production $\eta = 35\,\% = 0.35$, time $t = 1\,\text{h}$

          Find

          the electric power $P$ in megawatts

          Principle

          In one hour the peat releases the energy $E_\text{in} = Hm$. By the efficiency, only the share $\eta$ of it becomes electricity (chapter Efficiency). Power is energy per unit of time, so power = energy : time (chapter Power P = ΔE/Δt).

          Formula

          $E_\text{in} = Hm$, $E_\text{useful} = \eta\, E_\text{in}$ and $P = \dfrac{E_\text{useful}}{t}$, together $P = \dfrac{\eta H m}{t}$

          Conversions

          $36\,\text{t}$$= 36 \cdot 1000\,\text{kg}$
          $= 36\,000\,\text{kg}$

          and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$. Since $1\,\text{MJ/s} = 1\,\text{MW}$, the unit of power comes out directly in megawatts.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{in}$$= 10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 36\,000\,\text{kg}$
          $= 360\,000\,\text{MJ}$
          $E_\text{useful}$$= 0.35 \cdot 360\,000\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 126\,000\,\text{MJ}$

          and

          $P$$= 126\,000\,\text{MJ} : 3600\,\text{s}$
          $= 35\,\text{MJ/s}$
          $= 35\,\text{MW}$

          Answer

          $35\,\text{MW}$

          Two significant figures like the data $10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ and $35\,\%$.

          Reasonableness

          If all the energy of the peat became electricity, the power would be $360\,000\,\text{MJ} : 3600\,\text{s} = 100\,\text{MW}$. With an efficiency of $35\,\%$ we get $35\,\%$ of that, $35\,\text{MW}$, which is a typical power for a small power plant.
