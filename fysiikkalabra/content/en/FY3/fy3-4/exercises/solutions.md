## fy3-4-L1-01
**Given:** the skier's weight, that is, the force on the snow, $F = 720\,\text{N}$, total area of the skis $A = 0.30\,\text{m}^2$
**Find:** the pressure $p$ on the snow in pascals
**Principle:** Pressure is the ratio of the force acting perpendicular to a surface to the area of that surface. The weight is spread evenly over the bases of the skis, so the whole force $720\,\text{N}$ is spread over the whole area $0.30\,\text{m}^2$.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed, because the force is in newtons and the area in square metres: $1\,\text{N}/\text{m}^2 = 1\,\text{Pa}$.
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{720\,\text{N}}{0.30\,\text{m}^2} = 2400\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = 2400\,\text{Pa}$
**Answer:** $2400\,\text{Pa}$, two significant figures like the area $0.30\,\text{m}^2$ (the zeros are only place holders).
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $700\,\text{N} / 0.3\,\text{m}^2 \approx 2300\,\text{Pa}$. If the same person stood in boots whose soles have about a tenth of the area of the skis, the pressure would be ten times greater, about $24\,\text{kPa}$. That is why skis carry you on deep snow although boots sink in. The result is only about $2\,\%$ of atmospheric pressure ($101\,\text{kPa}$).

## fy3-4-L1-02
**Given:** depth $h = 95\,\text{m}$, density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the hydrostatic pressure $\Delta p$ in kilopascals
**Principle:** In a liquid at rest the pressure grows with depth, because deeper down there is more liquid above. Water is practically incompressible, so $\rho$ is constant at all depths and the pressure grows in direct proportion to the depth. The hydrostatic pressure asked for is only the part caused by the water; atmospheric pressure is not included.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = \rho g h$; the unknown $\Delta p$ is already alone on the left-hand side
**Conversions:** All quantities are in SI units, so the result comes in pascals. At the end $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta p = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 95\,\text{m} = 931\,950\,\text{Pa} = 931.95\,\text{kPa}$
**Answer:** $930\,\text{kPa}$, two significant figures like the depth $95\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Water adds about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, so $95\,\text{m}$ gives about $950\,\text{kPa}$. The exact value is slightly smaller, because $g < 10\,\text{m/s}^2$. The result is about $930/101 \approx 9.2$ atmospheres.

## fy3-4-L1-03
**Given:** depth $h = 18.0\,\text{m}$, atmospheric pressure at the surface $p_0 = 98.5\,\text{kPa}$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the total pressure $p$ at the gauge in kilopascals
**Principle:** The gauge feels both the weight of the water and the weight of the air above the water surface. The surface of the liquid transmits atmospheric pressure to the whole liquid unchanged, and the hydrostatic pressure $\rho g h$ is added on top of it, so the pressures are added.
**Formula:** $p = p_0 + \rho g h$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side
**Conversions:** Atmospheric pressure in pascals: $98.5\,\text{kPa} = 98\,500\,\text{Pa}$. At the end the result is converted back: $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.
**Substitution:** $\rho g h = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 18.0\,\text{m} = 176\,580\,\text{Pa}$; $p = 98\,500\,\text{Pa} + 176\,580\,\text{Pa} = 275\,080\,\text{Pa} = 275.08\,\text{kPa}$
**Answer:** $275\,\text{kPa}$, three significant figures like the depth $18.0\,\text{m}$ and the atmospheric pressure $98.5\,\text{kPa}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $18\,\text{m}$ of water is about $1.8$ atmospheres, that is, about $180\,\text{kPa}$, and atmospheric pressure of about $100\,\text{kPa}$ is added to it, giving about $280\,\text{kPa}$ in all. The result is greater than the hydrostatic pressure alone, $177\,\text{kPa}$, and almost three atmospheres.

## fy3-4-L1-04
**Given:** the weight of the stove, that is, the force on the floor, $F = 750\,\text{N}$, total area of the legs $A = 0.0025\,\text{m}^2$
**Find:** the pressure $p$ on the floor in kilopascals
**Principle:** Pressure is the ratio of force to area. The stove stands still, so the force on the floor is equal to the weight of the stove, and it is transmitted to the floor only through the small area of the legs.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side
**Conversions:** The force is in newtons and the area in square metres, so the result comes in pascals. At the end $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{750\,\text{N}}{0.0025\,\text{m}^2} = 300\,000\,\text{Pa} = 300\,\text{kPa}$
**Answer:** $300\,\text{kPa}$, two significant figures like the area $0.0025\,\text{m}^2$ (the zeros are place holders).
**Reasonableness:** The area of one leg is $0.0025\,\text{m}^2 / 4 \approx 6\,\text{cm}^2$, for example $2.5\,\text{cm} \cdot 2.5\,\text{cm}$, which is a believable size for a leg. The pressure is about three atmospheres, so the legs of a heavy stove can sink into a soft floor. If the area were ten times greater, the pressure would be only a tenth.

## fy3-4-L1-05
**Given:** depth $h = 0.45\,\text{m}$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the hydrostatic pressure $\Delta p$ in pascals
**Principle:** In the bucket the water is at rest and its density is constant, so the pressure grows in direct proportion to the depth. At the bottom the hydrostatic pressure depends only on the depth, not on the shape of the bucket or the amount of water.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = \rho g h$; the unknown $\Delta p$ is already alone on the left-hand side
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed, because all quantities are in SI units and the result is asked for in pascals.
**Substitution:** $\Delta p = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.45\,\text{m} = 4414.5\,\text{Pa}$
**Answer:** $4400\,\text{Pa}$, two significant figures like the depth $0.45\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Water adds about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, so in less than half a metre the pressure is about $4.5\,\text{kPa}$. The result is only about $4\,\%$ of atmospheric pressure, so the bottom does not feel a large gauge pressure.

## fy3-4-L2-01
**Given:** force $F = 600\,\text{N}$, contact area of the heel $A = 1.5\,\text{cm}^2$
**Find:** the pressure $p$ in megapascals
**Principle:** Pressure is the ratio of force to area. At the start of a step the whole weight $600\,\text{N}$ is transmitted to the floor through the small area of the heel, so the force is spread only over the area $1.5\,\text{cm}^2$.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{cm}^2 = (0.01\,\text{m})^2 = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$, so $A = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$. At the end $1\,\text{MPa} = 10^6\,\text{Pa}$.
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{600\,\text{N}}{1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2} = 4\,000\,000\,\text{Pa} = 4.0\,\text{MPa}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{MPa}$, two significant figures like the area $1.5\,\text{cm}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** The pressure is about $4.0\,\text{MPa} / 0.101\,\text{MPa} \approx 40$ atmospheres. A bare sole with an area of about $200\,\text{cm}^2$ would give only about $600\,\text{N} / 0.02\,\text{m}^2 = 30\,\text{kPa}$, so the pressure under the heel is more than a hundred times greater. That is why a heel leaves a dent in a parquet floor.

## fy3-4-L2-02
**Given:** gauge pressure $\Delta p = 350\,\text{kPa}$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the height $h$ of the water surface above the tap in metres
**Principle:** When the taps are closed, the water is at rest, so the gauge pressure at the tap is the hydrostatic pressure of the column of water, $\Delta p = \rho g h$. The water between the tower and the tap is continuous, so only the difference in height matters, not the length of the pipes.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = \rho g h$, so $h = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\rho g}$
**Conversions:** $350\,\text{kPa} = 350\,000\,\text{Pa}$, so that the units $\text{Pa} = \text{kg}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^2)$ cancel to metres.
**Substitution:** $h = \dfrac{350\,000\,\text{Pa}}{1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}} = 35.678\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $35.7\,\text{m}$, three significant figures: the gauge pressure $350\,\text{kPa} = 3.50\cdot10^{2}\,\text{kPa}$ and the density $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$ are given here to three figures.
**Reasonableness:** Water gives about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, so $350\,\text{kPa}$ corresponds to about $35\,\text{m}$. The exact value is slightly greater, because $g < 10\,\text{m/s}^2$. Checking backwards, $\rho g h = 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 35.7\,\text{Pa} \approx 350\,\text{kPa}$. Water towers are about this tall.

## fy3-4-L2-03
**Given:** from the graph, in both liquids $p_0 = 100\,\text{kPa}$; line A passes through the point $(10\,\text{m};\ 200\,\text{kPa})$ and line B through the point $(10\,\text{m};\ 250\,\text{kPa})$; $h_\text{B} = 4.0\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the depth $h_\text{A}$ at which the pressure in liquid A equals the pressure in liquid B at depth $h_\text{B}$
**Principle:** In a liquid $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, so the $(h, p)$ graph is a straight line whose slope $k = \rho g$ is greater for the denser liquid. The densities do not have to be calculated; the slopes are read from the graph.
**Reading the graph:** The lines start from the same point $(0\,\text{m};\ 100\,\text{kPa})$ and end at crossings of the grid. So $k_\text{A} = \dfrac{(200 - 100)\,\text{kPa}}{10\,\text{m}} = 10\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}}$ and $k_\text{B} = \dfrac{(250 - 100)\,\text{kPa}}{10\,\text{m}} = 15\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}}$.
**Formula:** $p_0 + k_\text{A} h_\text{A} = p_0 + k_\text{B} h_\text{B}$, so $h_\text{A} = \dfrac{k_\text{B}}{k_\text{A}}\, h_\text{B}$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed: the kilopascals cancel in the ratio $k_\text{B}/k_\text{A}$.
**Substitution:** $h_\text{A} = \dfrac{15\,\text{kPa/m}}{10\,\text{kPa/m}} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m} = 6.0\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $h_\text{A} = 6.0\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the depth $4.0\,\text{m}$. A value read by eye in the range $5.8$–$6.2\,\text{m}$ is accepted.
**Reasonableness:** Liquid A is less dense, so a greater depth than $4.0\,\text{m}$ is needed to reach the same pressure. Check: $100\,\text{kPa} + 10\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} = 160\,\text{kPa} = 100\,\text{kPa} + 15\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m}$.

## fy3-4-L2-04
**Given:** gauge pressure $\Delta p = 0.85\,\text{bar}$ (according to the problem $1\,\text{bar} = 100\,\text{kPa}$), area of the lid $A = 380\,\text{cm}^2$
**Find:** the force $F$ on the lid in kilonewtons
**Principle:** The gauge pressure pushes the lid from the inside at every point, and pressure is force divided by area. The total force is therefore the gauge pressure multiplied by the area of the lid. The pressure of the outside air is not part of this, because only the force caused by the gauge pressure is asked for.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = \dfrac{F}{A}$, so $F = \Delta p\,A$
**Conversions:** $0.85\,\text{bar} = 85\,\text{kPa} = 85\,000\,\text{Pa}$ and $380\,\text{cm}^2 = 380 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2 = 0.0380\,\text{m}^2$. At the end $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $F = 85\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0380\,\text{m}^2 = 3230\,\text{N} = 3.23\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $3.2\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the gauge pressure $0.85\,\text{bar}$.
**Reasonableness:** The force equals the weight of a mass of $3230\,\text{N} / 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 330\,\text{kg}$, which is a lot, although the gauge pressure is less than one atmosphere. Estimate: $80\,\text{kPa} \cdot 0.04\,\text{m}^2 = 3200\,\text{N}$. That is why the lid must not be opened before the gauge pressure has been released.

## fy3-4-L3-01
**Given:** mass with the rucksack $m = 85\,\text{kg}$, greatest permitted pressure $p_\text{max} = 6.0\,\text{kPa}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the whole weight on one foot
**Find:** the smallest area $A$ of one snowshoe in square metres
**Principle:** The hiker does not sink when the pressure on the snow is at most $p_\text{max}$. For a moment the whole weight is on one foot, so the force on this shoe is the whole weight $G = mg$, and the area is smallest when the pressure is exactly $p_\text{max}$.
**Formula:** $G = mg$ and $p_\text{max} = \dfrac{G}{A}$, so $A = \dfrac{G}{p_\text{max}} = \dfrac{mg}{p_\text{max}}$
**Conversions:** $6.0\,\text{kPa} = 6000\,\text{Pa}$. The mass is already in kilograms.
**Substitution:** $G = 85\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 833.85\,\text{N}$; $A = \dfrac{833.85\,\text{N}}{6000\,\text{Pa}} = 0.13898\,\text{m}^2$
**Answer:** $0.14\,\text{m}^2$, two significant figures like the pressure $6.0\,\text{kPa}$ and the mass $85\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Checking backwards, $833.85\,\text{N} / 0.14\,\text{m}^2 \approx 5960\,\text{Pa}$, which is less than $6000\,\text{Pa}$. The area is, for example, $25\,\text{cm} \cdot 56\,\text{cm}$, the size of an ordinary snowshoe. The area of the sole of an ordinary shoe is about $0.03\,\text{m}^2$, and with it the pressure would be about $28\,\text{kPa}$, so the shoe would sink.

## fy3-4-L3-02
**Given:** height of the hose above the lid $h = 4.0\,\text{m}$, area of the lid $A = 0.25\,\text{m}^2$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; amount of water in the hose $0.40\,\textsf{l}$
**Find:** the increase $\Delta F$ in the force on the lid in kilonewtons
**Principle:** The water in the barrel and the water in the hose are one liquid at rest, so at the lid the pressure grows by the hydrostatic pressure $\rho g h$ of the column of water in the hose. The pressure depends only on the height, not on the amount of water, so a thin hose is enough. This increase in pressure acts on the whole area of the lid.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = \rho g h$ and $\Delta F = \Delta p \, A$, so $\Delta F = \rho g h A$
**Conversions:** All quantities are in SI units. At the end $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta p = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m} = 39\,240\,\text{Pa}$; $\Delta F = 39\,240\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.25\,\text{m}^2 = 9810\,\text{N} = 9.81\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $9.8\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the height $4.0\,\text{m}$ and the area $0.25\,\text{m}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** The $0.40\,\textsf{l}$ of water in the hose weighs only $0.40\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 3.9\,\text{N}$, but almost $10\,\text{kN}$ acts on the lid. The ratio is $9810 / 3.9 \approx 2500$, which is the same as the ratio of the cross-sectional areas of the lid and the hose ($1\,\text{cm}^2$). That is why a thin hose can break the barrel.

## fy3-4-L3-03
**Given:** layer of water $h_2 = 1.60\,\text{m}$, $\rho_2 = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$; on top of it a layer of oil $h_1 = 0.40\,\text{m}$, $\rho_1 = 850\,\text{kg/m}^3$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the gauge pressure $\Delta p$ at the bottom of the well in kilopascals
**Principle:** The liquids are at rest, and the bottom carries the whole column of liquid above it. Because the density changes at the interface, the hydrostatic pressure of each layer is calculated with its own density and height, and the pressures are added. Gauge pressure means that atmospheric pressure is not included.
**Formula:** the pressures of the layers are added, so $\Delta p = \rho_1 g h_1 + \rho_2 g h_2$
**Conversions:** All quantities are in SI units, so the pressure comes in pascals. At the end $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.
**Substitution:** $\rho_1 g h_1 = 850\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.40\,\text{m} = 3335.4\,\text{Pa}$; $\rho_2 g h_2 = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 1.60\,\text{m} = 15\,696\,\text{Pa}$; $\Delta p = 3335.4\,\text{Pa} + 15\,696\,\text{Pa} = 19\,031.4\,\text{Pa}$
**Answer:** $19.0\,\text{kPa}$. In a sum the decimals are determined by the least precise term: the $3.3\,\text{kPa}$ of the oil layer (thickness $0.40\,\text{m}$, two figures) is precise to one decimal place, so $3.3 + 15.7 = 19.0\,\text{kPa}$.
**Reasonableness:** If the whole $2.00\,\text{m}$ were water, the gauge pressure would be $1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 2.00 = 19\,620\,\text{Pa}$. Oil is lighter, so the result is smaller: the difference is $(1000 - 850) \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.40\,\text{Pa} = 588.6\,\text{Pa}$ and $19\,620 - 588.6 = 19\,031.4\,\text{Pa}$, as calculated.
