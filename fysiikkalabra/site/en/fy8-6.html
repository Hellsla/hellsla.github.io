Light from an LED and measuring the Planck constant

Why does a blue LED need a higher voltage than a red LED to light up?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why the threshold voltage of an LED depends on the wavelength of its light.
  • determine the threshold voltage of an LED from a current–voltage graph.
  • determine the Planck constant from the slope of a line fitted to threshold voltages.

Prior knowledge

  • The energy of a photon is $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, and $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ is the energy that an electron receives in a voltage of $1\,\text{V}$chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon
  • When an electron moves from a higher energy level to a lower one, a photon is created whose energy is the difference of the energies of the levelschapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms
  • The states and energies of the electrons of an atom are quantisedchapter The wave-mechanical model of the atom and orbitals

1Think about it

A red, a green and a blue LED are connected in parallel to the same adjustable voltage source, and the voltage is raised slowly from zero. The LEDs do not light up at the same time, although their voltage is the same at every moment.

What decides the voltage at which an LED lights up, and how can the Planck constant be measured from this?

Optional Review: the energy of a photon
The wavelength of red light is $630\,\text{nm}$ and that of blue light $470\,\text{nm}$. Which light has a photon of greater energy?

  • Correct. The energy of a photon is $E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon). The energy of a photon of blue light is about $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 470\,\text{nm} \approx 2.64\,\text{eV}$ and that of red light about $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 630\,\text{nm} \approx 1.97\,\text{eV}$.

  • The same speed does not mean the same energy. The energy of a photon depends on the frequency, $E = hf$, and the frequency of blue light, whose wavelength is shorter, is greater, so the energy of its photon is greater.

  • The energy of a photon is $E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$: it decreases when the wavelength grows. The energy of a red photon is about $1.97\,\text{eV}$ and that of a blue one about $2.64\,\text{eV}$.

Simulation

How to use

Choose an LED from the menu and adjust the voltage $U$ between the terminals of the LED with the slider. The ammeter ($\text{A}$) shows the current $I$ through the LED and the voltmeter ($\text{V}$) the voltage of the LED. The LED glows in its own colour, the brighter the greater the current, and the wavy lines are the photons it emits. On the right (on a phone, below) is the graph $(U, I)$: every voltage you try stays on it as a point in the colour of the LED, and the large point is the present state. Clear the graph removes the points. The graph does not show currents above $30\,\text{mA}$, and the readouts then show a warning.

Assumptions of the model

An adjustable voltage source and a protective resistor set the voltage of the LED; the slider shows the reading of the voltmeter directly. In the model the current is practically zero when the voltage is smaller than the threshold voltage of the LED, and above it grows linearly. For all the LEDs of the model the current grows equally steeply above the threshold voltage, which corresponds to a resistance of $18\,\Omega$. The threshold voltages of the model are slightly smaller than $\tfrac{hc}{e\lambda}$, as in real LEDs. The curve of a real LED is slightly curved also above the threshold voltage, and its threshold voltage changes a little with temperature. The readouts are values calculated by the model.

How the calculation works

The current is calculated from the equation $I = \tfrac{s}{R} \ln\!\left(1 + \exp\tfrac{U - U_\text{k}}{s}\right)$, where $s = 0.035\,\text{V}$, $R = 18\,\Omega$ and $U_\text{k}$ is the threshold voltage of the LED. When $U$ is clearly greater than $U_\text{k}$, the equation is approximately $I = \tfrac{U - U_\text{k}}{R}$: a line that cuts the voltage axis at $U = U_\text{k}$. When $U$ is clearly smaller than $U_\text{k}$, the current is vanishingly small.

Above the threshold voltage the current of the LED grows almost linearly. The threshold voltage is defined as the voltage at which the extension of this straight part cuts the voltage axis ($I = 0$). The points of the curved beginning do not belong to the line, so they are left out of the fit.

Reading the graph

The points in the range $2.00$–$2.40\,\text{V}$ are on a line, and the points below $1.90\,\text{V}$ curve towards zero. Two points far from each other are chosen from the line, $(2.10\,\text{V}$; $12.23\,\text{mA})$ and $(2.40\,\text{V}$; $28.89\,\text{mA})$.

the slope is $k = \dfrac{I_2 - I_1}{U_2 - U_1}$ and the line is $I = k\,(U - U_\text{k})$; at $I = I_2$ we get $U_\text{k} = U_2 - \dfrac{I_2}{k}$

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    An LED is a semiconductor component

    An LED, or light-emitting diode, is a diode made of a semiconductor: it lets an electric current through in one direction only and emits light when the current flows. In the chapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms we saw that the electron of a single atom has only certain energy levels. In a solid the atoms are so close to each other that their energy levels merge into wide energy bands. In a semiconductor, between the almost full valence band and the almost empty conduction band there is an energy gap $E_\text{g}$: an energy interval in which an electron cannot have a state.

    The electron falls into a hole, and a photon is born

    An LED is made of two different semiconductors. In the conduction band of one, the n region, there are free electrons. In the valence band of the other, the p region, there are empty places for electrons, called holes. When a current flows through the LED, electrons and holes meet at the interface between the regions. An electron falls from the conduction band into a hole of the valence band and gives up its energy as one photon. The energy of the photon is approximately equal to the energy gap, $hf \approx E_\text{g}$. So the colour of an LED depends on the semiconductor material: in the semiconductor of a red LED the energy gap is about $1.9\,\text{eV}$, and in that of a blue LED about $2.6\,\text{eV}$.

    Threshold voltage

    When an electron passes across a voltage $U$, the electric field does work $eU$ on it, where $e$ is the elementary charge. This is exactly how the electronvolt was defined in the chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon. For an electron to be able to create a photon whose energy is $E_\text{g}$, it must receive at least approximately this energy from the voltage source. This is why an LED hardly conducts a current or emits light at a small voltage. The current starts to grow steeply when the voltage reaches the threshold voltage $U_\text{k}$ of the LED, for which $eU_\text{k} \approx E_\text{g} \approx hf$. The photon of blue light needs more energy than that of red light, so the threshold voltage of a blue LED is greater.

    A voltage greater than the threshold voltage increases the current, that is, more electrons pass through the LED every second and more photons are created. The energy of a single photon does not grow, however, because it is decided by the energy gap. The extra energy turns into heat in the LED. So the colour of an LED stays the same when the voltage grows; only the brightness of the light grows.

    LED, laser diode and incandescent lamp

    Light sources can be compared by measuring their spectra with a spectrometer, as in the chapter Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms. The hot filament of an incandescent lamp emits a continuous spectrum that contains all the wavelengths of visible light. The spectrum of an LED is a narrow band whose width is typically $20$–$40\,\text{nm}$, because the energies of the electrons and holes vary a little at the edges of the bands. The wavelength given for an LED is the wavelength of the peak of its spectrum. A laser diode is a semiconductor diode like an LED, in which the light is amplified: its spectrum is a very narrow line, about $1\,\text{nm}$ wide, and the light travels as a narrow beam. We return to the operation of the laser in the chapter Lasers and quantum structures. Never look into the beam of a laser diode.

    Threshold voltage and photon energy

    Threshold voltage and wavelength

    When the threshold voltage of an LED is reached, the energy that an electron receives from the voltage source is approximately the energy of one photon:

    $$eU_\text{k} \approx hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

    where $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ is the elementary charge, $U_\text{k}$ the threshold voltage of the LED ($\text{V}$), $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ the Planck constant, $f$ the frequency of the light ($\text{Hz}$), $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$ the speed of light and $\lambda$ the wavelength of the peak of the spectrum of the LED ($\text{m}$). For a red LED, $\lambda = 630\,\text{nm}$, this gives the threshold voltage $U_\text{k} \approx \tfrac{hc}{e\lambda}$. In the chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon we calculated $hc = 1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm}$, and because $1\,\text{eV} = e \cdot 1\,\text{V}$, we have $\tfrac{hc}{e} = 1240\,\text{V}\,\text{nm}$. Thus $U_\text{k} \approx 1240\,\text{V}\,\text{nm} : 630\,\text{nm} = 1.97\,\text{V}$. The measured threshold voltage is usually a little smaller, for example $1.88\,\text{V}$. The energy of a photon in electronvolts is approximately the same number as the threshold voltage in volts, because $1\,\text{eV}$ is the energy that an electron receives in a voltage of $1\,\text{V}$.

    Determining the threshold voltage

    The threshold voltage is measured by connecting the LED in series with a protective resistor and an ammeter to an adjustable voltage source, and a voltmeter across the terminals of the LED. The voltage is increased gradually, and the current is recorded at each voltage. In the $(U, I)$ graph the current is at first almost zero and then grows almost linearly. The threshold voltage is obtained when the line fitted to the straight part of the graph is extended to the voltage axis, where $I = 0$. This method is more repeatable than searching for the voltage at which the LED seems to start to glow, because the sensitivity of the eye varies with colour and from person to person.

    The Planck constant from the slope

    From the equation $eU_\text{k} = hf$ we get the threshold voltage as a function of frequency:

    $$U_\text{k} = \frac{h}{e}\,f$$

    When the threshold voltages of several LEDs of different colours are plotted as a graph $(f, U_\text{k})$, the points are approximately on a line whose slope is $k = \tfrac{h}{e}$. The Planck constant is obtained from the slope:

    $$h = ek$$

    where $k$ is the slope of the fitted line ($\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{Hz}} = \text{V}\,\text{s}$) and $e$ the elementary charge; $\text{C} \cdot \text{V}\,\text{s} = \text{J}\,\text{s}$. The slope is read from the fitted line, not from two measured points. In Graphical Analysis Pro the line is fitted with the choices Curve Fit and Linear, and the $m$ of the result box is the slope.

    In real series of measurements the measured points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty. In addition, the line usually does not pass exactly through the origin: in the data of the figure it cuts the vertical axis about $0.05\,\text{V}$ below zero. The measured threshold voltage is not exactly $\tfrac{hf}{e}$: the electrons also receive a little extra energy from thermal motion, and the threshold voltage is defined as the extension of the line. The difference is typically about $0.1\,\text{V}$. When the difference is almost the same for all the LEDs, it shifts the line but does not change its slope. This is why the Planck constant is determined from the line of many LEDs and not from one LED. The width of the spectrum of the LED also brings uncertainty: the wavelength of the peak is known only to within a few nanometres.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that by increasing the voltage of an LED its light becomes bluer, because the electrons receive more energy. The energy of a photon is decided by the energy gap of the semiconductor, however, not by the voltage. A voltage greater than the threshold voltage increases the current, so more photons are created and the LED shines brighter, but the energy of every photon is still approximately $E_\text{g}$. The extra energy heats the LED. So blue light cannot be obtained from a red LED at any voltage, and too large a current only breaks the LED.

    Another mistake is to think that the measurement has failed if the line of the graph $(f, U_\text{k})$ does not pass through the origin. The measured threshold voltage differs from the value $\tfrac{hf}{e}$ by almost the same amount for all the LEDs. The difference shifts the line up or down but does not change its slope $\tfrac{h}{e}$, from which the Planck constant is obtained.

    Summary

    • In an LED an electron falls from the conduction band of the semiconductor into a hole of the valence band and creates one photon whose energy is approximately equal to the energy gap: $hf \approx E_\text{g}$.
    • An LED starts to conduct and emit light when the voltage reaches the threshold voltage: $eU_\text{k} \approx hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. An LED of a shorter wavelength needs a greater threshold voltage.
    • The threshold voltage is obtained by extending the straight part of the $(U, I)$ graph to the voltage axis.
    • The slope of the graph $(f, U_\text{k})$ is $k = \tfrac{h}{e}$, so $h = ek$. The line does not have to pass through the origin.
    • The spectrum of an incandescent lamp is continuous, that of an LED a narrow band and that of a laser diode a very narrow line.

      Problem

      A group measured the threshold voltages of five LEDs of different colours and fitted a line to the points $(f, U_\text{k})$. The fitted line passes through the points $(4.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$; $1.74\,\text{V})$ and $(7.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$; $2.96\,\text{V})$. The elementary charge is $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. What value did the group get for the Planck constant?

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. The points of the fitted line are $(f_1, U_1) = (4.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$; $1.74\,\text{V})$ and $(f_2, U_2) = (7.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$; $2.96\,\text{V})$, and the elementary charge is $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. We are asked for the Planck constant $h$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. At the threshold voltage the energy received by an electron is approximately the energy of a photon, $eU_\text{k} \approx hf$, so the slope of the graph $(f, U_\text{k})$ is $k = \tfrac{h}{e}$. The slope is calculated from two points of the fitted line that are far from each other, not from individual measured points.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The slope is $k = \tfrac{U_2 - U_1}{f_2 - f_1}$, and $h$ is solved from the equation $k = \tfrac{h}{e}$:

      $$h = ek = \frac{e\,(U_2 - U_1)}{f_2 - f_1}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. None are needed: the voltages are in volts and the frequencies in hertz. From the units,

      $\text{C} \cdot \tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{Hz}}$$= \text{C} \cdot \text{V} \cdot \text{s}$
      $= \text{J}\,\text{s}$

      because $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$ and $1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. The differences are

      $U_2 - U_1$$= 2.96\,\text{V} - 1.74\,\text{V}$
      $= 1.22\,\text{V}$
      $f_2 - f_1$$= 7.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz} - 4.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$
      $= 3.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$

      $$\begin{aligned} h &= \frac{e\,(U_2 - U_1)}{f_2 - f_1} \\ &= \frac{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1.22\,\text{V}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}} \\ &= 6.515 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \end{aligned}$$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the peak wavelengths and threshold voltages of four LEDs: $625\,\text{nm}$ and $1.86\,\text{V}$, $590\,\text{nm}$ and $1.97\,\text{V}$, $520\,\text{nm}$ and $2.27\,\text{V}$, $465\,\text{nm}$ and $2.55\,\text{V}$; $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$

          Asked

          the Planck constant $h$ in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Principle

          At the threshold voltage $eU_\text{k} \approx hf$, so the points $(f, U_\text{k})$ are approximately on a line whose slope is $\tfrac{h}{e}$. The points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty, so the line is fitted to all the points. An almost equal deviation for all the LEDs shifts the line but does not change the slope.

          Reading the graph

          When the frequencies are calculated and the line is fitted in Graphical Analysis Pro with the choices Curve Fit and Linear, the result box shows $m = 4.212 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{Hz}}$ and $b = -0.164\,\text{V}$.

          Formula

          $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$ and $k = \dfrac{h}{e}$, so $h = ek$

          Conversions

          wavelengths to metres, for example $625\,\text{nm} = 625 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; $\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{Hz}} = \text{V}\,\text{s}$

          Substitution

          $f_1$$= \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{625 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
          $= 4.797 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$

          in the same way $f_2 = 5.081 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, $f_3 = 5.765 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ and $f_4 = 6.447 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$. From the slope of the fit,

          $h$$= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 4.212 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$
          $= 6.748 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $6.75 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Three significant figures like the threshold voltages.

          Reasonableness

          A check from the farthest points:

          $(2.55\,\text{V} - 1.86\,\text{V}) : (1.650 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz})$
          $= 4.18 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$

          close to the value of the fit. The result differs from the accepted value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ by about $+1.8\,\%$, which is a usual accuracy for this method.

        2. Exercise 6-17

          A student estimates the uncertainty of the Planck constant by drawing, for the points $(f, U_\text{k})$, the steepest and the gentlest line that still pass through the uncertainty bars of the points. The slope of the steepest line is $4.25 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$ and that of the gentlest $3.95 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$. The elementary charge is $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$.

          How great is the uncertainty $\Delta h$ of the Planck constant, when it is estimated as half the difference of the values given by these lines? Give the answer in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$.

          J s
          Answer

          $\Delta h \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-35}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          slope of the steepest line $k_\text{max} = 4.25 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, of the gentlest $k_\text{min} = 3.95 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$

          Asked

          the uncertainty $\Delta h$ of the Planck constant in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Principle

          Each slope gives a value for the Planck constant, $h = ek$. The steepest and the gentlest line that fit the points bound an estimated interval in which the slope probably lies; the uncertainty is estimated as half the width of the interval.

          Formula

          $h_\text{max} = ek_\text{max}$ and $h_\text{min} = ek_\text{min}$, so

          $\Delta h$$= \dfrac{h_\text{max} - h_\text{min}}{2}$
          $= \dfrac{e\,(k_\text{max} - k_\text{min})}{2}$

          Conversions

          None are needed: $\text{C} \cdot \text{V}\,\text{s} = \text{J}\,\text{s}$.

          Substitution

          $k_\text{max} - k_\text{min}$$= 4.25 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} - 3.95 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$
          $= 0.30 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$

          so

          $\Delta h$$= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 0.30 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} : 2$
          $= 2.403 \cdot 10^{-35}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $\Delta h \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-35}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures like the difference of the slopes $0.30 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The middle value is

          $e \cdot 4.10 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$
          $= 6.57 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

          so the result is $h = (6.6 \pm 0.2) \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$. The uncertainty is about $4\,\%$, and the accepted value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ is within this interval.

        3. Exercise 6-18

          An ultraviolet LED with the wavelength $365\,\text{nm}$ is used to check fluorescent markings. A current of $20\,\text{mA}$ flows through the LED, and it emits ultraviolet radiation with the power $15\,\text{mW}$. Use the values $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$ and $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$.

          What percentage of the electrons passing through the LED create a photon that gets out of the LED?

          %
          Answer

          $22\,\%$

          Show the solution

          Given

          wavelength $\lambda = 365\,\text{nm}$, current $I = 20\,\text{mA}$, radiated power $P = 15\,\text{mW}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$

          Asked

          the share of the number of photons that get out, of the number of electrons passing through the LED, as a percentage

          Principle

          The radiated power is the energy carried away by the photons per second, so the number of photons per second is the power divided by the energy of one photon. The current tells the number of electrons per second, $\tfrac{I}{e}$. One electron can create at most one photon, so the share is at most $100\,\%$.

          Formula

          $N_\text{f}$$= \dfrac{P}{hc/\lambda}$
          $= \dfrac{P\lambda}{hc}$

          $N_\text{e} = \dfrac{I}{e}$, so the share is

          $\dfrac{N_\text{f}}{N_\text{e}}$$= \dfrac{P\lambda e}{hcI}$

          Conversions

          $P = 15\,\text{mW} = 0.015\,\text{W}$, $I = 20\,\text{mA} = 0.020\,\text{A}$, $\lambda = 365 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; finally the share to a percentage by multiplying by $100\,\%$

          Substitution

          $N_\text{f}$$= \dfrac{0.015\,\text{J/s} \cdot 365 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}$
          $= 2.756 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
          $N_\text{e}$$= \dfrac{0.020\,\text{C/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}}$
          $= 1.2484 \cdot 10^{17}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$

          so

          $\dfrac{N_\text{f}}{N_\text{e}}$$= \dfrac{2.756 \cdot 10^{16}}{1.2484 \cdot 10^{17}}$
          $= 0.2208$
          $= 22.1\,\%$

          Answer

          about $22\,\%$, two significant figures like the power $15\,\text{mW}$ and the current $20\,\text{mA}$.

          Reasonableness

          The energy of a photon is $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 365\,\text{nm} \approx 3.4\,\text{eV}$, so if every electron created a photon, the radiated power would be about $3.4\,\text{V} \cdot 0.020\,\text{A} = 68\,\text{mW}$. The measured $15\,\text{mW}$ is about a fifth of that, as was obtained. The share is less than $100\,\%$, as it must be.

        5. Exercise 6-20

          A measurement made with LEDs in class gave for the line between the threshold voltage and the frequency, $U_\text{k} = kf + b$, the slope $k = 4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$ and the intercept $b = -0.10\,\text{V}$ (sample data).

          The threshold voltage of an unknown LED was measured with the same method as $U_\text{k} = 2.46\,\text{V}$, with a measurement uncertainty of the voltage of $\pm 0.05\,\text{V}$. A spectrometer showed the peak of the spectrum of the LED at $483\,\text{nm}$.

          Use the values $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$ and $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$.

          a) Calculate the frequency and the wavelength of the light of the unknown LED using the calibration line.

          b) Also calculate the wavelength from the equation $eU_\text{k} = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ and compare both with the result of the spectrometer. Which method is better and why?

          c) Estimate the uncertainty of the wavelength of part a) when the voltage may be off by $0.05\,\text{V}$.

          Write in the box the wavelength of part a) in nanometres.

          nm
          Answer

          $\lambda = 486\,\text{nm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          calibration line $U_\text{k} = kf + b$, $k = 4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, $b = -0.10\,\text{V}$; the unknown LED’s $U_\text{k} = 2.46\,\text{V}$ ($\pm 0.05\,\text{V}$), spectrometer peak $483\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$

          Find

          the wavelength $\lambda$ of the light of the unknown LED in nanometres using the calibration line, and its uncertainty

          Principle

          LEDs measured with the same method lie on the same line $U_\text{k}(f)$, so the frequency of the unknown LED is obtained by solving $f$ from the equation of the line. The line also contains the constant difference $b$ between the measured threshold voltage and the value $\tfrac{hf}{e}$, so it is more accurate than the equation $eU_\text{k} = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, which assumes $b = 0$.

          Formula

          $U_\text{k} = kf + b$, so $f = \dfrac{U_\text{k} - b}{k}$ and $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f}$. Uncertainty:

          $\Delta f$$= \dfrac{\Delta U}{k}$
          $\Delta\lambda$$= \lambda\,\dfrac{\Delta f}{f}$

          Comparison: $\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{eU_\text{k}}$.

          Conversions

          Not needed in part a); the results from metres to nanometres, $1\,\text{m} = 10^{9}\,\text{nm}$.

          Substitution

          $f$$= \dfrac{2.46\,\text{V} - (-0.10\,\text{V})}{4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}}$
          $= 6.169 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$
          $\lambda$$= \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{6.169 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}}$
          $= 4.860 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 486.0\,\text{nm}$

          Directly from the formula

          $\lambda$$= \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 2.46\,\text{V}}$
          $= 5.041 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 504.1\,\text{nm}$

          Uncertainty:

          $\Delta f$$= \dfrac{0.05\,\text{V}}{4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}}$
          $= 1.205 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{Hz}$
          $\Delta\lambda$$= 486.0\,\text{nm} \cdot \dfrac{1.205 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{Hz}}{6.169 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}}$
          $= 9.5\,\text{nm}$

          Answer

          $\lambda = 486\,\text{nm}$

          Three significant figures like the slope $4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$. The uncertainty is $\Delta\lambda \approx 10\,\text{nm}$, so $\lambda = (486 \pm 10)\,\text{nm}$. The calibration line differs from the spectrometer result $483\,\text{nm}$ by only $0.6\,\%$, whereas the direct formula $eU_\text{k} = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ gives $504\,\text{nm}$, $4.4\,\%$ too much. The line is the better method because it takes into account the constant difference $b = -0.10\,\text{V}$: the measured threshold voltage is about $0.1\,\text{V}$ smaller than $\tfrac{hf}{e}$, and the formula assumes that the difference is zero.

          Reasonableness

          Check backwards:

          $\tfrac{hc}{e\lambda}$$= 1240\,\text{V}\,\text{nm} : 486\,\text{nm}$
          $= 2.55\,\text{V}$

          and subtracting the difference $0.10\,\text{V}$ from this gives $2.45\,\text{V}$, close to the measured value $2.46\,\text{V}$. The spectrometer result $483\,\text{nm}$ is within the uncertainty interval $476\,\text{nm}$–$496\,\text{nm}$, but the result of the direct formula, $504\,\text{nm}$, is not.
