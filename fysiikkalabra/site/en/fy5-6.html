Satellites and planets in circular orbits

Why does the International Space Station ISS not fall to the Earth, and why do the astronauts float inside it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why a satellite stays in a circular orbit and why an astronaut is weightless.
  • calculate the orbital speed and orbital period of a satellite from the equation $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
  • estimate when the model of uniform circular motion describes an orbit well.

Prior knowledge

  • In uniform circular motion a body has a centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre, and the speed is $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle
  • The gravitational force between two bodies is $F = \gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2}$, and the acceleration of free fall at distance $r$ is $g = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2}$chapter The gravitational force between bodies

Assumptions of the model

Only the gravitational force of the Earth $F = \gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2}$ acts on the satellite, towards the centre of the Earth, where $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and the radius of the Earth is $R = 6371\,\text{km}$. Air resistance, the gravity of the Moon and the Sun and the motion of the Earth are not in the model, so, for example, an orbit that descends to an altitude of $155\,\text{km}$ would not last in reality. The Earth stays in place, because the satellite is much lighter. At the distance of the Moon the satellite is a test body: the real Moon's own mass would move the Earth too. All readings are values of the model.

How the calculation works

The orbit is not drawn ready-made; the motion is calculated in small time steps. At each step the acceleration $\tfrac{F}{m} = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2}$ towards the centre of the Earth is calculated, and the position and velocity are updated with it (the velocity Verlet method, step $\tfrac{1}{1500}$ of the orbital period of the local circular orbit). The orbital period $T$ is the time in which the satellite goes once round the Earth. The length of the red force arrow is the same for all masses at the moment of launch and decreases like $\tfrac{1}{r^2}$; the magnitude of the force is shown in the readings. The length of the blue velocity arrow is proportional to the speed.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A body that goes round another body under gravity is called a satellite. The Moon is a natural satellite of the Earth; the ISS and weather satellites are built by people. The path along which a satellite goes round is called an orbit. In space practically only one force acts on a satellite: the gravitational force of the Earth, which points towards the centre of the Earth. Because it is the only force, it is also the net force. In the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle we found that in uniform circular motion the net force points to the centre of the path and gives the body its centripetal acceleration. In a circular orbit the gravitational force does exactly this: it is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does not change the speed but turns the direction of the velocity all the time.

    So a satellite falls towards the Earth all the time, but it never hits it. Without gravity it would continue straight ahead along the tangent of the orbit. Gravity bends the path, and when the speed is just right, the path curves at the same rate as the surface of the Earth curves away beneath the satellite. Then the distance from the centre of the Earth stays the same and the orbit is a circle.

    If the speed is lower, gravity turns the path more sharply than a circle would require: the satellite descends lower and then returns to the starting altitude, or hits the Earth if the speed is much too low. If the speed is higher, the path curves more gently and the satellite rises farther away before returning. In these cases the orbit is not a circle but an ellipse, and the model of uniform circular motion does not describe it.

    Orbital speed in a circular orbit

    When a satellite of mass $m$ goes round a central body in a circular orbit, the gravitational force is the only force and therefore the net force. According to Newton's second law it must be equal to the force required by the circular motion, $m a_\text{n} = \tfrac{m v^2}{r}$:

    $$\gamma \frac{M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$

    where $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$ is the gravitational constant (many English books write it as G), $M$ is the mass of the central body ($\text{kg}$), $m$ is the mass of the satellite ($\text{kg}$), $r$ is the orbital radius ($\text{m}$) and $v$ is the orbital speed of the satellite ($\text{m/s}$). The orbital radius is measured from the centre of the central body: for a satellite of the Earth $r = R + h$, where $R = 6371\,\text{km}$ is the radius of the Earth and $h$ is the altitude of the satellite above the surface.

    The mass $m$ of the satellite is on both sides of the equation and cancels. When the equation is multiplied by $r$ and divided by the mass $m$, we get $v^2 = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r}$, so the orbital speed in a circular orbit is

    $$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{r}}$$

    The orbital speed depends only on the mass $M$ of the central body and on the orbital radius $r$, not on the mass of the satellite. A heavy space station and a small CubeSat orbit at the same altitude at the same speed. The farther away a satellite orbits, the lower its orbital speed.

    In one revolution the satellite travels the circumference $2\pi r$ of the circle, so the orbital period follows from the relation $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ of the chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle:

    $$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}$$

    where $T$ is the orbital period ($\text{s}$). The orbit of a faraway satellite is longer and its speed lower, so the orbital period grows quickly as the distance grows: the ISS goes round the Earth in about an hour and a half, the Moon in about $27\,\text{d}$. When the radius and the orbital period of a satellite are measured, the same equation can also be solved for the mass $M$ of the central body. This is how, for example, the masses of the Earth, the Sun and Mars have been determined.

    Geostationary orbit

    A geostationary satellite orbits above the equator in the same direction as the Earth rotates, and its orbital period is the same as the rotation period of the Earth, $23\,\text{h}\;56\,\text{min}$. It therefore seems to stay in place in the sky, and a satellite dish can be aimed at it once. Because the orbital period determines the orbital radius, all geostationary satellites orbit at the same altitude, about $35\,800\,\text{km}$.

    Weightlessness

    On a space station the astronauts float, and a scale would show zero. The reason is not the absence of gravity. At the altitude of the ISS, $420\,\text{km}$, the acceleration due to the gravitational force is $\tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2} \approx 8.6\,\text{m/s}^2$, about $88\,\%$ of the value at the surface of the Earth. On an astronaut of mass $70\,\text{kg}$ there acts a weight of about $605\,\text{N}$, that is, the gravitational force $F$ of the Earth, whereas at the surface it would be $687\,\text{N}$.

    The station and the astronaut are in free fall: only gravity acts on each, and each gets the same acceleration towards the Earth. Because they fall in the same way, the floor does not press on the astronaut and a scale does not have to support them, so the support force is zero. Weightlessness (apparent weightlessness) means that nothing supports the body: only gravity acts on it, and it falls freely together with its surroundings. The support force is zero, although the weight acts all the time.

    The same state can be experienced for a moment on the Earth too: during a jump, in a freely falling lift, or in an aeroplane whose engines are adjusted so that it flies along the path of a projectile. Weightlessness is not explained by any "centrifugal force" cancelling gravity. If some force cancelled gravity, the net force would be zero, and the station would continue straight ahead and would not orbit the Earth.

    When the circular model holds

    The model of uniform circular motion is a simplification. It describes the motion of a satellite well when

    • the central body is much more massive than the satellite, so it can be considered to stay in place,
    • the gravity of other bodies and air resistance are so small that only the gravity of the central body acts,
    • the speed of the satellite is exactly the circular-orbit speed of the radius of its orbit, so that the distance from the centre stays constant.

    Real satellite motion always deviates slightly from the model. In a low orbit the thin upper atmosphere slows the motion, the gravity of the Moon and the Sun pulls on the satellite, and the mass of the Earth is not distributed exactly spherically, because the Earth is slightly flattened at the poles. Because of the drag, for example, the orbit of the ISS is raised from time to time with rocket engines.

    Many real orbits are nearly circles. The distance of the Earth from the Sun varies by only about $3\,\%$ of the mean distance, so the circular model describes the orbit of the Earth well. The distance of the Moon from the Earth varies by about $11\,\%$, so the model gives a good approximation for the Moon. The orbit of Halley's comet, in contrast, is very elongated, and the circular model does not suit it at all.

    Small deviations from the model show up in precise measurements. The orbital speed of the Moon calculated from measured values, $1.023\,\text{km/s}$, is about $0.5\,\%$ greater than the $1.018\,\text{km/s}$ given by the model, because the mass of the Moon is about $\tfrac{1}{81}$ of the mass of the Earth: the Earth and the Moon go round their common centre of mass, and the Earth does not stay in place. At the altitude of the ISS thin air brakes the station and its orbit slowly descends, so the station is raised with engines regularly.

    Common misconception

    "There is no gravity in space, which is why astronauts float." This is wrong. At the altitude of the ISS gravity is almost as strong as at the surface of the Earth, and it is exactly what keeps the station in its orbit. Without gravity the station would fly straight away. The astronauts float because they and the station fall freely with the same acceleration.

    Another error is to put the altitude $h$ into the formula $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$ instead of the distance from the centre of the Earth, $r = R + h$. For the ISS this would give a speed of about $31\,\text{km/s}$, four times the real one. A third error is to think that a heavy satellite has to orbit faster: a greater mass experiences a greater gravitational force, but it also needs a greater force to turn, so the mass cancels.

    Summary

    • Only the gravitational force of the central body acts on a satellite. It is the net force, which in a circular orbit gives the centripetal acceleration: $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
    • The orbital speed in a circular orbit is $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$ and the orbital period $T = \tfrac{2\pi r}{v}$. The mass of the satellite has no effect, and a satellite orbiting farther away is slower and has a longer orbital period.
    • The orbital radius is measured from the centre of the central body: $r = R + h$.
    • Weightlessness means falling freely together with your surroundings with nothing to support you: gravity acts, but the support force is zero.
    • The circular model holds when the central body is much more massive than the satellite, other forces are small and the orbit is nearly a circle.

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} &v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{r}} \\ &\gamma M = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \\ &\quad \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg} \\ &\quad = 3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2 \\ &v = \sqrt{\frac{3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{6.791 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}} \\ &= 7659.7\,\text{m/s} \\[6pt] &T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} \\ &= \frac{2\pi \cdot 6.791 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}{7659.7\,\text{m/s}} \\ &= 5570.6\,\text{s} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The orbital speed is $v \approx 7.66\,\text{km/s}$ and the orbital period $T \approx 5570\,\text{s} \approx 92.8\,\text{min}$. The results are given to three significant figures, like the mass of the Earth $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. According to NASA the ISS goes round the Earth in about $92.9\,\text{min}$, so the model is very close to the measured value. Right at the surface of the Earth the speed of a circular orbit would be

      $\sqrt{gR}$$= \sqrt{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}$
      $\approx 7.9\,\text{km/s}$

      the ISS is slightly farther away, so its speed is slightly lower, as it should be.

      • The expression $\tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2}$ is an acceleration, not a speed; its unit is $\text{m/s}^2$. The orbital speed is obtained from the equation $v^2 = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r}$, so the square root belongs in the formula. The error is that the altitude $h$ has been put in the formula and not the distance $r = R + h$.

      • The mass of the station cancels from the equation $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$, so it is not needed. The error is in the distance: the altitude $420\,\text{km}$ has been put in the formula instead of the distance $r = R + h$.

      • Correct. The gravitational force depends on the distance from the centre of the Earth, not from the surface. With the correct radius $r = 6.791 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$ the speed is $7.66\,\text{km/s}$. The result $30.8\,\text{km/s}$ is almost four times the correct speed.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      The Hubble Space Telescope orbits the Earth in a nearly circular orbit, and according to NASA its orbital period is about $95.4\,\text{min}$.

      At what altitude above the surface does Hubble orbit? The mass of the Earth is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius $6371\,\text{km}$. Answer in kilometres.

      km
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      You now know the orbital period, not the speed. Which two equations connect the speed, the radius and the orbital period in a circular orbit?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Put $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ into the equation $v^2 = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r}$ and solve $r^3 = \tfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $T$$= 95.4 \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
      $= 5724\,\text{s}$

      so

      $r$$= \sqrt[3]{\tfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}}$
      $\approx 6.92 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

      Finally remember to subtract the radius of the Earth.

      Show the solution

      Given

      orbital period $T = 95.4\,\text{min}$, mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6371\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

      Find

      the altitude above the surface $h$ in kilometres

      Principle

      Only the gravitational force of the Earth acts on the telescope, so it is the net force of the circular motion: $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$. In addition, in uniform circular motion $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$. When the speed is substituted, an equation for the radius $r$ remains.

      Formula

      $\dfrac{\gamma M}{r}$$= v^2$
      $= \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}$

      from which

      $r^3$$= \dfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}$
      $r$$= \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}}$

      $h = r - R$

      Conversions

      $T$$= 95.4 \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
      $= 5724\,\text{s}$
      $R$$= 6371\,\text{km}$
      $= 6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

      Substitution

      $\gamma M$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$
      $= 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$
      $\gamma M T^2$$= 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}^2} \cdot (5724\,\text{s})^2$
      $= 1.3054 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{m}^3$
      $r$$= \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{1.3054 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{m}^3}{4\pi^2}}$
      $= \sqrt[3]{3.307 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{m}^3}$
      $= 6.915 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

      so

      $h$$= 6.915 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m} - 6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.544 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$
      $= 544\,\text{km}$

      Answer

      about $544\,\text{km}$, three significant figures as in the orbital period $95.4\,\text{min}$.

      Reasonableness

      The orbital period of Hubble is slightly longer than the $92.9\,\text{min}$ of the ISS, so it must orbit slightly higher than the ISS ($420\,\text{km}$), as the result says. Check: at the radius $6915\,\text{km}$ the orbital speed is

      $v$$= \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$
      $\approx 7.59\,\text{km/s}$

        $= 6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

        finally $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$.

        Substitution

        $\gamma M$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$
        $= 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$

        so

        $T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m})^3}{3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}}$
        $= 5062\,\text{s}$
        $= 5062 : 60\,\text{min}$
        $= 84.36\,\text{min}$

        Answer

        about $84.4\,\text{min}$, three significant figures as in the mass of the Earth.

        Reasonableness

        This is the orbital period of a satellite orbiting at the surface level, and the ISS orbits only a little higher, with a period of $92.8\,\text{min}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude and slightly shorter, as it should be. The real rotation period, about $24\,\text{h}$, is about $17$ times longer, so we are far from weightlessness.

      4. Exercise 6-20

        The table gives the orbital radius $r$ and the orbital period $T$ of four planets of a distant star (example data). The planets orbit the star in nearly circular orbits, and the mass of the star is much greater than the masses of the planets. The mass of the star is not known.

        The circular-orbit model gives $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\gamma M} r^3$, where $M$ is the mass of the star. The orbital period of one planet has been measured incorrectly.

        a) Calculate the ratio $\dfrac{r^3}{T^2}$ for each planet in SI units. Deduce which planet's value is incorrect and justify why it is left out.

        b) Determine the mass of the star from the mean of the ratios of the three other planets. Give the answer in the unit $10^{30}\,\text{kg}$.

        c) Deduce what the orbital period of the incorrect planet should be and suggest what kind of measurement error could explain the deviation.

        The answer field takes the result of part b).

        Orbital radius and orbital period of the planets of the star
        Planet$r\,(10^{9}\,\text{m})$$T\,(\text{d})$
        b6.03.29
        c10.57.57
        d17.07.82
        e27.031.4
        10³⁰ kg
        Answer

        $1.59 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        $r$ and $T$ for planets b, c, d and e from the table, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$; the mass of the star is unknown

        Find

        a) the incorrect orbital period and the justification, b) the mass of the star $M$ in the unit $10^{30}\,\text{kg}$, c) the correct orbital period of the incorrect planet

        Principle

        The only force on a planet is the gravitational force of the star, which is the net force of the circular motion: $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$ and $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$. It follows that the ratio $\tfrac{r^3}{T^2} = \tfrac{\gamma M}{4\pi^2}$ is the same for all planets, and the masses of the planets are not needed. The ratio is used to identify the deviating measurement.

        Formula

        $\dfrac{r^3}{T^2}$$= \dfrac{\gamma M}{4\pi^2}$

        from which

        $M$$= \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\gamma} \cdot \dfrac{r^3}{T^2}$

        Conversions

        $r$ in metres: $6.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}$, $1.05 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{m}$, $1.70 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{m}$ and $2.70 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{m}$. $T$ in seconds: $3.29 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s} = 284\,256\,\text{s}$, $7.57 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s} = 654\,048\,\text{s}$, $7.82 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s} = 675\,648\,\text{s}$ and $31.4 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s} = 2.713 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{s}$.

        Substitution

        a) The ratios $\tfrac{r^3}{T^2}$ are b: $2.67 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$, c: $2.71 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$, d: $1.08 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ and e: $2.67 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$. The ratio of planet d is about four times those of the others. b) The sum of the three other ratios is $8.054 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$, so the mean is $2.685 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ and

        $4\pi^2 \cdot 2.685 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$
        $= 1.0599 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$

        so

        $M$$= \dfrac{1.0599 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2}$
        $= 1.588 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg}$

        c)

        $T_\text{d}$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{r^3}{2.685 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}}$
        $= \sqrt{\dfrac{4.913 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{m}^3}{2.685 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}}$
        $= 1.353 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{s}$
        $= 15.7\,\text{d}$

        Answer

        the mass of the star is $1.59 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg}$, three significant figures, because the orbital periods are given with three digits and the mean evens out small differences (the radius $6.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}$ is given with two digits, but it is rounded from the exact value). Justification: the ratios of three planets differ from each other by less than $2\,\%$, but the ratio of planet d is about four times as great, so its orbital period does not fit the model. The comparison is the justification: when the radius grows by about $60\,\%$ from planet c to planet d, the orbital period grows by only about $3\,\%$, although it should more than double. Leaving it out is justified because the deviation is clear and has a logical explanation; the value must be checked by measuring again, not rejected merely because it deviates. The correct orbital period is about $15.7\,\text{d}$, and the listed $7.82\,\text{d}$ is roughly half of it, because in the ratio $\tfrac{r^3}{T^2}$ the orbital period is squared and a fourfold ratio corresponds to half the orbital period. Explanation: for each revolution two dimmings of brightness have been interpreted as transits (for example also the moment when the planet passes behind the star), or the transits of two different planets have been mixed up, so that the observed interval is half of the real orbital period.

        Reasonableness

        The mass of the star is $1.588 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg} : 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg} \approx 0.80$ solar masses, which is of the order of magnitude of an ordinary star. The corrected orbital period of planet d fits its position in the orbit: it lies between those of planets c ($7.57\,\text{d}$) and e ($31.4\,\text{d}$), just as the orbital radius $17.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}$ lies between the radii $10.5 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}$ and $27.0 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}$.

The radius of the Earth is $6371\,\text{km}$.

        At a certain moment the satellites are on the same straight line with the centre of the Earth and on the same side of the Earth.

        a) Calculate the orbital period of each satellite.

        b) How long does it take before the satellites are again on the same straight line with the centre of the Earth, on the same side, for the first time? Give the answer in hours. Do not round intermediate results.

        c) The masses of the satellites were not given. Justify why they are not needed.

        The answer field takes the result of part b).

        h
        Answer

        $12.9\,\text{h}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        altitudes $h_\text{A} = 400\,\text{km}$ and $h_\text{B} = 1000\,\text{km}$, mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6371\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$; air resistance and the effect of the masses of the satellites are assumed to be negligible

        Find

        a) the orbital periods $T_\text{A}$ and $T_\text{B}$, b) the time $t$ until the satellites are next on the same straight line, in hours, c) why the masses are not needed

        Principle

        The only force on each satellite is the gravitational force of the Earth, which is the net force of the circular motion: $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$ and $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$, which gives the orbital period. The mass $m$ cancels, so the masses are not needed (part c). The lower satellite A moves faster and has made one revolution more than B when the satellites are on the same straight line on the same side again for the first time.

        Formula

        a) $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{r^3}{\gamma M}}$, where $r = R + h$. b) In the time $t$ satellite A makes $\tfrac{t}{T_\text{A}}$ and B $\tfrac{t}{T_\text{B}}$ revolutions, and A has made one revolution more:

        $\dfrac{t}{T_\text{A}} - \dfrac{t}{T_\text{B}}$$= 1$

        from which

        $t$$= \dfrac{T_\text{A} T_\text{B}}{T_\text{B} - T_\text{A}}$

        Conversions

        $r_\text{A}$$= 6371\,\text{km} + 400\,\text{km}$
        $= 6771\,\text{km}$
        $= 6.771 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$
        $r_\text{B}$$= 6371\,\text{km} + 1000\,\text{km}$
        $= 7371\,\text{km}$
        $= 7.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

        finally $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

        $\gamma M = 3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$. a)

        $= 5546.0\,\text{s}$
        $= 92.4\,\text{min}$
        $T_\text{B}$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(7.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m})^3}{3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}}$

        In the time $46\,379\,\text{s}$ satellite A makes $46\,379 : 5546 \approx 8.36$ and B $46\,379 : 6299 \approx 7.36$ revolutions, so the difference is one revolution. The orbital periods are close to the $92.8\,\text{min}$ of the ISS (A) and slightly longer higher up (B), as they should be.

      2. Concept task: Orbital speed and distance

      Satellite A orbits the Earth in a circular orbit of radius $r$. Satellite B orbits in a circular orbit of radius $4r$.How does the orbital speed of B compare with the orbital speed of A?

      • Correct. $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$, so when the radius becomes four times as large, the speed changes by the factor $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{4}} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

      • A quarter would be the result if the speed were inversely proportional to the radius. The orbital speed is, however, $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$, so quadrupling the radius reduces the speed by the factor $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{4}} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

      • A longer orbit does not require a higher speed. Far away gravity is weaker, and a weaker force can turn only a more slowly moving body into a circular orbit: $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$ decreases as the radius increases.

      • The mass of the Earth is the same, but the orbital speed also depends on the radius: $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$. At four times the radius the speed is half.

      Model diagram

      Only one force acts on the satellite, the gravitational force of the Earth $\overline{F}$, which starts from the centre of the satellite towards the centre of the Earth, here straight down.
