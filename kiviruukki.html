I am a STEAM teacher, teacher trainer and educational content author based in Espoo, Finland. At Kiviruukin lukio I teach physics and mathematics and work in Ahjo, our makerspace, where science, technology, design and entrepreneurship meet hands-on making. I also teach dynamic geometry courses at the University of Helsinki and serve as an assistant member of the Matriculation Examination Board.

My work focuses on STEAM education, formative assessment, flipped learning and meaningful uses of technology and AI in teaching. I am especially interested in designing learning environments where students can experiment, receive feedback within their zone of proximal development, and gradually take more responsibility for their own learning.

Kiviruukin lukio is the new name and new home of Espoo Yhteislyseo, which moves into the Cleantech Garden building in Kivenlahti in August 2027. I also coordinate the school’s international work — Erasmus+, Nordplus and other projects — because together we are more than the sum of our parts.

02 — Ahjo Makerspace
Kiviruukki’s makerspace

Space to turn ideas into reality.

An ahjo is the forge hearth at the heart of an ironworks. Kiviruukki takes its name from the old ruukki, where water power and fire turned iron into tools — and a tight-knit village grew around the work. Today we forge knowledge instead of iron.

Ahjo is our makerspace and a cross-disciplinary STEAM learning environment. It brings together science, mathematics, programming, design, entrepreneurship and hands-on making. My job is to help students plan, build, test and improve their own projects, and to connect what they make with the physics and mathematics behind it.

Design. Experiment. Build. Develop.

  • 3D printing From a sketch to a printed prototype
  • Robotics Sensors, motors and code working together
  • Electronics Circuits, microcontrollers and measurement
  • Laser cutting Precise parts from wood and acrylic
  • Vinyl cutting Stickers, stencils and textile prints
  • Programming & data Apps, measurements and automation

How I teach in Ahjo

  1. 01

    Start from a real challenge

    Projects begin with a question worth solving. Collaboration with partners such as VTT Bioruukki brings real-world projects and competitions within students’ reach.

  2. 02

    Make, test, improve

    Students prototype early, measure what happens and iterate. Short check-ins and timely feedback keep the next step within their zone of proximal development.

  3. 03

    Let every spark grow into a flame

    Clear structure, self-assessment and a supportive community let students take more ownership over time — from a first experiment to a diploma project.

04 — From the classroom
Case study · Introductory physics · Autumn 2026

Flipped Learning and Self-Assessment

In my introductory physics course, I used a flipped and flexible learning approach that combined independent work, weekly deadlines, collaborative learning, and regular feedback. Students could plan their work within each week, while I monitored their progress and provided short individual feedback to help them reflect on their learning.

Student feedback was very positive: 28 respondents gave the course an overall average of 4.33/5. In particular, students valued the learning materials, the supportive classroom atmosphere, flexible pacing, and assessment that was not centred solely on the final exam. Many students said that weekly progress checks helped them study more regularly and understand how their learning was developing.

Randomly changing table groups also supported participation and community building. Some students reported that working with different classmates helped them become more active, confident, and socially connected.

The experience strengthened my view that learner autonomy works best when it is supported by clear structure, regular feedback, and opportunities for self-assessment and reflection.

4.33 / 5 Average across 12 feedback statements (scale 1–5) · 28 students
Students who agreed
  • The teacher’s materials supported my learning 96%
  • It was nice to come to lessons 96%
  • I liked that assessment did not centre on the exam 93%
  • Working at my own pace within the week suits me 86%
  • Group work supported my learning 82%
Course design
  • Independent work with weekly deadlines
  • Flexible pacing within each week
  • Weekly progress checks and short individual feedback
  • Self-assessment and reflection
  • Randomly changing table groups

Case study · Force and motion (FY04) · Autumn 2026

Knowledge Checks and Weekly Feedback

In my force and motion course, I kept the same flipped and flexible structure of weekly deadlines, independent work, group tasks, and short individual feedback, and added regular knowledge checks. Their purpose was to help students see what they had already mastered and what they still needed to practise, while I followed their progress each week.

Student feedback was positive, though more mixed than in the introductory course: 26 respondents gave the course an overall average of 3.98/5. Students especially enjoyed coming to lessons and felt that the teaching and guidance met their needs. Most found the number and rhythm of the knowledge checks appropriate, and many wrote that the weekly follow-up helped them study regularly, revise, and see what they still needed to work on.

The feedback also pointed to clear areas for development. Some students found the tasks within a chapter too similar, which lowered their motivation, and some wished for more time to practise at the end of lessons. These are concrete things I will change in the next implementation.

The course showed me that regular self-assessment helps students see their own progress, but it works best when the tasks around it are varied and there is time to act on what the checks reveal.

3.98 / 5 Average across 14 feedback statements (scale 1–5) · 26 students
Students who agreed
  • It was nice to come to lessons 88%
  • I received teaching and guidance that met my needs 85%
  • The number and rhythm of knowledge checks were right 81%
  • I understood better how mathematics describes physical situations 77%
  • Knowledge checks helped me see what I know and what to practise 69%
Next time
  • More varied tasks within each chapter
  • More time for practice at the end of lessons
  • Group-task instructions in Wilma for absent students
