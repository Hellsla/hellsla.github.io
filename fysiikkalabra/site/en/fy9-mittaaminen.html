Measurement, uncertainty and graphs

Why is a result obtained from a straight line fitted to a graph usually more reliable than one calculated from a single measurement?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • draw a graph of a data set and choose a suitable fit for it.
  • interpret the slope and the constant term with their units and their physical meaning.
  • estimate the uncertainty of a result and state the result to a justified precision.

Prior knowledge

  • A result is stated in the form $\bar{x} \pm \Delta x$, and the uncertainty is rounded to one significant figurechapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement
  • A straight line is fitted to the measurement points, and its slope is read from two points of the line far apartchapter Graphing and fitting a straight line
  • The mass of a substance is directly proportional to its volume, and the slope of the $(V, m)$ line is the densitychapter Density

1Think about it

An exam problem has a table of measurements. Liquid was poured into a measuring cylinder $20\,\text{ml}$ at a time, and after each addition the cylinder and the liquid were weighed together on a balance. Aino calculates the ratio of mass to volume on every row and takes the mean of the ratios. Eetu draws a $(V, m)$ graph, fits a straight line to the points and reads its slope. They get very different results, although the data are the same.

Whose method gives the density of the liquid, and what does the other method ignore?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting point 1: precision

The mass of a stone is $32.46\,\text{g}$. When the stone is lowered into a measuring cylinder, the reading rises from $52\,\text{ml}$ to $64\,\text{ml}$.How is the density of the stone stated to a justified precision?

  • Correct. The volume is the difference of the readings, $12\,\text{ml} = 12\,\text{cm}^3$, which has two significant figures. The result of a division gets as many significant figures as the least precise starting value, so $32.46\,\text{g} : 12\,\text{cm}^3 = 2.705\,\text{g/cm}^3 \approx 2.7\,\text{g/cm}^3$. The precision of a difference is discussed in the chapter Density.

  • One figure is too few: it shifts the result by more than $10\,\%$. The volume $12\,\text{ml}$ has two significant figures, so the density is stated with two: $2.7\,\text{g/cm}^3$. Review significant figures in the chapter Quantities, units and the SI system.

  • Not all the digits of the calculator are reliable. The volume is the difference of the readings, $64\,\text{ml} - 52\,\text{ml} = 12\,\text{ml}$, which has only two significant figures, so the density has two as well: $2.7\,\text{g/cm}^3$. Review significant figures in the chapter Quantities, units and the SI system.

  • Three figures would be right if the volume were known to three figures. The readings $52\,\text{ml}$ and $64\,\text{ml}$ have two figures, and their difference $12\,\text{ml}$ also has only two. Review the precision of a difference in the chapter Density.

Starting point 2: repetition

A kitchen scale shows $0.8\,\text{g}$ when empty, but it is not zeroed. A student weighs the same object ten times and calculates the mean of the results.What can be said about the mean?

  • The resolution only tells how small a change shows on the display. It does not tell whether the zero is right: a scale can show tenths and still be $0.8\,\text{g}$ off. The difference between resolution and uncertainty is discussed in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Only random errors, which are sometimes positive and sometimes negative, cancel each other. The zero error adds the same $0.8\,\text{g}$ to every weighing, so it is in the mean too. Review the types of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • A gross error is a single mistake that shows as a result different from the others. The zero error is the same in every weighing, so it is systematic, and it can be corrected: the result does not have to be discarded. Review the types of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. The zero error is systematic: it shifts every reading in the same direction, so repetition and the mean do not remove it. The error is corrected by subtracting $0.8\,\text{g}$ or by zeroing the scale. Systematic error is discussed in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Starting point 3: the slope

A student has fitted a straight line to eight measurement points. How should the student determine the slope of the line?

  • The points are far apart, but they are measurement points with errors of their own: the result does not use the other six points at all. The points are read from the fitted line. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. The line is fitted to all the points, so its slope uses the whole data set. Points far apart make the differences large, so a reading error matters little. Reading the slope is discussed in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The ratio $y : x$ is the slope only if the line passes through the origin. If the constant term is not zero, the ratios are wrong and change from point to point. Review the meaning of the constant term in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The differences between adjacent points are small, so the random error of either point changes the slope a lot. The slope is read from the fitted line at two points far apart. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

Starting point 4: the constant term

The length $h$ of a burning candle was measured every half hour. The equation of the line fitted to the points is $h = -0.80\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{h}} \cdot t + 18.0\,\text{cm}$, when the time $t$ is counted from lighting the candle.What does the constant term $18.0\,\text{cm}$ tell?

  • Direct proportionality is described by a line through the origin, whose constant term is zero. Here the constant term is $18.0\,\text{cm}$, and the length decreases as time passes. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The shortening per hour is the slope $0.80\,\text{cm/h}$, not the constant term. The constant term is the value of the line when $t = 0$: the length of the candle when it was lit. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. When $t = 0$, only the constant term is left of the equation of the line, so $18.0\,\text{cm}$ is the length of the candle when it was lit. The slope $-0.80\,\text{cm/h}$ tells how much the candle shortens in an hour. This is discussed in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Here the constant term has a physical meaning: it is the value of the line when $t = 0$. Forcing the line through the origin would claim that the candle had zero length when it was lit, and it would also change the slope. Review the meaning of the constant term in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

Starting point 5: linearisation

A trolley starts from rest and accelerates uniformly. Light gates measure the distance $s$ travelled by the trolley and the time $t$ from the start.The points of which graph lie on a straight line?

  • Correct. The distance is proportional to the square of the time, so the $(t^2, s)$ graph is a straight line through the origin with slope $\tfrac{a}{2}$. The equations of motion are reviewed in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • The quantity $\tfrac{1}{t}$ straightens the graph when $y$ is inversely proportional to $x$, as in Boyle's law. In uniformly accelerated motion $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, so the right horizontal axis is $t^2$. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • The distance grows, but faster and faster, because the speed grows. The $(t, s)$ graph curves upwards. Since $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, the straight line is given by the $(t^2, s)$ graph. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion and in the ICT guide Fitting a straight line to measurement points.

  • A square root would straighten a graph that curves downwards. In uniformly accelerated motion the $(t, s)$ graph curves upwards, and $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, so the right horizontal axis is $t^2$. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

Starting point 6: outside the measured range

The extension of a spring was measured with forces $0$–$10\,\text{N}$, and the points lie on a straight line through the origin.What can be said about the extension when the force is $40\,\text{N}$?

  • Nothing in the measurements says that the slope decreases. On the contrary, an overloaded spring can stretch more, and permanently. Outside the measured range the result is only an estimate. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. Extrapolation assumes that the relationship stays the same outside the measured range. The estimate is justified only if we know that the spring can take $40\,\text{N}$ without stretching permanently. This is discussed in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The measurements show that the line is valid only in the range $0$–$10\,\text{N}$. A large force can stretch the spring permanently, and then the relationship is no longer a straight line. Extrapolation is discussed in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • An estimate can be made by extending the line, as long as you remember that it is only an estimate. The reliability of the extrapolation has to be justified, for example by knowing where the spring begins to stretch permanently. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

3Investigate

Pressure and temperature of a gas

Made-up example data

The air in a closed, rigid container was heated in a water bath, and the pressure was measured with a sensor at five temperatures. The graph shows the measurement points without a fitted line. Fit a straight line to the points in your notebook or in a graphing program. Read its slope and constant term with their units, and think about what each tells about the gas in the container.

Extend the fitted line outside the measured range.

At what temperature does the line cross the horizontal axis, that is, at what temperature would the pressure be zero according to the model? Answer in degrees Celsius.

°C
Hint 1: a guiding question

Read two points of the line far apart, for example at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The equation of the line is $p = k\theta + b$. The pressure is zero when $k\theta + b = 0$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$\theta_0 = -\dfrac{b}{k}$, where $b \approx 75\,\text{kPa}$ and $k = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta \theta}$ in the unit $\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}$.

Show the solution

Given

the five measurement points of the graph at temperatures $0$–$100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; the container is closed and rigid, so the amount and the volume of the gas stay the same

Find

the temperature $\theta_0$ at which the pressure is zero according to the line, in ${}^\circ\text{C}$

Principle

The points lie approximately on a straight line, so the pressure depends linearly on the temperature: $p = k\theta + b$. The slope tells by how many kilopascals the pressure grows per degree Celsius, and the constant term is the pressure at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The temperature asked for is found by extending the line, that is, by extrapolating far outside the measured range.

Reading the graph

The line fitted among the points passes through the points $(0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $75.0\,\text{kPa})$ and $(100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $102.6\,\text{kPa})$, so the constant term is $b = 75.0\,\text{kPa}$.

Formula

$k = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta\theta}$, and the pressure is zero when $k\theta_0 + b = 0$, so $\theta_0 = -\dfrac{b}{k}$

Conversions

Not needed: the temperatures are in degrees Celsius and the pressures in kilopascals, and $\text{kPa}$ cancels.

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{102.6\,\text{kPa} - 75.0\,\text{kPa}}{100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= 0.276\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$\theta_0$$= -\dfrac{75.0\,\text{kPa}}{0.276\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= -271.7\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Answer

$\theta_0 \approx -270\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Two significant figures, because the pressures of the points are read from the graph to about two figures and the line is extended almost three times the length of the measured range. The slope $0.28\,\text{kPa}/{}^\circ\text{C}$ tells how fast the pressure grows on heating, and the constant term $75\,\text{kPa}$ is the pressure at the freezing point.

Reasonableness

The result is close to absolute zero, $-273.15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (chapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature). The extrapolation is, however, only a prediction of the model: a real gas condenses into a liquid long before its pressure would fall to zero, so in practice the pressure does not follow the line that far. A small change in the slope of the line moves the crossing point by tens of degrees, which shows in the precision of the answer.

4Theory

The big picture

Almost every matriculation exam in physics has a problem that gives a set of measurement data and asks for a quantity to be determined from it. The solution always proceeds in the same order: the measurement results and their uncertainty, the graph, the fit, the interpretation of the fit and finally the result with its uncertainty. Points can be gained or lost at every step.

The measurement result and the equation of a line

The result and its uncertainty

The best estimate of a repeated measurement is the mean $\bar{x}$. In this course the spread is estimated as half the range, and the result is stated in the form $\bar{x} \pm \Delta x$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta x &= \frac{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}}{2} \\ \varepsilon &= \frac{\Delta x}{\bar{x}} \cdot 100\,\% \end{aligned}$$

where $x_\text{max}$ and $x_\text{min}$ are the largest and the smallest result, $\Delta x$ the measurement uncertainty and $\varepsilon$ the relative uncertainty. In a single measurement, the uncertainty of a reading can in a simple situation be estimated as half the smallest scale division, but the method and other sources of error also affect the total uncertainty. The uncertainty is rounded to one significant figure (two, if the first digit is 1), and the result to the same decimal place. The result of a multiplication or a division gets as many significant figures as the least precise starting value.

Repetition reduces the effect of random error in the mean, but it does not remove systematic error. A gross error is looked for: a deviating result is checked, and it is not removed without a reason.

The equation of a line and its interpretation

If the points lie approximately on a straight line, a line is fitted among the points:

$$y = kx + b, \qquad k = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$$

where $k$ is the slope and $b$ the constant term, that is, the value of $y$ when $x = 0$. The slope is read from two points of the line far apart, not from the measurement points. Its unit is the unit of the vertical axis divided by the unit of the horizontal axis, and it usually has a physical meaning: in a $(V, m)$ graph it is the density. The model has been shown to be valid only in the measured range, so extrapolation outside it must be justified.

The uncertainty of the slope can be estimated with the steepest and the shallowest line that still pass through the uncertainty bars:

$$\Delta k = \frac{k_\text{max} - k_\text{min}}{2}$$

They bound an estimated range, not certain limits.

Linearisation

When the model is not a straight line, the axes are given quantities between which the relationship is a straight line. The model is written in the form $y = kx + b$:

$$\begin{aligned} s &= \tfrac{a}{2} \cdot t^2 \\ p &= C \cdot \tfrac{1}{V} \\ m &= \rho V + m_0 \end{aligned}$$

In uniformly accelerated motion from rest, when time is counted from the start of the motion, the $(t^2, s)$ graph is a straight line through the origin with slope $\tfrac{a}{2}$. When the amount and the temperature of a gas stay the same, according to Boyle's law the $(\tfrac{1}{V}, p)$ graph is a straight line; the constant $C$ is the product of the pressure and the volume. The total mass $m$ of a liquid weighed in a container is a straight line with respect to the volume $V$, whose slope is the density $\rho$ and whose constant term is the mass $m_0$ of the container.

Common mistakes

  • The points are joined with a broken line, or the line is forced through the origin without a physical reason.
  • The slope is calculated from two adjacent measurement points, or it is given without a unit.
  • An axis lacks its quantity or unit, or the scale is not evenly spaced.
  • The result keeps all the digits of the calculator, or the uncertainty and the result are rounded to different precisions.
  • Repetitions are believed to remove a systematic error.
  • A straight line is fitted to curved data, although the deviations form an arc.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the error: the form of the result

A student determined the acceleration of free fall from a graph and wrote the answer $g = (9.7634 \pm 0.38)\,\text{m/s}^2$. What is the correctly rounded form of the result?

  • The first digit of the uncertainty is $3$, so it is rounded to one significant figure: $0.4\,\text{m/s}^2$. The second digit $8$ is not reliable when already the first is uncertain. Review rounding in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • The uncertainty is right, but the result is rounded to a different precision. If already the tenths are uncertain, the hundredths and thousandths have no meaning: the result is rounded to the same decimal place, $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. Review in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. The uncertainty is rounded to one significant figure, $0.4\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the result to the same decimal place, $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The rounding rule is in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • The result is rounded too much: the tenths are included in the uncertainty, so they also belong in the result. The right form is $(9.8 \pm 0.4)\,\text{m/s}^2$. Review in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Choose the straight-line graph

A toy car drives along a $2.0\,\text{m}$ track at a constant speed. The track is driven many times at different speeds $v$, and each time the time $t$ taken to drive the track is measured.The points of which graph should lie on a straight line through the origin?

  • A square would straighten the graph if the time were proportional to the square of the speed. But the time gets shorter as the speed grows: $t = \tfrac{s}{v}$. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. The distance is constant, so $t = s \cdot \tfrac{1}{v}$, and the $(\tfrac{1}{v}, t)$ graph is a straight line through the origin whose slope is the length of the track $s$. Linearisation is discussed in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • A square root does not remove the inverse proportionality: the time still gets shorter as $\sqrt{v}$ grows. Since $t = \tfrac{s}{v}$, the horizontal axis gets $\tfrac{1}{v}$. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • When the speed grows, the time gets shorter, so the $(v, t)$ graph is a falling curve, not a straight line. The distance is the same every time, so $t = \tfrac{s}{v}$, that is, $t$ is proportional to the quantity $\tfrac{1}{v}$. Review linearisation in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

Evaluate the conclusion

According to the model, the extension $x$ of a spring is directly proportional to the force $F$. The constant term of the line fitted to the measurement points is, however, $0.4\,\text{cm}$, and the uncertainty of the extensions is $\pm 0.1\,\text{cm}$.Which conclusion is best justified?

  • A forced line would miss the points and change the slope. The data say that something has happened, and the reason is found out before the line is forced through the origin. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. The constant term $0.4\,\text{cm}$ is four times the uncertainty of the extensions, so random error does not explain it. The likely cause is systematic, for example the extensions were measured from the wrong zero point. The slope can still be right. Review in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The points are on a straight line, so the relationship is linear: only the position of the line has shifted. The shift points to a systematic error, such as the zero point, not to the model. Review the types of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Small and insignificant are not the same thing. The constant term is compared with the uncertainty, not with zero: $0.4\,\text{cm}$ is four times the uncertainty $0.1\,\text{cm}$. Review in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Identify the type of error

A student reads the scale of a measuring cylinder standing on the table every time while standing, from obliquely above, and not with the eyes at the level of the liquid surface.What kind of error appears in the results?

  • A random error is sometimes positive and sometimes negative. A reading taken from obliquely above is wrong in the same direction every time, so the mean does not correct it. Review the types of error in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. When the scale is always read from the same wrong angle, all the readings shift in the same direction. Repetition does not remove the error; it is corrected by reading with the eyes at the level of the liquid surface. Systematic error is discussed in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • The same way of reading is exactly what makes the error systematic: it repeats in the same way every time. The results can be close together but in the wrong place. Review repeatability and accuracy in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • A gross error is a single mistake. Here the same way of reading is repeated every time, so no reading differs from the others: they are all wrong in the same direction. Review in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

A stack of coins

With a ruler, a student measures the height of a stack of ten identical coins as $18.5\,\text{mm}$. The smallest scale division of the ruler is $1\,\text{mm}$, so the uncertainty of the height of the stack is estimated as $0.5\,\text{mm}$.

How large is the relative uncertainty of the thickness of one coin? Answer in percent.

%
Hint 1: a guiding question

How thick is one coin, and how large is the uncertainty of its thickness, when there are ten coins in the stack?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Both the thickness and its uncertainty are divided by ten: $d = 1.85\,\text{mm}$ and $\Delta d = 0.05\,\text{mm}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta d}{d} \cdot 100\,\%$
$= \dfrac{0.05\,\text{mm}}{1.85\,\text{mm}} \cdot 100\,\%$
Show the solution

Given

height of the stack $h = 18.5\,\text{mm}$, its uncertainty $\Delta h = 0.5\,\text{mm}$, number of coins $N = 10$

Find

the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the thickness of one coin in percent

Principle

The coins are identical, so the thickness of one coin is the height of the stack divided by ten. The height of the stack is read once, so the uncertainty of the whole reading is shared by ten coins. The relative uncertainty compares the uncertainty with the result itself.

Formula

$d = \dfrac{h}{N}$ and $\Delta d = \dfrac{\Delta h}{N}$, so

$\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta d}{d} \cdot 100\,\%$
$= \dfrac{\Delta h}{h} \cdot 100\,\%$

Conversions

Not needed: the millimetres cancel in the ratio.

Substitution

$d$$= 18.5\,\text{mm} : 10$
$= 1.85\,\text{mm}$
$\Delta d$$= 0.5\,\text{mm} : 10$
$= 0.05\,\text{mm}$
$\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.05\,\text{mm}}{1.85\,\text{mm}} \cdot 100\,\%$
$= 2.70\,\%$

Answer

$\varepsilon \approx 3\,\%$

($2.7\,\%$). The uncertainty $0.5\,\text{mm}$ is a one-significant-figure estimate, so one figure is enough; two figures are fine for an intermediate result. The thickness of a coin is $(1.85 \pm 0.05)\,\text{mm}$.

Reasonableness

If one coin were measured with the same ruler, the uncertainty would be $0.5\,\text{mm} : 1.85\,\text{mm} \approx 27\,\%$, ten times as large. Measuring a stack reduces the uncertainty for one coin, because the uncertainty of one reading is shared by ten coins.

The line of a spring

Different weights were hung from a spring, and with each load the total length $L$ of the spring was measured. The $(L, F)$ line fitted to the points passes through the points $(15.0\,\text{cm}$; $1.2\,\text{N})$ and $(35.0\,\text{cm}$; $17.0\,\text{N})$, where $F$ is the force stretching the spring.

What is the spring constant of the spring? Answer in the unit $\text{N/m}$.

N/m
Hint 1: a guiding question

The extension of the spring is $L - L_0$, where $L_0$ is the length of the spring without a load. Which quantity of the line is the spring constant: the slope or the constant term?

Hint 2: what is the connection

$F = k(L - L_0) = kL - kL_0$, so the spring constant is the slope $k = \dfrac{\Delta F}{\Delta L}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$k$$= \dfrac{17.0\,\text{N} - 1.2\,\text{N}}{0.350\,\text{m} - 0.150\,\text{m}}$
Show the solution

Given

points of the fitted line $(L_1$; $F_1) = (15.0\,\text{cm}$; $1.2\,\text{N})$ and $(L_2$; $F_2) = (35.0\,\text{cm}$; $17.0\,\text{N})$; $L$ is the total length of the spring

Find

the spring constant $k$ in the unit $\text{N/m}$

Principle

The extension of the spring is $L - L_0$, where $L_0$ is the length of the spring without a load, and the stretching force is proportional to the extension. Then $F = kL - kL_0$ is a straight line whose slope is the spring constant and whose constant term is $-kL_0$. The ratio $F : L$ is not the spring constant, because $L$ is not the extension.

Formula

$k$$= \dfrac{\Delta F}{\Delta L}$
$= \dfrac{F_2 - F_1}{L_2 - L_1}$

Conversions

$L_1$$= 15.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 15.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
$= 0.150\,\text{m}$
$L_2$$= 35.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
$= 0.350\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{17.0\,\text{N} - 1.2\,\text{N}}{0.350\,\text{m} - 0.150\,\text{m}}$
$= \dfrac{15.8\,\text{N}}{0.200\,\text{m}}$
$= 79.0\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$

Answer

$k \approx 79.0\,\text{N/m}$

Three significant figures as in the differences $15.8\,\text{N}$ and $0.200\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

The length of the spring without a load is

$L_0$$= L_1 - \tfrac{F_1}{k}$
$= 0.150\,\text{m} - \tfrac{1.2\,\text{N}}{79.0\,\text{N/m}}$
$\approx 0.135\,\text{m}$

which is shorter than any measured length, as it should be. A spring constant of $79\,\text{N/m}$ is of the usual order of magnitude for a school extension spring.

A ball in a channel

A ball rolls from rest down a sloping channel, and the time is measured from the moment of release. A student drew a $(t^2, s)$ graph and fitted to the points a straight line through the origin, which also passes through the point $(4.0\,\text{s}^2$; $2.48\,\text{m})$.

How large is the acceleration of the ball?

m/s²
Hint 1: a guiding question

What is the slope of the line, and what is its unit?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The equation of motion is $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$. When the horizontal axis has $t^2$, the slope is $k = \tfrac{a}{2}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$a$$= 2k$
$= 2 \cdot \dfrac{2.48\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s}^2}$
Show the solution

Given

a $(t^2, s)$ line through the origin, which passes through the point $(t^2$; $s) = (4.0\,\text{s}^2$; $2.48\,\text{m})$; the ball starts from rest

Find

the acceleration $a$ of the ball in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$

Principle

The $(t^2, s)$ points are on a straight line, so the acceleration is constant. In uniformly accelerated motion from rest, $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ when the time is counted from the start. The equation has the form $y = kx$, where $y = s$, $x = t^2$ and the slope $k = \tfrac{a}{2}$.

Formula

$k = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta (t^2)}$ and $k = \dfrac{a}{2}$, so $a = 2k$

Conversions

Not needed: $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$ is already the unit of acceleration.

Substitution

The line passes through the origin and the given point:

$k$$= \dfrac{2.48\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s}^2 - 0\,\text{s}^2}$
$= 0.620\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$a$$= 2 \cdot 0.620\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$= 1.24\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$

Answer

$a \approx 1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$

Two significant figures as in the number $4.0\,\text{s}^2$.

Reasonableness

3. Explanation task

Go back to the problem at the start of the lesson. Explain why the density of the liquid should be determined from the slope of the $(V, m)$ graph and not by calculating the ratio $m : V$ on every row, when the balance weighs the measuring cylinder and the liquid together.

Model answer

The balance weighs the measuring cylinder and the liquid together: $m = \rho V + m_0$, where $m_0$ is the mass of the measuring cylinder. The ratio $m : V = \rho + \tfrac{m_0}{V}$ is therefore always larger than the density, and it decreases as liquid is added, so Aino's mean is wrong. When liquid is added, the mass of the measuring cylinder stays the same, so the change in mass comes only from the liquid: the slope $\tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta V}$ is the density of the liquid, and $m_0$ cancels out. The constant term of the line gives the mass of the empty measuring cylinder. In addition, the line is fitted to all the points, so the random error of one point affects the result less than in a value calculated from one row. So Eetu's method gives the density.

    Exercise 1.2-1 Hand timing on sports day 14 points

    Made-up example data · Invented example data: the numbers are calculated from a model, and small random variation has been added to them, as in a measurement.

    On the school sports day there is a $100\,\text{m}$ race. The distance has been measured with a measuring wheel, and its uncertainty is estimated as $0.3\,\text{m}$. Three timekeepers stand at the finish. Each starts a stopwatch on seeing the starter's flag drop and stops it when the runner crosses the finish line. The watches show hundredths of a second.

    Runner $t_1\ (\text{s})$ $t_2\ (\text{s})$ $t_3\ (\text{s})$
    Liisa $13.52$ $13.90$ $13.71$
    Mira $13.80$ $14.12$ $13.96$
    1. a) Calculate Liisa's average speed in the units $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$. Justify the precision of the result with the spread of the watch readings. 4 p
    2. b) Estimate the uncertainty of Liisa's average speed from the uncertainties of the time and the distance, and state the speed with its uncertainty. 4 p
    3. c) Mira ran in the same heat. Can these measurements show that Liisa was faster? Justify. 3 p

    Skills from the chapters: Quantities, units and the SI system, Measuring and the reliability of a measurement, Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs

    Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    distance $s = 100.0\,\text{m}$; Liisa's times $13.52\,\text{s}$, $13.90\,\text{s}$ and $13.71\,\text{s}$

    Find

    Liisa's average speed $v$ in the units $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$

    Principle

    The average speed is the distance travelled divided by the time taken. The three watches measure the same run, so the best estimate of the time is their mean: in it the random errors partly cancel each other.

    Formula

    $\bar{t} = \dfrac{t_1 + t_2 + t_3}{3}$ and $v = \dfrac{s}{\bar{t}}$

    Conversions

    $1\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$$= \dfrac{0.001\,\text{km}}{\tfrac{1}{3600}\,\text{h}}$
    $= 3.6\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$

    Substitution

    $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{13.52\,\text{s} + 13.90\,\text{s} + 13.71\,\text{s}}{3}$
    $= 13.71\,\text{s}$
    $v$$= \dfrac{100.0\,\text{m}}{13.71\,\text{s}}$
    $= 7.294\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
    $= 7.294 \cdot 3.6\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$
    $= 26.26\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$

    Answer

    $v \approx 7.29\,\text{m/s} \approx 26.3\,\text{km/h}$

    Three significant figures: the watch readings differ from each other by tenths of a second, that is, by about one percent, so in the speed too only the hundredths of a metre per second are uncertain. (The uncertainty $0.12\,\text{m/s}$ of part b confirms this.)

    Reasonableness

    About $26\,\text{km/h}$ is a brisk sprinting speed for an upper secondary student. It is clearly below the roughly $36\,\text{km/h}$ of a top sprinter and clearly above the roughly $10\,\text{km/h}$ of jogging.

    b)

    Given

    $s = (100.0 \pm 0.3)\,\text{m}$; times $13.52\,\text{s}$, $13.90\,\text{s}$ and $13.71\,\text{s}$; from part a $\bar{t} = 13.71\,\text{s}$

    Find

    the uncertainty $\Delta v$ of the average speed and the speed with its uncertainty

    Principle

    The uncertainty of the time is estimated as half the range of the results of the three watches. The speed is largest when the distance is the longest and the time the shortest possible, and smallest the other way round. Half of the interval obtained in this way is taken as the uncertainty.

    Formula

    $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{t_\text{max} - t_\text{min}}{2}$
    $v_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{s + \Delta s}{\bar{t} - \Delta t}$
    $v_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{s - \Delta s}{\bar{t} + \Delta t}$
    $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{v_\text{max} - v_\text{min}}{2}$

    Conversions

    Not needed: the distance is in metres and the times in seconds.

    Substitution

    $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{13.90\,\text{s} - 13.52\,\text{s}}{2}$
    $= 0.19\,\text{s}$
    $v_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{100.3\,\text{m}}{13.52\,\text{s}}$
    $= 7.419\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
    $v_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{99.7\,\text{m}}{13.90\,\text{s}}$
    $= 7.173\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
    $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{7.419\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 7.173\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}{2}$
    $= 0.123\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

    Answer

    $v = (7.29 \pm 0.12)\,\text{m/s}$

    The first digit of the uncertainty is $1$, so it keeps two significant figures, and the speed is rounded to the same decimal place.

    Reasonableness

    The relative uncertainty of the time, $0.19 : 13.71 \approx 1.4\,\%$, is much larger than that of the distance, $0.3 : 100 = 0.3\,\%$, so the uncertainty of the speed comes almost entirely from the hand timing. About $0.2\,\text{s}$ is a typical uncertainty of a time taken by hand.

    c)

    Find

    whether the measurements can show that Liisa was faster than Mira

    Principle

    Two results are shown to differ only if their uncertainty intervals do not overlap. Overlapping intervals do not prove that the results are equal.

    Model answer

    Mira's mean is $\bar{t} = (13.80 + 14.12 + 13.96)\,\text{s} : 3 = 13.96\,\text{s}$ and the uncertainty $(14.12 - 13.80)\,\text{s} : 2 = 0.16\,\text{s}$, so Mira's time is $(13.96 \pm 0.16)\,\text{s}$ and Liisa's $(13.71 \pm 0.19)\,\text{s}$. Liisa's interval is $13.52$–$13.90\,\text{s}$ and Mira's $13.80$–$14.12\,\text{s}$, so the intervals overlap. The difference $0.25\,\text{s}$ is of the same order of magnitude as the uncertainties. So these measurements cannot show that Liisa was faster, although her mean is smaller. This does not, however, mean that the runners are equally fast: showing a difference would require more precise timing, for example a photo-finish camera.

    d)

    Find

    the systematic error of the timing, its effect on the speed and why the mean does not remove it

    Principle

    A systematic error shifts every result in the same direction. It cannot be seen from the spread of the results, and repetition or the mean does not remove it.

    Model answer

    The timekeeper starts the watch only after seeing the flag drop and reacting to it. The watch therefore starts one reaction time late, typically a few tenths of a second. The finish, on the other hand, the timekeeper sees coming, so the watch is stopped almost on time. The measured time is thus shorter than the true time, and the speed in part a is larger than the true speed. The same delay happens with all three timekeepers, so all the watches show too short a time and the mean is too small as well. The mean only reduces the random variation between the watches. The error is removed, for example, by electronic timing that starts itself from the start signal.

    Also accepted

    • In part b, the relative uncertainty of the speed as the sum of the relative uncertainties of the time and the distance: $\tfrac{0.19}{13.71} + \tfrac{0.3}{100.0} \approx 0.0169$, so $\Delta v \approx 0.0169 \cdot 7.294\,\text{m/s} \approx 0.12\,\text{m/s}$.
    • In part b, the typical $0.2\,\text{s}$ of hand timing as the uncertainty of the time, when the choice is justified. Then $\Delta v \approx 0.1\,\text{m/s}$ and $v = (7.3 \pm 0.1)\,\text{m/s}$ are also accepted.
    • In part d, a mention that a timekeeper $100\,\text{m}$ away who reacts to the sound of a starting pistol would start the watch even later, because sound travels the distance in about $0.3\,\text{s}$.

    Common mistakes

    • One watch or the fastest time is used as the time instead of the mean.
    • The speed is converted by multiplying or dividing the wrong way, for example $7.29 : 3.6 = 2.0\,\text{km/h}$; a reasonableness check reveals the error.
    • The speed is given to calculator precision, $7.2939\,\text{m/s}$, or the uncertainty and the speed to different precisions.
    • In part c, it is concluded from the difference of the means that Liisa was faster, or from the overlapping intervals that the runners were equally fast.
    • In part d, the reaction time is said to cause only a random error; its average part is systematic.

    Principle

    Model answer

    Systematic error. Every reading is larger than the known mass of the weight, and the difference repeats in the same way in every weighing.     Model answer

    a)

    Find

    the shortcomings of the report and why each weakens the result

    Principle

    A model is fitted to the measurement points, and its choice is justified physically. The slope is read from the fitted line, a deviating point is checked before it is left out, and the result is stated with its uncertainty.

    Model answer

    • A broken line. A line joining the points also follows the measurement errors, and it is not a model. In uniform motion the position changes evenly, so one straight line is fitted to the points.
    • Speed from two adjacent points. The marking error of each point affects the result fully: if each position can be $0.03\,\text{m}$ wrong, the speed can be $0.12\,\text{m/s}$ wrong. That is why the result $0.42\,\text{m/s}$ is uncertain.
    • A line through the origin. The timing began when the car was already moving, at about $0.35\,\text{m}$, so the position is not zero at the moment $t = 0$. The forced line is too steep, and the slope $0.63\,\text{m/s}$ is too large.
    • A point was removed without a reason. The point deviates by about $0.25\,\text{m}$, which is a lot. The reason should have been checked from the video, for example whether the car was marked in the wrong frame, and the omission recorded with its reason.
    • The result and the conclusion. The result has four significant figures and no uncertainty, and the result was chosen by what felt suitable, not on the basis of the method.

    b)

    Find

    the speed of the toy car with its uncertainty, and how the point $t = 1.5\,\text{s}$ is dealt with

    Graph

    The horizontal axis has the time $t\ (\text{s})$ and the vertical axis the position $x\ (\text{m})$. The points have uncertainty bars of $\pm 0.03\,\text{m}$, which is an estimate of the precision of marking from the video.

    Model answer

    Six points are close to one straight line, so the car moved uniformly. The point $t = 1.5\,\text{s}$ is about $0.25\,\text{m}$ above the line, almost at the same position as the point $t = 2.0\,\text{s}$, so it has probably been marked in the wrong frame. The point is checked from the video. If an error is found, it is corrected, or the point is left out and the reason recorded.

    The line is not forced through the origin. Two points of it far apart are read, $(0\,\text{s}$; $0.36\,\text{m})$ and $(3.0\,\text{s}$; $1.79\,\text{m})$, so the speed is $v = \dfrac{1.79\,\text{m} - 0.36\,\text{m}}{3.0\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} = 0.477\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$. The constant term $0.36\,\text{m}$ is the position of the car when the timing began. The steepest and the shallowest line through all the bars give slopes of about $0.492$ and $0.464\,\text{m/s}$, so $\Delta v = (0.492 - 0.464)\,\text{m/s} : 2 = 0.014\,\text{m/s}$.

    The result is $v = (0.477 \pm 0.014)\,\text{m/s}$, when the uncertainty keeps two figures because its first digit is $1$. The result agrees with the report's two-point value $0.42\,\text{m/s}$ only thanks to that value's large uncertainty, but not with the value of the line forced through the origin, $0.63\,\text{m/s}$.

    c)

    Find

    two justified improvements, one of which concerns a systematic error

    Principle

    The effect of random error is reduced by adding measurements and by lengthening the measured interval. A systematic error is revealed only by comparing with a known value or with another method.

    Model answer

    Checking the scale. If the measuring tape is closer to the camera than the car, or the camera films at an angle, a metre in the video is not a metre on the car's path. Then all the positions and the speed are wrong in the same proportion, and the error does not show in the spread of the points. The measuring tape is placed on the car's path, the camera perpendicular to the motion and far enough away, and the scale is checked by measuring some other known length from the video.

    More and longer data. When the car is filmed over a longer distance and the position is marked, for example, every $0.1\,\text{s}$, the slope is determined from more points and over a longer interval. Then the marking error of one point matters less, and the difference between the steepest and the shallowest line gets smaller. Repeated runs also tell whether the speed of the car varies from run to run.

    Also accepted

    • In part b, a calculator or spreadsheet fit of a straight line without the point $1.5\,\text{s}$: $v = 0.477\,\text{m/s}$ and constant term $0.358\,\text{m}$. Speeds $0.46$–$0.50\,\text{m/s}$ are accepted.
    • In part b, keeping the point, when its effect is checked: the point is in the middle of the measured range, so the slope does not change, but the points are no longer on the line within their uncertainties.
    • In part b, the uncertainty $\pm 0.02\,\text{m/s}$ and the result $(0.48 \pm 0.02)\,\text{m/s}$.

    Common mistakes

    • Shortcomings of the report are named without justifying why they weaken the result.
    • Removing a deviating point is considered always wrong or always right; what matters is whether a reason is found for the deviation and whether it is recorded.
    • In part b, the line is forced through the origin or the speed is calculated from one point as $x : t$.
    • In part c, only "more precise measuring" is suggested without saying what is done and why it helps.

    Model answer

    a)

    Find

    whether the pressure is inversely proportional to the syringe reading $V$

    Principle

    If $p$ is inversely proportional to the quantity $V$, the product $pV$ is constant. The claim is tested by calculating the product on different rows and seeing whether it varies randomly or changes systematically in the same direction.

    Model answer

    On the first row $pV = 189.1\,\text{kPa} \cdot 12\,\text{ml} = 2269\,\text{kPa} \cdot \text{ml}$ and on the last $81.8\,\text{kPa} \cdot 30\,\text{ml} = 2454\,\text{kPa} \cdot \text{ml}$. The products of the rows in between grow steadily row by row, and the total growth is $(2454 - 2269) : 2269 \approx 8\,\%$. The pressures are given to a tenth of a kilopascal, and the product grows in the same direction on every row. A random error would vary haphazardly, so the steady growth of $8\,\%$ is not caused by it. So the claim does not hold for the syringe reading $V$. The growth of the product suggests that the true volume of the air is larger than the reading: part of the air is in the tube and the sensor, and its share is largest when the volume of the syringe is small.

    b)

    Find

    a graph in which the relationship is linear, and a straight line fitted to it

    Graph

    The true volume of the air is $V + V_0$. When the temperature and the amount of air stay the same, according to Boyle's law $p(V + V_0) = C$, so $V = C \cdot \tfrac{1}{p} - V_0$. The horizontal axis is therefore given $\tfrac{1}{p}$ and the vertical axis $V$. For example, on the first row $\tfrac{1}{p} = \tfrac{1}{189.1\,\text{kPa}} = \tfrac{1}{0.1891\,\text{MPa}} = 5.29\,\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}}$ and on the last $12.2\,\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}}$.

    Model answer

    The points lie well on a straight line, so the model $p(V + V_0) = C$ fits the data. The line does not pass through the origin: it is extended to the vertical axis, which it crosses on the negative side. If there were no air in the tube, the line would pass through the origin. The unit $\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}}$ has been chosen so that the numbers on the axis are easy to read.

    c)

    Given

    the line of part b; $T = 22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $R = 8.314\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol} \cdot \text{K}}$

    Find

    the air volume $V_0$ of the tube and the sensor and the amount of substance $n$ of the air

    Principle

    The equation of the line is $V = C \cdot \tfrac{1}{p} - V_0$, so the constant term is $-V_0$ and the slope $C = p(V + V_0)$. According to the ideal gas law $p(V + V_0) = nRT$, so the amount of substance is obtained from the slope. In the ideal gas law the temperature is in kelvins.

    Reading the graph

    The line crosses the vertical axis at $-1.8\,\text{ml}$. Two points of the line far apart are read, $(4.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}}$; $8.6\,\text{ml})$ and $(12.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}}$; $29.5\,\text{ml})$.

    Formula

    $V_0 = -b$, $C = \dfrac{\Delta V}{\Delta (1/p)}$ and $C = nRT$, so $n = \dfrac{C}{RT}$

    Conversions

    $1\,\text{MPa} \cdot \text{ml}$$= 10^{6}\,\text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^3$
    $= 1\,\text{Pa} \cdot \text{m}^3$
    $= 1\,\text{J}$
    $T$$= (22 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
    $= 295.15\,\text{K}$

    Substitution

    $V_0$$= 1.8\,\text{ml}$
    $C$$= \dfrac{29.5\,\text{ml} - 8.6\,\text{ml}}{12.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}} - 4.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{MPa}}}$
    $= 2.61\,\text{MPa} \cdot \text{ml}$
    $= 2.61\,\text{J}$
    $n$$= \dfrac{2.61\,\text{J}}{8.314\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 295.15\,\text{K}}$
    $= 1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{mol}$

    Answer

    $V_0 \approx 1.8\,\text{ml}$

    And $n \approx 1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{mol}$. The volume has two significant figures, because it is read from the graph to about $0.1\,\text{ml}$; the amount of substance has three, as the slope does.

    Reasonableness

    At normal pressure and room temperature a mole of gas takes up about $24\,\text{l}$, so about $26\,\text{ml}$ of air is about

    $26\,\text{ml} : 24\,000\,\text{ml/mol}$
    $\approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{mol}$

    An air space of a few millilitres in the tube and the sensor is plausible for a short tube. When $V_0$ is added, the product $p(V + V_0)$ is $189.1\,\text{kPa} \cdot 13.8\,\text{ml} = 2610\,\text{kPa} \cdot \text{ml}$ on the first row and $81.8\,\text{kPa} \cdot 31.8\,\text{ml} = 2601\,\text{kPa} \cdot \text{ml}$ on the last, that is, almost constant.

    d)

    Find

    the assumptions of the result and their effect

    Principle

    The model $p(V + V_0) = nRT$ assumes that the amount and the temperature of the air stay the same and that the whole volume has been measured. For each assumption we consider in which direction the result would change if it did not hold.

    Model answer

    The temperature stays the same. Compressing is work that increases the internal energy of the air. When the plunger is pushed slowly, the air has time to give off the heat through the walls of the syringe and stays at room temperature. In a fast compression the air would warm up, the pressure would momentarily be larger than the model gives, and the product $p(V + V_0)$ would grow during the compression.

    No air leaks. If air leaked past the plunger at high pressure, the amount of substance would decrease during the measurement, and the product $p(V + V_0)$ would decrease at small volumes.

    The whole volume is included. Without $V_0$ the product $pV$ would seem to grow with the volume, and one could wrongly conclude from the data that Boyle's law does not hold. The amount of substance would also be calculated from the wrong volume.

    Conclusion. When $V_0 = 1.8\,\text{ml}$ is added to the reading, $p(V + V_0)$ is about $2600\,\text{kPa} \cdot \text{ml}$ over the whole measured range, with less than half a percent of variation. So the data agree with the model, which supports the view that the assumptions held.

    Common mistakes

    • In part a, $pV$ is said to be "almost constant" without looking at the direction in which the product changes row by row, so the systematic growth goes unnoticed.
    • In part b, a $(\tfrac{1}{V}, p)$ graph is drawn and the line is forced through the origin; the air space of the tube then does not show.
    • In part c, the temperature is substituted in degrees Celsius, or the volume in millilitres and the pressure in kilopascals without conversion, so the amount of substance is wrong by a factor of a thousand.
    • In part d, assumptions are listed without saying in which direction they would change the result.
