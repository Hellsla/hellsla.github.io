Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle

Why is the first hill of a roller coaster always the highest of the track, even though the train is no longer pulled up after it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain when mechanical energy is conserved and what it turns into when friction or air resistance does work.
  • calculate a speed or a height using the law of conservation of mechanical energy.
  • calculate the work done by forces other than weight using the work–energy principle.

Prior knowledge

  • Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ and $E_\text{p} = mgh$chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy
  • A force does work when an object moves in the direction of the force, and work transfers energychapter Work and energy

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

A roller coaster train leaves the station, and a chain lift slowly pulls it to the top of the first hill, at a height of $40\,\text{m}$. After that nothing pulls the train any more. The train plunges into the valley, speeds over the next hill and the one after that, and finally rolls to the brakes at the station. In the drawings of the track each hill is lower than the one before.

Where does the train get its speed without an engine, and why can no later hill be as high as the first?

Optional Review: kinetic energy
The speed of a roller coaster car doubles in the valley. How does its kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ change?

  • Kinetic energy is not directly proportional to the speed but to its square: $\tfrac{1}{2} m (2v)^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$. Doubling the speed gives four times the kinetic energy.

  • Kinetic energy depends on both the mass and the speed. When the speed doubles, the kinetic energy becomes four times as large even though the mass does not change.

  • Correct. Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, so doubling the speed gives $2^2 = 4$ times as much kinetic energy (chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy).

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation. The car is a point-like object that stays on the track. The bottom of the valley is $6\,\text{m}$ long and horizontal, and the slopes are either arcs with a radius of $21\,\text{m}$ or straight $45^\circ$ slopes. The joint between a straight slope and the bottom is assumed to be rounded so gently that the speed of the car does not change there. The resistive force $F_\text{res}$ stands for rolling resistance and air resistance together: it is constant in magnitude and always points against the motion. The normal force of the track is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work.

How the calculation works

The motion of the car is calculated from Newton's second law along the track in small time steps. The energies are calculated from the state of the car: $E_\text{p} = mgh$ from the height $h$ above the bottom of the valley and $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ from the speed, with $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$, and $\Delta E_\text{mech}$ is its change from the starting moment. The work done by the resistive force is calculated from the definition $W_\text{other} = -F_\text{res}\,s$, where $s$ is the distance travelled along the track. In the bars $W_\text{other}$ is negative, so it is drawn downward from the zero level.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Mechanical energy changes its form

    In the chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy you learned that the mechanical energy of an object is the sum of its kinetic energy and potential energy, $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$. When a roller coaster car rolls into a valley, its height decreases and its speed increases: potential energy turns into kinetic energy. When the car climbs the opposite slope, kinetic energy turns back into potential energy. If there is no friction or air resistance, the car rises exactly to its starting height and stops there for a moment. The mechanical energy is then equal at every moment, even though its form changes. This is called conservation of mechanical energy.

    Not all the forces acting on the car change its energy. The weight does work when the car goes down or up, and it is exactly this work that turns potential energy into kinetic energy and back. The normal force of the track is perpendicular to the surface of the track and therefore also to the motion, so it does no work. That is why the mechanical energy is conserved when only the weight does work, however twisting the track is. The speed of the car at any point then depends only on how much lower it has descended from the starting point, not on the shape of the track or the mass of the car.

    Work transfers energy

    In the chapter Work and energy you learned that a force does work when an object moves in the direction of the force. Now we also need the sign of the work. A force does positive work when it has a component in the direction of the motion: it increases the energy of the object. A force that opposes the motion, such as friction, does negative work: it decreases the energy of the object. A force perpendicular to the motion, such as the normal force, does no work at all. The unit of work is the same as that of energy, the joule.

    Friction turns mechanical energy into internal energy

    In a real roller coaster the rolling resistance of the wheels and the air resistance oppose the motion the whole way. They do negative work, and the mechanical energy of the car decreases by exactly this work. Energy does not disappear but turns into internal energy of the wheels, the rails and the air, which warm up slightly. The total energy is always conserved. Mechanical energy is conserved only when there is no friction or air resistance, or when they are so small that they can be ignored.

    You remember the difference between the weight, that is gravity, and friction from the chapter Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy: the weight is a conservative force. What is new is what follows from this for calculating energy: the work done by the weight depends only on the difference in height, so when an object returns to the same height, it gets back the same energy, whatever route it took. That is why a potential energy can be associated with the weight. The negative work done by friction and air resistance grows with every metre travelled, so it depends on the route. For such a non-conservative force a potential energy cannot be defined.

    Answer to the opening question

    A chain lift lifts the train to the top of the first hill. After that the train has only the mechanical energy it got during the lift, and nothing adds to it. The resistive forces decrease it with every metre. If the next hill were as high as the first, the train would not reach its top. Every later hill must be lower than the first and lower than the height that the remaining energy would reach, so that the train still has some kinetic energy at the top.

    the work–energy principle

    Work and kinetic energy

    When an object moves in a straight line a distance $s$ and a constant force $\overline{F}$ acts on it, the work done by the force is

    $$W = F_\parallel\, s$$

    where $F_\parallel$ is the component of the force in the direction of the motion ($\text{N}$), negative when the component points against the motion, and $s$ is the distance travelled ($\text{m}$). The unit of work is $1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\,\text{J}$.

    When a constant net force $\Sigma F$ in the direction of the motion acts on an object, it accelerates uniformly, and its acceleration is $a = \tfrac{\Sigma F}{m}$. In the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion you learned that in uniformly accelerated motion the acceleration is $a = \tfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t}$ and the distance travelled is the average speed times the time, $s = \tfrac{v_1 + v_2}{2}\,t$. When these are multiplied together, the time cancels:

    $$\begin{aligned} a s &= \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t} \cdot \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2}\,t \\ \phantom{as} &= \tfrac12 \left(v_2^2 - v_1^2\right) \end{aligned}$$

    When the equation is multiplied on both sides by the mass $m$ and $ma = \Sigma F$ is substituted, we get $\Sigma F\, s = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} m v_1^2$. So the work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy:

    $$W_\text{net} = \Delta E_\text{k}$$

    where $W_\text{net}$ is the work done together by all the forces acting on the object ($\text{J}$) and $\Delta E_\text{k} = E_{\text{k},2} - E_{\text{k},1}$ is the change in kinetic energy ($\text{J}$). For example, when the mass of a cart is $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and a net force of $12\,\text{N}$ accelerates it from rest over a distance of $3.0\,\text{m}$, the force does the work $36\,\text{J}$, and the speed of the cart at the end is $6.0\,\text{m/s}$.

    The work–energy principle

    The total work is divided into two parts: the work done by the weight $W_G$ and the work done by all the other forces $W_\text{other}$. Other forces include, for example, friction, air resistance, a pulling rope and a pushing hand. When an object descends a height $h$, the weight does the work $mgh$ and the potential energy decreases by the same amount, so $W_G = -\Delta E_\text{p}$. Substituting this into the equation $W_G + W_\text{other} = \Delta E_\text{k}$ gives the work–energy principle of mechanics:

    $$\begin{aligned} &E_{\text{k},1} + E_{\text{p},1} + W_\text{other} \\ \quad &= E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2} \end{aligned}$$

    where the index $1$ means the initial situation and $2$ the final situation, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ is the kinetic energy, $E_\text{p} = mgh$ is the potential energy at a height $h$ from the chosen zero level, and $W_\text{other}$ is the work done by forces other than the weight ($\text{J}$). In words: the change in mechanical energy equals the work done by forces other than the weight, $\Delta E_\text{mech} = W_\text{other}$. The zero level can be chosen freely, for example at the lowest point of the situation, because only the change in height affects the result.

    When an object moves against a stationary surface or air, friction and air resistance oppose the motion and do negative work, so they decrease the mechanical energy. (A moving surface or the wind can also do positive work, for example a conveyor belt carrying a box.) A pulling rope or a motor can do positive work and increase it, like the chain lift that lifts the train. If the other forces do no work, $W_\text{other} = 0$, and the work–energy principle becomes the law of conservation of mechanical energy:

    $$\begin{aligned} &E_{\text{k},1} + E_{\text{p},1} \\ \quad &= E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2} \end{aligned}$$

    When an object starts from rest at a height $h$ and descends to the zero level, the conservation law $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ gives $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. The mass cancels, so the speed does not depend on the mass.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a heavier object is faster at the bottom of a hill. When friction and air resistance can be ignored, the mass cancels from the conservation law: a full and an empty car are equally fast at the bottom of the valley. The heavier car has more kinetic energy at the bottom, but it also had more potential energy at the start.

    Another mistake is to say that friction destroys energy. Friction decreases the mechanical energy, but an equal amount of energy turns into internal energy, and the total energy is conserved. A third mistake is to calculate the work of friction from the difference in height. The work of friction is the frictional force times the distance travelled along the track. When the resistive force is the same, on a long gentle track more mechanical energy turns into internal energy than on a short steep one, even if the difference in height is the same.

    Summary

    • The mechanical energy $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$ is conserved when only the weight does work, that is, when friction and air resistance can be ignored and nothing pulls or pushes. The normal force does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion.
    • A force does work with its component in the direction of the motion, $W = F_\parallel s$: positive in the direction of the motion and negative against it.
    • The work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy, $W_\text{net} = \Delta E_\text{k}$.
    • The work–energy principle: the change in mechanical energy is the work done by forces other than the weight, $\Delta E_\text{mech} = W_\text{other}$. Friction and air resistance turn mechanical energy into internal energy, and the total energy is conserved.
    • The weight is a conservative force, because its work depends only on the difference in height. Friction and air resistance are non-conservative forces, because their work grows with the distance travelled.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A child slides down a hill on a sled. The combined mass of the child and the sled is $32\,\text{kg}$. The sled starts from rest at the top of the hill, which is $5.0\,\text{m}$ higher than the foot of the hill. At the foot of the hill the speed of the sled is measured to be $7.6\,\text{m/s}$. How much work do friction and air resistance together do on the sled during the descent?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $m = 32\,\text{kg}$, difference in height $h = 5.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$, final speed $v_2 = 7.6\,\text{m/s}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the work $W_\text{other}$ done together by friction and air resistance.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The weight, the normal force of the snow, friction and air resistance act on the sled. The normal force is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work. The work of the weight is taken into account as the change in potential energy. Friction and air resistance are other forces, so we use the work–energy principle. The zero level is chosen at the foot of the hill.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. At the start $E_{\text{k},1} = 0$ and $E_{\text{p},1} = mgh$, at the end $E_{\text{k},2} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$ and $E_{\text{p},2} = 0$:

      $$\begin{aligned} &mgh + W_\text{other} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 \\ &W_\text{other} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 - mgh \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: all quantities are in SI units, and $1\,\text{kg}\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} = 1\,\text{J}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate the kinetic energy at the end and the potential energy at the start, and then their difference:

      $$\begin{aligned} &\tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 \\ &= \tfrac12 \cdot 32\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(7.6\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 \\ &= 924.16\,\text{J} \\[6pt] &mgh \\ &= 32\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{m} \\ &= 1569.6\,\text{J} \\[6pt] &W_\text{other} \\ &= 924.16\,\text{J} - 1569.6\,\text{J} \\ &= -645.44\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. Friction and air resistance together do the work $W_\text{other} \approx -6.5 \cdot 10^2\,\text{J}$. The result is given with two significant figures like the height $5.0\,\text{m}$ and the speed $7.6\,\text{m/s}$. The work is negative, because friction and air resistance oppose the motion: about $650\,\text{J}$ of mechanical energy turned into internal energy of the snow, the runners and the air.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Without friction and air resistance the speed of the sled at the foot of the hill would be $\sqrt{2gh} \approx 9.9\,\text{m/s}$, so the measured $7.6\,\text{m/s}$ is smaller, as it should be. About $41\,\%$ of the initial potential energy turned into internal energy. If the slope is $24\,\text{m}$ long, the average resistive force is $645\,\text{J} : 24\,\text{m} \approx 27\,\text{N}$, less than a tenth of the weight $314\,\text{N}$, which is believable for a sliding sled.

      Optional Find the error
      A box of mass $5.0\,\text{kg}$ slides from rest down a ramp $1.5\,\text{m}$ high. Friction does the work $-30\,\text{J}$ on the box. A student calculates the speed of the box at the bottom of the ramp like this: $E_\text{k} = mgh + 30\,\text{J} = 73.6\,\text{J} + 30\,\text{J} = 103.6\,\text{J}$, so $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{2E_\text{k}}{m}} \approx 6.4\,\text{m/s}$.Where is the error?

      • The work done by the weight and the change in potential energy depend only on the difference in height, $mgh = 73.6\,\text{J}$. The error is in the sign of the work done by friction: negative work decreases the kinetic energy.

      • The kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ always tells the speed, also with friction. Friction affects how much kinetic energy there is. The error is in the sign of the work done by friction.

      • Correct. By the work–energy principle $mgh + W_\text{other} = E_\text{k}$, and $W_\text{other} = -30\,\text{J}$. The kinetic energy is $43.6\,\text{J}$ and the speed about $4.2\,\text{m/s}$. The student's result $6.4\,\text{m/s}$ is greater than the frictionless case $5.4\,\text{m/s}$, which is impossible.

      Apply to a new situation

      A child sits on a swing at a playground. The swing is pulled back so that the seat is $1.20\,\text{m}$ higher than at the lowest point of the swing, and it is released from rest. Air resistance and the friction at the attachment of the chains are ignored.

      What is the speed of the seat at the lowest point of the swing? Answer in $\text{m/s}$.

      m/s
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which forces act on the seat and the child, and which of them do work? The tension force of the chains is perpendicular to the motion at every moment.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Only the weight does work, so the mechanical energy is conserved: $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$. Choose the zero level at the lowest point.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The mass cancels:

      $v$$= \sqrt{2gh}$
      $= \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 1.20\,\text{m}}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      difference in height $h = 1.20\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance and friction are ignored

      Find

      the speed $v$ at the lowest point in $\text{m/s}$

      Principle

      The weight and the tension force of the chains act on the seat and the child. The seat moves along a circular arc, and the force of the chains is perpendicular to the motion at every moment, so it does no work. Only the weight does work, so the mechanical energy is conserved. The zero level is chosen at the lowest point.

      Formula

      $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$

      Conversions

      The height is in metres, so no conversions are needed. The mass is not needed, because it cancels.

      Substitution

      $v$$= \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.20\,\text{m}}$
      $= \sqrt{23.544\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
      $= 4.852\,\text{m/s}$

      Answer

      $4.85\,\text{m/s}$

      Three significant figures like the height $1.20\,\text{m}$.

      Reasonableness

      The speed is about $17\,\text{km/h}$, which suits brisk swinging. The same speed would be reached by dropping an object straight down from a height of $1.20\,\text{m}$: the curved path of the swing does not change the speed, because the work done by the weight depends only on the difference in height.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      Three identical balls are thrown from the same balcony with the same speed: one straight up, one horizontally and one straight down. Air resistance is ignored.Which ball has the greatest speed when it hits the ground?

      • The ball thrown down reaches the ground first, but not at the greatest speed. Kinetic energy does not depend on the direction of the velocity, so all the balls have the same mechanical energy at the start and the same speed at the ground.

      • The ball thrown up rises higher, but its kinetic energy first turns into potential energy and back. At the level of the balcony it moves downward with the same speed at which it was thrown. All the balls have the same speed at the ground.

      • Correct. All the balls have the same kinetic energy and the same potential energy at the start. Only the weight does work, so at the ground they have the same kinetic energy and thus the same speed. Energy does not depend on the direction of the velocity.

      2. Calculating

      A roller coaster car starts from rest at the top of a hill at a height of $18.0\,\text{m}$ and goes over the top of the next hill, which is at a height of $7.0\,\text{m}$. Friction and air resistance are ignored.

      What is the speed of the car at the top of the second hill? Answer in $\text{m/s}$.

      m/s
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Does any force other than the weight do work? If not, the mechanical energy is conserved.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Choose the zero level at the ground: $mgh_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2 + mgh_2$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{2g(h_1 - h_2)}$, and the difference in height is $11.0\,\text{m}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      starting height $h_1 = 18.0\,\text{m}$, top of the second hill $h_2 = 7.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; friction and air resistance are ignored

      Find

      the speed $v_2$ at the top of the second hill in $\text{m/s}$

      Principle

      The weight and the normal force of the track act on the car. The normal force is perpendicular to the motion and does no work, so only the weight does work and the mechanical energy is conserved. The zero level is chosen at the ground.

      Formula

      $mgh_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2 + mgh_2$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{2g(h_1 - h_2)}$

      Conversions

      The heights are in metres, so no conversions are needed. The mass is not needed, because it cancels.

      Substitution

      $v_2$$= \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (18.0 - 7.0)\,\text{m}}$
      $= \sqrt{215.82\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
      $= 14.69\,\text{m/s}$

      Answer

      $14.7\,\text{m/s}$

      Three significant figures like the difference in height $11.0\,\text{m}$.

      Reasonableness

      The speed is about $53\,\text{km/h}$. It is smaller than the speed $\sqrt{2g \cdot 18.0\,\text{m}} \approx 18.8\,\text{m/s}$ at ground level, as it should be, because at the top the car still has potential energy.

      3. Explain

      Use energy to explain why the first hill of a roller coaster is the highest of the track and the later hills are lower.

      Where does the mechanical energy of the train go during the ride?

      Model answer

      The chain lift does work on the train and lifts it to the top of the first hill. After that only the weight, the normal force of the track and the resistive forces act on the train, and none of them adds to the mechanical energy of the train. If there were no resistive forces, the mechanical energy would be conserved and the train could rise at most to the starting height, and even then it would stop at the top. In reality rolling resistance and air resistance do negative work all the time, and it grows with every metre travelled. So the mechanical energy of the train decreases during the whole ride. Each hill must be lower than the height that the remaining mechanical energy would reach, so that the train still has kinetic energy at the top. That is why the hills usually get lower towards the end of the track. Energy does not disappear: it turns into internal energy of the wheels, the rails and the air, and finally at the station the brakes turn the rest of the kinetic energy into internal energy.

        1. Core

          A ball is thrown straight down from a balcony at a height of $15.0\,\text{m}$ at a speed of $8.00\,\text{m/s}$. Air resistance is ignored.

          At what speed does the ball hit the ground?

          m/s
          Answer

          $18.9\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          height $h = 15.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 8.00\,\text{m/s}$ downward, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is ignored

          Find

          the speed $v_2$ at the ground in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          After the throw only the weight acts on the ball, so the mechanical energy is conserved. The zero level is chosen at the ground: at the start the ball has both kinetic and potential energy, at the end only kinetic energy. Kinetic energy does not depend on the direction of the velocity.

          Formula

          $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2 + mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2gh}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The mass cancels.

          Substitution

          First

          $v_1^2$$= (8.00\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 64.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          and

          $2gh$$= 2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 15.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 294.3\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          so

          $v_2$$= \sqrt{(64.0 + 294.3)\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
          $= 18.93\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $18.9\,\text{m/s}$

          Three significant figures like the height $15.0\,\text{m}$ and the speed $8.00\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          A plain drop would give $\sqrt{294.3}\,\text{m/s} \approx 17.2\,\text{m/s}$, and the throw adds speed, so the result must be greater than this. The speeds do not simply add, however: $18.9\,\text{m/s}$ is much less than $8.0 + 17.2\,\text{m/s}$, because energies add, not speeds.

        2. Core

          A car of mass $1200\,\text{kg}$ drives on a horizontal road at a speed of $72\,\text{km/h}$. The driver brakes, and the frictional force of the road on the tyres is $6.0\,\text{kN}$ during the braking. Air resistance is ignored.

          How long is the braking distance of the car?

          m
          Answer

          $40\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, initial speed $v_1 = 72\,\text{km/h}$, final speed $v_2 = 0$, frictional force $F_\mu = 6.0\,\text{kN}$ against the motion; the road is horizontal and air resistance is ignored

          Find

          the braking distance $s$ in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          The road is horizontal, so the potential energy does not change. The weight and the normal force of the road are perpendicular to the motion and do no work. The only force that does work is friction, which is also the net force, so its work is the change in kinetic energy.

          Formula

          $-F_\mu s = 0 - \tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2$, so $s = \dfrac{mv_1^2}{2F_\mu}$

          Conversions

          $v_1$$= 72\,\text{km/h}$
          $= 72 : 3.6\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 20\,\text{m/s}$

          and

          $F_\mu$$= 6.0\,\text{kN}$
          $= 6.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{N}$
          $= 6000\,\text{N}$

          Substitution

          $s$$= \dfrac{1200\,\text{kg} \cdot (20\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 6000\,\text{N}}$
          $= \dfrac{480\,000\,\text{J}}{12\,000\,\text{N}}$
          $= 40\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $40\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the speed $72\,\text{km/h}$ and the force $6.0\,\text{kN}$.

          Reasonableness

          The deceleration is $6000\,\text{N} : 1200\,\text{kg} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the equations of motion give the same distance: $\tfrac{(20\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2} = 40\,\text{m}$. Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, so at double the speed the braking distance would be four times as long.

          Answer

          $= E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2}$

        1. A cyclist coasts down a hill without pedalling or braking. The combined mass of the cyclist and the bicycle is $80\,\text{kg}$. The speed is $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ at the top of the hill and $13.0\,\text{m/s}$ at the bottom. The height of the hill is $12.0\,\text{m}$, and the road down the hill is $150\,\text{m}$ long.

          What is the average resistive force that slows the cyclist, when air resistance and rolling resistance are added together?

          N
          Answer

          $22\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 80\,\text{kg}$, speed at the top of the hill $v_1 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$ and at the bottom $v_2 = 13.0\,\text{m/s}$, height $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, length of the road $s = 150\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the average resistive force $F_\text{res}$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The weight, the normal force of the road, and air resistance and rolling resistance act on the cyclist. The normal force does no work. The resistive forces are forces other than the weight, so the work–energy principle gives their work. The resistive force points against the motion all the way, so its work is $-F_\text{res} s$. The zero level is chosen at the bottom of the hill.

          Formula

          $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2 + mgh - F_\text{res} s = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$, so

          $F_\text{res}$$= \dfrac{\tfrac{m}{2}(v_1^2 - v_2^2) + mgh}{s}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          First

          $\tfrac{m}{2}(v_1^2 - v_2^2)$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 80\,\text{kg} \cdot (16 - 169)\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
          $= -6120\,\text{J}$

          and

          $mgh$$= 80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 12.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 9417.6\,\text{J}$

          so

          $F_\text{res}$$= \dfrac{-6120\,\text{J} + 9417.6\,\text{J}}{150\,\text{m}}$
          $= \dfrac{3297.6\,\text{J}}{150\,\text{m}}$
          $= 21.98\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $22\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $80\,\text{kg}$ and the speed $4.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The resistive forces took $3.3\,\text{kJ}$, about a third of the potential energy $9.4\,\text{kJ}$. At a speed of about $13\,\text{m/s}$ the air resistance on a cyclist is a few tens of newtons, so the average $22\,\text{N}$ for the whole hill is believable.

        2. A roller coaster car with a mass of $500\,\text{kg}$ including passengers goes over the top of a $35.0\,\text{m}$ high hill at a speed of $2.0\,\text{m/s}$. The track to the top of the next hill is $120\,\text{m}$ long, and an average resistive force of $150\,\text{N}$ acts on the car all the way.

          What is the greatest height the next hill may have so that the car reaches its top?

          m
          Answer

          $31.5\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 500\,\text{kg}$, height of the first top $h_1 = 35.0\,\text{m}$, speed there $v_1 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, track distance $s = 120\,\text{m}$, resistive force $F_\text{res} = 150\,\text{N}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the greatest height $h_2$ of the next hill in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          The weight, the normal force of the track and the resistive force act on the car. The normal force does no work, and the resistive force does the work $-F_\text{res} s$, so we use the work–energy principle. The car reaches the highest possible hill when its speed at the top is just zero. The zero level is chosen at the ground.

          Formula

          $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2 + mgh_1 - F_\text{res} s = mgh_2$, so

          $h_2$$= h_1 + \dfrac{v_1^2}{2 \cdot g} - \dfrac{F_\text{res} s}{mg}$

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          $\dfrac{v_1^2}{2g}$$= \dfrac{(2.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 0.204\,\text{m}$

          and

          $\dfrac{F_\text{res} s}{mg}$$= \dfrac{150\,\text{N} \cdot 120\,\text{m}}{500\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 3.670\,\text{m}$

          so

          $h_2$$= (35.0 + 0.204 - 3.670)\,\text{m}$
          $= 31.53\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          at most $31.5\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the height $35.0\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The resistive force takes $150\,\text{N} \cdot 120\,\text{m} = 18\,\text{kJ}$, which corresponds to a height of about $3.7\,\text{m}$ for a $500\,\text{kg}$ car, and the small initial speed adds only $0.2\,\text{m}$. So the hill is a little lower than the previous one, as in roller coasters. In reality the hill is made even lower so that the car goes over the top at speed.

          Show the solution

          Given

          height $h = 20.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 12.0\,\text{m/s}$ at an angle of $35^\circ$ to the horizontal, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is ignored

          Find

          the speed $v_2$ of the stone at the water surface in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          After the throw only the weight acts on the stone, so the mechanical energy is conserved. Energy is a scalar: the kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ depends only on the magnitude of the velocity, not on its direction, so the angle of the throw is not needed. The zero level is chosen at the water surface.

          Formula

          $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_1^2 + mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2gh}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The mass cancels.

          Substitution

          First

          $v_1^2$$= (12.0\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 144\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          and

          $2gh$$= 2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 20.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 392.4\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          so

          $v_2$$= \sqrt{(144 + 392.4)\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
          $= 23.16\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $23.2\,\text{m/s}$

          Three significant figures like the height $20.0\,\text{m}$ and the speed $12.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The result is greater than the speed from a plain drop, $\sqrt{392.4}\,\text{m/s} \approx 19.8\,\text{m/s}$, and greater than the throwing speed, as it should be. If the stone were thrown with the same speed horizontally or downward, it would hit the water at the same speed $23.2\,\text{m/s}$ but in a different direction.
