Applied lesson: heat phenomena in everyday life

Why does the ice pack in a cool bag keep drinks cold for hours in hot weather, but once the ice has melted, the drinks warm up quickly?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • combine heat, latent heats of fusion and vaporisation, and conducted power into one energy balance.
  • justify whether the incoming energy is used for heating or for a change of state.
  • solve multi-step problems about everyday heat phenomena.

Prior knowledge

  • The quantity of heat when heating is $Q = cm\Delta T$chapter Heating and cooling of a substance: Q = cmΔT
  • Melting and vaporisation need energy (freezing and condensation release it), but the temperature does not change, $Q = sm$ and $Q = rm$chapter Changes of state
  • The power conducted through a building part is $P = UA\Delta T$chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 6.

1Think about it

On a trip, a cool bag contains an ice pack and bottles of drinks. It is hot outside, and the walls of the bag let in a little heat all the time. For a few hours the drinks are ice cold. Then there is nothing left of the ice but water, and a couple of hours later the drinks are no longer cold.

At the start, the same power comes through the wall all the time. Why do the drinks stay cold for hours but then warm up quickly?

Optional Revise the previous lesson
Two cool bags have the same insulation and the same temperature difference between the inside and the outside, but the total area of the walls of one is twice as large.How much more power is conducted through the walls of the larger bag, when $P = UA\Delta T$?

  • In the formula $A$ is a factor and not a divisor. The larger the surface, the more heat can pass through it, so the power increases.

  • Correct. When $U$ and $\Delta T$ stay the same and $A$ doubles, $P = UA\Delta T$ also doubles.

  • The insulation and the temperature difference are the same, but the power also depends on the area: $P = UA\Delta T$ is directly proportional to the area $A$. More heat is conducted through a larger surface.

  • The formula $P = UA\Delta T$ contains the area only once. Doubling the area doubles the power, it does not quadruple it.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Everyday heat phenomena, such as heating a sauna, a cool bag staying cold and cooling an engine, are solved with the same principle: energy bookkeeping. Energy is conserved, so every joule needs a source and a destination. The source is a power, which means energy per second, or another object that gives out heat. There are two destinations. The energy raises the temperature, $Q = cm\Delta T$, or it changes the state of matter, $Q = sm$ or $Q = rm$. During a change of state the temperature does not change. When ice melts or water boils, all the energy goes into the change, and the temperature rises only after the change is over.

    A power $P = UA\Delta T$ flows into the cool bag through its walls, even though the insulation is good. As long as there is ice left, all this energy melts ice and the inside stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Melting takes a lot of energy: melting one kilogram of ice takes $334\,\text{kJ}$, the same as heating the same amount of water by 80 kelvins. When the ice has melted, the same power starts to raise the temperature of the water, and the drinks warm up. This is why it is worth putting ice in the bag and not just cold water.

    A sauna stove heats stones with a specific heat capacity of about $800\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$. When you throw water on the hot stones, the water warms up and evaporates, and the energy is taken from the stones. Most of it goes into vaporisation: $0.20\,\text{kg}$ of water warms from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ with $67\,\text{kJ}$ of energy, but vaporising it takes $452\,\text{kJ}$. This is why the stones cool down at once and the stove has to heat them again. When the steam condenses on your skin, the same energy is released again, and this is why the steam feels hot.

    A car's coolant carries the waste heat of the engine to the radiator. Power is energy per second, so the power transferred depends on the mass of liquid carried per second and on its rise in temperature in the engine. With the same power, a larger mass flow means a smaller rise in temperature. Water has a large specific heat capacity, $4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, but it freezes in frost and boils easily, so glycol is mixed with it. The mixture withstands frost and heat, but its specific heat capacity is lower, about $3500\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$.

    Energy balance

    When energy is transferred into a system with power $P$ during a time $t$, the energy $Pt$ is shared between the heating of the parts of the system and the changes of state:

    $$\begin{aligned} Pt &= Q_1 + Q_2 + \dots \\ Q_\text{heating} &= c\,m\,\Delta T \\ Q_\text{melting} &= s\,m \\ Q_\text{vaporisation} &= r\,m \end{aligned}$$

    where $P$ is power (W), $t$ is time (s), $Q$ is quantity of heat (J), $c$ is specific heat capacity (J/(kg·K)), $m$ is mass (kg), $\Delta T$ is change in temperature (K), $s$ is specific latent heat of fusion and $r$ is specific latent heat of vaporisation (J/kg). For water $s = 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$ and $r = 2260\,\text{kJ/kg}$. You calculate each stage separately, and melting and vaporisation involve no change in temperature.

    The power conducted through a building part, such as the wall of a cool bag, is

    $$P = U A \Delta T$$

    where $U$ is the U-value (W/(m²·K)), $A$ is the total area of the walls (m²) and $\Delta T$ is the difference between the outside and inside temperatures (K). As long as there is ice in the bag, the inside temperature is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $\Delta T$ is the outside temperature in degrees Celsius. Then all the energy coming through the wall melts ice, $UA\Delta T\,t = sm$, and the time the ice lasts is

    $$t = \frac{s\,m}{U A \Delta T}$$

    The graph shows an example of the inside temperature of a cool bag calculated with the model. The temperature stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ for a time $t_0$, until the ice has melted. After that the water warms up towards the outside temperature. The rise is steep at first and slows down, because the power $UA\Delta T$ becomes smaller as the temperature difference becomes smaller. This is the bag of the example; the bag in the Investigate step has a smaller $UA$.

    In the cooling circuit of an engine, the liquid takes the heat power $P$ from the engine, so the heating formula $Q = cm\Delta T$ is divided by the time $t$:

    $$P = c\,\frac{m}{t}\,\Delta T$$

    where $m/t$ is the mass of liquid carried per second (kg/s) and $\Delta T = T_2 - T_1$ is the rise in the temperature of the liquid in the engine (K). In steady running the radiator gives the same power $P$ out to the air, so the temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ of the liquid stay constant.

    When two objects or substances are in an insulated container, the quantity of heat given out by the hot one is equal to the quantity of heat taken in by the cold one:

    $$Q_\text{released} = Q_\text{absorbed}$$

    The problem goes like this: 1. Define the system and decide where the energy comes from and where it goes. 2. Write the energy of the source: $Pt$ or $cm\Delta T$ of the substance that gives out heat. 3. Go through the stages in order of temperature: warming up to the melting point, melting, warming up. During melting the temperature is constant. 4. Check whether the energy is enough: compare the energy available with what the next change of state needs. If it is not enough, the change stays unfinished and the temperature is the melting point. If it is just enough, the change finishes and the temperature is exactly the melting point. 5. Check the units and the order of magnitude.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that ice produces cold that flows into the drinks. Cold does not flow: because of the temperature difference, energy moves on net from the warmer object to the colder one, and ice cools things because it takes in energy. Another mistake is to assume that the temperature rises steadily all the time. During melting the temperature stays constant, even though energy keeps coming in.

    A third mistake in a mixing problem is to assume that the ice always melts completely and that the end result is an average. When $1.0\,\text{kg}$ of ice and $1.5\,\text{kg}$ of water at 20 °C are mixed, the water can give out at most

    $$\begin{aligned} Q_\text{water} &= c\,m\,\Delta T \\ &= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 20\,\text{K} \\ &\approx 126\,\text{kJ} \end{aligned}$$

    but melting all the ice takes $334\,\text{kJ}$. The ice melts only partly, and the temperature of the mixture is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

    Summary

    • An everyday heat phenomenon is solved with energy bookkeeping: the incoming energy ($Pt$ or the quantity of heat given out by another object) is used for heating, $Q = cm\Delta T$, or for a change of state, $Q = sm$ or $Q = rm$.
    • During a change of state the temperature stays constant. Melting ice takes as much energy as heating the same amount of water by 80 kelvins.
    • The power coming through the wall of a cool bag is $P = UA\Delta T$, and the time the ice lasts is $t = sm/(UA\Delta T)$.
    • The power transferred by coolant is $P = c\,(m/t)\,\Delta T$. A lower specific heat capacity needs a larger mass flow to transfer the same power.
    • In a mixing problem, first check whether the energy given out is enough to melt all the ice. When the ice is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at the start and the energy is not enough, the temperature is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      On a trip, a cool bag contains a $1.0\,\text{kg}$ ice pack at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $0.50\,\text{kg}$ of drinks in bottles at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The total area of the walls of the bag is 0.80 m² and the U-value is $1.5\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{}\cdot\text{K)}$, and it is $28\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ outside. Assume that the drinks cool to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at once, melting ice, and that the inside stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ until the ice has melted. How long does the ice last? The specific heat capacity of the drinks is the value for water.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The mass of the ice is $m_\text{ice} = 1.0\,\text{kg}$, the mass of the drinks is $m_\text{j} = 0.50\,\text{kg}$ and their drop in temperature is $\Delta T_\text{j} = 20\,\text{K}$. For the walls $UA = 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m}^2$ and the temperature difference from outside to inside is $\Delta T_\text{u} = 28\,\text{K}$. From the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$. You are asked for the time $t$ after which the ice has run out.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Energy is conserved: the energy $s\,m_\text{ice}$ needed to melt the ice comes from two sources, the cooling drinks and the heat coming through the wall. While there is ice, the inside stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the temperature difference $\Delta T_\text{u}$ and the power through the wall $P = UA\Delta T_\text{u}$ stay constant, and the energy from the wall is $P\,t$. The ice melts but stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so melting takes $s\,m_\text{ice}$ and nothing goes into raising the temperature.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The cooling drinks give part of the energy $s\,m_\text{ice}$ needed to melt the ice, $c\,m_\text{j}\Delta T_\text{j}$. The rest comes through the wall, $UA\Delta T_\text{u}\,t$:

      $$\begin{aligned} UA\Delta T_\text{u}\,t &= s\,m_\text{ice} - c\,m_\text{j}\Delta T_\text{j} \\ t &= \frac{s\,m_\text{ice} - c\,m_\text{j}\Delta T_\text{j}}{UA\Delta T_\text{u}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Changes in temperature are the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius: the drinks cool by $20\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 20\,\text{K}$ and the difference across the wall is $28\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 28\,\text{K}$. The latent heat of fusion is

      $s$$= 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
      $= 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

      so that the energies are in joules and the power in watts. Finally the time is converted from seconds to hours: $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the energy given out by the drinks and the power through the wall:

      $$\begin{aligned} &c\,m_\text{j}\Delta T_\text{j} \\ \ &= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 20\,\text{K} \\ \ &= 41\,900\,\text{J} \\[6pt] P &= UA\Delta T_\text{u} \\ \ &= 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 28\,\text{K} \\ \ &= 33.6\,\text{W} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. Melting the ice takes $s\,m_\text{ice} = 334\,000\,\text{J}$. Substitute into the formula:

      $$\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{s\,m_\text{ice} - c\,m_\text{j}\Delta T_\text{j}}{UA\Delta T_\text{u}} \\ &= \frac{334\,000\,\text{J} - 41\,900\,\text{J}}{33.6\,\text{W}} \\ &= \frac{292\,100\,\text{J}}{33.6\,\text{W}} \\ &\approx 8.7 \cdot 10^3\,\text{s} \\ &\approx 2.4\,\text{h} \end{aligned}$$

      The ice lasts about $2.4\,\text{h}$. The result is given with two significant figures, because all the initial values have two figures: the mass of the ice $1.0\,\text{kg}$, the mass of the drinks $0.50\,\text{kg}$, their drop in temperature $20\,\text{K}$, the U-value $1.5$, the area $0.80\,\text{m}^2$ and the temperature difference $28\,\text{K}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Without the warm drinks the ice would last

      $\tfrac{334\,000\,\text{J}}{33.6\,\text{W}}$$\approx 9940\,\text{s}$
      $\approx 2.8\,\text{h}$

      so the result is moderately shorter. Cooling the drinks melts a share

      $\tfrac{41\,900\,\text{J}}{334\,000\,\text{J}}$$\approx 0.13$
      $= 13\,\%$

      of the ice, and the time becomes shorter by the same share: $2.8\,\text{h} \cdot 0.87 \approx 2.4\,\text{h}$. When the ice has melted, the bag contains $1.5\,\text{kg}$ of water with a heat capacity of $4190 \cdot 1.5 = 6285\,\text{J/K}$. At first the power raises its temperature by

      $\tfrac{33.6\,\text{W}}{6285\,\text{J/K}} \cdot 3600\,\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{h}}$
      $\approx 19\,\tfrac{\text{K}}{\text{h}}$

          where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta T$$= 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 80\,\text{K}$

          the same in kelvins.

          $r$$= 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

          and finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $Q_\text{heating}$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg} \cdot 80\,\text{K}$
          $= 83\,800\,\text{J}$

          and

          $Q_\text{vaporisation}$
          $= 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg}$
          $= 565\,000\,\text{J}$

          so

          $Q$$= 648\,800\,\text{J}$
          $= 648.8\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $650\,\text{kJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $0.25\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          Vaporisation takes $\tfrac{565}{648.8} \approx 87\,\%$ of the total energy, even though the temperature does not change during it. Estimate: $0.25\,\text{kg}$ needs about $2.6\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ per kilogram, so about $650\,\text{kJ}$. If vaporisation were forgotten, the result would be only $84\,\text{kJ}$.

        3. An engine transfers a power of $24\,\text{kW}$ to the coolant ($c = 3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$). The liquid may warm by at most $8.0\,\text{K}$ in the engine.

          What mass of liquid has to be circulated through the engine every second? Give your answer in kg/s.

          kg/s
          Answer

          $0.86\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $P = 24\,\text{kW}$, $c = 3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, greatest allowed rise in temperature $\Delta T = 8.0\,\text{K}$

          Find

          mass of liquid to be circulated per second $\tfrac{m}{t}$ in $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$

          Principle

          All the power transferred from the engine goes into warming the liquid, and the liquid does not boil, so its state does not change. The mass $\tfrac{m}{t}$ passing per second absorbs the quantity of heat $c\,\tfrac{m}{t}\,\Delta T$, and this is equal to the power $P$.

          Formula

          $P = c\,\dfrac{m}{t}\,\Delta T$, so $\dfrac{m}{t} = \dfrac{P}{c\,\Delta T}$

          Conversions

          $24\,\text{kW}$$= 24\,000\,\text{W}$
          $= 24\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}$

          The change in temperature is already in kelvins.

          Substitution

          $\dfrac{m}{t}$$= \dfrac{24\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}}{3500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 8.0\,\text{K}}$
          $= \dfrac{24\,000}{28\,000}\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$
          $= 0.857\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$

          Answer

          $0.86\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$

          Two significant figures like the values $24\,\text{kW}$ and $8.0\,\text{K}$.

          Reasonableness

          Check: $3500 \cdot 0.857 \cdot 8.0\,\text{W} = 24\,\text{kW}$. With pure water ($c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$) about $0.72\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$ would be enough, so a liquid containing glycol, with a smaller $c$, needs a mass flow about a fifth larger. The direction is right: the worse the liquid stores energy, the more of it has to be circulated.

        4. To make a cold drink, $0.33\,\text{kg}$ of lemonade at $22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, with a specific heat capacity equal to that of water, is poured into a glass. Enough ice is added to the glass.

          How many grams of ice melt when the lemonade cools to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$? Assume that no heat is transferred from the surroundings.

          g
          Answer

          $91\,\text{g}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          lemonade $m_\text{j} = 0.33\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ (the value for water); from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          mass of melted ice $m_\text{ice}$ in grams

          Principle

          Energy is conserved: when no heat comes in from the surroundings, the energy that the lemonade gives out as it cools is all used to melt ice. There is enough ice, so the temperature settles at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the melted ice does not warm up.

          Formula

          $c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T = s\,m_\text{ice}$, so

          $m_\text{ice}$$= \dfrac{c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T}{s}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta T$$= 22\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 22\,\text{K}$

          the same in kelvins. $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$, and finally $1\,\text{kg} = 1000\,\text{g}$.

          Substitution

          $m_\text{ice}$$= \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.33\,\text{kg} \cdot 22\,\text{K}}{334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}}$
          $= \dfrac{30\,419.4\,\text{J}}{334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}}$
          $= 0.0911\,\text{kg}$
          $= 91.1\,\text{g}$

          Answer

          $91\,\text{g}$

          Two significant figures like the values $0.33\,\text{kg}$ and $22\,\text{K}$.

          Reasonableness

          The mass of melted ice is $\tfrac{91}{330} \approx 28\,\%$ of the mass of the lemonade, because melting needs the energy of heating by about 80 kelvins per kilogram: $\tfrac{22}{80} \approx 0.28$. The mass is positive and smaller than that of the lemonade, as it should be.

        1. A sauna stove contains $25\,\text{kg}$ of stones ($c = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$). $0.20\,\text{kg}$ of water at 20 °C is thrown on the stones, and it warms to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and vaporises completely. Assume that all the energy needed is taken from the stones and that the warming of the steam is not taken into account.

          By how many kelvins does the temperature of the stones drop?

          K
          Answer

          $26\,\text{K}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          stones $m_\text{k} = 25\,\text{kg}$, $c_\text{k} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; water $m_\text{v} = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, warms to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and vaporises; from the table $c_\text{v} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          drop in the temperature of the stones $\Delta T_\text{k}$ in kelvins

          Principle

          Energy is conserved: when all the energy is taken from the stones, the energy given out by the stones is equal to the energy absorbed by the water. The water absorbs energy in two stages (warming up and vaporisation), and the stones only cool down, so their state does not change. The warming of the steam and the heating of the stove are not taken into account.

          Formula

          $c_\text{k}\,m_\text{k}\,\Delta T_\text{k}$$= m_\text{v}(c_\text{v}\,\Delta T_\text{v} + r)$

          so

          $\Delta T_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{m_\text{v}(c_\text{v}\,\Delta T_\text{v} + r)}{c_\text{k}\,m_\text{k}}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta T_\text{v}$$= 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
          $= 80\,\text{K}$

          the same in kelvins.

          $r$$= 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

          Substitution

          $c_\text{v}\,\Delta T_\text{v}$$= 4190 \cdot 80\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 335\,200\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
          $Q_\text{v}$$= 0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot (335\,200 + 2\,260\,000)\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 519\,040\,\text{J}$

          and

          $\Delta T_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{519\,040\,\text{J}}{800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 25.95\,\text{K}$

          Answer

          $26\,\text{K}$

          Two significant figures like the masses $0.20\,\text{kg}$ and $25\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          Vaporisation takes $452\,\text{kJ}$, which is $87\,\%$ of the energy, and warming alone would lower the temperature of the stones by only about $3.4\,\text{K}$. The drop in the stones is small compared with their temperature of $300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, because there are a lot of stones. Warming the steam would make the drop slightly larger, and a stove that keeps heating the stones would make it smaller; $26\,\text{K}$ is the drop without heating by the stove.

        2. $0.500\,\text{kg}$ of ice at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $2.00\,\text{kg}$ of water at $30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are mixed in an insulated container.

          What is the temperature of the mixture when thermal equilibrium has been reached? First check whether all the ice melts.

          °C
          Answer

          $8.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          ice $m_\text{ice} = 0.500\,\text{kg}$, $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; water $m_\text{v} = 2.00\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{v} = 30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; the container is insulated; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          final temperature of the mixture $T$ in degrees Celsius

          Principle

          Energy is conserved, because the container is insulated: the energy given out by the water melts the ice and warms the melted water to the final temperature. This works only if all the ice melts. First check: as it cools to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ the water can give out at most $c\,m_\text{v}\,T_\text{v} = 251\,400\,\text{J}$, and melting the ice needs only $s\,m_\text{ice} = 167\,000\,\text{J}$. Since $251\,400\,\text{J} > 167\,000\,\text{J}$, all the ice melts and the final temperature is above $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          Formula

          $c\,m_\text{v}(T_\text{v} - T) = s\,m_\text{ice} + c\,m_\text{ice}\,T$, so

          $T$$= \dfrac{c\,m_\text{v}\,T_\text{v} - s\,m_\text{ice}}{c\,(m_\text{v} + m_\text{ice})}$

          Conversions

          The ice is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the change in temperature of the melted water is $T - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = T$ and the water cools by $T_\text{v} - T$; the differences are the same in kelvins, and $T$ comes directly in degrees Celsius. $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$.

          Substitution

          The numerator is

          $4190 \cdot 2.00 \cdot 30.0\,\text{J} - 334\,000 \cdot 0.500\,\text{J}$
          $= 251\,400\,\text{J} - 167\,000\,\text{J}$
          $= 84\,400\,\text{J}$

          and the denominator is

          $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 2.50\,\text{kg}$
          $= 10\,475\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}$

          so

          $$T = \dfrac{84\,400\,\text{J}}{10\,475\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}} = 8.057\,{}^\circ\text{C}$$

          Answer

          $8.1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

          Two significant figures. The initial values $0.500\,\text{kg}$, $2.00\,\text{kg}$ and $30.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ have three figures, but the subtraction in the numerator $251\,400 - 167\,000 = 84\,400\,\text{J}$ loses one figure, so two figures are left in the result.

          Reasonableness

          The result is between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and closer to the temperature of the ice, because melting also cools the water. Energy balance: the water gives out $4190 \cdot 2.00 \cdot (30.0 - 8.057)\,\text{J} = 183\,880\,\text{J}$, and the ice takes in

          $167\,000\,\text{J} + 4190 \cdot 0.500 \cdot 8.057\,\text{J}$
          $= 183\,880\,\text{J}$

          If the energy of the water had been less than $167\,000\,\text{J}$, the result would be $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and some ice would be left.

        3. The total area of the walls of a cool bag is $0.50\,\text{m}^2$ and the U-value is $1.5\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K}}$. $0.50\,\text{kg}$ of drinks at $24\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, with a specific heat capacity equal to that of water, $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, is packed into the bag, along with an ice pack taken from the freezer at $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($c_\text{ice} = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$). It is $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ outside. The ice must last for 6.0 hours. Assume that the drinks cool to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at once, that the ice first warms to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and then melts, and that the same power comes through the wall all the time as if the inside were at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

          What is the smallest mass the ice pack must have? Give your answer in kilograms.

          kg
          Answer

          $1.5\,\text{kg}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $U = 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}}$, $A = 0.50\,\text{m}^2$, outside $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and inside $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so $\Delta T = 30\,\text{K}$, $t = 6.0\,\text{h}$; drinks $m_\text{j} = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, $\Delta T_\text{j} = 24\,\text{K}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; ice $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C} \to 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c_\text{ice} = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          smallest mass $m$ of the ice pack in kilograms

          Principle

          Energy is conserved: during the 6.0 hours the ice has to absorb the energy coming through the wall and the energy given out by the drinks. The ice first warms from $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice}$) and then melts ($s$). By the assumption of the problem the inside is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the power through the wall stays constant.

          Formula

          $m\,(c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice} + s)$
          $= U A\,\Delta T\,t + c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T_\text{j}$

          so

          $m$$= \dfrac{U A\,\Delta T\,t + c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T_\text{j}}{c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice} + s}$

          Conversions

          $t = 6.0\,\text{h} = 21\,600\,\text{s}$ and $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$. The change in the temperature of the ice is

          $\Delta T_\text{ice}$$= 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-10\,{}^\circ\text{C})$
          $= 10\,\text{K}$

          the same in kelvins.

          Substitution

          Energy from the wall:

          $U A\,\Delta T\,t$$= 1.5 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 30 \cdot 21\,600\,\text{J}$
          $= 486\,000\,\text{J}$

          Energy from the drinks:

          $c\,m_\text{j}\,\Delta T_\text{j}$$= 4190 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 24\,\text{J}$
          $= 50\,280\,\text{J}$

          Energy taken by the ice per kilogram:

          $c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T_\text{ice} + s$
          $= 21\,000 + 334\,000$
          $= 355\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

          Smallest mass:

          $m$$= \dfrac{486\,000\,\text{J} + 50\,280\,\text{J}}{355\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}}$
          $= 1.511\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $1.5\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures, because the U-value $1.5$, the area $0.50\,\text{m}^2$, the mass of the drinks $0.50\,\text{kg}$ and the time $6.0\,\text{h}$ all have two figures.

          Reasonableness

          The wall brings in $486\,\text{kJ}$ and the drinks $50\,\text{kJ}$, about $536\,\text{kJ}$ in total. If the ice were already at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, we would need $\tfrac{536\,\text{kJ}}{334\,\text{kJ/kg}} \approx 1.6\,\text{kg}$. Warming freezer ice takes about $\tfrac{21}{334} \approx 6\,\%$ more energy per kilogram, so the mass needed is slightly smaller, $1.51\,\text{kg}$, which with two figures gives the smallest suitable mass $1.5\,\text{kg}$.
