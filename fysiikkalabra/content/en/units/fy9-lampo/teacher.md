## Lesson plan (75 min)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** Snow into drinking water over a campfire. In pairs, students estimate in words which takes more energy: melting a kilogram of snow or heating the same water to boiling. The answer is not given yet: it is in the Formula part of the theory summary ($Q = sm = 334\,\text{kJ}$ and $Q = cm\Delta T = 419\,\text{kJ}$, warming the snow to zero degrees only about $21\,\text{kJ}$).
- **5–15 min, Predict (starting-point test).** Six questions alone, without notes. Students write the numbers of the questions they got wrong in their notebooks.
- **15–25 min, Investigate.** The heating curve of a block of ice in pairs: each reads the energy and temperatures of one stage from the curve, and the pair calculates the specific heat capacity of ice. Discuss together: why the curve has a horizontal part and why the ice part is steeper than the water part.
- **25–35 min, Theory summary.** Go through the concept map and the heating curve. For each branch, ask the students for one everyday example. Return to Think about it.
- **35–55 min, Apply.** Four multiple-choice questions in pairs (about $6\,\text{min}$), then four calculations alone. Students who finish quickly continue without hints, others use the hints one at a time.
- **55–65 min, Check your understanding and exit ticket.** Alone. The explanation question is self-assessed with the criteria.
- **65–75 min, Tasks.** Ten minutes is enough for the beginning of one long problem in exam conditions, without the model answer. Recommendation: *New ice for the ice rink* a (calculation) or *Brakes on a fell road* a–b (combining modules). The rest of the problem and the other five problems are homework, two or three at a time before the next lesson. *Vaporisation of boiling water* ($20$ points) is done at home on graph paper or with a spreadsheet.

## Using the starting-point test
The Predict questions are a diagnostic test, not a grade. Each question matches one chapter:

- Question 1: a temperature difference in kelvins (chapter *Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature*).
- Question 2: temperature or internal energy (chapter *Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy*).
- Question 3: specific heat capacity (chapter *Heating and cooling of matter*).
- Question 4: boiling at constant temperature (chapter *Changes of state*).
- Question 5: a mixture of ice and water (chapter *Thermal balance during a change of state*).
- Question 6: thermal expansion (chapter *Thermal expansion*).

If more than a third of the group answers questions 4 or 5 wrong, go through one balance problem on the board in which it is first checked whether the warm water is enough to melt all the ice. This is the most common stumbling block in the matriculation exam. Errors in question 2 are usually corrected in the Investigate task and in the claim questions of the Apply part.

## Pair work
- Investigate: one reads from the curve and says the readings aloud, the other calculates. The roles change at every stage.
- Apply multiple-choice questions: each first chooses alone and only then justifies the choice to the partner. The pair answers only when they agree.
- Long problem *A lake in winter* (written): pairs write one part together and assess it with another pair using the marking scheme.

## Fast and slow students
- **Fast students** do the Apply calculations without hints and go straight to the long problem *Heat of fusion of ice with a calorimeter*, in which a measurement report is assessed and a better experiment is planned. Extra question: how large a heat capacity of the cup would explain the difference that remains once the initial temperature of the ice has been taken into account?
- **Slower students** do the Apply calculations *Bath water* and *Ice for the drinks* with the hints and leave *Power cut in the frost* for home. The *Review if needed* links of the theory summary lead straight to the original chapters, which have basic-level exercises.

## Assessment
The long problems are self-assessed with the marking scheme. If the teacher assesses them, the same criteria work. In a calculation part the justification of the principle, the symbolic solution, the unit conversions by substitution, the precision and the reasonableness are assessed. In a graph part the axes, the points, the fit and reading from the line, not from the measured points, are assessed. All the data are invented examples, not measurements.
