## fy3-7-L1-01
**Given:** ice, $m = 1.20\,\text{kg}$, temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ needed to melt the ice, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The ice is already at the melting temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and turns into water at 0 °C. The temperature does not change, so $Q = cm\Delta T$ does not apply. Instead we need the latent heat of fusion: the quantity of heat is proportional to the mass that melts.
**Formula:** $Q = sm$, and the quantity we want, $Q$, is already alone on the left-hand side: $Q = sm$
**Conversions:** None needed: the mass is in kilograms and $s$ is in kilojoules per kilogram, so the result comes out directly in kilojoules.
**Substitution:** $Q = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 1.20\,\text{kg} = 400.8\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $401\,\text{kJ}$, three significant figures like the mass $1.20\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Melting one kilogram takes $334\,\text{kJ}$, so melting a little over a kilogram takes a little over $334\,\text{kJ}$: the estimate is $334\,\text{kJ} \cdot 1.2 \approx 400\,\text{kJ}$. $Q$ is positive because the ice absorbs energy. The meltwater does not warm up in the calculation, because the temperature stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

## fy3-7-L1-02
**Given:** boiling water from a pan, $m = 0.40\,\text{kg}$; from the table $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ used for vaporisation, in megajoules
**Principle:** The water is already boiling and turns into steam at a constant temperature of $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The temperature does not change, so we do not use $c m \Delta T$ but the latent heat of vaporisation: the quantity of heat is proportional to the mass that vaporises.
**Formula:** $Q = rm$, and $Q$ is already solved for: $Q = rm$
**Conversions:** We first get the result in kilojoules: $r m = 904\,\text{kJ}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 1000\,\text{kJ}$, so $904\,\text{kJ} = 0.904\,\text{MJ}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.40\,\text{kg} = 904\,\text{kJ} = 0.904\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $0.90\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.40\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $2300\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.4\,\text{kg} \approx 920\,\text{kJ}$. Vaporising takes a lot of energy: the same $0.90\,\text{MJ}$ would heat $\dfrac{904\,\text{kJ}}{4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 80\,\text{K}} \approx 2.7\,\text{kg}$ of water from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. That is why a pan with a lid saves energy.

## fy3-7-L1-03
**Given:** water freezing at the surface of a lake, $m = 45\,\text{kg}$ for every square metre, temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ released in freezing, in megajoules
**Principle:** The water freezes into ice at 0 °C at a constant temperature. When a substance freezes, it releases the same quantity of heat that the same mass would absorb when melting, so we use the latent heat of fusion $s$ and not a temperature change.
**Formula:** $Q = sm$ (the quantity of heat released is given as a positive number), and $Q$ is already solved for: $Q = sm$
**Conversions:** The result comes out in kilojoules: $15\,030\,\text{kJ}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 1000\,\text{kJ}$, so $15\,030\,\text{kJ} = 15.03\,\text{MJ}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 45\,\text{kg} = 15\,030\,\text{kJ} = 15.03\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $15\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $45\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $330\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 45\,\text{kg} \approx 15\,000\,\text{kJ}$. The thickness of the layer of water is $\dfrac{45\,\text{kg}}{1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 1\,\text{m}^2} = 4.5\,\text{cm}$, which fits a layer that freezes during a week of frost. The heat released slows down the freezing, so a lake does not freeze in an instant.

## fy3-7-L1-04
**Given:** steam that condenses into $100$-degree water, $m = 0.030\,\text{kg}$; from the table $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ given out by the steam, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The steam condenses into water at the same temperature of $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the temperature does not change. When steam condenses, it releases the same quantity of heat that would be needed to vaporise it, so we use the latent heat of vaporisation $r$.
**Formula:** $Q = rm$ (the quantity of heat released is given as a positive number), and $Q$ is already solved for: $Q = rm$
**Conversions:** None needed: the mass is in kilograms and $r$ is in kilojoules per kilogram, so the result comes out in kilojoules.
**Substitution:** $Q = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.030\,\text{kg} = 67.8\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $68\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.030\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $2300\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.03\,\text{kg} \approx 69\,\text{kJ}$. This quantity of heat would warm $0.3\,\text{kg}$ of milk by about $\dfrac{67.8\,\text{kJ}}{4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.3\,\text{kg}} \approx 54\,\text{K}$, so a small amount of steam warms a whole jug of milk. For the same reason steam burns skin worse than water that is just as hot.

## fy3-7-L1-05
**Given:** mass of the ice $m = 0.60\,\text{kg}$; the temperature as a function of the energy transferred in the graph
**Find:** the specific latent heat of fusion of ice $s$
**Principle:** On the horizontal part of the graph the temperature stays constant at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ even though energy is transferred: the ice melts, and all the energy transferred is used for melting, $Q = sm$. The energy used for melting is the length of the horizontal part on the horizontal axis.
**Reading the graph:** The horizontal part starts at the grid crossing $(20\,\text{kJ};\ 0\,{}^\circ\text{C})$ and ends at the crossing $(220\,\text{kJ};\ 0\,{}^\circ\text{C})$. So the energy used for melting is $Q = 220\,\text{kJ} - 20\,\text{kJ} = 200\,\text{kJ}$.
**Formula:** $Q = sm$, from which $s = \dfrac{Q}{m}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the energy is read in kilojoules, and the answer is requested in the unit $\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$.
**Substitution:** $s = \dfrac{200\,\text{kJ}}{0.60\,\text{kg}} = 333\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Answer:** $333\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$, three significant figures. The values are read exactly from the grid, so $323\ldots343\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$ is accepted.
**Reasonableness:** The result is close to the table value $334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$. Melting takes $200\,\text{kJ}$, whereas warming the ice before melting took only $20\,\text{kJ}$, so the horizontal part is long, and the temperature rises more gently after melting because the specific heat capacity of water is greater than that of ice.

## fy3-7-L2-01
**Given:** $P = 2.0\,\text{kW}$, mass vaporised from boiling water $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, all the energy from the hotplate goes into the water; from the table $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the time $t$ in minutes
**Principle:** The water is already boiling, so its temperature does not rise and all the energy $Pt$ from the hotplate is used for vaporisation, $Q = rm$. Energy is conserved when there are no losses, so $Pt = rm$.
**Formula:** $Pt = rm$, from which $t = \dfrac{rm}{P}$
**Conversions:** $P = 2.0\,\text{kW} = 2000\,\text{W}$ and $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}} = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$. The time comes out in seconds and is finally converted to minutes: $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg}}{2000\,\text{W}} = 565\,\text{s} = \dfrac{565}{60}\,\text{min} = 9.42\,\text{min}$
**Answer:** $9.4\,\text{min}$, two significant figures like the power $2.0\,\text{kW}$ and the mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: about $1.1\,\text{MJ}$ of energy at a power of $2\,\text{kW}$ takes about $550\,\text{s}$, which is just under $10\,\text{min}$. Half a kilogram boiling away in about ten minutes matches experience. Units: $\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{W}} = \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{J/s}} = \text{s}$.

## fy3-7-L2-02
**Given:** ice, $m = 750\,\text{g}$, $T_1 = -18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, ending as water at 0 °C; from the table $c_\text{ice} = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the total quantity of heat $Q$ in kilojoules
**Principle:** The process has two stages. First the ice warms from $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to the melting temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($Q_1 = cm\Delta T$), and then it melts at a constant temperature ($Q_2 = sm$). The total quantity of heat is the sum of these.
**Formula:** $Q = Q_1 + Q_2$, and $Q = c_\text{ice} m \Delta T + sm$, where $\Delta T = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - T_1$
**Conversions:** $m = 750\,\text{g} = 0.750\,\text{kg}$. The temperature change is the same in kelvins: $\Delta T = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}) = 18\,\text{K}$. $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$ and finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q_1 = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.750\,\text{kg} \cdot 18\,\text{K} = 28\,350\,\text{J}$ and $Q_2 = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.750\,\text{kg} = 250\,500\,\text{J}$, so $Q = 28\,350\,\text{J} + 250\,500\,\text{J} = 278\,850\,\text{J} = 278.85\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $280\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the temperature $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: melting takes $0.75 \cdot 334 \approx 250\,\text{kJ}$ and warming less than $30\,\text{kJ}$, about $280\,\text{kJ}$ in total. Melting takes $\dfrac{250.5}{278.85} \approx 90\,\%$ of the energy, even though the temperature rises only in the heating stage. If you did not convert the grams to kilograms, the result would be a thousand times too large.

**Principle:** The ice is already at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so it does not warm up and all the energy is used for melting. So the quantity of heat needed for melting is $Q = sm$, and we get the mass that melts by solving for $m$.
**Formula:** $Q = sm$, from which $m = \dfrac{Q}{s}$

The thickness of the layer is $\dfrac{35.93\,\text{kg}}{920\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 1\,\text{m}^2} \approx 3.9\,\text{cm}$, so a sunny day thins the ice by a few centimetres, which fits observations in spring. If you mixed up $\text{MJ}$ and $\text{kJ}$, the result would be a thousand times wrong.

## fy3-7-L2-04
**Given:** water $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_2 = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, part that vaporises $m_\text{v} = 0.10\,\text{kg}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the total quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the water, in megajoules
**Principle:** The process has two stages. First all the water warms to the boiling point ($Q_1 = cm\Delta T$), and then only the part that vaporises turns into steam at a constant temperature ($Q_2 = rm_\text{v}$). The total quantity of heat is the sum of these.
**Formula:** $Q = Q_1 + Q_2$, and $Q = cm(T_2 - T_1) + rm_\text{v}$
**Conversions:** The temperature change is the same in kelvins: $T_2 - T_1 = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 15\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 85\,\text{K}$. $r = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$ and finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q_1 = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 85\,\text{K} = 712\,300\,\text{J}$ and $Q_2 = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.10\,\text{kg} = 226\,000\,\text{J}$, so $Q = 938\,300\,\text{J} = 0.9383\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $0.94\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures like the masses $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and $0.10\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Estimate: $4\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 2\,\text{kg} \cdot 85\,\text{K} \approx 680\,\text{kJ}$ and $2300 \cdot 0.1 = 230\,\text{kJ}$, about $0.9\,\text{MJ}$ in total. The mass that vaporises is only $0.10\,\text{kg}$, not the whole $2.0\,\text{kg}$. Even so, that small amount takes $\dfrac{226}{938.3} \approx 24\,\%$ of the energy, because $r$ is large.

## fy3-7-L3-01
**Given:** juice (the $c$ of water) $m_1 = 0.35\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; ice $m_2 = 60\,\text{g}$, $T_0 = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; no heat losses; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $s = 334\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the final temperature $T$ when all the ice has melted
**Principle:** Energy is conserved when no heat passes to the glass or the air: the quantity of heat the juice gives out equals the quantity used to melt the ice and warm the meltwater. The ice melts completely if the juice has enough energy: cooling to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ it would give out $36\,662.5\,\text{J}$, but melting needs only $20\,040\,\text{J}$.
**Formula:** $cm_1(T_1 - T) = sm_2 + cm_2(T - T_0)$, from which $T = \dfrac{cm_1T_1 + cm_2T_0 - sm_2}{c(m_1 + m_2)}$
**Conversions:** $m_2 = 60\,\text{g} = 0.060\,\text{kg}$ and $s = 334\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$. Since $T_0 = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the term $cm_2T_0$ is zero, and the temperatures can be substituted in degrees Celsius because the formula contains temperature differences.
**Substitution:** The numerator is $4190 \cdot 0.35 \cdot 25 - 334\,000 \cdot 0.060 = 36\,662.5 - 20\,040 = 16\,622.5$ and the denominator is $4190 \cdot 0.41 = 1717.9$, so

$$T = \dfrac{16\,622.5}{1717.9}\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 9.68\,{}^\circ\text{C}$$

**Answer:** $9.7\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.35\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The result is between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. If the $60\,\text{g}$ were already water at $0$ degrees, the final temperature would be $\dfrac{0.35 \cdot 25}{0.41}\,{}^\circ\text{C} \approx 21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Ice cools much more, because melting absorbs $20\,040\,\text{J}$ of heat without any change of temperature: this is why the drink cools by over $11\,\text{K}$ more.

## fy3-7-L3-02
**Given:** water $m = 0.25\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, warms to $T_2 = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and vaporises completely; stones $m_\text{s} = 30\,\text{kg}$, $c_\text{s} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Find:** the drop in temperature $\Delta T$ of the stones, in kelvins
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: all the energy the water needs comes from the stones. The water first needs $cm(T_2 - T_1)$ to warm up and then $rm$ to vaporise, during which the temperature does not change. The quantity of heat the stones give out is $c_\text{s}m_\text{s}\Delta T$.
**Formula:** $c_\text{s}m_\text{s}\Delta T = cm(T_2 - T_1) + rm$, from which $\Delta T = \dfrac{cm(T_2 - T_1) + rm}{c_\text{s}m_\text{s}}$
**Conversions:** $T_2 - T_1 = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. $r = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$.
**Substitution:** $Q_1 = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg} \cdot 80\,\text{K} = 83\,800\,\text{J}$ and $Q_2 = 2\,260\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg} = 565\,000\,\text{J}$, so $\Delta T = \dfrac{648\,800\,\text{J}}{800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 30\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{648\,800\,\text{J}}{24\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}} = 27.03\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $27\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.25\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Vaporisation takes $\dfrac{565}{648.8} \approx 87\,\%$ of the energy and warming only $13\,\%$, even though the temperature rises by $80\,\text{K}$: vaporising one kilogram takes the same energy as warming it by about $\dfrac{2260}{4.19} \approx 540\,\text{K}$. The heat capacity of the stones is $24\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}$, so one ladleful cools the stones by a few tens of kelvins, which fits the experience of a sauna.

## fy3-7-L3-03
**Given:** $P_1 = 800\,\text{W}$, $\Delta m_1 = 196\,\text{g}$; $P_2 = 400\,\text{W}$, $\Delta m_2 = 90.0\,\text{g}$; in both $t = 10.0\,\text{min}$; the water is at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the power $P_\text{l}$ passing to the air is the same
**Find:** the specific latent heat of vaporisation $r$ in $\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Principle:** The water boils all the time, so its temperature does not change and the energy that stays in the water is used for vaporisation: $(P - P_\text{l})t = r\Delta m$. There are two equations and two unknowns ($r$ and $P_\text{l}$), so they can be solved together.
**Formula:** $(P_1 - P_\text{l})t = r\Delta m_1$ and $(P_2 - P_\text{l})t = r\Delta m_2$. Subtract the equations: $(P_1 - P_2)t = r(\Delta m_1 - \Delta m_2)$, from which $r = \dfrac{(P_1 - P_2)t}{\Delta m_1 - \Delta m_2}$
**Conversions:** $t = 10.0\,\text{min} = 600\,\text{s}$, $\Delta m_1 = 0.196\,\text{kg}$ and $\Delta m_2 = 0.0900\,\text{kg}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $r = \dfrac{(800\,\text{W} - 400\,\text{W}) \cdot 600\,\text{s}}{0.196\,\text{kg} - 0.0900\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{240\,000\,\text{J}}{0.106\,\text{kg}} = 2\,264\,151\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} = 2.264\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
**Answer:** $2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, three significant figures: the difference $196\,\text{g} - 90.0\,\text{g} = 106\,\text{g}$ is accurate to the nearest gram and has three figures.
**Reasonableness:** The energy passing to the air, $P_\text{l}t$, is the same in both measurements and cancels in the difference. The individual measurements would give $\dfrac{800\,\text{W} \cdot 600\,\text{s}}{0.196\,\text{kg}} \approx 2.45\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ and $\dfrac{400\,\text{W} \cdot 600\,\text{s}}{0.0900\,\text{kg}} \approx 2.67\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, both too large, because the energy passing to the air would also be counted as vaporisation. The loss power is $800\,\text{W} - 2.264 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot \dfrac{0.196\,\text{kg}}{600\,\text{s}} \approx 60\,\text{W}$, and the result agrees with the table value $2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.
