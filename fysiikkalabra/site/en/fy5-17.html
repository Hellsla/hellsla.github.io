Applied chapter: ultrasound imaging and earthquake waves

Why can an ultrasound machine draw a picture of the inside of the body using nothing but echoes, and why is gel spread under the probe first?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the depth of a boundary from the travel time of an echo and read the echo curve of an ultrasound machine.
  • explain how the frequency of ultrasound affects the sharpness of the image and the imaging depth.
  • determine the distance of an earthquake from the difference in arrival times of the P wave and the S wave.

Prior knowledge

  • The speed, frequency and wavelength of a wave are related by the equation $v = f\lambda$, and a wave can be transverse or longitudinalchapter How waves form and travel
  • A wave is partly reflected from the boundary of two substances, and an obstacle bends a wave more the greater the wavelength is compared with the obstaclechapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap
  • Sound is a longitudinal wave, and a person hears frequencies from about $20\,\text{Hz}$ to $20\,\text{kHz}$chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect

1Think about it

In the examination room a patient lies on their back. A nurse spreads cold, translucent gel on the skin of the abdomen and presses a probe the size of a palm on top of it. A grey fan appears on the screen, showing the edges of organs. The probe sends out neither light nor X-rays. It sends sound that the ear cannot hear, and listens for echoes.

How can the depth of a structure in the body be worked out from echoes alone, and what is the gel needed for?

Review: wavelength

A bat sends out a call with a frequency of $40\,\text{kHz}$. Sound travels in air at a speed of $343\,\text{m/s}$. What is the wavelength of the call?

  • Correct. From the equation $v = f\lambda$ we get $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f} = \tfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{40\,000\,\text{Hz}} \approx 0.0086\,\text{m}$ (chapter How waves form and travel). The wavelength is of the same order of magnitude as the size of an insect, and this explains why bats use ultrasound.

  • You divided the frequency by the speed, and the unit of the result is then $1/\text{m}$, not metres. The wavelength is $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$: the speed divided by the frequency (chapter How waves form and travel).

  • You multiplied the speed by the frequency. In the equation $v = f\lambda$ the wavelength is the speed divided by the frequency, $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$. A high frequency means a short wavelength, not a long one (chapter How waves form and travel).

4Theory

Key idea

An echo tells the depth

Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above $20\,\text{kHz}$, that is, too high for a person to hear. In medical imaging frequencies of about $2\text{–}15\,\text{MHz}$ are used. The probe sends a short ultrasound pulse into the body and then listens for echoes. When the pulse meets the boundary of two tissues, part of it is reflected back and the rest continues deeper (chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap). So every boundary gives an echo of its own. The machine measures how long it takes for each echo to return. Because the pulse travels to the boundary and back, during the travel time of the echo it covers twice the depth of the boundary. The machine assumes that sound travels in soft tissue at an average speed of $1540\,\text{m/s}$, and calculates the depth of every echo.

When the probe is tilted or it has a row of small transmitters, pulses are sent in many directions. From the depths and strengths of the echoes a two-dimensional greyscale image, the B-scan, is formed. The simplest display is the A-scan, the echo curve: its horizontal axis shows the time from sending the pulse and its vertical axis the strength of the echo. A peak in the echo curve means a boundary, and the position of the peak tells how deep the boundary is.

Why gel is needed

How large a part of the wave is reflected depends on how different the substances at the boundary are as far as the propagation of sound is concerned. Soft tissues resemble each other, so only about one per cent is reflected from their boundaries. Therefore most of the pulse continues deeper and produces echoes also from deeper boundaries. From the boundary of bone and soft tissue about $40\,\%$ is reflected, and bone also strongly attenuates sound, so behind a bone there is a shadow that gives no echoes. From the boundary of air and skin about $99.9\,\%$ is reflected. If even a thin layer of air is left between the probe and the skin, almost the whole pulse is reflected back already at the skin. Gel displaces the air: from the boundary of gel and skin only about $0.2\,\%$ is reflected. The percentages are typical model values for perpendicular incidence: the real fraction depends on the tissues and on the angle at which the wave meets the boundary.

Frequency: a sharp image or an image that reaches deep

The wavelength is obtained from the equation $v = f\lambda$ (chapter How waves form and travel). At the frequency $5\,\text{MHz}$ the wavelength of ultrasound in soft tissue is about $0.3\,\text{mm}$. The smallest details that can be distinguished are roughly a few wavelengths in size, so a high frequency and a short wavelength give a sharper image. On the other hand, tissue turns part of the energy of the sound wave into internal energy, and this attenuation grows approximately in direct proportion to the frequency. A high-frequency pulse therefore weakens quickly, and its echo does not return from deep down strongly enough. Organs deep down, such as the heart and the liver, are imaged with a low frequency, and structures near the surface, such as tendons and the thyroid gland, with a high one.

$f$ $\lambda$ Depth Target
$2.5\,\text{MHz}$ $0.62\,\text{mm}$ $20\,\text{cm}$ heart, abdomen
$5\,\text{MHz}$ $0.31\,\text{mm}$ $10\,\text{cm}$ pregnancy
$10\,\text{MHz}$ $0.15\,\text{mm}$ $5\,\text{cm}$ thyroid gland
$15\,\text{MHz}$ $0.10\,\text{mm}$ $3\,\text{cm}$ tendons, skin

In the table $f$ is the frequency, $\lambda$ the wavelength in tissue and the depth the typical imaging depth. The imaging depths are only indicative: they depend on the machine and the tissue. Ultrasound can also be used to measure the flow speed of blood. The frequency of the echo reflected from moving blood cells is slightly different from that of the transmitted pulse, and from the change in frequency the machine calculates the speed. This is the Doppler effect (chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect).

Echolocation in nature and technology (extra)

Bats and dolphins locate their prey on the same principle. A bat sends out short ultrasonic calls whose frequency, depending on the species, is about $20$–$100\,\text{kHz}$, and works out the distance from the delay of the echoes. At the frequency $50\,\text{kHz}$ the wavelength in air is about $7\,\text{mm}$. From an object much smaller than the wavelength only a very weak echo returns, because the wave bends around it (chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap). That is why bats that hunt insects use high frequencies. The sonar of a ship measures the depth of the water in the same way and detects shoals of fish; sound travels in water at about $1400\text{–}1500\,\text{m/s}$, depending on the temperature.

Earthquake waves

In an earthquake the bedrock breaks, and earthquake waves set out from the point of fracture. Two kinds of waves travel through the interior of the Earth. The P wave is a longitudinal wave in which the rock is compressed and rarefied in the direction of propagation. It is the fastest and arrives at a station first. The S wave is a transverse wave in which the rock moves sideways. It is slower than the P wave. In the upper part of the crust the speed of the P wave is typically about $6\,\text{km/s}$ and that of the S wave about $3.5\,\text{km/s}$. Surface waves, which travel along the surface, are slower still, but their amplitude is the greatest, and they cause most of the damage.

A transverse wave needs a medium that resists a change of shape: when a layer of rock is displaced sideways, the neighbouring layers pull it back. A liquid does not resist a change of shape in the same way, so the S wave does not travel in a liquid. The P wave also travels in a liquid, like sound in water. When earthquakes were detected on different sides of the globe, it was noticed that S waves are not detected on the half opposite the earthquake, when the angular distance, the angle between the earthquake and the station measured at the centre of the Earth, is more than about $103^\circ$. A shadow zone is left for S waves because the outer core of the Earth is liquid.

Tsunami (extra)

An earthquake on the sea floor can suddenly raise or lower a large mass of water, and a tsunami sets out from it. Its wavelength is tens or hundreds of kilometres, much greater than the depth of the ocean. Then the water moves from the bottom all the way to the surface, and the speed of the wave depends only on the depth of the water: in the deep ocean a tsunami travels at the speed of an aeroplane. When the wave comes into shallow water near the shore, it slows down, its wavelength shortens and its energy is concentrated in a smaller amount of water. That is why the height of the wave grows many times over near the coast.

Distance from the travel time of a wave

When a pulse travels to a boundary and back along the same path at a constant speed, the distance of the boundary is

$$d = \frac{v t}{2}$$

where $d$ is the distance of the boundary (m), $v$ the speed of sound in the medium (m/s) and $t$ the travel time of the echo, that is, the time from sending the pulse to the return of the echo (s). In an ultrasound machine $v = 1540\,\text{m/s}$. The formula also holds for the echoes of a bat and a sonar, when the speed is the speed of sound in air or water. An A-scan is read by taking the time of each peak from the time axis and converting it to a depth with this formula. The thickness of a layer between two boundaries is obtained from the time difference of the echoes with the same formula.

A seismograph station does not know when the earthquake happened, but it measures the difference $\Delta t$ in the arrival times of the P wave and the S wave. When the waves cover the same distance $d$ at constant speeds, the S wave falls behind the P wave the more, the longer the distance is:

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta t &= \frac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \frac{d}{v_\text{P}} \\[6pt] d &= \frac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t \end{aligned}$$

where $d$ is the distance of the station from the point where the earthquake started (km), $v_\text{P}$ and $v_\text{S}$ the speeds of the P wave and the S wave (km/s) and $\Delta t$ the difference between the arrival times of the S wave and the P wave (s). When $v_\text{P} = 6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and $v_\text{S} = 3.5\,\text{km/s}$, every second of time difference corresponds to a distance of $8.4\,\text{km}$. When the distance is known from three stations, the location of the earthquake is at the intersection of the circles drawn around them.

When the wavelength is much greater than the depth of the water, the speed of a shallow-water wave, such as a tsunami, is

$$v = \sqrt{g h}$$

where $v$ is the speed of the wave (m/s), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ the acceleration of free fall and $h$ the depth of the water (m). The equation is given here without derivation. At a depth of four kilometres the speed is about $198\,\text{m/s}$, or about $710\,\text{km/h}$, but in shallow water $10\,\text{m}$ deep only about $9.9\,\text{m/s}$.

Sources of the values

The speed of sound $1540\,\text{m/s}$ is the standard assumption of ultrasound machines for soft tissue (for example Hoskins, Martin and Thrush: Diagnostic Ultrasound, 3rd edition, 2019). The speeds of waves in the crust are approximations from the Earth speed model IASP91 (Kennett and Engdahl 1991), in which the values for the uppermost crust are $5.8\,\text{km/s}$ and $3.36\,\text{km/s}$. The percentages of the reflected fractions have been calculated from the typical acoustic properties of the tissues, and they are approximations.

Common misconception

The most common calculation error is to forget that the echo travels there and back. If an ultrasound echo returns after $65\,\mu\text{s}$, the product $v t$ is $10\,\text{cm}$, but the boundary is only at a depth of $5.0\,\text{cm}$. Another error is to think that the highest frequency is always best, because it gives the sharpest image. High-frequency ultrasound is attenuated quickly, so deep structures are imaged with a lower frequency and sharpness has to be compromised. A third error is to think of ultrasound as radiation like X-rays. Ultrasound is mechanical wave motion that needs a medium, and it does not ionise tissue. Being non-ionising does not on its own make it harmless, however: intense ultrasound heats tissue and can create gas bubbles (cavitation). That is why the power of the devices and the length of an examination are limited, and an examination is done only when needed.

Summary

  • Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above $20\,\text{kHz}$; medical imaging uses frequencies of about $2\text{–}15\,\text{MHz}$.
  • Part of the pulse is reflected from every boundary. The depth of a boundary is obtained from the travel time of the echo, $d = \tfrac{vt}{2}$, when $v = 1540\,\text{m/s}$.
  • Gel displaces the air between the probe and the skin; from the boundary of air and skin almost the whole pulse would be reflected.
  • A high frequency gives a short wavelength and a sharp image, but it is attenuated quickly; deep structures are imaged with a low frequency.
  • The P wave is longitudinal and the fastest, the S wave transverse and slower. The S wave does not travel in a liquid, which shows that the outer core is liquid.
  • The distance of an earthquake is obtained from the difference in the arrival times of the S wave and the P wave: $d = \tfrac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\Delta t$.
  • The speed of a tsunami depends on the depth of the water, $v = \sqrt{gh}$; near the coast the wave slows down and grows tall.

    Example 1: Distance to an earthquake

    A seismograph station records an earthquake. The clock of the station showed $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 18\,\text{s}$ when the P wave arrived and $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 43\,\text{s}$ when the S wave arrived. The speed of the P wave in the crust is $6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and that of the S wave $3.5\,\text{km/s}$. How far from the station did the earthquake occur? Assume that the waves travel along the same straight path at constant speeds.

    Step 1/7

    Given and find. $v_\text{P} = 6.0\,\text{km/s}$, $v_\text{S} = 3.5\,\text{km/s}$, arrival times $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 18\,\text{s}$ and $12\,\text{h}\ 04\,\text{min}\ 43\,\text{s}$. We are asked for the distance $d$ to the point where the earthquake started.

    Step 2/7

    Principle. Each wave covers the same distance $d$ at a constant speed, so the travel time is the distance divided by the speed. The moment of the earthquake is not known, but the difference $\Delta t$ in the arrival times is the difference of the travel times of the S wave and the P wave.

    Step 3/7

    Formula solved. Take $d$ as a common factor and solve:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Delta t &= d\left(\frac{1}{v_\text{S}} - \frac{1}{v_\text{P}}\right) \\[6pt] d &= \frac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4/7

    Unit conversions. The hours and minutes are the same, so the difference in arrival times is $43\,\text{s} - 18\,\text{s} = 25\,\text{s}$. The speeds are in km/s and the time in seconds, so the distance comes out directly in kilometres.

    Step 5/7

    Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} d &= \frac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t \\ &= \frac{6.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 3.5\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}}{6.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} - 3.5\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}} \cdot 25\,\text{s} \\ &= 8.4\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 25\,\text{s} \\ &= 210\,\text{km} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 6/7

    Result. The earthquake occurred about $210\,\text{km}$ from the station. The result is given with two significant figures, because the speeds $6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and $3.5\,\text{km/s}$ are known to two figures.

    Step 7/7

    Reasonableness. Let us check the travel times: the P wave covers $210\,\text{km} : 6.0\,\text{km/s} = 35\,\text{s}$ and the S wave $210\,\text{km} : 3.5\,\text{km/s} = 60\,\text{s}$. Their difference is $25\,\text{s}$, as measured. So the earthquake occurred $35\,\text{s}$ before the arrival of the P wave, that is, when the clock of the station showed $12\,\text{h}\ 03\,\text{min}\ 43\,\text{s}$.

    The wavelength of a tsunami is much greater than the depth of the ocean, so it travels as a shallow-water wave at the speed $v = \sqrt{gh}$. When the depth is assumed to be the same all the way, the speed is constant and the travel time is the distance divided by the speed.

    Formula

    $v = \sqrt{gh}$ and $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{\sqrt{gh}}$

    Conversions

    $h$$= 4.0\,\text{km}$
    $= 4.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}$
    $= 4000\,\text{m}$
    $s$$= 2000 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}$
    $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

    and finally $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

    Substitution

    $t$$= \dfrac{2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}{\sqrt{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 4000\,\text{m}}}$
    $= \dfrac{2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}{198.1\,\text{m/s}}$
    $= 10\,096\,\text{s}$
    $= 2.80\,\text{h}$

    Answer

    $2.8\,\text{h}$

    Two significant figures like the depth $4.0\,\text{km}$.

    Reasonableness

    The speed $198\,\text{m/s}$ is about $710\,\text{km/h}$, of the order of a jet aircraft, and covering $2000\,\text{km}$ at this speed takes just under three hours: $\tfrac{2000\,\text{km}}{710\,\text{km/h}} \approx 2.8\,\text{h}$. In reality the wave slows down in the shallow water near the coast, so the warning time is slightly longer.

    Example 3: Find the error

    A seismograph station measures the difference in the arrival times of the P wave and the S wave to be $20\,\text{s}$, when $v_\text{P} = 6.0\,\text{km/s}$ and $v_\text{S} = 3.5\,\text{km/s}$.A student reasons: “The S wave falls behind the P wave by $2.5\,\text{km}$ every second, so $d = (v_\text{P} - v_\text{S})\,\Delta t = 2.5\,\text{km/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 50\,\text{km}$.” Where is the error?

    • Correct. The student's product $(v_\text{P} - v_\text{S})\,\Delta t$ is the distance between the waves $20\,\text{s}$ after the earthquake, not the distance of the station. The time difference builds up over the whole distance, and the travel times are the distance divided by the speed. Solving the equation $\Delta t = \tfrac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \tfrac{d}{v_\text{P}}$ for $d$ gives $d = 8.4\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 168\,\text{km}$. Check: the P wave covers it in $28\,\text{s}$ and the S wave in $48\,\text{s}$.

    • The mean would give $95\,\text{km}$, and that does not match the measurement either: the P wave would cover it in $16\,\text{s}$ and the S wave in $27\,\text{s}$, and the difference would be only $11\,\text{s}$. The time difference is the difference of the travel times, $\Delta t = \tfrac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \tfrac{d}{v_\text{P}}$.

    • The units are consistent: km/s times s gives kilometres. A conversion would not change the result. The error is in the equation itself: the time difference is the difference of the travel times $\tfrac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \tfrac{d}{v_\text{P}}$.

      a seismogram as a graph, $v_\text{P} = 7.0\,\text{km/s}$, $v_\text{S} = 4.0\,\text{km/s}$

      $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \dfrac{d}{v_\text{P}}$

      $= 9.33\,\text{km/s} \cdot 18\,\text{s}$
      $= 168\,\text{km}$

      $168\,\text{km}$

    3. Calculation task: The length of an eye

    Before cataract surgery the length of the eye is measured with an A-scan. The echo from the retina returns $31.0\,\mu\text{s}$ later than the echo from the surface of the cornea. Sound travels in the eye at an average speed of $1550\,\text{m/s}$.

    How long is the eye from the cornea to the retina? Answer in centimetres.

    cm
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The pulse travels through the eye to the retina and back.

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    $d = \tfrac{v \Delta t}{2}$, when $\Delta t = 31.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{s}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    $\tfrac{1550\,\text{m/s} \cdot 31.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{s}}{2}$

    Show the solution

    Given

    time difference of the echoes $\Delta t = 31.0\,\mu\text{s}$, speed of sound in the eye $v = 1550\,\text{m/s}$

    Find

    the length of the eye $d$ in centimetres

    Principle

    In the time difference of the echoes the pulse travels from the cornea to the retina and back, so it covers the length of the eye twice. The speed is assumed to be the same all the way.

    Formula

    $2d = v \Delta t$, so $d = \dfrac{v \Delta t}{2}$

    Conversions

    $31.0\,\mu\text{s} = 31.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{s}$; finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.

    Substitution

    $d$$= \dfrac{1550\,\text{m/s} \cdot 31.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{s}}{2}$
    $= 0.024\,025\,\text{m}$
    $= 2.40\,\text{cm}$

    Answer

    $2.40\,\text{cm}$

    Three significant figures like the time difference $31.0\,\mu\text{s}$.

    Reasonableness

    The eyeball of an adult is about $2.4\,\text{cm}$ in diameter, so the result is plausible. Had the return trip been forgotten, the eye would have been almost $5\,\text{cm}$ long, which is clearly too much.

    4. Explanation task: Explain the choice of frequency

    The heart is usually imaged with a probe of about $3\,\text{MHz}$, but a tendon of the wrist with a probe of about $12\,\text{MHz}$. Explain why the frequencies are chosen like this. Use the concepts wavelength and attenuation.

    Model answer

    The wavelength is $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$, so the wavelength of $12\,\text{MHz}$ ultrasound in tissue is about $0.13\,\text{mm}$ and that of $3\,\text{MHz}$ about $0.5\,\text{mm}$. The smallest details that can be distinguished are a few wavelengths in size, so a higher frequency gives a sharper image. However, attenuation grows approximately in direct proportion to the frequency, so a $12\,\text{MHz}$ pulse weakens quickly and its echo does not return from deeper than a few centimetres. A tendon is right under the skin, so a small depth is enough for imaging it and a high frequency can be used. The heart extends to a depth of over $10\,\text{cm}$, so a low frequency is used that reaches that far, and sharpness has to be compromised.
