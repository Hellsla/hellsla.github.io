## fy7-9-L1-01
**Given:** frequency of the radio wave $f = 94.0\,\text{MHz}$, speed of the wave $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** wavelength $\lambda$ in metres
**Principle:** A radio wave is an electromagnetic wave, which travels one wavelength during one period. So the speed, frequency and wavelength are related by $c = f\lambda$.
**Formula:** $c = f\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f}$
**Conversions:** $f = 94.0\,\text{MHz} = 94.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{Hz}$
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{94.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{Hz}} = 3.191\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $3.19\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the frequency $94.0\,\text{MHz}$.
**Reasonableness:** Units: $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{Hz}} = \tfrac{\text{m/s}}{1/\text{s}} = \text{m}$. The wavelength at about $100\,\text{MHz}$ in FM radio is about $3\,\text{m}$, and a slightly lower frequency gives a slightly longer wave. The value is over a metre, as it should be for radio waves.

**Given:** surface temperature of the skin $T = 307\,\text{K}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the skin is assumed to be a black body

**Substitution:** $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{307\,\text{K}} = 9.440 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} = 9.440\,\mu\text{m}$

**Reasonableness:** The peak is in the infrared region, far beyond the $750\,\text{nm} = 0.75\,\mu\text{m}$ of visible light, so the skin does not glow. The result is slightly shorter than the $9.7\,\mu\text{m}$ of a body at room temperature, because the skin is slightly warmer.

## fy7-9-L2-03
**Given:** initial temperature $t_1 = 27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, final temperature $t_2 = 327\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the plate is assumed to be a black body
**Find:** shift of the peak of the spectrum $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_1 - \lambda_2$ in micrometres
**Principle:** According to Wien's displacement law the wavelength of the peak is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature, so on heating the peak shifts to shorter wavelengths. We calculate the peak at both temperatures and take the difference. The difference cannot be found from the temperature difference, because $\lambda_\text{max}$ does not depend linearly on the temperature.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = \dfrac{b}{T}$ and $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{b}{T_1} - \dfrac{b}{T_2}$
**Conversions:** $T_1 = (27 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 300.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = (327 + 273.15)\,\text{K} = 600.15\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{300.15\,\text{K}} - \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{600.15\,\text{K}} = 9.655\,\mu\text{m} - 4.829\,\mu\text{m} = 4.826\,\mu\text{m}$
**Answer:** $4.83\,\mu\text{m}$ towards shorter wavelengths, three significant figures, because the temperatures are given to the nearest degree.
**Reasonableness:** The absolute temperature almost doubles, so the wavelength of the peak almost halves, from about $9.7\,\mu\text{m}$ to about $4.8\,\mu\text{m}$; the shift is therefore about half of the original wavelength. Both peaks are in the infrared region, so the plate does not glow.

## fy7-9-L1-06
**Find:** how the electric field $\overline{E}$ and the magnetic field $\overline{B}$ are oriented relative to each other and to the direction of propagation in an electromagnetic wave.
**Principle:** In an electromagnetic wave a changing electric field produces a magnetic field and a changing magnetic field produces an electric field, so the fields carry the wave forward even in a vacuum. The fields oscillate perpendicular to each other and perpendicular to the direction of propagation, that is, the wave is transverse.
**Model answer:** The fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation, because the wave is transverse; each field produces the other. The claim that the fields oscillate along the direction of propagation is wrong, because it describes a longitudinal wave like sound, and an electromagnetic wave is transverse. The claim that the magnetic field stays constant is wrong, because the magnetic field oscillates in the wave together with the electric field and does not need a permanent magnet. The claim of fields in the same direction is wrong, because the fields are perpendicular to each other.

The altitude of the satellite $2.02 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m}$ is about half of the circumference, so the travel time is about half of this, about $0.067\,\text{s}$.

## fy7-9-L2-07
**Given:** measurements $(T, \lambda_\text{max})$: $(2000\,\text{K}, 1.47\,\mu\text{m})$, $(2400\,\text{K}, 1.18\,\mu\text{m})$, $(2800\,\text{K}, 1.06\,\mu\text{m})$ and $(3200\,\text{K}, 0.89\,\mu\text{m})$; constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the filament is assumed to be a black body

## fy7-9-L3-04
**Given:** peaks of the spectra $\lambda_A = 830\,\text{nm}$, $\lambda_B = 480\,\text{nm}$ and $\lambda_C = 260\,\text{nm}$ and the observed colours red, yellow-white and blue-white; constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the stars radiate like black bodies
**Find:** a) the surface temperatures $T_A$, $T_B$ and $T_C$; b) the ratio $T_C : T_A$; c) why the position of the peak and the visible colour are not the same thing
**Principle:** The temperature of each star follows from the wavelength of its peak by Wien's displacement law, and the peak of a hotter star is at a shorter wavelength. In the ratio of the temperatures the constant $b$ cancels out, so the ratio is the inverse ratio of the wavelengths of the peaks. The colour, on the other hand, is determined by the whole spectrum and not by the peak alone.
**Formula:** $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$, and $\dfrac{T_C}{T_A} = \dfrac{b / \lambda_C}{b / \lambda_A} = \dfrac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_C}$
**Conversions:** $830\,\text{nm} = 830 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$, $480\,\text{nm} = 480 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $260\,\text{nm} = 260 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $T_A = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{830 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3492\,\text{K}$, $T_B = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{480 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 6038\,\text{K}$ and $T_C = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{260 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 11\,146\,\text{K}$. The ratio is $\dfrac{T_C}{T_A} = \dfrac{11\,146\,\text{K}}{3492\,\text{K}} = 3.192$, and the same is obtained directly from the peaks: $\dfrac{830\,\text{nm}}{260\,\text{nm}} = 3.192$.
**Answer:** a) $T_A = 3.5 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{K}$, $T_B = 6.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{K}$ and $T_C = 1.1 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{K}$. b) Star C is $3.19$ times hotter than star A; three significant figures like the wavelengths $830\,\text{nm}$ and $260\,\text{nm}$. c) The spectrum is wide, and the eye sees only its visible part. The long-wavelength edge of star C covers the whole visible region and blue is the strongest in it, so the star looks blue-white, although the peak is in the ultraviolet region. The short-wavelength edge of star A extends into visible light, where red is the strongest, so the star looks red, although the peak is in the infrared. So the colour depends on the whole spectrum and not on the peak alone.
**Reasonableness:** Star B is almost like the Sun: the peak $480\,\text{nm}$ and the temperature $6.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{K}$ (for the Sun $502\,\text{nm}$ and $5772\,\text{K}$). Star A is cooler than the Sun and its peak is longer, $830\,\text{nm} > 502\,\text{nm}$, and star C is hotter than the Sun and its peak is shorter, $260\,\text{nm} < 502\,\text{nm}$, as it should be.

## fy7-9-L3-05
**Given:** measurements $(t, \lambda_\text{max})$: $(0\,\text{min}, 1.80\,\mu\text{m})$, $(4\,\text{min}, 2.05\,\mu\text{m})$, $(8\,\text{min}, 2.35\,\mu\text{m})$ and $(12\,\text{min}, 2.75\,\mu\text{m})$; constant of Wien's displacement law $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the bar is a black body and the glow is visible when $T > 800\,\text{K}$
**Find:** a) the temperatures $T$; b) the average cooling rate $\dfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta t}$ in the unit $\text{K/min}$; c) the moment $t_\text{off}$ when the temperature is $800\,\text{K}$, if the cooling continues at the average rate, and the direction of the error of the estimate
**Principle:** The temperature at each moment follows from the wavelength of the peak by Wien's displacement law. The cooling rate is the change in temperature divided by the time. When the rate is assumed to stay constant, the temperature falls steadily, and the moment when it reaches the value $800\,\text{K}$ is obtained by extending from the last measurement.
**Formula:** $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$, rate $v_T = \dfrac{T_0 - T_{12}}{\Delta t}$ and $t_\text{off} = t_{12} + \dfrac{T_{12} - T_\text{limit}}{v_T}$, where $T_\text{limit}$ is the lower limit of the visibility of the glow
**Conversions:** $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$, so for example $\lambda_\text{max} = 1.80\,\mu\text{m} = 1.80 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** a) $T_0 = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{1.80 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 1610\,\text{K}$, $T_4 = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{2.05 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 1414\,\text{K}$, $T_8 = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{2.35 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 1233\,\text{K}$ and $T_{12} = \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{2.75 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 1054\,\text{K}$. b) $v_T = \dfrac{(1610 - 1054)\,\text{K}}{12\,\text{min}} = 46.3\,\text{K/min}$. c) $t_\text{off} = 12\,\text{min} + \dfrac{(1054 - 800)\,\text{K}}{46.3\,\text{K/min}} = 12\,\text{min} + 5.5\,\text{min} = 17.5\,\text{min}$
**Answer:** c) According to the estimate, the glow goes out about $17.5\,\text{min}$ after the first measurement, three significant figures like the wavelengths. Intermediate results: the temperatures $1610\,\text{K}$, $1414\,\text{K}$, $1233\,\text{K}$ and $1054\,\text{K}$ and the average cooling rate $46.3\,\text{K/min}$. The actual glowing time is longer than the estimate: the cooling rates over the intervals of the table are $49.1\,\text{K/min}$, $45.1\,\text{K/min}$ and $44.8\,\text{K/min}$, so the rate decreases. When the cooling is slower than average, the temperature reaches the value $800\,\text{K}$ later than estimated.
**Reasonableness:** The temperature $1054\,\text{K}$ at the last measurement is over $800\,\text{K}$, so the glow is still visible and it must go out later than $12\,\text{min}$. Check: the temperature at the moment $17.5\,\text{min}$ is $1610\,\text{K} - 46.3\,\text{K/min} \cdot 17.5\,\text{min} = 800\,\text{K}$. The peak corresponding to the temperature $800\,\text{K}$ is $\dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{800\,\text{K}} = 3.6\,\mu\text{m}$, like the dark red glow in the lesson.
