## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–6 min, Think about it.** Read the stadium comparison aloud; the review question on Rutherford's nucleus. Ask: "If protons repel each other, why is the nucleus not exploding right now?"
- **6–12 min, Predict.** The three predictions individually, each with a short written reason. Do not discuss the answers yet.
- **12–32 min, Investigate.** Pairs on one device: the Isotope Builder tasks (one student builds, the other reads the notation and the stability line aloud, then swap), the table of stable nuclei with the $N/Z$ calculation, and the pair conclusion with the word bank.
- **32–47 min, Theory summary.** Read *Inside the atom*, the key idea and the formula; the vocabulary match; *The force that holds the nucleus*; back to the predictions.
- **47–62 min, Apply.** The worked example (iron-56: its size and density) step by step, then the Coulomb-force task individually.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks and the explanation task individually, the summary and the French corner in pairs.

## Organisation and preparation
- Pairs share one device for the builder; each student keeps their own predictions and written answers.
- Show a periodic table: the atomic number is printed in every square, and isotopes share a square.
- If students ask about the masses in the particle table: the next unit (*Mass defect and binding energy*) explains why the mass of a nucleus is not $Z \cdot m_\text{p} + N \cdot m_\text{n}$.
- Fast pairs: the optional error-spotting task and the biography of James Chadwick; ask them to find in the builder the heaviest stable isotope of an element of their choice and explain why it has more neutrons.
- Running out of time: do the worked example together on the board and leave the Coulomb-force task as homework; keep the three checks in class, because they show whether the main ideas are in place.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade, use the same criteria: the conclusion says that $Z$ decides the element, gives an isotope example from the builder and uses a value of $N/Z$ from the table; the explanation task gives $Z$ and $N$ of tritium and helium-3 and names a true isotope of tritium.
- Language: correct use of at least three key words (atomic number, mass number, isotope, nucleon, half-life) and a sentence starter used to argue.

## Common difficulties
- Mixing up $A$ and $N$: students read the upper number as "the neutrons". The error-spotting task and the second check target this.
- Thinking that the mass number is the mass in kilograms or that it equals the mass in $\text{u}$ exactly.
- Thinking that neutrons "cancel" the charge of protons. Ask: "What is the charge of a nucleus with $8$ protons and $9$ neutrons?"
- The builder draws the nucleons as packed balls; a real nucleus has no sharp surface and no fixed positions. The legend under the drawing says so.

## Extension ideas
- Use the builder to find the isotopes of carbon and of oxygen that are stable, and compare with the half-lives of the others. Which nuclei lie far from $N \approx Z$, and what happens to them?
- Calculate the ratio of the gravitational force to the electric force between two protons, $G m_\text{p}^2 / (k e^2)$, and discuss why gravity is ignored in nuclear physics.
- Find out how Harold Urey discovered deuterium in 1931 and why heavy water is useful in some fission reactors.
