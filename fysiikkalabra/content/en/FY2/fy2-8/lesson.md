# Energy production, the environment and climate change

> **Why** does the way we produce electricity decide how much carbon dioxide ends up in the atmosphere, even though the electricity from a socket at home is always the same?

## Key idea
The Sun warms the ground by radiating energy to it, as visible light and near-infrared radiation. The atmosphere lets most of this radiation through. The warm ground sends energy back into space as thermal radiation, and the **greenhouse gases** in the atmosphere absorb it. The most important ones are water vapour, carbon dioxide $\text{CO}_2$ and methane $\text{CH}_4$. They radiate the energy out again in all directions, so part of it returns to the ground.

So greenhouse gases let sunlight through but slow down the escape of thermal radiation. The ground stays warmer than it would be without them: the **greenhouse effect** raises the mean temperature of the Earth from about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. This natural effect keeps the Earth habitable, because without it the water in the oceans would be frozen.

The problem is that the effect is getting stronger. When people add carbon dioxide and methane to the atmosphere, thermal radiation escapes more slowly and the mean temperature of the Earth rises. This long-term change is called **climate change**. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has grown from about $280\,\text{ppm}$ (before industrialisation) to about $420\,\text{ppm}$ (NOAA measurements, 2023). Even so, carbon dioxide is less than half a part per thousand of the air. A small share is enough, because nitrogen and oxygen, the main gases of the air, hardly absorb thermal radiation: it is the few greenhouse gas molecules that decide how easily thermal radiation escapes.

The greenhouse effect is treated with calculations on the FY3 course in the chapter *Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change*. Here it is enough to understand what the gases do to radiation and where the extra gases come from.

### Fuels link energy and climate
A fossil fuel, such as coal, oil or natural gas, is chemical energy from ancient living things: coal formed mostly from plants, oil and natural gas from marine plankton. When a fuel burns, its carbon combines with oxygen: $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$. The chemical energy is released as heat (chapter *Fuels and heat of combustion E = Hm*), and the carbon dioxide leaves with the flue gases into the atmosphere. The more energy we get by burning, the more carbon dioxide is released.

The carbon of a fossil fuel has been underground for millions of years, so burning it adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. The carbon of biomass, for example wood, was bound into plants only over a few decades. Even so, burning biomass is not emission-free: the carbon dioxide from burning stays in the atmosphere until new growth binds it back over decades, and growing, harvesting and transport cause their own emissions.

### Ways of producing energy are compared by life-cycle emissions
Emissions come from more than just burning. A power plant must be built from steel, concrete and copper, the fuel must be mined or collected and transported, and in the end the plant is dismantled. That is why ways of producing energy are compared by **life-cycle emissions**: the emissions of all stages are added together and divided by the energy produced over the whole life cycle.

For comparison, methane and other greenhouse gases are converted into carbon dioxide equivalents ($\text{CO}_2$-eq.), that is, the amount of carbon dioxide that has the same warming effect.

### Emissions are not the only criterion
Besides the effect on the climate, ways of producing energy are weighed by land use, transport, waste and safety:

- **Coal and natural gas:** the largest emissions. The fuels are mined and transported by ship, train or pipeline, and the flue gases also contain other pollutants.
- **Biomass:** takes up forest and field area. Biomass is bulky and its energy content per mass is small, so it is not worth transporting far.
- **Solar and wind power:** need a lot of area per unit of energy produced, and wind power affects the landscape and birds. Production depends on the weather, so regulating power or storage is needed.
- **Hydropower:** dams and reservoirs change the river environment and block the passage of fish, but production can be regulated.
- **Nuclear power:** needs little area, and only a small mass of fuel, so there is little transport. Spent fuel is radioactive waste that must be isolated for a long time, and the consequences of an accident could be serious, although the risk is small.

A **sustainable energy economy** produces and uses energy so that meeting today's needs does not take away the possibilities of future generations. There is no best power plant: the choice weighs the climate effect, land use, waste, safety, price and whether electricity is always available (chapter *Ways of producing energy and how they compare*).

## Formula: Carbon dioxide emission
The emission is obtained by multiplying the energy used or produced by the emission factor:

```latex
m = e \cdot E
```
where $m$ is the emission ($\text{g}$ or $\text{kg}$ of carbon dioxide equivalent), $e$ is the **emission factor** ($\text{g/kWh}$) and $E$ is the energy ($\text{kWh}$).

The emission factor tells how many grams of carbon dioxide equivalent are produced per kilowatt-hour. The unit comes along: when $\text{g/kWh}$ is multiplied by kilowatt-hours, grams are left. The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$. The emission of a fuel is calculated in the same way when the factor is, for example, $\text{g/MJ}$ and the energy is in megajoules.

| Way of producing energy | $e\ (\text{g/kWh})$ |
|---|---|
| Coal | 820 |
| Natural gas | 490 |
| Biomass | 230 |
| Solar power | 41–48 |
| Hydropower | 24 |
| Nuclear power | 12 |
| Wind power | 11–12 |

The source of the table is the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2014), Working Group III, Annex III: medians of life-cycle emissions, rounded, in the unit grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilowatt-hour. The lower limit for solar power is for rooftop panels and the upper limit for utility-scale plants; the lower limit for wind power is onshore and the upper limit offshore. The figure for biomass applies to biomass grown for energy. It includes the emissions of growing, harvesting, transport and the power plant, but the carbon dioxide from burning is counted as zero, because new growth binds it back. So the figure does not show that the binding takes decades.

## Worked example
An example family uses $4800\,\text{kWh}$ of electricity a year. The electricity is produced with natural gas, whose life-cycle emission factor is $490\,\text{g/kWh}$. How many tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent are produced per year by the family's electricity use? (The family is an invented example, not statistics.)

<!-- vaihe -->

**Given and find.** $E = 4800\,\text{kWh}$ and $e = 490\,\text{g/kWh}$. We want the emission $m$ in tonnes.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Principle.** The emission is directly proportional to the energy produced, and the emission factor gives the emission per kilowatt-hour. So the life-cycle emission is obtained by multiplying the energy by the emission factor.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Rearranged formula.**
```latex
m = e \cdot E
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Unit conversions.** The kilowatt-hours cancel, so the result comes out in grams. Grams are converted into tonnes: $1\,\text{g} = 0.001\,\text{kg}$ and $1\,\text{kg} = 0.001\,\text{t}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Substitution.**
```latex
\begin{aligned}
m &= e \cdot E \\
&= 490\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 4800\,\text{kWh} \\
&= 2\,352\,000\,\text{g} \\
&= 2\,352\,000 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} \\
&= 2352\,\text{kg} \\
&= 2352 \cdot 0.001\,\text{t} \\
&= 2.352\,\text{t}
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Result.** The emission is $m \approx 2.4\,\text{t}$ of carbon dioxide equivalent per year, to two significant figures like the emission factor $490\,\text{g/kWh}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Reasonableness.** With rounded numbers $500\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 5000\,\text{kWh} = 2\,500\,000\,\text{g} = 2\,500\,000 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{t} = 2.5\,\text{t}$, which is close to the result. If the same electricity were produced with wind power, the emission would be $12\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 4800\,\text{kWh} = 57\,600\,\text{g} = 57\,600 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} \approx 58\,\text{kg}$, about $41$ times smaller ($490 : 12 \approx 41$).

## Common misconception
**"Wind, solar and nuclear power are emission-free."** They are low-emission, not emission-free: building the plants, making the fuel and transport cause emissions, about $12\,\text{g/kWh}$ for wind and nuclear power. For coal the figure is about $820\,\text{g/kWh}$.

**"The greenhouse effect is a harmful phenomenon."** The natural greenhouse effect is necessary, because without it the mean temperature of the Earth would be about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The problem is the strengthening of the effect when carbon dioxide and methane are added to the atmosphere.

## Summary
- Greenhouse gases let most of the Sun's radiation through but slow down the escape of thermal radiation from the ground. The natural greenhouse effect raises the mean temperature from about $-18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to about $+15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
- Burning fuels adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, strengthens the greenhouse effect and leads to climate change.
- The emission is the emission factor times the energy: $m = e \cdot E$. Ways of producing energy are compared by life-cycle emission factors, which range from about $12\,\text{g/kWh}$ (nuclear and wind power) to about $820\,\text{g/kWh}$ (coal).
- The emission factor does not tell everything: land use, transport, waste, safety and the availability of electricity are weighed separately when looking for a sustainable energy economy.
