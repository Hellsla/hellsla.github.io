Road safety: braking, seat belts and airbags

Why is the stopping distance of a car travelling at $100\,\text{km/h}$ almost three times that at $50\,\text{km/h}$, and why does a seat belt protect you when the car cannot stop in time?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate reaction, braking and stopping distances and explain why braking distance grows with speed squared.
  • estimate how speed, reaction time and road conditions affect stopping and the collision speed.
  • explain with the impulse–momentum principle why the seat belt and the airbag reduce the force on a person in a collision.

Prior knowledge

  • Displacement is the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axischapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion
  • Friction opposes sliding, and it is at most the product of the coefficient of friction and the normal forcechapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane
  • The change in momentum is the impulse: $\Delta\overline{p} = \overline{F}\,\Delta t$chapter Momentum and impulse

1Think about it

Driving school teaches that even a small increase in speed lengthens the stopping distance a lot and that the seat belt is fastened even for a short trip. According to the Finnish Crash Data Institute (OTI), in the fatal accidents investigated in 2017–2021, $198$ people died in cars and vans without a seat belt. The investigation teams estimated that $114$ of them could have survived if the belt had been fastened.

Why does the stopping distance grow so steeply with speed, and how can a seat belt decide whether a person survives a collision?

The speed is converted: $v_0\,(\text{m/s}) = v_0\,(\text{km/h}) : 3.6$. The reaction distance is $s_\text{r} = v_0\,t_\text{r}$.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Stopping starts with a reaction

    When a driver notices danger, the car does not start to slow down at once. The driver must first notice the danger, decide to brake and move a foot to the brake pedal. This takes the reaction time $t_\text{r}$, usually about one second. For example, the crash speed calculator of Liikenneturva, the Finnish Road Safety Council, assumes a reaction time of $1\,\text{s}$. Fatigue, alcohol and a glance at the phone make the reaction time longer. During the reaction time the car still moves at its original speed and travels the reaction distance $s_\text{r}$. Only then do the brakes begin to slow the car, and it travels the braking distance $s_\text{b}$ before it stops. The reaction distance and the braking distance together are the stopping distance $s_\text{stop}$.

    The road stops the car by friction

    The brakes slow the rotation of the wheels, but it is the road that slows the car. On a horizontal road the friction between the tyres and the road is the only horizontal force, when air resistance is neglected. Friction points backwards, so by Newton's second law the acceleration also points backwards and the car slows down. In braking, the magnitude of the acceleration is called the deceleration $a$.

    Friction has an upper limit that depends on the tyres and the road surface. On dry asphalt the deceleration can be about $9\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the Liikenneturva calculator uses this value too. On a wet, snowy or icy road the friction and therefore the deceleration are smaller, on ice only a fraction of the value on a dry road. Anti-lock brakes (ABS) stop the wheels from locking. The surface of a rolling tyre does not slide relative to the road, so static friction acts on the tyre. It can be greater than the kinetic friction of a sliding tyre, and the car stays steerable.

    Speed makes the braking distance grow steeply

    The reaction distance is directly proportional to the speed: at double the speed the car travels double the distance during the reaction time. The braking distance grows more steeply. With the same deceleration, stopping from double the speed takes twice as long, and for that whole time the car moves twice as fast. The braking distance is therefore four times as long. In the figure the reaction time is $1.0\,\text{s}$ and the deceleration $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ at both speeds.

    This gives the answer to the first part of the opening question. When the speed doubles from $50\,\text{km/h}$ to $100\,\text{km/h}$, the reaction distance doubles from $13.9\,\text{m}$ to $27.8\,\text{m}$, but the braking distance quadruples from $10.7\,\text{m}$ to $42.9\,\text{m}$. The stopping distance therefore grows from $24.6\,\text{m}$ to $70.6\,\text{m}$, almost three times as long.

    In a collision the change in momentum is always the same

    If the obstacle is closer than the stopping distance, the car collides with it. In a frontal collision the front of the car crumples and the car stops in about a tenth of a second. The passenger's momentum $p = mv$ then changes from $mv$ to zero, whether or not the passenger wears a seat belt: the collision speed determines the change in momentum. The only thing that can be changed is how long the collision time $\Delta t$ is, that is, the time in which the passenger stops. In the chapter Momentum and impulse you learned that the change in momentum equals the impulse $\overline{I} = \overline{F}\,\Delta t$. The longer the collision time, the smaller the force needed for the same change in momentum.

    Without a seat belt the passenger keeps moving at almost the original speed while the car slows down around them: by Newton's first law no force is yet slowing them down. They hit the steering wheel, the dashboard or the windscreen and stop in hundredths of a second. The seat belt fastens the passenger to the car, so they slow down together with the crumpling front of the car. The belt also stretches a little and spreads the force over the strong bones of the pelvis, chest and shoulder.

    The airbag inflates in hundredths of a second and catches the head and upper body. It lengthens the stopping time and spreads the force over a large area, so the pressure on the body is smaller. An airbag is designed to work together with the seat belt, not instead of it.

    This is the answer to the second part of the opening question. The difference also shows in accident statistics. According to the Finnish Crash Data Institute (OTI), in the fatal accidents investigated by the road accident investigation teams in 2017–2021, 278 people wearing a seat belt and 198 people not wearing one died in cars and vans. The investigation teams estimated that 114 of those without a belt could have survived if the belt had been fastened. In Liikenneturva's monitoring, almost everyone in the front seat wears a belt.

    Stopping distance and the force of a collision

    Reaction distance and braking distance

    During the reaction time the car moves at a constant speed $v_0$, so the reaction distance is

    $$s_\text{r} = v_0\,t_\text{r}$$

    where $s_\text{r}$ is the reaction distance ($\text{m}$), $v_0$ is the speed of the car before braking ($\text{m/s}$) and $t_\text{r}$ is the reaction time ($\text{s}$). In braking the deceleration is assumed to be constant, so on the $(t,v)$ graph the speed falls in a straight line to zero. As in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion, the distance travelled is the area between the graph and the time axis.

    The speed falls by $v_0$ at deceleration $a$, so the braking time is

    $$t_\text{b} = \frac{v_0}{a}$$

    where $t_\text{b}$ is the braking time ($\text{s}$) and $a$ is the deceleration, that is, the magnitude of the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). The braking distance is the area of the triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,t_\text{b}$. Substituting the braking time gives

    $$s_\text{b} = \frac{v_0^2}{2a}$$

    where $s_\text{b}$ is the braking distance ($\text{m}$). The stopping distance is the sum of these:

    $$s_\text{stop} = v_0\,t_\text{r} + \frac{v_0^2}{2a}$$

    where $s_\text{stop}$ is the stopping distance ($\text{m}$). The model assumes that the road is horizontal, the deceleration is constant during braking and air resistance is neglected. The speed is substituted in $\text{m/s}$.

    Deceleration from friction

    On a horizontal road the car does not accelerate vertically, so the normal force equals the weight, $N = mg$. When braking as hard as the grip allows, the friction is $F_\mu = \mu N$, where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction between the tyres and the road. By Newton's second law $\mu mg = ma$, and the mass cancels:

    $$a = \mu g$$

    where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction (no unit) and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ of dry asphalt corresponds to a coefficient of friction of $0.92$. In this model the braking distance does not depend on the mass of the car. The braking distance of a heavy lorry is still often longer, because its brakes and tyres cannot always use all of the grip.

    Average force in a collision

    When the passenger's speed changes by $\Delta v$ in a collision in time $\Delta t$, the impulse–momentum principle $\overline{F}_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = \Delta\overline{p}$ gives the magnitude of the average force acting on them:

    $$F_\text{avg} = \frac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$

    where $F_\text{avg}$ is the average force ($\text{N}$), $m$ is the passenger's mass ($\text{kg}$), $\Delta v$ is the magnitude of the change in speed ($\text{m/s}$) and $\Delta t$ is the collision time ($\text{s}$). When a $75\,\text{kg}$ passenger stops from $50\,\text{km/h}$, the change in momentum is about $1040\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. With a belt it is spread over about $0.10\,\text{s}$, and the average force is about $10\,\text{kN}$. Without a belt the time is about $0.010\,\text{s}$ and the force is ten times as large.

    The areas of the rectangles in the graph are equal, because each is the same change in momentum $m\,\Delta v$. In reality the force varies during the collision, and the greatest force is larger than the average.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that double the speed means double the stopping distance. The reaction distance doubles, but the braking distance quadruples, because it depends on the square of the speed. Another mistake is to think that a seat belt reduces the change in momentum. The momentum changes from $mv$ to zero in any case; the belt lengthens the time in which the change happens and therefore reduces the force. A third mistake is to believe that in a slow crash you can brace yourself with your hands. Even in a $50\,\text{km/h}$ collision the average force is about $10\,\text{kN}$ even with a belt, more than ten times a person's weight, and the muscle force of the hands is not enough against it.

    Summary

    • The stopping distance is the reaction distance $s_\text{r} = v_0\,t_\text{r}$ plus the braking distance $s_\text{b} = \tfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$, when the deceleration is constant.
    • The reaction distance is proportional to the speed and the braking distance to the square of the speed: double the speed gives four times the braking distance.
    • In braking, the friction between the tyres and the road slows the car. On a horizontal road $a = \mu g$, so a slippery road multiplies the braking distance.
    • In a collision the passenger's change in momentum is the same with and without a belt. The seat belt and the airbag lengthen the collision time, so the average force $F_\text{avg} = \tfrac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is smaller.
    • The airbag spreads the force over a large area and works together with the seat belt, not instead of it.

      Problem

      A car is driving on dry asphalt at $80\,\text{km/h}$. The driver glances at their phone, and their reaction time in the dangerous situation is $2.0\,\text{s}$. The deceleration of the car in braking is $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. How long is the stopping distance of the car?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $v_0 = 80\,\text{km/h}$, $t_\text{r} = 2.0\,\text{s}$ and $a = 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. We are asked for the stopping distance $s_\text{stop}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. During the reaction time the car moves at a constant speed, so the reaction distance is speed times time. After that the car slows down at a constant deceleration, when the road is horizontal and air resistance is neglected, so the braking distance is the area of the triangle on the $(t,v)$ graph. The stopping distance is the sum of these.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} s_\text{stop} &= s_\text{r} + s_\text{b} \\ s_\text{stop} &= v_0\,t_\text{r} + \frac{v_0^2}{2a} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The speed is converted to $\text{m/s}$:

      $80\,\text{km/h}$$= \tfrac{80 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
      $\approx 22.22\,\text{m/s}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate each partial distance and then the sum:

      $$\begin{aligned} s_\text{r} &= v_0\,t_\text{r} \\ \phantom{s_\text{r}} &= 22.22\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{s} \\ \phantom{s_\text{r}} &= 44.44\,\text{m} \\ s_\text{b} &= \frac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2} \\ \phantom{s_\text{b}} &= 27.43\,\text{m} \\ s_\text{stop} &= (44.44 + 27.43)\,\text{m} \\ \phantom{s_\text{stop}} &\approx 72\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The stopping distance is about $72\,\text{m}$, to two significant figures like the reaction time $2.0\,\text{s}$ and the deceleration $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. The reaction distance of $44\,\text{m}$ is more than half of it.

      Step 7/7

      3. Explain

      Explain with the impulse–momentum principle why the seat belt and the airbag reduce the force on a passenger in a frontal collision, even though the passenger stops from the same speed as without them. Also tell why the airbag does not replace the seat belt.

      Model answer

      The passenger stops from the same speed, so their momentum changes from $mv$ to zero in both cases. By the impulse–momentum principle $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = m\,\Delta v$, so the same change in momentum is produced by a smaller average force the longer the collision time. The belt fastens the passenger to the car, and they slow down with the crumpling front of the car in about a tenth of a second. Without a belt they keep moving and stop against the dashboard or the windscreen in about a hundredth of a second, and the force is about ten times as large. The airbag lengthens the stopping time of the head and upper body and spreads the force over a large area, so the pressure on the body decreases. It is designed for a passenger who is fastened with a belt: without a belt the body slides forward and may meet the inflating bag too early or miss it, and nothing keeps the passenger in the car.

        1. A driver drives at $72\,\text{km/h}$ and looks at their phone for $2.0\,\text{s}$. How far does the car travel while the driver is not looking at the road?
          Answer

        2. A loose $10\,\text{kg}$ bag lies on the back seat. Why is it dangerous in a frontal collision?
          Answer

          When the car stops, nothing slows the bag down, so it keeps moving according to Newton's first law. It stops only when it hits the front seat or a passenger in a very short time. Its momentum is large, and the short stopping time makes the force many times the weight of the bag.

        3. Why does the stopping distance not halve when the braking distance halves after the tyres are changed to ones with better grip?
          Answer

          Better tyres increase the deceleration and therefore shorten only the braking distance. The reaction distance $v_0\,t_\text{r}$ depends on the speed and the reaction time, not on the tyres, so it stays the same.

        1. The speed limit at a school is $30\,\text{km/h}$. A driver notices a child running onto the road at exactly the distance at which they would be able to stop from $30\,\text{km/h}$. The reaction time is $1.0\,\text{s}$ and the deceleration $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          At what speed would the car reach the child if the driver were driving at $40\,\text{km/h}$? Answer in $\text{km/h}$.

          km/h
          Answer

          $37\,\text{km/h}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          limit $30\,\text{km/h}$, actual speed $v_0 = 40\,\text{km/h}$, reaction time $t_\text{r} = 1.0\,\text{s}$, deceleration $a = 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$; the child is at a distance $d$ equal to the stopping distance from $30\,\text{km/h}$

          Find

          the speed of the car $v$ at the child in $\text{km/h}$

          Principle

          First the distance $d$ is calculated from the formula for the stopping distance. At $40\,\text{km/h}$ the car travels the reaction distance at constant speed and brakes for the rest of the distance. If the speed at the child is $v$, stopping would still need the distance $\tfrac{v^2}{2a}$, so the whole braking distance is the distance braked up to the child plus this.

          Formula

          $d = u\,t_\text{r} + \dfrac{u^2}{2a}$, where $u = 30\,\text{km/h}$, and

          $\dfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$$= (d - v_0\,t_\text{r}) + \dfrac{v^2}{2a}$

          so $v = \sqrt{v_0^2 - 2a\,(d - v_0\,t_\text{r})}$

          Conversions

          $u$$= 30\,\text{km/h}$
          $\approx 8.333\,\text{m/s}$

          and

          $v_0$$= 40\,\text{km/h}$
          $\approx 11.11\,\text{m/s}$

          the answer is finally multiplied by $3.6$.

          Substitution

          $d$$= 8.333\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.0\,\text{s} + \dfrac{(8.333\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 8.333\,\text{m} + 3.858\,\text{m}$
          $= 12.19\,\text{m}$
          $d - v_0\,t_\text{r}$$= 12.19\,\text{m} - 11.11\,\text{m}$
          $= 1.080\,\text{m}$
          $v^2$$= (11.11\,\text{m/s})^2 - 2 \cdot 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.080\,\text{m}$
          $= 104.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          so

          $v$$= \sqrt{104.0}\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 10.20\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 36.7\,\text{km/h}$

          Answer

          $37\,\text{km/h}$

          Two significant figures like the reaction time $1.0\,\text{s}$ and the deceleration $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          At $40\,\text{km/h}$ the reaction distance alone, $11.1\,\text{m}$, takes almost the whole $12.2\,\text{m}$, so only about a metre is left for braking, and the speed has time to fall only a little. The car would therefore hit the child at almost its original speed, even though the speed exceeds the limit by only $10\,\text{km/h}$.

        2. A car collides at $50.0\,\text{km/h}$. A $70.0\,\text{kg}$ passenger wearing a belt moves, relative to the car and with the car, a total of $0.600\,\text{m}$ before stopping. Assume that their deceleration is constant.

          How large is the average force on the passenger?

          N
          Answer

          $11\,300\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 70.0\,\text{kg}$, $v_0 = 50.0\,\text{km/h}$, stopping distance $s = 0.600\,\text{m}$, deceleration constant

          Find

          the average force $F_\text{avg}$ in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The force follows from the impulse–momentum principle when the stopping time is known. The time is not given, but with a constant deceleration the average speed is half the initial speed, so the distance is $s = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,\Delta t$ (the area of the triangle on the $(t,v)$ graph). The time is solved from this.

          Formula

          $s = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,\Delta t$, so $\Delta t = \dfrac{2s}{v_0}$, and $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = m\,v_0$, so

          $F_\text{avg}$$= \dfrac{m\,v_0}{\Delta t}$

          Conversions

          $50.0\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{50.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $\approx 13.89\,\text{m/s}$

          Substitution

          $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 0.600\,\text{m}}{13.89\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= 0.08640\,\text{s}$

          and

          $F_\text{avg}$$= \dfrac{70.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 13.89\,\text{m/s}}{0.08640\,\text{s}}$
          $= 11\,253\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $11\,300\,\text{N}$

          That is, $11.3\,\text{kN}$, three significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          The stopping time is about $0.09\,\text{s}$, the same order of magnitude as the crumpling time of the car, and the force is about $16$ times the passenger's weight $687\,\text{N}$. If the passenger stopped without a belt in a few centimetres, the force would be tens of times greater.

        3. A car is driving at $80\,\text{km/h}$ behind another car moving at the same speed. The car in front suddenly brakes at a deceleration of $9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. The reaction time of the driver behind is $1.0\,\text{s}$, and the deceleration of their car is only $6.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ because of worn tyres.

          What is the smallest gap between the cars that lets the driver behind stop before hitting the car in front?

          m
          Answer

          $36\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed of both cars $v_0 = 80\,\text{km/h}$, deceleration of the car in front $a_1 = 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, deceleration of the car behind $a_2 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ and reaction time $t_\text{r} = 1.0\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the smallest gap $d$ in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          The car in front starts to brake at once and stops after its braking distance. The car behind first travels the reaction distance and then a longer braking distance, because its deceleration is smaller. The car in front is all the time moving more slowly than the car behind, so the gap shrinks until the car behind stops. There is no collision if the gap is at least the difference between the distances the cars travel.

          Formula

          $d + \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a_1}$$= v_0\,t_\text{r} + \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a_2}$

          so

          $d$$= v_0\,t_\text{r} + \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a_2} - \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a_1}$

          Conversions

          $80\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{80 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $\approx 22.22\,\text{m/s}$

          Substitution

          $d$$= 22.22\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.0\,\text{s} + \dfrac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 6.0\,\text{m/s}^2} - \dfrac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.0\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 22.22\,\text{m} + 41.15\,\text{m} - 27.43\,\text{m}$
          $= 35.94\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $36\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the reaction time $1.0\,\text{s}$ and the decelerations.

          Reasonableness

          If the decelerations of the cars were the same, the reaction distance $22\,\text{m}$ would be enough for the gap. The worn tyres add about $14\,\text{m}$ to the gap needed. A gap of $36\,\text{m}$ corresponds to a driving time of about $1.6\,\text{s}$ at $22\,\text{m/s}$.
