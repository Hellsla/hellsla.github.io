## Key idea

### A plan answers six questions
In the matriculation exam, a “plan an experiment” task is marked on whether someone else could carry out the experiment from your answer. A good plan says **what is changed**, **what is measured and with which instrument**, **what is kept constant**, **how many measurements and repetitions are made**, **how the result is calculated from a graph** and **which sources of error are the largest and how they are reduced**. In an experiment one variable is changed at a time, because otherwise the cause of a change in the result cannot be inferred.

### Error, uncertainty and resolution
The **measurement error** is the unknown difference between the result and the true value. The **measurement uncertainty** is an estimate of how large the error can be. The **resolution** is the smallest step the instrument shows, and on its own it does not give the uncertainty: because of the reaction time, the uncertainty of a hand-timed interval is of the order of tenths of a second, even though the watch shows hundredths.

A random error shows as the spread of repeated results, and in the mean its effect is damped. A **systematic error** shifts all results in the same direction, and repetition does not remove it. It is revealed by comparing with a known value or from a graph: the line does not pass through the origin, although according to the model it should.

## Formula: Processing a measurement result
The best estimate of repeated measurements is the mean, and in this course the estimate of the spread is half the range:

```kaavat
mean :: \bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n}
uncertainty of a measurement :: \Delta x = \frac{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}}{2}
```

where $n$ is the number of measurements, $x_\text{max}$ the largest and $x_\text{min}$ the smallest result. The result is given in the form $\bar{x} \pm \Delta x$, and the error estimate is rounded to one significant figure (two if the first digit is 1). The relative uncertainty

```kaavat
relative uncertainty :: \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{\bar{x}}
```

tells whether the uncertainty is small compared with the size of the result. The measured range is chosen so large that $\varepsilon$ stays small: the time is measured, for example, from ten swings.

### The result from the slope of a graph
When according to the model the quantities depend linearly on each other, a straight line is fitted to the measurement points

```kaavat
equation of a line :: y = kx + b
```

where $k = \tfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ is the slope, read from two points of the line far apart, and $b$ is the point where the line cuts the vertical axis. The model holds only in the measured range, and the line is extended outside it only with good reason. When the quantity sought comes from the slope, all the points affect the result and the random error of a single point less so.

If according to the model the line should pass through the origin but $b$ clearly differs from zero, the measurement probably has a systematic error that is the same at every point. ## Common mistakes {Yleisiä virheitä}
- The plan says “measure and calculate”, but it does not say what is kept constant, what is measured with and how the result is obtained from a graph.
- Two variables change at the same time, so the cause of the change in the result cannot be inferred.
- The resolution of the instrument is given as the uncertainty, although the measuring method, such as the reaction time, brings a larger uncertainty.
- Repetition is claimed to remove all errors. It only reduces the effect of random error on the mean.
- The slope is calculated from two adjacent measurement points and not from the fitted line.
- A deviating result is quietly removed. It is checked, and leaving it out is justified and recorded.
- In the conclusion the hypothesis is “proved”. A measurement can only support a hypothesis within its uncertainty.
- “Human error” is written as a source of error without saying what the error is, in which direction it takes the result and how it is reduced.
