## fy5-9-L1-01
**Given:** mass $m = 0.80\,\text{kg}$, spring constant $k = 50\,\text{N/m}$, frictionless surface
**Find:** the period $T$ in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** The surface is frictionless, so the only force along the motion acting on the body is the harmonic force of the spring, $F = -kx$. The motion is then simple harmonic motion, and its period depends only on the mass and the spring constant.
**Formula:** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and the spring constant in $\text{N/m}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{0.80\,\text{kg}}{50\,\text{N/m}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.016\,\text{s}^2} = 2\pi \cdot 0.1265\,\text{s} = 0.7948\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $0.79\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the mass $0.80\,\text{kg}$ and the spring constant $50\,\text{N/m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Unit: $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{N/m}} = \tfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2} = \text{s}^2$, so the square root gives seconds. A period of less than a second is usual for a school spring and a weight of less than a kilogram: the oscillations can be counted by eye.

## fy5-9-L1-02
**Given:** length of the pendulum $l = 0.600\,\text{m}$, small swings, air resistance neglected, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the period $T$ in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** The pendulum is a simple pendulum: a small bob on a string without air resistance. For small swings the component of the weight along the path is proportional to the displacement, so the motion is simple harmonic and the period depends only on the length and the acceleration of free fall.
**Formula:** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}$
**Conversions:** The length is in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{0.600\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.06116\,\text{s}^2} = 2\pi \cdot 0.2473\,\text{s} = 1.554\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $1.55\,\text{s}$, three significant figures as in the length $0.600\,\text{m}$ and the acceleration of free fall $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** The period of a pendulum about a metre long is about $2\,\text{s}$. The length is $0.6$ times as large, so the period is $\sqrt{0.6} \approx 0.77$ times as large, about $1.55\,\text{s}$. The result fits.

## fy5-9-L1-03
**Given:** mass $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$, spring constant $k = 80\,\text{N/m}$
**Find:** the frequency $f$ in $\text{Hz}$
**Principle:** The figure oscillates under the harmonic force of the spring, so the period is obtained from the formula for a mass on a spring. The frequency is the reciprocal of the period: it tells us how many cycles occur in a second.
**Formula:** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$ and $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$, so $f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{80\,\text{N/m}}{0.20\,\text{kg}}} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{400\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}} = \dfrac{20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}}{2\pi} = 3.183\,\text{Hz}$
**Answer:** $3.2\,\text{Hz}$, two significant figures as in the mass $0.20\,\text{kg}$ and the spring constant $80\,\text{N/m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The period is $\tfrac{1}{3.2\,\text{Hz}} \approx 0.31\,\text{s}$, so the figure bobs about three times a second, which suits a light toy on a stiff spring.

## fy5-9-L1-04
**Given:** spring constant $k = 120\,\text{N/m}$, amplitude $A = 0.050\,\text{m}$, frictionless oscillation
**Find:** the energy of the oscillation $E$ in $\text{J}$
**Principle:** In frictionless oscillation the mechanical energy is conserved. At the extreme position the speed is zero, so all the energy is the elastic potential energy of the spring at the displacement $A$.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$
**Conversions:** The amplitude is in metres, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 120\,\text{N/m} \cdot (0.050\,\text{m})^2 = 60\,\text{N/m} \cdot 0.0025\,\text{m}^2 = 0.15\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $0.15\,\text{J}$, two significant figures as in the amplitude $0.050\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The unit $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot \text{m}^2 = \text{N}\cdot\text{m} = \text{J}$. The force of the spring grows from zero to $kA = 120\,\text{N/m} \cdot 0.050\,\text{m} = 6.0\,\text{N}$, so the mean force is $3.0\,\text{N}$ and the work over the distance $0.050\,\text{m}$ is $3.0\,\text{N} \cdot 0.050\,\text{m} = 0.15\,\text{J}$. The same value confirms that the energy of a small oscillation is a fraction of a joule.

## fy5-9-L1-05
**Given:** amplitude $A = 3.0\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** the distance $s$ travelled during one cycle in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** During one cycle the body starts from an extreme position, passes through the equilibrium position to the other extreme position and returns. An extreme position is an amplitude away from the equilibrium position, so a cycle consists of four parts, each as long as the amplitude.
**Formula:** $s = 4A$
**Conversions:** $3.0\,\text{cm} = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $s = 4 \cdot 0.030\,\text{m} = 0.12\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $0.12\,\text{m}$, two significant figures as in the amplitude $3.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The distance between the extreme positions is $2A = 6.0\,\text{cm}$, and during a cycle it is covered twice, first in one direction and then in the other: $2 \cdot 6.0\,\text{cm} = 12\,\text{cm}$.

## fy5-9-L2-01
**Given:** period $T = 1.50\,\text{s}$, small swings, air resistance neglected, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the length of the pendulum $l$ in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** For small swings a pendulum is a simple harmonic oscillator whose period depends only on the length and the acceleration of free fall. The length is solved from the formula for the period.
**Formula:** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}$, so $T^2 = 4\pi^2\dfrac{l}{g}$ and $l = \dfrac{gT^2}{4\pi^2}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $l = \dfrac{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (1.50\,\text{s})^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{22.07\,\text{m}}{39.48} = 0.5591\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $0.559\,\text{m}$, three significant figures as in the period $1.50\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check: with the length $0.559\,\text{m}$ the formula gives a period of $1.50\,\text{s}$. Also, the period of a pendulum about a metre long is about $2\,\text{s}$, so a pendulum with a period of less than $2\,\text{s}$ is shorter than a metre.

## fy5-9-L2-02
**Given:** mass of the weight $m_1 = 0.30\,\text{kg}$, the spring stays the same, the period doubles: $T_2 = 2T_1$
**Find:** the mass to be added $\Delta m$ in $\text{kg}$
**Principle:** The period of a mass on a spring is proportional to the square root of the mass when the spring constant stays the same. Doubling the period therefore requires four times the mass.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \sqrt{\dfrac{m_2}{m_1}}$, so $m_2 = \left(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^2 m_1 = 4m_1$ and $\Delta m = m_2 - m_1 = 3m_1$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\Delta m = 3 \cdot 0.30\,\text{kg} = 0.90\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $0.90\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures as in the mass $0.30\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The total mass is $1.20\,\text{kg}$, four times the original, and $\sqrt{4} = 2$. A common mistake is to double the mass, but then the period would grow by a factor of only $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.4$.

## fy5-9-L2-03
**Given:** mass $m = 0.40\,\text{kg}$, spring constant $k = 25\,\text{N/m}$, amplitude $A = 8.0\,\text{cm}$, frictionless track, released from rest
**Find:** the greatest speed $v_\text{max}$ in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** There is no friction on the track, so the mechanical energy of the oscillator is conserved. At the extreme position all the energy is the elastic potential energy of the spring, at the equilibrium position all of it is kinetic energy, and there the speed is greatest.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{1}{2}mv_\text{max}^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$, so $v_\text{max} = A\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$
**Conversions:** $8.0\,\text{cm} = 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.080\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $v_\text{max} = 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{25\,\text{N/m}}{0.40\,\text{kg}}} = 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot 7.906\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} = 0.6325\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $0.63\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the data.
**Reasonableness:** Energy check: $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 25\,\text{N/m} \cdot (0.080\,\text{m})^2 = 0.080\,\text{J}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.40\,\text{kg} \cdot (0.6325\,\text{m/s})^2 = 0.080\,\text{J}$. At its fastest the cart moves a little over half a metre per second, which is usual on an air track.

## fy5-9-L2-04
**Given:** period on the Earth $T_\text{E} = 2.00\,\text{s}$, $g_\text{E} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, on Mars $g_\text{Mars} = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$, small swings
**Find:** the period on Mars $T_\text{Mars}$ in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** For small swings the period of a pendulum is $2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$. The length of the pendulum stays the same, so the period is inversely proportional to the square root of the acceleration of free fall. The length does not need to be calculated.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{T_\text{Mars}}{T_\text{E}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{g_\text{E}}{g_\text{Mars}}}$, so $T_\text{Mars} = T_\text{E}\sqrt{\dfrac{g_\text{E}}{g_\text{Mars}}}$
**Conversions:** The unit of both accelerations of free fall is $\text{m/s}^2$, and the unit cancels, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $T_\text{Mars} = 2.00\,\text{s} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{3.71\,\text{m/s}^2}} = 2.00\,\text{s} \cdot \sqrt{2.644} = 2.00\,\text{s} \cdot 1.626 = 3.252\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $3.25\,\text{s}$, three significant figures as in the period $2.00\,\text{s}$ and the acceleration of free fall $3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** On Mars the restoring force is smaller, so the pendulum swings more slowly and the period increases. The acceleration of free fall is about a third, and $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.7$, so the period is about $1.6$ to $1.7$ times as large.

## fy5-9-L3-01
**Given:** mass $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, spring constant $k = 200\,\text{N/m}$, amplitude $A = 0.100\,\text{m}$, displacement $x = 0.0500\,\text{m}$, frictionless oscillation
**Find:** the speed $v$ at the displacement $x$ in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** In frictionless oscillation the mechanical energy is conserved. At every point the sum of the kinetic energy and the elastic potential energy of the spring equals the energy at the extreme position, where the speed is zero. At the displacement $x$ some of the energy is still in the spring, so the speed is smaller than at the equilibrium position.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$, so $v^2 = \dfrac{k}{m}(A^2 - x^2)$ and $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}(A^2 - x^2)}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** First $\dfrac{k}{m} = \dfrac{200\,\text{N/m}}{0.50\,\text{kg}} = 400\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$ and $A^2 - x^2 = (0.100\,\text{m})^2 - (0.0500\,\text{m})^2 = 0.00750\,\text{m}^2$, so $v = \sqrt{400\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.00750\,\text{m}^2} = \sqrt{3.00\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 1.732\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $1.7\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** At the equilibrium position the speed would be $A\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{m}} = 0.100\,\text{m} \cdot 20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$. At half the amplitude the spring holds only a quarter of the energy, because $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so three quarters of it is kinetic energy and the speed is $\sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.87$ times the greatest, that is, $1.7\,\text{m/s}$. So the speed is not half of the greatest.

## fy5-9-L3-02
**Given:** mass of the passenger $m_\text{p} = 80\,\text{kg}$, mass of the car $1200\,\text{kg}$, sinking $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; model: one body on one spring, no damping
**Find:** the period $T$ of the oscillation of the car body in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** Two ideas. First the spring constant: when the passenger has sat down and the car is at rest again, the extra force of the spring equals the weight of the passenger, so $k\Delta x = m_\text{p}g$. Then the period: in vertical oscillation the weight only shifts the equilibrium position, so the period is $2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$, where $m$ is the whole oscillating mass, the car and the passenger.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{m_\text{p}g}{\Delta x}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$, so $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m\,\Delta x}{m_\text{p}g}}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$; the oscillating mass $m = 1200\,\text{kg} + 80\,\text{kg} = 1280\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.030\,\text{m}} = 26\,160\,\text{N/m}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1280\,\text{kg}}{26\,160\,\text{N/m}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.04893\,\text{s}^2} = 2\pi \cdot 0.2212\,\text{s} = 1.390\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $1.4\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the sinking $3.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** Cars rock on their suspension with periods of about a second, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. In reality the shock absorbers damp the oscillation to one or two swings, and each wheel has its own spring, so the model gives only the order of magnitude.

## fy5-9-L3-03
**Given:** in spring the length of the pendulum $l = 0.994\,\text{m}$, in summer it is longer by $\Delta l = 0.50\,\text{mm}$, small swings, a day $t = 86\,400\,\text{s}$
**Find:** how much the clock runs slow in a day, $\Delta t$ in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** For small swings the period is $2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, so a longer pendulum swings more slowly. The clock counts cycles and adds the period $T$ it had in spring for each of them, although the real cycle lasts $T'$. In a day it manages to make only $\tfrac{t}{T'}$ of the longer cycles $T'$, so it shows the time $\tfrac{t}{T'}T$ and falls behind. The acceleration of free fall cancels in the ratio of the periods.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{T'}{T} = \sqrt{\dfrac{l + \Delta l}{l}}$ and $\Delta t = t - \dfrac{t}{T'}T$, so $\Delta t = t\left(1 - \dfrac{T}{T'}\right) = t\left(1 - \sqrt{\dfrac{l}{l + \Delta l}}\right)$
**Conversions:** $\Delta l = 0.50\,\text{mm} = 0.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m} = 0.00050\,\text{m}$; a day $t = 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 86\,400\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $\Delta t = 86\,400\,\text{s} \cdot \left(1 - \sqrt{\dfrac{0.994\,\text{m}}{0.9945\,\text{m}}}\right) = 86\,400\,\text{s} \cdot (1 - 0.999\,749) = 86\,400\,\text{s} \cdot 0.000\,251 = 21.7\,\text{s}$. The intermediate result must not be rounded: even rounding $1 - 0.9997 = 0.0003$ would give $26\,\text{s}$.
**Answer:** $22\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the lengthening $0.50\,\text{mm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The length increases by the relative amount $\tfrac{0.50\,\text{mm}}{994\,\text{mm}} \approx 0.050\,\%$, and because of the square root the period increases by about half of that, $0.025\,\%$. Of a day that is $0.00025 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s} \approx 22\,\text{s}$. That is why accurate pendulum clocks used pendulums that compensate for changes in temperature.

## fy5-9-L1-06
**Find:** how the period of a pendulum changes when the bob is replaced with one twice as heavy.
**Principle:** For small swings the period of a pendulum is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, and the formula contains only the length and the acceleration of free fall. A greater weight acts on a heavier bob, but the inertia of the bob increases in the same proportion, so the acceleration stays the same.
**Model answer:** The period stays the same. A restoring force twice as great acts on a bob twice as heavy, but the bob is also twice as hard to accelerate, so the acceleration $a = \tfrac{F}{m}$ does not change. The same phenomenon can be seen in free fall, where a heavy and a light body fall equally fast. The period does not get shorter or longer, and the greatest speed depends on the starting height, not on the mass.

## fy5-9-L1-07
**Find:** how the period of a body on a spring changes when the spring constant doubles and the mass stays the same.
**Principle:** The period of a body on a spring is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$. The spring constant is in the denominator inside the square root, so the period does not change in the same proportion but by the square root of it.
**Model answer:** The period gets shorter, because a stiffer spring accelerates the body more: at the same displacement the restoring force is greater and the body returns faster. When $k$ doubles, the period decreases by the factor $\sqrt{2}$, to about $0.71$ times its value. The period therefore does not halve, because it is not inversely proportional to the spring constant but to its square root. The period does not stay the same or get longer, because besides the mass the spring constant also affects it.

## fy5-9-L1-08
**Find:** how the period changes when the amplitude of a body oscillating on a spring doubles.
**Principle:** In simple harmonic motion the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement, and the period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ does not contain the amplitude. A greater amplitude means a longer distance but also a greater force.
**Model answer:** The period stays the same. When the amplitude doubles, the body travels a distance twice as long, but at the start the force of the spring is twice as great and the body reaches a greater speed. The greater speed compensates for the longer distance exactly so that one cycle takes the same time. The energy becomes four times as great, because $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$, but this does not affect the period.

## fy5-9-L2-05
**Given:** the points of the sample data $(l;\ T)$: $(0.20\,\text{m};\ 0.90\,\text{s})$, $(0.40\,\text{m};\ 1.27\,\text{s})$, $(0.60\,\text{m};\ 1.55\,\text{s})$, $(0.80\,\text{m};\ 1.80\,\text{s})$ and $(1.00\,\text{m};\ 2.01\,\text{s})$; small swings

The square of the period is directly proportional to the length, and the points $(l, T^2)$ lie on a line through the origin whose slope is $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$. The line passes through the origin because the period of a pendulum of zero length is zero.
**Reading the graph:** A suitable scale for the axes is $0$–$1.0\,\text{m}$ and $0$–$4.5\,\text{s}^2$. The squares of the periods are $0.81\,\text{s}^2$, $1.61\,\text{s}^2$, $2.40\,\text{s}^2$, $3.24\,\text{s}^2$ and $4.04\,\text{s}^2$. They lie on a rising line with points on both sides of it. The fitted line passes through the origin and approximately through the point $(1.00\,\text{m};\ 4.04\,\text{s}^2)$. The slope is read from points of the fitted line that are far apart, not from adjacent measured points.
**Formula:** $a = \dfrac{T^2}{l} = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$, so $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{a}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the length is in metres and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{4.04\,\text{s}^2}{1.00\,\text{m}} = 4.04\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$ and $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{4.04\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}} = 9.772\,\text{m/s}^2$

**Reasonableness:** An acceleration of free fall of $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ would give the slope $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{9.81} = 4.02\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$, which is close to the value read, $4.04\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$. The result differs from the table value by about $0.4\,\%$. When the result is substituted into the formula for the period, a length of $0.60\,\text{m}$ gives $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.60 : 9.772}\,\text{s} = 1.56\,\text{s}$, as in the table, $1.55\,\text{s}$.

## fy5-9-L2-07

## fy5-9-L3-04
**Given:** the points of the sample data $(m;\ T)$: $(0.050\,\text{kg};\ 0.334\,\text{s})$, $(0.100\,\text{kg};\ 0.433\,\text{s})$, $(0.150\,\text{kg};\ 0.521\,\text{s})$, $(0.200\,\text{kg};\ 0.587\,\text{s})$, $(0.250\,\text{kg};\ 0.656\,\text{s})$ and $(0.300\,\text{kg};\ 0.708\,\text{s})$; model $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{k}(m + m_0)$

The line does not pass through the origin, because the mass of the spring oscillates along with the weight. The spring constant is therefore obtained from the slope, and $m_0$ from the ratio of the constant term to the slope.
**Reading the graph:** The squares of the periods are $0.112\,\text{s}^2$, $0.187\,\text{s}^2$, $0.271\,\text{s}^2$, $0.345\,\text{s}^2$, $0.430\,\text{s}^2$ and $0.501\,\text{s}^2$. ## fy5-9-L3-05
**Given:** length of the pendulum $l = 1.20\,\text{m}$, starting angle $\alpha_0 = 10.0\,{}^\circ$, released from rest, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; point-like bob, the mass of the string and air resistance are neglected
**Find:** a) the time from the extreme position to the lowest point, b) the greatest speed $v$ in $\text{m/s}$ and the height of the extreme position, c) the speed with the harmonic model and the difference of the results
**Principle:** Three ideas. With a small starting angle the period is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, and the time from the extreme position to the lowest point is a quarter of a cycle. Energy is conserved, because only the weight does work and the string is always perpendicular to the motion, so the potential energy at the extreme position changes into kinetic energy at the lowest point. In the harmonic model $\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{mg}{l}A^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, and it is an approximation, because it uses $\sin\alpha \approx \alpha$.
**Formula:** $t = \dfrac{T}{4}$; $h = l(1 - \cos\alpha_0)$ and $mgh = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$; with the harmonic model $A = l\alpha_0$ and $v_\text{h} = A\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{l}}$
**Conversions:** The angle in radians: $\alpha_0 = 10.0\,{}^\circ = 10.0 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{180} = 0.17453$.
**Substitution:** Part a): $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1.20\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}} = 2.198\,\text{s}$, so $t = \dfrac{2.198\,\text{s}}{4} = 0.549\,\text{s}$. Part b): $h = 1.20\,\text{m} \cdot (1 - \cos 10.0\,{}^\circ) = 0.01823\,\text{m}$ and $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.01823\,\text{m}} = 0.5981\,\text{m/s}$. Part c): $A = 1.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.17453 = 0.2094\,\text{m}$ and $v_\text{h} = 0.2094\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{9.81}{1.20}}\,\dfrac{1}{\text{s}} = 0.5988\,\text{m/s}$, so the difference is $\dfrac{0.5988 - 0.5981}{0.5981} \cdot 100\,\% = 0.13\,\%$ and the harmonic model gives the greater speed.
**Answer:** $0.598\,\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the length $1.20\,\text{m}$ and the angle $10.0\,{}^\circ$.
**Reasonableness:** The kinetic energy per unit mass is $\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 = 0.1788\,\text{J/kg}$ and the decrease in potential energy is $gh = 9.81 \cdot 0.01823 = 0.1788\,\text{J/kg}$, so energy is conserved. The harmonic model gives a result that differs by only $0.13\,\%$, because at a small angle $\sin\alpha$ is almost the same as $\alpha$ in radians. The difference is positive, because the model uses $mg\alpha$ as the restoring force, which is greater than the real $mg\sin\alpha$: the model does slightly more work, so the speed is slightly greater ($0.5988\,\text{m/s} > 0.5981\,\text{m/s}$). The same order of magnitude can be seen in the table of the lesson: at the starting angle $10\,{}^\circ$ the period differs from the formula by only $0.2\,\%$.

## fy5-9-L2-08
**Find:** how the periods of a pendulum and a spring oscillator change when they are taken from Earth to the Moon, and which justification explains it.
**Principle:** The period of a spring oscillator is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$. The force of the spring $F = -kx$ depends only on the displacement, and in a vertical spring the weight only shifts the equilibrium position, so $g$ does not affect the period. In a pendulum the restoring force is the component of the weight along the path, $mg\sin\alpha$. The mass cancels, and for small swings $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, that is, the period is proportional to $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{g}}$.
**Model answer:** The mass $m$ and the spring constant $k$ of the spring oscillator are the same on the Moon as on Earth, so its period stays $1.00\,\text{s}$. The spring stretches less on the Moon, but that only changes the position of the equilibrium.

The length of the pendulum stays the same, so the ratio of the periods depends only on the accelerations of free fall:

$$\begin{aligned}
&\frac{T_\text{M}}{T_\text{E}} = \sqrt{\frac{g_\text{E}}{g_\text{M}}} \\
&\phantom{\frac{T_\text{M}}{T_\text{E}}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2}} \\
&\phantom{\frac{T_\text{M}}{T_\text{E}}} \approx 2.461
\end{aligned}$$

$T_\text{M} = 2.461 \cdot 1.00\,\text{s} \approx 2.46\,\text{s}$

So the period of the pendulum becomes about $2.5$ times as long, and the period of the spring stays the same. Justification: the restoring force of the pendulum is a part of the weight, which is smaller on the Moon, but the mass to be accelerated does not decrease. The force of the spring depends only on the displacement and the spring constant. The acceleration of free fall on the Moon is about one sixth of the value on Earth, but because of the square root the period becomes only about $\sqrt{6.1} \approx 2.5$ times as long, not six times.
