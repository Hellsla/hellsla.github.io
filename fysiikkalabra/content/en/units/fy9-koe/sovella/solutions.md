## fy9-koe-s1

### a)
**Given:** mass $m = 82.0\,\text{kg}$, increase in height $h = 45.0\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the increase in potential energy $\Delta E_\text{p}$
**Principle:** Near the Earth the gravitational potential energy grows with height when a body rises. The zero level is chosen at the bottom of the road, so the increase in potential energy depends only on the difference in height, not on the length or the shape of the road.
**Formula:** $\Delta E_\text{p} = mgh$
**Conversions:** None needed: the mass is in kilograms and the height in metres. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta E_\text{p} = 82.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 45.0\,\text{m} = 36\,198.9\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $\Delta E_\text{p} \approx 36.2\,\text{kJ}$, three significant figures like the mass and the height.
**Reasonableness:** Roughly $80 \cdot 10 \cdot 45\,\text{J} = 36\,\text{kJ}$. The result is positive, because the cyclist rises, and it is about the same as the energy a $2\,\text{kW}$ kettle uses in $18\,\text{s}$.

### b)
**Given:** $\Delta E_\text{p} = 36\,198.9\,\text{J}$ (part a), time $t = 4\,\text{min}\ 30\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the average power needed for lifting $P_\text{lift}$
**Principle:** Power is the work done per unit time. The increase in potential energy is divided by the time the climb takes.
**Formula:** $P_\text{lift} = \dfrac{\Delta E_\text{p}}{t}$
**Conversions:** $t = 4 \cdot 60\,\text{s} + 30\,\text{s} = 270\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $P_\text{lift} = \dfrac{36\,198.9\,\text{J}}{270\,\text{s}} = 134.07\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $P_\text{lift} \approx 134\,\text{W}$, three significant figures like the result of part a.
**Reasonableness:** A fit amateur can keep up $100$–$250\,\text{W}$ for a long time, so lifting alone already takes a large part of the power.

### c)
**Given:** $\Delta E_\text{p} = 36\,198.9\,\text{J}$, resistive force $F_\text{r} = 18\,\text{N}$, length of the road $s = 1.20\,\text{km}$, $t = 270\,\text{s}$; speed constant
**Find:** the average mechanical power $P$ of the cyclist
**Principle:** By the work–energy principle the work $W$ the muscles do on the pedals covers the increase in potential energy and the energy the resistive forces turn into heat. The kinetic energy does not change, because the speed stays the same. The resistive forces act along the whole road, so their work is calculated with the length of the road, not the height.
**Formula:** $W = \Delta E_\text{p} + F_\text{r} s$ and $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$, so $P = \dfrac{\Delta E_\text{p} + F_\text{r} s}{t}$
**Conversions:** $s = 1.20\,\text{km} = 1.20 \cdot 1000\,\text{m} = 1200\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $F_\text{r} s = 18\,\text{N} \cdot 1200\,\text{m} = 21\,600\,\text{J}$ and $P = \dfrac{36\,198.9\,\text{J} + 21\,600\,\text{J}}{270\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{57\,798.9\,\text{J}}{270\,\text{s}} = 214.07\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $P \approx 210\,\text{W}$, two significant figures, because the resistive force $18\,\text{N}$ is known to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The power is larger than the $134\,\text{W}$ of part b, as it should be, because the resistive forces take more. The share of the resistive forces is $80\,\text{W}$, about a third, which fits a slow uphill ride where air resistance is small.

### d)
**Given:** mechanical work $W = 57\,798.9\,\text{J}$ (part c), efficiency of the muscles $\eta = 22\,\% = 0.22$
**Find:** the energy $E$ used from food in kilojoules
**Principle:** Efficiency is the useful energy divided by the energy used. The useful part is the work done on the pedals, and the energy used comes from food. The rest becomes heat in the muscles.
**Formula:** $\eta = \dfrac{W}{E}$, so $E = \dfrac{W}{\eta}$
**Conversions:** $22\,\% = 0.22$ and finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{57\,798.9\,\text{J}}{0.22} = 262\,722\,\text{J} = 262.7\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $E \approx 260\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures like the efficiency $22\,\%$ and the resistive force.
**Reasonableness:** The energy from food is about $4.5$ times the mechanical work, which matches the efficiency $\tfrac{1}{0.22} \approx 4.5$. Most of the energy heats the body, which the cyclist feels as sweating.

### e)
**Find:** whether the claim is true that the climb uses up the energy $2200\,\text{kJ}$ of a chocolate bar
**Principle:** The result of part d is compared with the energy of the bar, and what the model leaves out is assessed.
**Model answer:** The climb uses about $260\,\text{kJ}$ of energy from food. That is $\tfrac{262.7\,\text{kJ}}{2200\,\text{kJ}} \approx 0.12$, about $12\,\%$ of the energy of the bar, so the energy of the bar equals about eight similar climbs. The model assumes a constant speed, an average resistive force and a muscle efficiency of $22\,\%$. It does not include the resting metabolism, which the body uses anyway, or braking on the way down. Even if the efficiency were smaller, for example $18\,\%$, the consumption would be only about $320\,\text{kJ}$. So the claim is not true: one climb uses only a small part of the energy of the bar.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part c the work of the resistive force is calculated with the height $45.0\,\text{m}$, although the force acts along the whole length of the road $1200\,\text{m}$.
- In part c the potential energy is forgotten and only the power of the resistive forces calculated, or the kinetic energy is added, although the speed does not change.
- In part d the work is multiplied by the efficiency, so the energy from food comes out smaller than the mechanical work.
- The time is left in minutes or written as $4.30\,\text{min}$, although $4\,\text{min}\ 30\,\text{s} = 4.5\,\text{min} = 270\,\text{s}$.
- In part e the answer only says "not true" without comparing the numbers or mentioning the assumptions of the model.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part c the answer $214\,\text{W}$ is accepted if the precision is justified by the result of part a; the full point for precision needs the justification.
- In part e the conclusion may also be based on the ratio "the energy of the bar is enough for about eight climbs".

### a)
**Find:** why the time of ten periods is measured and not of one
**Principle:** In hand timing the starting and stopping of the watch are late or early by the reaction time. The error arises at the start and at the end, not in every period.
**Model answer:** The uncertainty of hand timing is about $0.2\,\text{s}$, which is as much as $20\,\%$ of one period of about $1\,\text{s}$. When the time of ten periods is measured, the same uncertainty is shared by ten periods: the uncertainty of the period is about $0.02\,\text{s}$ and the relative uncertainty a tenth. In addition, in a long time it is easier to notice if the watch was started at the wrong moment.

### c)
**Given:** the line fitted in part b, $T^2$ as a function of the length $L$
**Find:** the free-fall acceleration $g$
**Principle:** By the pendulum model $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\,l$, so the slope of the line is $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$. The slope is read from the fitted line and not from single points, because the line gathers the information of all the measurements.
**Reading the graph:** Two points far apart are read from the line: $(0.100\,\text{m};\ 0.46\,\text{s}^2)$ and $(0.900\,\text{m};\ 3.68\,\text{s}^2)$. The slope is $k = \tfrac{\Delta (T^2)}{\Delta L}$.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$, so $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the lengths are in metres and the times in seconds, so $k$ is in the unit $\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$ and $g$ in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{3.68\,\text{s}^2 - 0.46\,\text{s}^2}{0.900\,\text{m} - 0.100\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{3.22\,\text{s}^2}{0.800\,\text{m}} = 4.025\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$ and $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{4.025\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}} = 9.808\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures, because the value $0.46\,\text{s}^2$ read from the line has two digits.
**Reasonableness:** The result is close to the value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ (the difference is smaller than the reading uncertainty). The uncertainty of reading the graph, about $\pm 0.05\,\text{s}^2$ at each point, changes the result by at most about $\pm 0.3\,\text{m/s}^2$.

### d)
**Find:** why the line does not pass through the origin and whether it affects the result of part c
**Principle:** In the model $l$ is the length of the pendulum from the point of suspension to the centre of mass of the bob. If $L$ differs from it by a constant amount, the line shifts but its slope does not change.
**Model answer:** The line crosses the vertical axis at about $0.06\,\text{s}^2$, and extended it crosses the $L$ axis at about $L \approx -1.5\,\text{cm}$. The reason is the way of measuring: $L$ was measured to the top of the bob, but the length of the pendulum reaches to the centre of the bob. So every length is too short by the radius of the bob, about $1.5\,\text{cm}$. The error is systematic and equal in every measurement, so it shifts all the points equally to the left. The slope does not change, so the result of part c is reliable. If $g$ were calculated from a single point with the formula $g = \tfrac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2}$, the result would be too small: with the shortest string about $9.1\,\text{m/s}^2$. Using the slope removes this error.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- $T$ is plotted as a function of the length $L$ and a straight line is fitted to the points, although the points lie on a curve.
- $t_{10}$ is squared and divided only by ten instead of a hundred, so $T^2$ is ten times too large.
- The line is forced through the origin, which changes the slope and $g$.
- The slope is calculated from two neighbouring measured points or read from one point by dividing $T^2 : L$.
- In part d the offset is explained by random errors, although all the points have shifted in the same direction.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b the graph $(T^2, L)$, that is the axes the other way round: then the slope is $\tfrac{g}{4\pi^2}$ and $g = 4\pi^2 k$.
- In part c the slope $4.03\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$ of the program's fit; results of $9.6$–$10.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ are accepted when the reading is shown.

## fy9-koe-s3

### a)
**Find:** how the structure of the flask slows down the cooling for each way of heat transfer
**Principle:** Heat is transferred by conduction, by convection and as thermal radiation. Because of the temperature difference there is a net transfer of energy from warmer to colder, so the flask has to slow down every way.
**Model answer:** *Conduction.* Energy is conducted when particles collide and give kinetic energy to their neighbours. Between the walls there is a vacuum with no particles, so energy is not conducted from the inner wall to the outer wall. *Convection.* In convection a warmed liquid or gas flows and carries energy with it. A vacuum has no air that could flow, and the stopper stops the flow of warm air and steam at the mouth of the flask. *Thermal radiation.* Thermal radiation also travels through a vacuum. The silvered surfaces reflect most of the radiation from the side of the tea back, and they also radiate little themselves. *Evaporation.* The stopper stops water vapour from escaping. Evaporation would take energy from the tea. There is still a net transfer of energy from the tea to the surroundings, but slowly: mainly through the mouth and the stopper and through the joint of the walls, where the glass conducts heat.

### b)
**Find:** why the tea cools more slowly in the afternoon
**Principle:** The heat flow from the tea to the surroundings grows when the temperature difference between the tea and the surroundings grows.
**Model answer:** In the morning the temperature difference between the tea and the outdoor air is about $65\,\text{K}$, in the afternoon only about $50\,\text{K}$. When the temperature difference decreases, a smaller heat flow passes through the walls and the stopper of the flask. The smaller the heat flow leaving the tea, the more slowly its temperature falls, because giving off the same heat takes longer. So the cooling slows down all the time, and the tea approaches the outdoor temperature ever more slowly.

### c)
**Given:** $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, the temperature falls from $85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, time $t = 6.0\,\text{h}$
**Find:** the average heat flow $P$ with which energy is transferred from the tea to the surroundings
**Principle:** The tea cools and its state does not change, so the heat it gives off is $Q = cm\Delta T$. The stopper is closed, so evaporation is not taken into account. The average power is the heat given off divided by the time.
**Formula:** $Q = cm\Delta T$ and $P = \dfrac{Q}{t}$, so $P = \dfrac{cm\Delta T}{t}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 15.0\,\text{K}$ and $t = 6.0 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 21\,600\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $Q = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 15.0\,\text{K} = 31\,425\,\text{J}$ and $P = \dfrac{31\,425\,\text{J}}{21\,600\,\text{s}} = 1.4549\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $P \approx 1.5\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$ and the time $6.0\,\text{h}$.
**Reasonableness:** The power is very small: in an open mug the same tea would cool as much in about ten minutes, which would be tens of watts. That is why the vacuum flask keeps the tea hot all day.

### d)
**Find:** whether the vacuum flask also keeps ice water cold
**Principle:** A vacuum flask produces neither cold nor heat; it slows down the transfer of energy in either direction.
**Model answer:** Yes. When the flask holds ice water, there is a net transfer of energy from the warm surroundings to the cold water. The vacuum stops conduction and convection in the same way as with the tea, and the mirror surfaces reflect the thermal radiation from the surroundings back outwards. If the temperature difference is about the same, energy is transferred about as slowly. As long as there is ice in the water, the energy transferred goes into melting the ice, and the water stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a it is said that the vacuum also stops thermal radiation, although radiation travels through a vacuum; it is reduced by the mirror surfaces.
- "Cold is transferred" into the flask; there is always a net transfer of energy from warmer to colder.
- In part c the time is left in hours, so the power comes out as over $5000\,\text{W}$.
- In part c the temperature difference used is between the tea and the outdoor air, not the change in the temperature of the tea.
- In part b the only explanation is that "the tea has already cooled", without mentioning the connection between the temperature difference and the heat flow.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part c the result $1.45\,\text{W}$, if the precision is justified by the temperature difference $15.0\,\text{K}$; two figures are better justified, because the mass and the time are known to two figures.

## fy9-koe-s4

### a)
**Find:** a plan for an experiment that finds out how the efficiency depends on the amount of water
**Principle:** In an experiment one variable is changed, its effect is measured, and the other factors that affect the result are kept constant. The efficiency is the heat transferred to the water divided by the electrical energy used.
**Model answer:** *Variables.* The quantity changed is the mass of the water, for example $0.300$, $0.500$, $0.750$, $1.00$ and $1.50\,\text{kg}$. The quantity measured as the result is the efficiency $\eta = \tfrac{cm\Delta T}{E}$. *Constants.* The same kettle and the same initial temperature are used, for example tap water whose temperature has been measured, and the water is always heated to the same final temperature, for example to $90\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, before boiling, so that little water evaporates. The lid is closed, and the kettle is cooled to room temperature between the measurements so that it is not already warm. *Measurements.* The water is weighed in the kettle on the scales (the mass of the kettle subtracted), the initial temperature is measured before heating and the final temperature right after switching off, stirred. The electrical energy is read from the energy meter, which is reset before every measurement. *Repeats.* Every amount of water is measured three times, and the mean and the scatter of the efficiencies are calculated.

### b)
**Given:** $m = 0.500\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 15.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_2 = 95.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, electrical energy $E = 0.054\,\text{kWh}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ (from the table)
**Find:** the efficiency $\eta$ of the kettle
**Principle:** The heat transferred to the water counts as useful. The water warms up without a change of state, so $Q = cm\Delta T$. The energy used is the reading of the energy meter.
**Formula:** $\eta = \dfrac{Q}{E} = \dfrac{cm(T_2 - T_1)}{E}$
**Conversions:** $E = 0.054\,\text{kWh} = 0.054 \cdot 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 194\,400\,\text{J}$ and $T_2 - T_1 = 80.0\,\text{K}$
**Substitution:** $Q = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.500\,\text{kg} \cdot 80.0\,\text{K} = 167\,600\,\text{J}$ and $\eta = \dfrac{167\,600\,\text{J}}{194\,400\,\text{J}} = 0.8621$
**Answer:** $\eta \approx 86\,\%$, two significant figures like the reading of the energy meter $0.054\,\text{kWh}$.
**Reasonableness:** The efficiency is below $100\,\%$, because part of the energy heats the body and the element of the kettle and goes to the surroundings. A check with the power: a $2.0\,\text{kW}$ kettle uses $194\,400\,\text{J}$ in about $97\,\text{s}$, which fits experience of boiling half a litre.

### c)
**Find:** the sources of error of the experiment and ways to reduce their effect
**Principle:** A systematic error changes every result in the same direction, and repeating does not remove it; random variation is reduced by repeating.
**Model answer:** *Evaporation.* Near the boiling point water evaporates, and evaporation takes up energy that is not counted as useful. The efficiency looks too small. Remedy: heat only to, for example, $90\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and keep the lid closed. *The temperature of the kettle.* If the kettle is warm from the previous measurement, less energy goes into heating its body and the efficiency looks larger. Remedy: cool the kettle and rinse it with cold water between the measurements. *Measuring the temperature.* The water has different temperatures in different places. Remedy: stir before the reading. *The precision of the energy meter.* The last digit of the reading, $0.001\,\text{kWh}$, is about $2\,\%$ of the reading $0.054\,\text{kWh}$, and an even larger share with a small amount of water. Remedy: a larger rise in temperature or repeats whose mean is calculated.

### d)
**Find:** how to present the results and a result that would show a dependence
**Principle:** A dependence shows in a graph with the quantity changed on the horizontal axis and the result on the vertical axis. A difference is real only if it is larger than the uncertainty of the measurements.
**Model answer:** The results are presented as a table and as an $(m, \eta)$ graph: the mass of the water $m\ (\text{kg})$ on the horizontal axis and the efficiency $\eta\ (\%)$ on the vertical axis. The scatter of the repeats is marked at every point as an error bar. If the points rise clearly as the amount of water grows and the rise is larger than the error bars, the efficiency depends on the amount of water. The physical explanation would be that the energy used to heat the body and the element of the kettle is a larger part of the total energy with a small amount of water. If the points lie on a horizontal line within the error bars, a dependence cannot be shown, but that does not prove there is none.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The energy used is calculated from the rated power of the kettle and the time, although the real power may differ from the rated one; the energy meter measures it directly.
- The water is heated until it boils, and the final temperature is always written as $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ without measuring.
- The plan changes the amount of water and the initial temperature at the same time, so their effects cannot be separated.
- In part b the kilowatt-hours are not converted to joules, so the efficiency comes out in millions.
- In part d the conclusion is drawn from one measurement with each amount of water, without repeats.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a the electrical energy can also be found by measuring the time with a stopwatch, if the real power of the kettle has first been measured with the energy meter; the plan must then justify why the power is constant.
- In part b the answer $0.86$ without a percent sign.
