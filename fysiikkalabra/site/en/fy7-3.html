Magnetic field of a wire and a coil

Why can the magnet of a scrapyard crane lift a tonne of scrap car into the air and drop it at the press of a button, when a fridge magnet never lets go?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • use the right-hand rule to work out the direction of the magnetic field of a straight wire and of a coil.
  • calculate the magnetic flux density of a straight wire and of a long coil.
  • determine the permeability of free space and its uncertainty from a straight line fitted to measurements.

Prior knowledge

  • The direction of a magnetic field is the direction of the north end of a compass needle, and outside a bar magnet the field lines go from the north pole to the south polechapter Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field
  • The unit of magnetic flux density $B$ is the tesla, and the Earth's magnetic field in Finland is about $50\,\mu\text{T}$chapter Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

A compass lies on a table, and a north–south copper wire runs above it. When a current of $3\,\text{A}$ is switched on in the wire, the needle turns almost across the wire while the wire is directly above the needle. When the direction of the current is reversed, the needle turns the other way, and when the current is switched off, the needle goes back to pointing north. Copper is not a magnetic material: a copper nail does not stick to a magnet.

Where does the magnetic field that turns the compass come from, and how can the same effect be made so strong that it lifts a scrap car?

Optional Revise: a compass in a magnetic field
A compass is placed next to a bar magnet. In which direction does the north end of the compass needle turn?

  • Perpendicularity applies to the force on a moving charge (chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields). A compass needle is a magnetic dipole, and it turns to point along the field.

  • Near a magnet its field is usually much stronger than the Earth's field (about $50\,\mu\text{T}$), so the needle turns along the field line of the magnet.

  • Like poles repel each other. The north end of the needle turns along the field line, and outside a magnet the field lines go from the north pole to the south pole.

  • Correct. A compass is a small magnet that turns to point along the field; that is why it can be used to explore the direction of the field (chapter Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field). In this lesson the compass will also reveal the field of a current-carrying wire.

Assumptions of the model

The model calculates the field only on the axis of the coil by adding together the fields of all the turns. The coil has no iron core, and the Earth's magnetic field is not included. How the calculation works

At the centre of the coil and on its axis at a distance $x$ from the centre, the model calculates

$$\begin{aligned} B &= \frac{\mu_0\, N I}{2 l}\,(k_1 - k_2) \\ k_1 &= \frac{x + \tfrac{l}{2}}{\sqrt{(x + \tfrac{l}{2})^2 + R^2}} \\ k_2 &= \frac{x - \tfrac{l}{2}}{\sqrt{(x - \tfrac{l}{2})^2 + R^2}} \end{aligned}$$

where $R = 2.0\,\text{cm}$ is the radius of the coil and $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$. When the coil is much longer than its radius, the value at the centre is almost $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$: for example, with the default values $1.23\,\text{mT}$, when the formula for a long coil gives $1.26\,\text{mT}$. The turn density is $n = N : l$.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    An electric current creates a magnetic field

    In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed during a lecture that a compass needle turned when a current was switched on in a wire next to it. When the current was switched off, the needle went back to pointing north. The observation joined electricity and magnetism: every current-carrying wire has a magnetic field around it. The field is created by moving charges, so the wire does not have to be made of a magnetic material; even a copper wire creates a field. When the current grows, the field becomes stronger, and when the direction of the current is reversed, the direction of the field is reversed too.

    The field lines of a straight wire are concentric circles around the wire, perpendicular to it. The field therefore does not point away from the wire or towards it, but goes round it. Compasses placed around the wire turn along the tangent of the circle when the field of the wire is much stronger than the Earth's magnetic field. The farther from the wire we are, the weaker the field.

    The right-hand rule

    The direction in which the field goes round is given by the right-hand rule: when the thumb of the right hand points in the direction of the current, the curled fingers go round in the direction of the field lines. In the figure the current goes upwards, so seen from above the field goes round anticlockwise. In figures a current towards the viewer is marked with a dot $\odot$ and a current away from the viewer with a cross $\otimes$, like the tip and the tail feathers of an arrow.

    The field of a coil

    When a wire is wound into a spiral, called a coil, the field of each turn points the same way inside the coil, and the fields strengthen each other. Inside a long coil the field lines are almost straight and evenly spaced: the field is almost uniform and parallel to the axis of the coil. At the ends of the coil the field lines spread out, and outside the coil the field is like that of a bar magnet in the chapter Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field. The end from which the field lines emerge is the north pole N. The right-hand rule works for a coil like this: when the fingers curl along the turns in the direction of the current, the thumb points inside the coil in the direction of the field, that is, towards the north pole.

    The electromagnet

    When an iron core is pushed inside a coil, the field becomes many times stronger. The field of the coil turns the magnetic domains of the iron to point the same way, and the magnetised iron adds its own field to the field of the coil. A coil like this is an electromagnet. When the current is switched off, the field of the coil disappears, and the domains of soft iron return almost to disorder, so the core loses almost all of its magnetisation. That is why the scrapyard crane holds the scrap only as long as the current flows. A permanent magnet, by contrast, is made of a material whose domains stay aligned, so it cannot be switched off.

    Magnetic field of a straight wire and of a coil

    The magnetic flux density of a long straight wire at a distance $r$ from the wire is

    $$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$

    where $B$ is the magnetic flux density (T), $I$ the current in the wire (A), $r$ the distance from the wire (m) and $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$ the permeability of free space, a constant of nature. In air the same value is used. The field is directly proportional to the current and inversely proportional to the distance: doubling the distance halves the field. The formula holds when the wire is much longer than the distance $r$ and other wires are far away. For example, the field of a $10\,\text{A}$ wire at a distance of $1\,\text{cm}$ is $200\,\mu\text{T}$, about four times the Earth's magnetic field of about $50\,\mu\text{T}$.

    Inside a long coil, far from its ends, the magnetic flux density is

    $$B = \mu_0 \frac{N I}{l}$$

    where $N$ is the number of turns of the coil, $I$ the current (A) and $l$ the length of the coil (m). The field depends on the turn density $N : l$, that is, on how many turns there are per metre, and not on the thickness of the coil. The formula holds when the coil is much longer than it is wide and has only air inside it. At the ends of the coil the field is about half of its value at the centre, and an iron core makes the field many times stronger.

    Determining the magnetic flux density inside a coil

    The field is measured with a magnetic field sensor whose tip is placed at the centre of the coil, parallel to the axis. The current is varied with a power supply, and at each current the current and the reading $B$ of the sensor are recorded. When the measurements are plotted in an $(I, B)$ graph, for example with the Vernier Graphical Analysis program, a straight line $B = A I + B_0$ is fitted to them. According to the formula the slope is $A = \mu_0 \tfrac{N}{l}$, so the measurement gives an estimate for the permeability of free space, $\mu_0 = A l : N$. When the uncertainty of the slope is $\Delta A$ and that of the length $\Delta l$, the relative uncertainty of the result is estimated by adding the relative uncertainties of the factors: $\Delta \mu_0 : \mu_0 = \Delta A : A + \Delta l : l$. The constant term $B_0$ is usually small: it is affected by the zero point of the sensor and by the Earth's magnetic field.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the magnetic field of a current-carrying wire points away from the wire, like the electric field of a charged wire. The field lines, however, go round the wire as closed circles, and they have neither a starting point nor an end point. A compass next to the wire therefore turns across the wire, not towards it.

    Another mistake is to think that the field of a coil depends on the total number of turns. If the same coil of $200$ turns is stretched to twice its length, the turn density $N : l$ is halved and the field at the centre of the coil is about halved, even though there are just as many turns. A third mistake is to forget that the formula for a long coil does not hold at the ends of the coil: there the field is only about half of its value at the centre.

    Summary

    • An electric current creates a magnetic field around it (Ørsted 1820). The field lines of a straight wire are circles going round the wire, and their direction is given by the right-hand rule: thumb in the direction of the current, fingers in the direction of the field.
    • The field of a long straight wire is $B = \tfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$: it grows with the current and weakens as the distance grows.
    • Inside a long coil the field is almost uniform, $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$. It depends on the turn density $N : l$ and the current. Outside, the field of the coil is like that of a bar magnet.
    • An iron core turns a coil into an electromagnet, whose field is many times stronger and which can be switched on and off.
    • By measuring $B$ as a function of the current and fitting a straight line, we get $\mu_0 = A l : N$ from the slope.

      Example 1: Magnetic field of a coil

      A school coil has $600$ turns and its length is $15.0\,\text{cm}$. A current of $2.50\,\text{A}$ flows in the coil. How great is the magnetic flux density at the centre of the coil? How many times greater is it than the Earth's magnetic field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$? The coil is long compared with its width, and it has air inside it.

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. Number of turns $N = 600$, length of the coil $l = 15.0\,\text{cm}$, current $I = 2.50\,\text{A}$ and the constant $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$. We are asked for the magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil and its ratio to the Earth's field $B_\text{Earth} = 50\,\mu\text{T}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The coil is long, it has air inside, and we want the field at the centre, far from the ends, so the formula for a long coil holds. The field depends only on the turn density and the current.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The formula gives the asked quantity directly, and the ratio is found by dividing:

      $$\begin{aligned} B &= \mu_0 \frac{N I}{l} \\ k &= \frac{B}{B_\text{Earth}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The length in metres:

      $l$$= 15.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.150\,\text{m}$

      The Earth's field in tesla: $50\,\mu\text{T} = 50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$. For the units,

      $\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{A}}{\text{m}}$$= \tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{m}^2}$
      $= \text{T}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} B &= \mu_0 \frac{N I}{l} \\ &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{600 \cdot 2.50\,\text{A}}{0.150\,\text{m}} \\ &= 0.012566\,\text{T} \\[6pt] k &= \frac{0.012566\,\text{T}}{50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}} \\ &= 251.3 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The magnetic flux density at the centre of the coil is $12.6\,\text{mT}$, to three significant figures like the length $15.0\,\text{cm}$ and the current $2.50\,\text{A}$. It is about $250$ times the Earth's magnetic field; the ratio is given to two figures because the Earth's field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$ is known only to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The turn density is $600 : 0.150\,\text{m} = 4000$ turns per metre. One ampere and a thousand turns per metre give about $1.3\,\text{mT}$, so $2.5\,\text{A}$ and four times the density give about $1.3\,\text{mT} \cdot 2.5 \cdot 4 = 13\,\text{mT}$. The result is about $250$ times the Earth's field, so the field of the coil is easy to measure, and it is clearly smaller than the roughly $1.5\,\text{T}$ of an MRI scanner, which needs a superconducting coil and a current of hundreds of amperes.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      A student calculates the magnetic flux density in a coil with $500$ turns, a length of $25\,\text{cm}$ and a current of $2.0\,\text{A}$. She writes $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l} = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \tfrac{500 \cdot 2.0\,\text{A}}{25} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$ and concludes that the field of the coil is as strong as the Earth's field.What is wrong with the calculation?

      • The formula for a straight wire gives the field around a single straight wire. Inside a coil the fields of the turns strengthen each other, and then the formula $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$ is used. The error is in the unit of the length.

      • The order of calculation does not change the result: $\tfrac{N}{l} \cdot I$ and $\tfrac{N I}{l}$ are the same thing. The error is that the length was substituted in centimetres.

      • Correct. The unit of $\mu_0$ contains the metre, so the length must be substituted in metres: $25 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.25\,\text{m}$. The result becomes a hundred times greater, $5.0\,\text{mT}$, which is about a hundred times the Earth's field.

      • The fields of school coils are typically millitesla, hundreds of times stronger than the Earth's field. Check the units: the unit of $\mu_0$ requires the length in metres.

      Optional Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      In a laboratory an electron beam is to be studied without the effect of the Earth's magnetic field. The beam travels along the axis of a long coil, and the coil is oriented so that its axis is parallel to the Earth's magnetic field. The length of the coil is $0.80\,\text{m}$ and it has $120$ turns.

      How great a current must flow in the coil so that its field cancels the Earth's magnetic field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$ inside the coil? The coil is much longer than it is wide.

      A
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      How great must the field of the coil be, and in which direction must it point, for the total field to be zero?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The magnitude of the field of the coil must be $50\,\mu\text{T}$, opposing the Earth's field. Solve the current from the formula of the coil $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $I$$= \dfrac{B l}{N \mu_0}$
      $= \dfrac{50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 120}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      length of the coil $l = 0.80\,\text{m}$, turns $N = 120$, Earth's magnetic field $B_\text{Earth} = 50\,\mu\text{T}$; the coil is long and its axis is parallel to the Earth's field; $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

      Find

      current $I$ of the coil in amperes

      Principle

      Magnetic fields are added as vectors. When the field of the coil is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the Earth's field, the total field inside the coil is zero. The coil is long, so inside it the field is almost uniform and the formula for a long coil holds. The direction of the field is chosen by the direction of the current according to the right-hand rule.

      Formula

      $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$, from which $I = \dfrac{B l}{N \mu_0}$, when $B = B_\text{Earth}$

      Conversions

      $B$$= 50\,\mu\text{T}$
      $= 50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$

      the length is already in metres.

      Substitution

      $I$$= \dfrac{50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 120}$
      $= 0.2653\,\text{A}$

      Answer

      $0.27\,\text{A}$

      Two significant figures like the Earth's field $50\,\mu\text{T}$ and the length $0.80\,\text{m}$.

      Reasonableness

      The turn density is $120 : 0.80\,\text{m} = 150$ turns per metre. A thousand turns per metre and $1\,\text{A}$ give about $1.3\,\text{mT}$, so $150$ turns per metre and $0.27\,\text{A}$ give about

      $1.3\,\text{mT} \cdot 0.15 \cdot 0.27$
      $\approx 0.05\,\text{mT}$
      $= 50\,\mu\text{T}$

      as it should. The Earth's field is weak, so a small current is enough.

Assume that the power supply gives the current needed and that the resistance of the circuit is only the resistance of the wire. The coil is long enough for the formula for a long coil to hold.

        a) Calculate the number of turns $N$ and the length $l$ of the coil.

        b) Calculate the resistance of the wire, $R = \rho L : A$, and the current $I$ of the coil.

        c) Calculate the magnetic flux density at the centre of the coil in millitesla.

        d) Justify what would happen to the field at the centre of the coil if the wire were $60\,\text{m}$ long and the voltage stayed the same. Would the field increase, decrease or stay the same? The coil stays a single layer.

        Give the result of part c) in the answer field.

        mT
        Answer

        $5.3\,\text{mT}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        length of the wire $L = 30\,\text{m}$, diameter of the metal $0.50\,\text{mm}$, with insulation $d_\text{i} = 0.55\,\text{mm}$, $\rho = 1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m}$, diameter of the tube $d = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, voltage $U = 6.0\,\text{V}$; $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

        Find

        magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil in millitesla

        Principle

        The length of wire in one turn is $\pi d$, and in a tight layer each turn takes up a length equal to the thickness $d_\text{i}$ of the insulated wire. The current is limited by the resistance of the wire, $R = \rho L : A$ and $I = U : R$ (FY6). The coil is assumed to be long, so the field is $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$.

        Formula

        $N = \dfrac{L}{\pi d}$,

        $l$$= N d_\text{i}$
        $A$$= \pi \left(\dfrac{d_\text{m}}{2}\right)^2$

        $R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}$, $I = \dfrac{U}{R}$ and $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$

        Conversions

        $d = 0.030\,\text{m}$, $d_\text{i} = 0.55 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$ and the diameter of the metal $d_\text{m} = 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.

        Substitution

        a)

        $N$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{m}}{\pi \cdot 0.030\,\text{m}}$
        $= 318$
        $l$$= 318.3 \cdot 0.55 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$
        $= 0.175\,\text{m}$

        The length is about $5.8$ times the diameter, so the coil is long enough. b)

        $A$$= \pi \cdot (0.25 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2$
        $= 1.96 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$
        $R$$= \dfrac{1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m} \cdot 30\,\text{m}}{1.96 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2}$
        $= 2.60\,\Omega$
        $I$$= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{2.60\,\Omega}$
        $= 2.31\,\text{A}$

        c)

        $B$$= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{318.3 \cdot 2.31\,\text{A}}{0.175\,\text{m}}$
        $= 5.28 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
        $= 5.28\,\text{mT}$

        Answer

        $5.3\,\text{mT}$

        Two significant figures like the voltage $6.0\,\text{V}$ and the diameter $3.0\,\text{cm}$.

        Reasonableness

        In a tight single-layer coil $N : l = 1 : d_\text{i}$, so

        $B$$= \mu_0 I : d_\text{i}$
        $= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 2.31\,\text{A} : 0.55 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$
        $= 5.28\,\text{mT}$

        the same result. The field is about $100$ times the Earth's field. The power is

        $U I$$= 6.0\,\text{V} \cdot 2.31\,\text{A}$
        $= 13.9\,\text{W}$

        so the thin wire warms up and the electromagnet can be used only for short periods.

        Justification for part d)

        When the wire is twice as long, the resistance $R = \rho L : A$ doubles and the current $I = U : R$ halves to $1.16\,\text{A}$. There are twice as many turns, but they are in a single layer, so the length $l$ of the coil doubles and the turn density $N : l$ stays the same. The field $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N}{l} I$ therefore halves to $2.6\,\text{mT}$. If there were two layers, $l$ would stay the same and the field would stay the same, but in this problem the coil is a single layer.

      3. Calculation task: Field of a wire

      A current of $150\,\text{A}$ flows in the cable of a welding machine.

      How great is the magnetic flux density of the cable at a distance of $2.0\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the cable? The cable is straight, and the return wire is far away. Give the answer in millitesla.

      mT
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which formula gives the field of a straight wire at a distance $r$?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, and the distance is substituted in metres.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $B$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 150\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.020\,\text{m}}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      current $I = 150\,\text{A}$, distance $r = 2.0\,\text{cm}$, $\mu_0 \approx 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$; straight cable, return wire far away

      Find

      magnetic flux density $B$ in millitesla

      Principle

      The cable is straight and much longer than the distance of $2\,\text{cm}$, and the return wire is far away, so the formula for a long straight wire holds and no other wire weakens the field.

      Formula

      $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$

      Conversions

      $r$$= 2.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.020\,\text{m}$

      finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.

      Substitution

      $B$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 150\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.020\,\text{m}}$
      $= 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
      $= 1.5\,\text{mT}$

      Answer

      $1.5\,\text{mT}$

      Two significant figures like the distance $2.0\,\text{cm}$.

      Reasonableness

      In the formula $\mu_0 : 2\pi = 2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$, so

      $B$$= 2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 150 : 0.02\,\text{T}$
      $= 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$

      The field is about $30$ times the Earth's field, which fits the fact that a welding cable clearly turns a compass nearby when it carries a direct current.

      Optional 4. Explanation task: The electromagnet of the crane

      Explain why the magnet of a scrapyard crane has a coil and an iron core, and why the crane lets the scrap go when the current is switched off. Use the concepts current, magnetic field, magnetic domain and turn density.

      Model answer

      A current in the coil creates a magnetic field, which inside the coil is stronger the greater the current and the turn density $N : l$. In the iron core the field of the coil turns the magnetic domains of the iron to point the same way, so the iron becomes magnetised and its own field is added to the field of the coil: the total field is many times greater. The strong field also magnetises the steel of the scrap, and the magnet attracts it. When the current is switched off, the field of the coil disappears, and the domains of soft iron return almost to disorder, so the magnet loses its pull and the scrap falls. The domains of a permanent magnet stay aligned, and it cannot be switched off.
