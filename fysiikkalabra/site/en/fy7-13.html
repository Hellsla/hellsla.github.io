Interference and diffraction of light

Why does the beam of a laser pointer draw a whole row of dots of light on the wall when it passes through a thin transparent film?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how the interference and diffraction of light arise in slits and in a grating.
  • calculate the directions of the maxima, the grating spacing and the wavelength of light with the grating equation.
  • determine the wavelength of a laser from the grating and distances measured on the screen.

Prior knowledge

  • In a prism violet light is refracted most, because the refractive index of glass is largest for itchapter Dispersion: the prism and the rainbow

1Think about it

In a physics lesson the teacher switches off the lights and aims the beam of a red laser pointer through a slide frame. The frame holds a thin transparent film that looks just like ordinary plastic. Instead of a single spot, a horizontal row of evenly spaced red dots appears on the wall.

How can one straight ray of light split into many directions, and what decides the positions of the dots?

Optional Recap: interference
Two sources of the same frequency, oscillating in the same phase, emit waves. Point P is $1.5$ wavelengths farther from one source than from the other.What happens at point P?

  • Interference occurs everywhere the waves overlap. The path difference decides whether the waves reinforce or weaken each other. With a path difference of $1.5\lambda$ they weaken each other.

  • Constructive interference needs a path difference that is a whole number of wavelengths: $0$, $\lambda$, $2\lambda$ and so on. The path difference $1.5\lambda$ is half a wavelength more, so the waves arrive in opposite phases and weaken each other.

  • Correct. The path difference $1.5\lambda$ is half a wavelength more than a whole number, so the crest of one wave meets the trough of the other (chapter Interference of waves). Today the same condition explains the bright and dark places of light.

How the calculation works

The grating spacing is calculated from the line density:

$$d = \frac{1\,\text{mm}}{N}$$

The maximum of order $k$ is in the direction for which $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$. A maximum forms only when $k\lambda < d$, so the number of maxima is $2k_\text{max} + 1$, where $k_\text{max}$ is the largest whole number that satisfies this condition. On the screen the distance of a maximum from the central maximum is $x = L\tan\theta$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Light as a wave

    In the chapter Interference of waves we saw that the waves from two sources of the same frequency, oscillating in the same phase, form a stable interference pattern. At places where the path difference $\Delta s$ of the waves is a whole number of wavelengths, the waves reinforce each other. At places where the path difference is half a wavelength more than a whole number, they weaken each other. Light is electromagnetic wave motion (chapter Electromagnetic radiation and the black-body spectrum), so light should interfere, too. However, the wavelength of visible light is only $400$–$700\,\text{nm}$, so the details of an interference pattern of light are very small.

    Coherent light

    A stable pattern forms only when the sources are coherent: they have the same frequency, and their phase difference stays constant. Two ordinary lamps do not produce a pattern. The light of a lamp is emitted by countless atoms independently of each other, so the phase difference of the light of two lamps varies randomly and very rapidly. The places of constructive and destructive interference then keep swapping, and the eye sees only an even illumination. The light of a laser is coherent. Coherent sources can also be obtained by dividing the light of one source into two parts, for example with two slits.

    Diffraction at a slit

    When light passes through a narrow slit, it does not continue as a sharp beam but spreads out behind the slit, also into directions that would be in shadow. This is diffraction, which is familiar from water waves (chapter Reflection and diffraction of waves). Diffraction is strong when the width of the slit is of the order of the wavelength. The narrower the slit, the wider the light spreads: when the slit is narrowed, the bright central region on the screen widens instead of narrowing. Light coming through a window does not seem to bend, because the window is millions of times wider than the wavelength.

    Young's double-slit experiment

    At the beginning of the 19th century Thomas Young carried out an experiment that showed light to be a wave motion. He directed the same light at two narrow slits close together. Because of diffraction, the light spreads widely from each slit, and the waves from the slits overlap. Because the light at both slits comes from the same wavefront, the slits act as coherent sources. Evenly spaced bright and dark fringes form on the screen. In the middle there is a bright fringe, because the distance from each slit to that point is equal. In the next bright fringe the path difference is one wavelength. The dark fringes are the hallmark of interference: they receive less light from the two slits together than from either slit alone.

    Grating

    A diffraction grating is a plate or film with a large number of evenly spaced narrow slits or lines. The distance between adjacent slits is the grating spacing $d$. School gratings usually have $100$, $300$ or $600$ lines per millimetre. The bright maxima of a grating form in the same directions as the bright fringes of a double slit with the same slit spacing, that is in directions where the path difference of adjacent slits is a whole number of wavelengths. Then the wave from every slit arrives in the same phase. Even a little to the side, the waves from different slits are in different phases and cancel each other almost completely. That is why the maxima of a grating are narrow and bright, and almost dark between them. The light of a laser draws sharp dots on the screen, and the positions of the dots can be measured accurately.

    White light in a grating

    The longer the wavelength, the larger the path difference and therefore the larger the angle of deviation required by a maximum. All the colours of white light go in the same direction into the central maximum, so the central maximum is white. On both sides of it the light is spread into a spectrum: violet is deviated least and red most. In a prism the order is the opposite, because the refractive index is largest for violet light (chapter Dispersion: the prism and the rainbow). Farther from the central maximum the spectra of different orders partly overlap. In spectrometers the wavelengths of light are measured with a grating.

    A CD as a reflection grating

    The data track of a CD winds over the surface of the disc as a dense spiral, and the distance between adjacent turns of the track is $1.6\,\mu\text{m}$ (standard IEC 60908, 1999). On a DVD the distance is $0.74\,\mu\text{m}$ (standard ECMA-267, 2001). Light reflected from the surface of the disc behaves in the same way as light passing through a grating: the waves reflected from the evenly spaced tracks interfere, and each wavelength is reinforced in its own direction. That is why rainbow colours are seen on the surface of the disc, changing with the viewing angle. Such a grating is called a reflection grating.

    Grating equation

    When light arrives perpendicular to the grating, the path difference of rays leaving adjacent slits at angle $\theta$ comes from the right-angled triangle in the figure: $\Delta s = d\sin\theta$. The angle $\theta$ is measured from the direction of the incident light, that is from the direction of the central maximum. A maximum forms when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:

    $$d \sin\theta = k\lambda$$

    where $d$ is the grating spacing ($\text{m}$), $\theta$ the direction of the maximum relative to the direction of the incident light, $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ the order of the maximum and $\lambda$ the wavelength of the light ($\text{m}$). The order $k = 0$ is the central maximum. On both sides of it there are similar maxima, so the orders on the other side are often written as negative.

    Grating spacing from the line density

    The manufacturer of a grating usually states how many lines the grating has per millimetre. If there are $N$ lines per millimetre, the distance between adjacent lines is

    $$d = \frac{1\,\text{mm}}{N}$$

    For example, when there are $300$ lines per millimetre, $d = 1\,\text{mm} : 300 = 1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} : 300 = 3.33 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$, that is $3.33\,\mu\text{m}$. The denser the grating, the smaller $d$ is and the larger the angle at which the maximum of the same order lies.

    How many maxima form

    The value of a sine is at most $1$, so the order must satisfy the condition $k\lambda < d$. At the limit $k\lambda = d$ the light would travel along the surface of the grating and would not hit the screen. For example, when the grating has $600$ lines per millimetre and the wavelength of the light is $650\,\text{nm}$, $d : \lambda = 1.667\,\mu\text{m} : 0.650\,\mu\text{m} = 2.56$. Maxima form in the orders $k = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2$, that is five of them.

    The angle from the screen

    The angle is usually not measured directly; instead, distances are measured on the screen. When the screen is perpendicular to the beam of the laser at distance $L$ from the grating and a maximum is at distance $x$ from the central maximum, the right-angled triangle in the figure gives

    $$\tan\theta = \frac{x}{L}$$

    The sine and the tangent are nearly equal only at small angles, so the angle is first calculated from the tangent and only then the sine of the angle. For example, when the grating has $600$ lines per millimetre and the wavelength of a green laser is $532\,\text{nm}$, $\theta_1 = 18.6^\circ$, and on a screen one metre away $x_1 = 33.7\,\text{cm}$.

    Measuring the wavelength of a laser

    A grating can be used to measure the wavelength of a laser in a school laboratory. The laser, the grating and the screen are set in a line so that the beam hits both the grating and the screen perpendicularly. With a tape measure, the distance $L$ from the grating to the screen and the separation of two symmetric maxima $+k$ and $-k$ are measured; half of the separation is $x$. Measuring the separation of two dots is more accurate than estimating the position of the central maximum. Never look into the beam of a laser pointer, and never aim it at anyone's eyes.

    The largest uncertainty usually comes from deciding where the centre of a dot is. If $x$ can be measured with an uncertainty of, for example, $\Delta x = 0.2\,\text{cm}$, a maximum farther away gives a smaller relative uncertainty, because its $x$ is larger. The result is compared with the value stated by the manufacturer: the wavelength of a red laser pointer is usually about $650\,\text{nm}$ and that of a green one $532\,\text{nm}$.

    Common misconception

    Many assume that a grating bends blue and violet light the most, like a prism. In a grating it is the opposite: the direction of a maximum is decided by the condition $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so the longest wavelength, that is red light, is deviated most. In a prism violet is refracted most, because the refractive index of glass is largest for violet light. Another mistake is to think that a narrower slit makes the beam of light narrower. When the slit is of the order of the wavelength, diffraction spreads the light the more, the narrower the slit is.

    Summary

    • Light is a wave motion: the light of coherent sources interferes, and bright and dark places form on the screen.
    • In diffraction light spreads out behind a narrow slit. The narrower the slit, the wider the light spreads.
    • The maxima of a grating are in the directions for which $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, and the grating spacing is $d = 1\,\text{mm} : N$.
    • From distances measured on the screen, $\tan\theta = x : L$. This is how a grating is used to measure the wavelength of a laser.
    • White light is spread by a grating into a spectrum in which red light is deviated most. A CD is a reflection grating.

      Optional 3. Explain: the colours of a CD

      Explain why rainbow colours are seen on the surface of a CD in white light and why the colours change when the disc is tilted.

        2. Why do two ordinary lamps not produce on a wall a pattern of bright and dark fringes, although a laser and a double slit do?
          Answer

          The light of two lamps is not coherent: the countless atoms of the lamps emit light independently of each other, so the phase difference of the lights varies randomly and very rapidly. The places of constructive and destructive interference keep swapping, and the eye sees an even illumination. In a double slit both slits get their light from the same wavefront, so their phase difference stays the same.

        1. Exercise 13-10

          White light containing all wavelengths between $400$ and $700\,\text{nm}$ hits a grating perpendicularly. In the same direction as the red edge ($700\,\text{nm}$) of the second-order spectrum lies a third-order maximum of another wavelength.

          What is this wavelength? What does the result tell about the second- and third-order spectra? Give the answer in nanometres.

          nm
          Answer

          $467\,\text{nm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          wavelength of the second order ($k = 2$) $\lambda_2 = 700\,\text{nm}$, third order $k = 3$, wavelengths of white light $400$–$700\,\text{nm}$

          Find

          the wavelength $\lambda_3$ whose third-order maximum is in the same direction, and what this tells about the spectra

          Principle

          In the same direction $\theta$ the left-hand side $d\sin\theta$ of the grating equation is the same for both wavelengths, so the right-hand sides are equal too. The grating spacing is not needed, because it cancels: the result holds for every grating.

          Formula

          $d\sin\theta = 2\lambda_2$ and $d\sin\theta = 3\lambda_3$, so $3\lambda_3 = 2\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3 = \dfrac{2\lambda_2}{3}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the wavelengths stay in nanometres, because the unit cancels in the ratio $2 : 3$.

          Substitution

          $\lambda_3$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 700\,\text{nm}}{3}$
          $= 466.7\,\text{nm}$

          Answer

          $467\,\text{nm}$

          Three significant figures like the wavelength $700\,\text{nm}$. Blue $467\,\text{nm}$ is present in white light, and the third-order spectrum begins from violet, $400\,\text{nm}$, at an even smaller angle. So the red end of the second-order spectrum and the violet and blue part of the third-order spectrum overlap.

          Reasonableness

          The first- and second-order spectra do not overlap, because the second-order violet, $2 \cdot 400\,\text{nm} = 800\,\text{nm}$, is more than the first-order red, $700\,\text{nm}$. Between the second and third orders $3 \cdot 400\,\text{nm} = 1200\,\text{nm}$ is less than $2 \cdot 700\,\text{nm} = 1400\,\text{nm}$, so overlap is to be expected.

        2. Exercise 13-11

          A student studies a CD as a reflection grating. She makes a hole in a cardboard screen and directs the beam of a red laser, with a wavelength of $650\,\text{nm}$, through the hole perpendicularly onto the surface of the disc. The screen is parallel to the disc, $10.0\,\text{cm}$ from it. The first-order maxima reflected from the disc are seen on the screen on both sides of the hole, $89\,\text{mm}$ apart.

          What is the distance between adjacent tracks of the disc? Give the answer in micrometres.

          µm
          Answer

          $1.6\,\mu\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          wavelength $\lambda = 650\,\text{nm}$, distance of the screen from the disc $L = 10.0\,\text{cm}$, separation of the maxima $\pm 1$ $89\,\text{mm}$, order $k = 1$

          Find

          the track spacing $d$ in micrometres

          Principle

          The evenly spaced tracks of a CD act as a reflection grating: for light arriving perpendicularly the reflected maxima obey the same grating equation as in a transmission grating, and $d$ is the track spacing. The screen is parallel to the disc, that is perpendicular to the incident beam, so the angle comes from a right-angled triangle. The maximum is half of the separation of the dots from the hole.

          Formula

          $\tan\theta = \dfrac{x}{L}$ and $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so $d = \dfrac{k\lambda}{\sin\theta}$

          Conversions

          $x$$= 89\,\text{mm} : 2$
          $= 44.5\,\text{mm}$
          $= 4.45\,\text{cm}$

          for the angle it is enough that $x$ and $L$ are in the same unit. $650\,\text{nm} = 0.650\,\mu\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $\theta$$= \tan^{-1}\dfrac{4.45\,\text{cm}}{10.0\,\text{cm}}$
          $= \tan^{-1} 0.445$
          $= 23.99^\circ$
          $d$$= \dfrac{1 \cdot 0.650\,\mu\text{m}}{\sin 23.99^\circ}$
          $= \dfrac{0.650\,\mu\text{m}}{0.4066}$
          $= 1.599\,\mu\text{m}$

          Answer

          $1.6\,\mu\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the separation of the dots $89\,\text{mm}$.

          Reasonableness

          The track spacing given in the CD standard is $1.6\,\mu\text{m}$, so the measurement hits the mark. The track spacing is about $2.5$ times the wavelength, so there are only two orders on each side, and the first-order angle of $24^\circ$ is reasonable.

        3. Exercise 13-12

          The line density of a grating is not known. When the beam of a red laser, with a wavelength of $650\,\text{nm}$, hits the grating perpendicularly, the first-order maximum on a screen $1.00\,\text{m}$ away is $19.9\,\text{cm}$ from the central maximum. In the same set-up the first-order maximum of a green laser is $16.2\,\text{cm}$ from the central maximum.

          What is the wavelength of the light of the green laser? Give the answer in nanometres.

          nm
          Answer

          $533\,\text{nm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          wavelength of the red laser $\lambda_\text{r} = 650\,\text{nm}$, distance of the screen $L = 1.00\,\text{m}$, distance of the first maximum of red $x_\text{r} = 19.9\,\text{cm}$ and of green $x_\text{g} = 16.2\,\text{cm}$, order $k = 1$

          Find

          the wavelength $\lambda_\text{g}$ of the green laser in nanometres

          Principle

          The grating is the same for both lasers, so the measurement of the red laser, whose wavelength is known, first gives the grating spacing; that is, the grating is calibrated. After that the wavelength of green light comes from the same grating equation. The angles come from right-angled triangles, because the screen is perpendicular to the beam.

          Formula

          $\tan\theta = \dfrac{x}{L}$ and $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so

          $d$$= \dfrac{\lambda_\text{r}}{\sin\theta_\text{r}}$

          $\lambda_\text{g} = d\sin\theta_\text{g}$

          Conversions

          $x_\text{r}$$= 19.9 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.199\,\text{m}$

          $x_\text{g} = 0.162\,\text{m}$ and $650\,\text{nm} = 650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $\theta_\text{r}$$= \tan^{-1}\dfrac{0.199\,\text{m}}{1.00\,\text{m}}$
          $= 11.25^\circ$
          $d$$= \dfrac{650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{\sin 11.25^\circ}$
          $= \dfrac{650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{0.1952}$
          $= 3.330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$
          $\theta_\text{g}$$= \tan^{-1} 0.162$
          $= 9.202^\circ$
          $\lambda_\text{g}$$= 3.330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot \sin 9.202^\circ$
          $= 3.330 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot 0.1599$
          $= 5.326 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 532.6\,\text{nm}$

          Answer

          $533\,\text{nm}$

          Three significant figures like the distances $19.9\,\text{cm}$ and $16.2\,\text{cm}$.

          Reasonableness

          The stated wavelength of a green laser pointer is $532\,\text{nm}$, and the green maximum is closer to the central maximum than the red one, because a shorter wavelength is deviated less. The grating spacing $3.33\,\mu\text{m}$ corresponds to $300$ lines per millimetre, which is a common school grating.
