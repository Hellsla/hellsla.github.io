Applied lesson: lift, parachute and sledding hill

Why can two sleds stop equally far from the top of a hill, although one goes down a steep slope and the other a gentle one?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • choose, based on the free-body diagram, whether to solve a situation with the equation of motion or the work–energy principle.
  • draw a free-body diagram and calculate the normal force or the acceleration in an accelerating lift.
  • calculate the speed and sliding distance of a sled and the work done by air resistance with the work–energy principle.

Prior knowledge

  • The net force points in the direction of the acceleration, and $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$chapter Newton's second law
  • On a slope the normal force is $mg\cos\alpha$ and the kinetic friction is $\mu N$chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane
  • The change in mechanical energy is the work done by forces other than the weightchapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 2.

1Think about it

A physics group takes a bathroom scale into the lift of a shopping centre. When the lift starts up, the reading rises for a moment, and when the lift stops, it falls. Through the window you can see a skydiver who descends under a parachute at a steady speed, although the weight pulls the skydiver down all the time. On a sledding hill two children go down different sides of the same hill: one a steep, short slope, the other a gentle, long slope. The one who came down the steep side slides much farther on the flat, but when the stopping points are measured, both are equally far from the top of the hill.

Familiar forces act in all three situations. When should you solve a situation with forces and when with energy, and why do the sleds stop equally far from the top?

Optional Review: the work–energy principle
A box slides on a horizontal floor and stops. At the start its kinetic energy is $40\,\text{J}$. Nobody pushes or pulls it.What happens to the mechanical energy of the box while it slides?

  • Potential energy changes only when the height changes. The box slides on a horizontal floor, so the kinetic energy does not turn into potential energy but, because of friction, into internal energy.

  • The total energy is conserved, but the mechanical energy is not: friction turns it into internal energy of the box and the floor. Mechanical energy is conserved only when forces other than the weight do no work.

  • The normal force is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work. The box is stopped by friction, which points against the motion and does negative work.

  • Correct. In the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle you learned that the change in mechanical energy is the work done by forces other than the weight, $\Delta E_\text{mech} = W_\text{other}$. The height does not change, so all of the kinetic energy $40\,\text{J}$ turns into internal energy. In this chapter the same principle solves the sliding of the sled.

Prediction 1: a scale in a lift

You stand on a bathroom scale in a lift. The lift starts up from the ground floor, travels for a while at a steady speed and stops at an upper floor.How does the reading of the scale change during the trip compared with what it shows at rest?

  • Correct. In the graph of the Investigate stage the reading is $650\,\text{N}$ at the start, about $589\,\text{N}$ at a steady speed, as at rest, and $550\,\text{N}$ when stopping. The reading follows the acceleration: at the start the acceleration points up, when stopping it points down.

  • In the graph it is the other way round. When the lift starts up, the person has to be accelerated upwards, and the normal force of the floor does that, so it is larger than the weight. When stopping, the acceleration points down, and the normal force is smaller than the weight.

  • In the graph the reading during the steady speed is the same as at rest, although the lift moves up. When the speed does not change, the acceleration and the net force are zero, so the normal force is equal to the weight.

  • The mass does not change, but a scale does not measure mass. It measures the normal force, and when the lift accelerates, the normal force must differ from the weight. In the graph the reading changes at the start and when stopping.

Prediction 2: which is faster?

Two identical sleds start from rest, one down the steep side and one down the gentle side of the same hill. The height of both sides is the same, and the snow is the same all the way, so friction cannot be ignored.Which sled is faster at the foot of the slope?

  • Correct. In the table of the Investigate stage the sled from the $30^\circ$ slope has a speed of $9.87\,\text{m/s}$ at the foot and the one from the $10^\circ$ slope only $7.14\,\text{m/s}$. On a steep slope the normal force and the friction are smaller and the distance is shorter, so the work done by friction is smaller.

  • This would hold without friction: then both would have the speed $\sqrt{2gh}$ at the foot. But friction does negative work along the whole slope, and in the table the speeds at the foot differ clearly.

  • On a slope the normal force is $mg\cos\alpha$, not $mg$, so the friction force is $\mu mg\cos\alpha$ and depends on the angle. In addition, the slopes have different lengths. In the table the speeds at the foot are not the same.

  • On a gentle slope there is more time, but the acceleration is small and friction does work over a longer distance. In the table the sled from the gentle slope is slower at the foot.

A force sensor in a lift

Made-up example data

A group takes a force-sensor plate that measures the normal force in newtons into a lift. A student of mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$ stands on the plate. The lift starts up from the ground floor, travels for a while at a steady speed and stops at an upper floor. The graph shows the reading of the plate as a function of time. The numbers are invented example data.

First draw the free-body diagram of the student in your notebook. Then read the normal force at the start from the graph and calculate the acceleration of the lift. Give the answer in $\text{m/s}^2$.

m/s²
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which two forces act on the student, and which one is larger at the start?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Choose up as the positive direction: $N - mg = ma$. Read $N$ from the graph between $1.0\,\text{s}$ and $3.0\,\text{s}$.

$N = 650\,\text{N}$ and

Show the solution

Given

mass of the student $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$; from the graph the normal force in the start phase $N = 650\,\text{N}$ (from $1.0\,\text{s}$ to $3.0\,\text{s}$); $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the acceleration $a$ of the lift in the start phase in $\text{m/s}^2$

Principle

The student stands still in the lift, so the student's acceleration is the acceleration of the lift. By Newton's second law it is determined by the net force acting on the student, the sum of the weight and the normal force.

Free-body diagram

The student as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the normal force $\overline{N}$ of the plate from the bottom surface up, longer than the weight. The acceleration up is marked separately and not as a force.

Reading the graph

In the start phase, from $1.0\,\text{s}$ to $3.0\,\text{s}$, the curve is horizontal and meets the grid line at $650\,\text{N}$. At rest the reading is about $589\,\text{N}$, so at the start the normal force is larger than the weight.

Formula

$N - mg = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{N}{m} - g$

Conversions

The mass is in kilograms and the force in newtons, and $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} = \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$a$$= \dfrac{650\,\text{N}}{60.0\,\text{kg}} - 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 10.833\,\text{m/s}^2 - 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 1.023\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$1.02\,\text{m/s}^2$

Upwards, three significant figures like the mass $60.0\,\text{kg}$ and the $650\,\text{N}$ read from the graph.

Reasonableness

The normal force is about $60\,\text{N}$ larger than the weight, and $60\,\text{N}$ accelerates a $60\,\text{kg}$ student by about $1\,\text{m/s}^2$. The acceleration of a lift is typically about $1\,\text{m/s}^2$, and it is only slightly felt in the legs.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    First the free-body diagram, then the tool

    The mechanics problems of this module are solved with two tools, and both start from the same place. First you choose the body and draw its free-body diagram, as in the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces. The diagram shows which forces act and in which directions. Then you choose the tool according to what is asked. When the question is about a force or an acceleration at some moment, you write the equation of motion (chapter Newton's second law). When the question is about the speed at some point or the change in energy over some distance, you use the work–energy principle (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). Even then you need the free-body diagram, because it shows which forces do work.

    Lift: a scale measures the normal force

    A bathroom scale does not measure the weight but the normal force $N$ with which it pushes the person standing on it upwards. The reading is shown in kilograms, because the scale divides the force by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. Only the weight and the normal force act on a person standing still in a lift. When the positive direction is up, the equation of motion is $N - mg = ma$, so $N = m(g + a)$. When the lift starts up and accelerates at $1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, the normal force on a $60.0\,\text{kg}$ person is $649\,\text{N}$, rounded $650\,\text{N}$ as in the graph, and the scale shows $66\,\text{kg}$. At a steady speed $a = 0$, and the scale shows the same as at home, whether the lift moves up or down. So the reading depends on the acceleration, not on the speed.

    Parachute: at terminal velocity the mechanical energy decreases

    A skydiver descending under a parachute soon falls at terminal velocity, that is, at a steady speed (chapter Air resistance and falling through air). By the equation of motion the acceleration is zero, so the air resistance is equal to the weight, $F_\text{d} = mg$. In terms of energy, the kinetic energy does not change, but the potential energy decreases all the time. By the work–energy principle the change in mechanical energy is the work done by air resistance. It is negative, because air resistance points against the motion: over a height $h$, $W_\text{d} = -F_\text{d} h = -mgh$. When an $80\,\text{kg}$ skydiver descends $100\,\text{m}$, about $78\,\text{kJ}$ of mechanical energy is changed into motion of the air and finally into internal energy. Each tool tells the same thing in its own way.

    Sledding hill: friction takes energy all the way

    A sled slides down a straight slope. The free-body diagram shows that the component of the weight perpendicular to the slope and the normal force cancel each other, so $N = mg\cos\alpha$, as in the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane. The kinetic friction is then $F_\mu = \mu mg\cos\alpha$. The normal force does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion. On the slope the weight does work $mgh$, and friction does negative work $-F_\mu L$, when the length of the slope is $L$. On a hill of the same height, the normal force and the friction are smaller on a steep slope and the slope is shorter, so friction takes less energy and the sled is faster at the foot. On the flat the sled slides until friction has turned all the kinetic energy into internal energy.

    Equation of motion and work–energy principle

    The first tool of an applied problem is Newton's second law:

    $$\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$$

    where $\Sigma\overline{F}$ is the sum of the forces acting on the body, that is, the net force ($\text{N}$), $m$ is the mass of the body ($\text{kg}$) and $\overline{a}$ its acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). The law is written from the free-body diagram in components, $\Sigma F_x = ma_x$ and $\Sigma F_y = ma_y$, once the axes and the positive directions have been chosen. The second tool is the work–energy principle of mechanics:

    $$\begin{aligned} &E_{\text{k},1} + E_{\text{p},1} + W_\text{other} \\ \quad &= E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2} \end{aligned}$$

    where the index $1$ means the initial situation and $2$ the final situation, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 mv^2$ is the kinetic energy, $E_\text{p} = mgh$ the potential energy from the chosen zero level and $W_\text{other}$ the work done by forces other than the weight ($\text{J}$). The work–energy principle needs neither the acceleration nor the time, so it fits when the question is about the speed at a given point.

    The work done by friction depends on the horizontal distance

    On a straight slope with an angle of inclination $\alpha$ and a length $L$, the kinetic friction is $\mu mg\cos\alpha$ and the work it does is $-\mu mg\cos\alpha \cdot L$. The figure shows that $L\cos\alpha$ is the horizontal length $x_1$ of the slope. On the flat $N = mg$, and the work done by friction over a distance $x_2$ is $-\mu mg x_2$. In both cases the work done by kinetic friction is

    $$W_\mu = -\mu m g x$$

    where $\mu$ is the coefficient of kinetic friction, $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$), $g$ the acceleration of free fall ($\text{m/s}^2$) and $x$ the horizontal length of the distance travelled ($\text{m}$). The result requires that the surface is straight, that the coefficient of friction is the same all the way and that only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface, so the sled is not, for example, pulled with a rope.

    Why the sleds stop equally far away

    A sled starts from rest at a height $h$, goes down the slope and stops on the flat. When the zero level is chosen at the flat, the work–energy principle gives $mgh - \mu mg(x_1 + x_2) = 0$. The mass cancels, and the whole horizontal distance of the sled from the top to the stopping point is

    $$x_1 + x_2 = \frac{h}{\mu}$$

    The angle of inclination does not appear in the result. A sled coming down the steep side travels a short horizontal distance on the slope, so it has more distance left on the flat. A sled coming down the gentle side uses a larger part of the horizontal distance on the slope. This answers the question at the start. The model assumes that the coefficient of friction is the same all the way, that air resistance is small and that the bend at the foot of the slope is short. In a sharp bend the normal force and the friction grow for a moment, so a real sled stops a little earlier below a steep hill. In addition, the slope must be steep enough for the sled to slide: $\tan\alpha > \mu$.

    Steps of the solution

    1. Choose the body and draw its free-body diagram: for every force its cause and direction.
    2. Decide the tool: the force or acceleration at a moment from the equation of motion, the speed at a given point from the work–energy principle.
    3. For the equation of motion choose the axes and the positive directions. For the work–energy principle choose the zero level and write the work of the other forces with their signs: a force opposing the motion does negative work, and a force perpendicular to the motion does no work.
    4. Solve the asked quantity in symbols, substitute only then, and check the result with a limiting case, for example without friction.

    Common misconception

    Many think that a scale in a lift shows more because the lift moves upwards. The reading depends on the acceleration, not on the speed: in a lift moving steadily up or down the scale shows the same as at home, and in a lift moving up that slows down to stop it shows less.

    Another mistake is to think that at terminal velocity the mechanical energy is conserved, because the speed does not change. The kinetic energy stays the same, but the potential energy decreases, and the negative work done by air resistance changes it into other forms of energy. A third mistake is to write the friction force on a slope as $\mu mg$. On a slope the normal force is $mg\cos\alpha$, so the friction force is $\mu mg\cos\alpha$, and the work done by friction is $-\mu mg$ times the horizontal distance, not times the length of the slope.

    Summary

    • A mechanics problem always starts with choosing the body and drawing its free-body diagram. The force or acceleration at a moment comes from the equation of motion $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$, the speed at a given point from the work–energy principle.
    • In a lift a scale measures the normal force $N = m(g + a)$, when the positive direction is up. The reading depends on the acceleration, not on the speed.
    • At terminal velocity $F_\text{d} = mg$, and the work done by air resistance is $-mgh$: the mechanical energy decreases although the speed does not change.
    • On a straight slope $N = mg\cos\alpha$, and the work done by kinetic friction is $W_\mu = -\mu mgx$, where $x$ is the horizontal distance. A sled that starts from rest at a height $h$ stops at a horizontal distance $h/\mu$ from the top, when the coefficient of friction is the same all the way.
    • When a body slides along a stationary surface, the normal force does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion. In a lift the normal force of the floor is in the direction of the motion and does work.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A child and a sled, together $32.0\,\text{kg}$, start from rest at the top of a straight slope. The length of the slope is $25.0\,\text{m}$, the angle of inclination $20.0^\circ$ and the coefficient of kinetic friction between the sled and the snow $0.200$. How large is the speed of the sled at the foot of the slope? Air resistance is ignored.

      Step 1/8

      Given and asked. $m = 32.0\,\text{kg}$, $L = 25.0\,\text{m}$, $\alpha = 20.0^\circ$, $\mu = 0.200$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the speed $v_2$ at the foot of the slope.

      Step 2/8

      Principle. The question is about the speed at a given point, so we use the work–energy principle. We choose the sled and the child together as the body. The weight does work, which the work–energy principle takes into account as a change in potential energy. The normal force does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion. Kinetic friction points against the motion and does negative work $W_\text{other} = -F_\mu L$. The friction force comes from the free-body diagram.

      Step 3/8

      Free-body diagram. The sled is drawn as a rectangle along the slope. The forces on it are from the Earth (the weight $\overline{G}$), from the snow (the normal force $\overline{N}$) and the kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ of the snow, which points up the slope, because the sled slides down. The axes are chosen so that $x$ points down the slope and $y$ perpendicularly out of the slope. In the $y$ direction the sled does not accelerate, so $N - mg\cos\alpha = 0$ and $F_\mu = \mu N = \mu mg\cos\alpha$.

      Step 4/8

      Formula solved. The zero level is chosen at the foot of the slope, so at the start $E_\text{p,1} = mgh$, where $h = L\sin\alpha$, and at the end $E_\text{p,2} = 0$. The speed is solved from the work–energy principle:

      $$\begin{aligned} &mgL\sin\alpha - \mu mg\cos\alpha \cdot L \\ \quad &= \tfrac12 mv_2^2 \\ v_2 &= \sqrt{2gL(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha)} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 5/8

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the quantities are in SI units, the coefficient of friction has no unit and the angle is in degrees with the calculator in degree mode.

      Step 6/8

      Substitution. First we calculate the expression in brackets and then the speed:

      $$\begin{aligned} &\sin 20.0^\circ - 0.200\cdot\cos 20.0^\circ \\ \quad &\approx 0.3420 - 0.1879 = 0.1541 \\ v_2 &= \sqrt{2\cdot 9.81\cdot 25.0\cdot 0.1541}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \\ \phantom{v_2} &\approx 8.694\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 7/8

      Result. The speed of the sled at the foot of the slope is $8.69\,\text{m/s}$, or about $31\,\text{km/h}$, to three significant figures like the length of the slope $25.0\,\text{m}$, the angle $20.0^\circ$ and the coefficient of friction $0.200$.

      Step 8/8

      Reasonableness. Without friction the speed would be

      $\sqrt{2gh}$$= \sqrt{2\cdot 9.81\cdot 8.55}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
      $\approx 13.0\,\text{m/s}$

      so friction reduces it, as it should. The same result comes from the equation of motion: in the $x$ direction $mg\sin\alpha - \mu mg\cos\alpha = ma$, so $a \approx 1.51\,\text{m/s}^2$, and from the result $aL = \tfrac12 v_2^2$ of the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle we get $v_2^2 \approx 75.6\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$ as above. The mass cancelled, so a heavier and a lighter sled are equally fast at the foot, when the coefficient of friction is the same.

      Draw it yourself: a suitcase in a lift

      A hook hangs from the ceiling of a lift, and from it hangs a spring scale with an $18.0\,\text{kg}$ suitcase on its hook. The lift starts downwards and accelerates. Draw the free-body diagram of the suitcase on paper: the suitcase as a rectangle and every force at its point of application. Mark the directions of the velocity and the acceleration separately next to the diagram. Then write the equation of motion when the positive direction is down.

      Does the scale show more or less than when the lift is at rest?

      Model diagram

      Only the Earth (the weight $\overline{G}$) and the scale, whose hook holds the suitcase (the tension $\overline{T}$), act on the suitcase. The lift and the ceiling act on the suitcase only through the scale, so they get no forces of their own. The lift moves down and accelerates, so the velocity and the acceleration point down. The net force must point down, so the tension is smaller than the weight. When the positive direction is down, the equation of motion is $mg - T = ma$, from which $T = m(g - a)$. The scale shows less than at rest. For example, when $a = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$, $T = 18.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 8.31\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 150\,\text{N}$, when the weight is $177\,\text{N}$.

        Apply to a new situation

        A skydiver whose mass with equipment is $78.0\,\text{kg}$ descends under an open parachute at a steady speed of $5.0\,\text{m/s}$.

        How much mechanical energy is changed into other forms of energy as the skydiver descends $125\,\text{m}$? First draw the free-body diagram. Give the answer in kilojoules.

        kJ
        Hint 1: a guiding question

        What happens to the kinetic energy and the potential energy of the skydiver when the speed is constant?

        Hint 2: what is the connection

        The speed is constant, so $F_\text{d} = mg$. The work done by air resistance is $W_\text{d} = -F_\text{d} h$, and it is the change in mechanical energy.

        Hint 3: an intermediate step

        $mgh = 78.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 125\,\text{m}$. Change the joules into kilojoules.

        Show the solution

        Given

        $m = 78.0\,\text{kg}$, the speed is constant $v = 5.0\,\text{m/s}$, the change in height $h = 125\,\text{m}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Find

        the mechanical energy changed into other forms of energy $|\Delta E_\text{mech}|$ in $\text{kJ}$

        Principle

        By the work–energy principle the change in mechanical energy is the work done by forces other than the weight, here the work $W_\text{d}$ done by air resistance. The magnitude of the air resistance comes from the equation of motion: the speed is constant, so the net force is zero.

        Free-body diagram

        The skydiver with the parachute as a rectangle, positive direction down. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ from the top surface up, equally long. The velocity down is marked separately and not as a force.

        Formula

        $mg - F_\text{d} = 0$, so $F_\text{d} = mg$. The air resistance points against the motion, so $W_\text{d} = -F_\text{d} h = -mgh$, and $|\Delta E_\text{mech}| = mgh$

        Conversions

        We calculate in joules and finally change to kilojoules: $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$. The speed is not needed, because the kinetic energy does not change.

        Substitution

        $|\Delta E_\text{mech}|$$= 78.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 125\,\text{m}$
        $= 95\,647.5\,\text{J}$
        $\approx 95.6\,\text{kJ}$

        Answer

        $95.6\,\text{kJ}$

        Three significant figures like the mass $78.0\,\text{kg}$ and the height $125\,\text{m}$.

        Reasonableness

        The weight of one kilogram is about $10\,\text{N}$, so $80\,\text{kg}$ and $125\,\text{m}$ give the estimate $800\,\text{N}\cdot 125\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{kJ}$. The kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2 \approx 1\,\text{kJ}$ stays the same, so the whole change in potential energy is changed into other forms of energy. Without air resistance this energy would turn into kinetic energy.

        A lift travels upwards between floors at a steady speed of $2.0\,\text{m/s}$. A person of mass $60\,\text{kg}$ stands on a scale in the lift.What does the scale show?

        • The cable acts on the lift car, not on the person. Only the weight and the normal force of the floor act on the person, and at a steady speed they are equal.

        • Correct. At a steady speed the net force is zero, so $N = mg$, and the scale shows the same as at rest. The direction of the velocity does not matter.

        • The reading depends on the acceleration, not on the speed. In a lift moving steadily the scale shows the same as at rest, whatever the speed.

        • The normal force is larger than the weight only when the acceleration points up. At a steady speed the acceleration is zero, so the normal force and the weight are equal.

        2. Calculation

        A student of mass $55.0\,\text{kg}$ stands on a bathroom scale in a lift. The scale measures the normal force and shows it in kilograms, that is, divides it by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The lift starts downwards and accelerates at $1.20\,\text{m/s}^2$.

        What does the scale show? Give the answer in kilograms.

        kg
        Hint 1: a guiding question

        Which is larger, the weight or the normal force, when the acceleration points down?

        Hint 2: what is the connection

        Choose up as the positive direction: $N - mg = ma$, where $a = -1.20\,\text{m/s}^2$.

        Hint 3: an intermediate step

        $N = 55.0\,\text{kg}\cdot(9.81 - 1.20)\,\text{m/s}^2$. Finally divide by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

        Show the solution

        Given

        $m = 55.0\,\text{kg}$, the acceleration of the lift $1.20\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards, the scale shows the normal force divided by $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

        Find

        the reading of the scale $N/g$ in $\text{kg}$

        Principle

        The student stands still in the lift, so the student's acceleration is the acceleration of the lift. By Newton's second law the acceleration is determined by the sum of the weight and the normal force. The scale measures the normal force.

        Free-body diagram

        The student as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface up, shorter than the weight, because the acceleration points down. The acceleration down is marked separately and not as a force.

        Formula

        $N - mg = ma$, so $N = m(g + a)$, and the reading is $\dfrac{N}{g} = \dfrac{m(g + a)}{g}$

        Conversions

        The acceleration points down, so with up as the positive direction $a = -1.20\,\text{m/s}^2$. No other conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $\dfrac{N}{g}$$= \dfrac{55.0\,\text{kg}\cdot(9.81 - 1.20)\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
        $= \dfrac{473.55\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
        $= 48.27\,\text{kg}$

        Answer

        $48.3\,\text{kg}$

        Three significant figures like the mass $55.0\,\text{kg}$ and the acceleration $1.20\,\text{m/s}^2$.

        Reasonableness

        The acceleration is about one eighth of the acceleration of free fall, so the reading decreases by about one eighth: $55\,\text{kg} - 7\,\text{kg} \approx 48\,\text{kg}$. The reading is smaller than the mass, as it should be when the acceleration points down.

        3. Energy at terminal velocity

        A skydiver descends under an open parachute at a steady speed. Which statement about the skydiver's energy is correct?

        • The speed is constant, so the kinetic energy does not increase. The change in potential energy is turned into other forms of energy through the negative work done by air resistance.

        • Correct. The kinetic energy stays the same, but the potential energy decreases. The change in mechanical energy is the work done by air resistance, $W_\text{d} = -F_\text{d} h = -mgh$. The energy does not disappear but turns into motion of the air and finally into internal energy.

        • The sign of the work is decided by the direction of the force relative to the motion. The skydiver moves down and air resistance points up, so the work is negative.

        • The speed and the kinetic energy do not change, but the potential energy decreases all the time. Mechanical energy is conserved only when forces other than the weight do no work, and here air resistance does.

        Exit ticket

        Finally three quick questions. Answer first on your own and open the answer only then.

        1. A sled slides down a straight slope. Why does the normal force of the snow do no work on the sled, although it is a large force?
          Answer

          The normal force is perpendicular to the slope and so to the motion of the sled. Only the component of the force along the motion does work, $W = F_\parallel s$, and for the normal force it is zero. The normal force does act indirectly, however, because the kinetic friction is $\mu N$.

        2. When do you choose the equation of motion and when the work–energy principle? Give one example of each from the situations of this chapter.
          Answer

          The equation of motion $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$ fits when the question is about a force or an acceleration at some moment, for example the reading of a scale in an accelerating lift. The work–energy principle fits when the question is about the speed at some point or the change in energy over a distance, for example the speed of a sled at the foot of a slope. In both you start from the free-body diagram.

        3. Why does the horizontal distance from the top to the stopping point of a sled starting from rest not depend on the mass of the sled?
          Answer

          The work $mgh$ done by the weight and the work $-\mu mgx$ done by friction are both proportional to the mass. In the work–energy principle $mgh - \mu mgx = 0$ the mass cancels, and $x = h/\mu$, when the coefficient of friction is the same all the way and air resistance is ignored.

        4. A sled arrives from the foot of a slope onto a horizontal snow field at a speed of $6.0\,\text{m/s}$. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the sled and the snow is $0.080$, and air resistance is ignored.

          m
          Answer

          22.93578 m

          Show the solution

          Given

          initial speed $v_1 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, final speed $v_2 = 0$, $\mu = 0.080$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the sliding distance $s$ in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          The question is about the distance over which the kinetic energy is used up, so we use the work–energy principle.           The sled as a rectangle.           $\tfrac12 mv_1^2 - \mu mg s = 0$, so $s = \dfrac{v_1^2}{2\mu g}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The mass cancels.

          Substitution

          $s$$= \dfrac{(6.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{2\cdot 0.080\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{36\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{1.5696\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 22.94\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $23\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the speed $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the coefficient of friction $0.080$.

          The deceleration is $\mu g \approx 0.8\,\text{m/s}^2$, so stopping takes about $6\,\text{s} : 0.8 \approx 7.5\,\text{s}$, and at the average speed $3\,\text{m/s}$ the distance is about $23\,\text{m}$. On the flat of a sledding hill this is a usual sliding distance.

        2. Core

          A sled starts from rest at the top of a straight slope. The slope is $18.0\,\text{m}$ long and at an angle of $12.0^\circ$ to the horizontal, and at the foot of the slope the speed of the sled is $5.10\,\text{m/s}$. Air resistance is ignored.

          How large is the coefficient of kinetic friction between the sled and the snow?

          Answer

          0.137262

          Show the solution

          Given

          $L = 18.0\,\text{m}$, $\alpha = 12.0^\circ$, $v_1 = 0$, $v_2 = 5.10\,\text{m/s}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu$ (no unit)

          Principle

          The speed is known at the start and at the end of the slope, so we use the work–energy principle.           Free-body diagram

          The sled as a rectangle along the slope, $x$ down the slope and $y$ perpendicularly out of the slope. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre straight down, the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface perpendicularly out of the slope and the kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the upper corner of the bottom surface up the slope.           The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The mass cancels.

        3. A skydiver whose mass with equipment is $80.0\,\text{kg}$ falls at a speed of $52.0\,\text{m/s}$ and opens the parachute. While falling $145\,\text{m}$, the skydiver's speed decreases to $6.0\,\text{m/s}$. Draw the free-body diagram of the skydiver.

          How much mechanical energy is changed into other forms of energy over this distance?

          kJ
          Answer

          220.516 kJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 80.0\,\text{kg}$, $v_1 = 52.0\,\text{m/s}$, $v_2 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, drop $h = 145\,\text{m}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Formula

          Zero level at the height of the final situation: $\Delta E_\text{mech} = \left(\tfrac12 mv_2^2 - \tfrac12 mv_1^2\right) - mgh$, and

          $= 106\,720\,\text{J} + 113\,796\,\text{J}$
          $= 220\,516\,\text{J}$

          $221\,\text{kJ}$

          Three significant figures like the mass $80.0\,\text{kg}$ and the speed $52.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Each part is about $110\,\text{kJ}$: the kinetic energy decreases almost completely ($\tfrac12\cdot 80\cdot 52^2\,\text{J} \approx 108\,\text{kJ}$), and the potential energy decreases by

          $mgh$$\approx 800\,\text{N}\cdot 145\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 116\,\text{kJ}$

          The energy does not disappear but turns into motion of the air and finally into internal energy.

        4. The combined mass of a lift car and its load is $850\,\text{kg}$. A motor raises the car with a cable at a steady speed $12.0\,\text{m}$ upwards, and the lift takes $15.0\,\text{s}$.

          How large is the power the cable gives to the car?

          kW
          Answer

          6.6708 kW

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 850\,\text{kg}$, lifting height $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, time $t = 15.0\,\text{s}$, the speed is constant; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the power $P$ of the cable in $\text{kW}$

          Principle

          The speed is constant, so by Newton's second law the tension of the cable is equal to the weight. The cable pulls in the direction of the motion, so it does positive work $W = Th$, which increases the potential energy of the car. Power is work divided by time, as in the chapter Hydro and wind power as energy converters, $E = Pt$.

          Free-body diagram

          The lift car with its load as a rectangle, positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the tension $\overline{T}$ of the cable from the top surface up, equally long. The velocity up is marked separately and not as a force.

          Formula

          $T - mg = 0$, so $T = mg$. The work is $W = Th = mgh$, and the power $P = \dfrac{W}{t} = \dfrac{mgh}{t}$

          Conversions

          We calculate in watts and finally change to kilowatts: $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$.

          Substitution

          $P$$= \dfrac{850\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 12.0\,\text{m}}{15.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= \dfrac{100\,062\,\text{J}}{15.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 6670.8\,\text{W}$
          $\approx 6.67\,\text{kW}$

          Answer

          $6.67\,\text{kW}$

          Three significant figures like the height $12.0\,\text{m}$ and the time $15.0\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The speed is $12.0\,\text{m} : 15.0\,\text{s} = 0.80\,\text{m/s}$, and the tension is about $8.3\,\text{kN}$. The product $8.3\,\text{kN}\cdot 0.80\,\text{m/s} \approx 6.7\,\text{kW}$ matches. The power of a lift motor is typically a few kilowatts, and because of losses the motor needs more.

          Principle

          The sled is at rest at the start and at the end, so by the work–energy principle the negative work done by friction is equal to the decrease in potential energy. On a straight surface the work done by friction is $-\mu mg$ times the horizontal distance, because on a slope $N = mg\cos\alpha$ and $L\cos\alpha$ is the horizontal length of the slope. So the angles of the parts of the slope are not needed.

          Free-body diagram

          On each part of the slope the sled as a rectangle along the slope: the weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down, the normal force $\overline{N}$ perpendicularly out of the slope, $N = mg\cos\alpha$, and the kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ up the slope. On the flat $N = mg$ and the friction points backwards. The normal force does no work anywhere.

          Formula

          Horizontal distances of the parts $x_\text{a}$, $x_\text{b}$ and $x_\text{c}$: $mgh - \mu mg(x_\text{a} + x_\text{b} + x_\text{c}) = 0$, so

          $x$$= x_\text{a} + x_\text{b} + x_\text{c}$
          $= \dfrac{h}{\mu}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units and the coefficient of friction has no unit, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $x$$= \dfrac{4.50\,\text{m}}{0.120}$
          $= 37.5\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $37.5\,\text{m}$

          Three significant figures like the height $4.50\,\text{m}$ and the coefficient of friction $0.120$.

          Reasonableness

          The inclination of both parts of the slope is greater than $\tan^{-1} 0.120 \approx 6.8^\circ$, so the sled slides down the whole slope and does not stop halfway. The result does not depend on the mass or on the shape of the slope. A smaller friction would give a longer distance, as it should. In reality the normal force and the friction grow for a moment in the bends, so the sled would stop a little earlier.

        2. A person of mass $75.0\,\text{kg}$ stands on a force-sensor plate in a lift that starts downwards from an upper floor and stops at a lower one. The graph shows the reading of the plate, that is, the normal force, as a function of time.

          How large is the greatest speed of the lift during the trip?

          m/s
          Answer

          2.286667 m/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 75.0\,\text{kg}$; from the graph the normal force $N = 650\,\text{N}$ from $1.0\,\text{s}$ to $3.0\,\text{s}$; the lift starts from rest; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the greatest speed $v$ of the lift in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          The lift accelerates downwards when the normal force is smaller than the weight, and the speed is greatest at the end of this phase. By Newton's second law the acceleration comes from the forces. When the forces are constant, the acceleration is constant, and by the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion $v = v_0 + at$.

          Free-body diagram

          The person as a rectangle, positive direction down. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface up, shorter than the weight.

          Reading the graph

          The weight is $75.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 735.75\,\text{N}$, and the reading is that large at rest and at a steady speed. From $1.0\,\text{s}$ to $3.0\,\text{s}$ the curve is on the grid line at $650\,\text{N}$, smaller than the weight, so the lift accelerates downwards for $\Delta t = 2.0\,\text{s}$. From $6.0\,\text{s}$ on the reading is larger than the weight, so the lift slows down.

          Formula

          $mg - N = ma$, so $a = g - \dfrac{N}{m}$, and starting from rest

          $v$$= a\Delta t$
          $= \left(g - \dfrac{N}{m}\right)\Delta t$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \left(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - \dfrac{650\,\text{N}}{75.0\,\text{kg}}\right)\cdot 2.0\,\text{s}$
          $= 1.1433\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot 2.0\,\text{s}$
          $= 2.287\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $2.3\,\text{m/s}$

          Downwards, two significant figures like the time $2.0\,\text{s}$ read from the graph.

          Reasonableness

          In the slowing phase the reading is $800\,\text{N}$, so the deceleration is $(800 - 735.75)\,\text{N} : 75.0\,\text{kg} \approx 0.86\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the speed $2.3\,\text{m/s}$ disappears in about $2.7\,\text{s}$, which matches the slowing phase of the graph from about $6.0\,\text{s}$ to $8.7\,\text{s}$. The speed of an ordinary lift is $1$–$3\,\text{m/s}$.

        3. A skydiver whose mass with equipment is $70.0\,\text{kg}$ opens the parachute at a speed of $50.0\,\text{m/s}$. After falling $60.0\,\text{m}$ the speed has decreased to $6.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          How large is the air resistance on average over this distance, if it is assumed to be constant?

          kN
          Answer

          2.124033 kN

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 70.0\,\text{kg}$, $v_1 = 50.0\,\text{m/s}$, $v_2 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, drop $h = 60.0\,\text{m}$, air resistance assumed constant; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the magnitude of the air resistance $F_\text{d}$ in $\text{kN}$

          Principle

          The speeds are known at the start and at the end of the distance, so we use the work–energy principle. Air resistance is the only force other than the weight, and it points against the motion, so the work it does is $W_\text{d} = -F_\text{d} h$. The work comes from the work–energy principle, and the force from the work.

          Free-body diagram

          The skydiver with the parachute as a rectangle. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{d}$ from the top surface up, much longer than the weight, because the skydiver slows down. The velocity down is marked separately and not as a force.

          Formula

          The kinetic energies are $E_{\text{k},1} = \tfrac12 mv_1^2$ and $E_{\text{k},2} = \tfrac12 mv_2^2$. Zero level at the height of the final situation: $E_{\text{k},1} + mgh - F_\text{d} h = E_{\text{k},2}$, so

          $F_\text{d}$$= mg + \dfrac{E_{\text{k},1} - E_{\text{k},2}}{h}$

          Conversions

          We calculate in newtons and finally change to kilonewtons: $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.

          Substitution

          $E_{\text{k},1}$$= \tfrac12\cdot 70.0\,\text{kg}\cdot(50.0\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 87\,500\,\text{J}$

          and

          $E_{\text{k},2}$$= \tfrac12\cdot 70.0\,\text{kg}\cdot(6.0\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 1260\,\text{J}$

          so

          $F_\text{d}$$= 70.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + \dfrac{87\,500\,\text{J} - 1260\,\text{J}}{60.0\,\text{m}}$
          $= 686.7\,\text{N} + 1437.3\,\text{N}$
          $= 2124\,\text{N}$
          $\approx 2.12\,\text{kN}$

          Answer

          $2.12\,\text{kN}$

          Upwards, three significant figures like the mass $70.0\,\text{kg}$, the speed $50.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the distance $60.0\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The air resistance is about three times the weight $687\,\text{N}$, so the net force points up and the skydiver slows down, as it should. From the equation of motion the deceleration is $(2124 - 687)\,\text{N} : 70.0\,\text{kg} \approx 20\,\text{m/s}^2$, and then

          $\tfrac12(v_1^2 - v_2^2) : a$
          $\approx 1232 : 20.5$
          $\approx 60\,\text{m}$

          as in the problem.
