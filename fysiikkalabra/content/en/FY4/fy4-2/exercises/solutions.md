## fy4-2-L1-01
**Given:** initial velocity $v_0 = 0$ (from rest), final velocity $v = 25\,\text{m/s}$, time $\Delta t = 8.0\,\text{s}$; the acceleration is uniform
**Find:** the car's acceleration $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it took. The acceleration is uniform, so the velocity grows by the same amount every second and the average over the whole interval equals the acceleration at every moment.
**Formula:** $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$, where $\Delta v = v - v_0$. So $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** The velocity is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the result comes out directly in $\text{m/s}^2$. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{25\,\text{m/s} - 0\,\text{m/s}}{8.0\,\text{s}} = 3.125\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $3.1\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures like the velocity $25\,\text{m/s}$ and the time $8.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The velocity grows by about $3\,\text{m/s}$ every second, and $8 \cdot 3\,\text{m/s} = 24\,\text{m/s}$, which is close to the given $25\,\text{m/s}$. Reaching $25\,\text{m/s} = 90\,\text{km/h}$ in eight seconds is a brisk acceleration for an ordinary passenger car. The acceleration is positive because the velocity grows.

## fy4-2-L1-02
**Given:** initial velocity $v_0 = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$, acceleration $a = -1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$, time $t = 3.0\,\text{s}$; the direction of motion is positive
**Find:** the velocity $v$ at $t = 3.0\,\text{s}$ in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** The acceleration of braking is constant and opposite to the velocity, so the velocity decreases by $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ every second. In uniformly accelerated motion the velocity is the initial velocity plus the product of the acceleration and the time.
**Formula:** $v = v_0 + a\,t$. The formula is already solved for the velocity.
**Conversions:** All quantities are in SI units, and $\text{m/s}^2 \cdot \text{s} = \text{m/s}$. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = 6.0\,\text{m/s} + (-1.5\,\text{m/s}^2) \cdot 3.0\,\text{s} = 6.0\,\text{m/s} - 4.5\,\text{m/s} = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $1.5\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the given values. The difference in the calculation is known to the nearest tenth.
**Reasonableness:** The cyclist would stop in the time $6.0\,\text{m/s} : 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2 = 4.0\,\text{s}$, so at $3.0\,\text{s}$ she is still moving slowly forward. The velocity is positive and smaller than at the start, as it should be in braking.

**Substitution:** $s = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (1.50\,\text{s})^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 2.25\,\text{s}^2 \approx 11.04\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $11.0\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the time $1.50\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The final velocity is $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.50\,\text{s} \approx 14.7\,\text{m/s}$, so the average velocity is about $7.4\,\text{m/s}$ and $7.4\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.50\,\text{s} \approx 11\,\text{m}$. **Principle:** The braking is uniform, so the acceleration is constant. The acceleration comes from the change in velocity and the time, and then the distance comes from the equation of position. The car does not turn round, so the distance equals the displacement.

**Conversions:** The velocity is converted by substituting units: $72\,\text{km/h} = 72 \cdot \tfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20\,\text{m/s}$.

**Answer:** $40\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the velocity $72\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $4.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The velocity decreases steadily from $20\,\text{m/s}$ to zero, so the average velocity is $10\,\text{m/s}$ and $10\,\text{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s} = 40\,\text{m}$, the same result. The magnitude of the acceleration, $5\,\text{m/s}^2$, is about half of $g$, which is an ordinary hard braking on a dry road.

**Reading the graph:** The tangent crosses the time axis at $t = 2.0\,\text{s}$, where $x = 0$, and passes through the grid crossing $(6.0\,\text{s};\, 16\,\text{m})$. These points are far apart and are read exactly.

**Conversions:** The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so the result comes out in $\text{m/s}$. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{16\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{6.0\,\text{s} - 2.0\,\text{s}} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the values read from the graph.
**Reasonableness:** In the graph the cart is at the position $8.0\,\text{m}$ at $4.0\,\text{s}$, so its average velocity from the start is $2.0\,\text{m/s}$. For an object accelerating uniformly from rest the final velocity is twice the average velocity, that is, $4.0\,\text{m/s}$, the same as from the tangent.

## fy4-2-L2-03

**Find:** the height of the rise $h$ from the point of release in metres
**Principle:** Take upwards as the positive direction, so the acceleration is $-g$ during the whole flight. At the highest point the velocity is zero for a moment. First solve the time of the rise from the condition $v = 0$, then the height from the equation of position.

**Answer:** $11.5\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the initial velocity $15.0\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** During the rise the velocity decreases steadily from $15.0\,\text{m/s}$ to zero, so the average velocity is $7.5\,\text{m/s}$ and $7.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.529\,\text{s} \approx 11.5\,\text{m}$. A height of about three storeys is plausible for a hard throw.

**Find:** the Moon's acceleration of free fall $g_\text{M}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

## fy4-2-L3-01
**Given:** initial velocity $v_0 = 8.00\,\text{m/s}$ upwards, point of release $6.00\,\text{m}$ above the ground, acceleration of free fall from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is small
**Find:** the time $t$ from the throw to hitting the ground, in seconds
**Principle:** The ball's acceleration is $g$ downwards during the whole flight, also at the highest point, so the whole flight is one uniformly accelerated motion. Place the origin at the point of release and take upwards as positive. The ground is then at the position $y = -6.00\,\text{m}$, and the time comes from a quadratic equation.
**Formula:** $y = v_0\,t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^2$. When $y = -h$: $\tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^2 - v_0\,t - h = 0$, whose positive solution is $t = \dfrac{v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g}$
**Conversions:** All quantities are in SI units. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** Under the root $v_0^2 + 2gh = (8.00\,\text{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 6.00\,\text{m} = 181.72\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, and $\sqrt{181.72\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} \approx 13.48\,\text{m/s}$. So $t = \dfrac{8.00\,\text{m/s} + 13.48\,\text{m/s}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 2.19\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $2.19\,\text{s}$, three significant figures like the initial velocity and the height. The other solution, $t \approx -0.56\,\text{s}$, is rejected because it is before the throw.
**Reasonableness:** The rise takes $8.00\,\text{m/s} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.82\,\text{s}$ and lifts the ball $3.26\,\text{m}$, so from the highest point to the ground it is $9.26\,\text{m}$. A fall from rest takes $\sqrt{2 \cdot 9.26\,\text{m} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 1.37\,\text{s}$, and $0.82\,\text{s} + 1.37\,\text{s} = 2.19\,\text{s}$, the same result.

## fy4-2-L2-08
**Find:** how the braking distance $s$ of the cyclist changes when the initial speed is the same $8.0\,\text{m/s}$ but the magnitude of the acceleration decreases from $4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ to $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Principle:** The braking is uniform, so the acceleration is constant and the speed decreases by the same amount every second, $v = v_0 + a\,t$. The direction of motion is chosen as positive, so the acceleration in braking is negative. The braking distance is the area between the $(t, v)$ graph and the time axis, because the speed is positive all the time: a triangle whose height is the initial speed $v_0$ and whose width is the braking time $t$, that is $s = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,t$. So the distance depends both on the initial speed and on how long stopping takes.
**Model answer:** The braking distance becomes twice as long. When the cyclist stops, $v = 0$, so the braking time is $t = \dfrac{0 - v_0}{a}$.

Dry road: $t_1 = \dfrac{0 - 8.0\,\text{m/s}}{-4.0\,\text{m/s}^2}$, that is $t_1 = 2.0\,\text{s}$

Wet road: $t_2 = \dfrac{0 - 8.0\,\text{m/s}}{-2.0\,\text{m/s}^2}$, that is $t_2 = 4.0\,\text{s}$

The braking distances are the areas of the triangles:

$s_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.0\,\text{s}$, that is $s_1 = 8.0\,\text{m}$

$s_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s}$, that is $s_2 = 16\,\text{m}$

The ratio is $16\,\text{m} : 8.0\,\text{m} = 2.0$. An acceleration half as large doubles the braking time, and when the height $v_0$ of the triangle stays the same, the area also doubles.

The answer "stays the same" forgets that the distance also depends on the braking time. The answer "is halved" reads the dependence the wrong way round: a smaller acceleration makes stopping last longer. The answer "becomes 4 times as long" makes the distance grow with the square of the time but forgets that the acceleration is halved at the same time.

Check with the formula $x - x_0 = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$, term by term:

$v_0\,t = 8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s}$, that is $v_0\,t = 32\,\text{m}$

$\tfrac{1}{2}\,a = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (-2.0\,\text{m/s}^2)$, that is $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a = -1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

$\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 = -1.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (4.0\,\text{s})^2$, that is $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 = -16\,\text{m}$

$s_2 = 32\,\text{m} - 16\,\text{m}$, that is $s_2 = 16\,\text{m}$

## fy4-2-L1-09
**Given:** drop height $h = 20\,\text{m}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 0$; from the table (MAFY table, Solar System, Jupiter) free-fall acceleration $g = 24.79\,\text{m/s}^2$
