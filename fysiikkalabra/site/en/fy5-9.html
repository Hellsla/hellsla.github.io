The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum

Why does every swing, pendulum and body bobbing on a spring oscillate at its own pace, and what determines that pace?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • recognise simple harmonic motion and explain which quantities affect the period.
  • calculate the period and the frequency of a mass on a spring and of a pendulum.
  • determine the period of a pendulum from a measurement, with its uncertainty.

Prior knowledge

  • The period $T$ is the duration of one cycle, the frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$ and the amplitude is the greatest displacement from the equilibrium positionchapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude
  • A harmonic force $F = -kx$ points towards the equilibrium position, and the elastic potential energy of a spring is $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$chapter Spring force and spring energy

Assumptions of the model

The surface of the mass on a spring is frictionless, and the only force along the motion is the harmonic force of the spring, $F = -kx$. The pendulum is a simple pendulum: a point mass on a massless string without air resistance, and its motion is calculated from the exact equation also at large angles, so the small-angle approximation has not been made. There is no damping, so the energy is conserved. The period, the frequency and the energies are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

The motion is calculated in small time steps: at each step the acceleration is obtained from the force ($a = -\tfrac{k}{m}x$ for the spring and the tangential acceleration $a_\text{t} = -g\sin\alpha$ for the pendulum), and the speed and the position are updated from it. The period $T$ is the time measured from the motion, from the starting moment until the body has again stopped at the same extreme position; it is not calculated from the formula for the period. The frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$. The energy is $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$ for the spring and $E = mgl(1 - \cos\alpha_0)$ for the pendulum, when the zero level of the potential energy is at the lowest point of the pendulum; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

      4Theory summary

      Key idea

      In the chapter Spring force and spring energy the force of a spring was $F = -kx$: it is directly proportional to the displacement $x$ from the equilibrium position and always points towards the equilibrium position. When a body is pulled aside and released, the force accelerates it towards the equilibrium position. At the equilibrium position the force is zero, but the body has speed, so it carries on to the other side. There the force slows it down, stops it at the extreme position and turns it back. The motion repeats itself in the same way: the body oscillates. When the oscillation is caused by a harmonic force, the motion is called simple harmonic motion.

      The figure shows the rhythm of the motion. At the extreme positions $x = A$ and $x = -A$ the speed is zero, but the force and the acceleration are greatest. At the equilibrium position the situation is the opposite: the force and the acceleration are zero and the speed is greatest. The acceleration always points towards the equilibrium position, in the same direction as the force.

      Position as a function of time

      When the position of a simple harmonic oscillator is plotted as a function of time, the result is a sine-shaped curve. The concepts of the chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude can be seen in it directly. The amplitude $A$ is the greatest displacement from the equilibrium position. The period $T$ is the time in which the body makes one complete oscillation back and forth and returns to the same point moving in the same direction, for example the interval between two successive peaks. The frequency $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$ tells us the number of oscillations per second.

      Why the amplitude does not change the period

      A simple harmonic oscillator has a remarkable property. If the amplitude is doubled, the distance travelled in one cycle doubles. At the same time the force at every corresponding point also doubles, because $F$ is proportional to the displacement. A doubled force gives a doubled acceleration, and in the same time a doubled acceleration makes the speed grow twice as much. So the longer distance is covered at a greater speed, and the time taken is the same. The period of a simple harmonic oscillator does not depend on the amplitude. It depends only on how large the mass in motion is and how stiff the spring that restores it is.

      Period of a simple harmonic oscillator

      Mass on a spring

      When a body oscillates on a spring and the only force along the motion is the harmonic force of the spring, the period is

      $$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

      where $T$ is the period ($\text{s}$), $m$ the mass of the oscillating body ($\text{kg}$) and $k$ the spring constant of the spring ($\text{N/m}$). A greater mass accelerates more slowly under the same force, so the period grows as the mass grows. A stiffer spring gives a greater force, so the period becomes shorter. The square root means that the mass must be quadrupled to double the period. The formula holds for a spring hanging vertically too: the weight only shifts the equilibrium position, and the displacement from it obeys the same law.

      The formula can be justified with circular motion. When a body moves uniformly around a circle of radius $A$, its shadow on a wall in the direction of a diameter moves back and forth. The acceleration of the shadow is a component of the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{4\pi^2 A}{T^2}$, and it is $-\tfrac{4\pi^2}{T^2}x$, that is, proportional to the displacement. For a mass on a spring Newton's second law gives $a = -\tfrac{k}{m}x$. When the coefficients are set equal, $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{T^2} = \tfrac{k}{m}$, and the period can be solved from this.

      Simple pendulum

      In a simple pendulum a point mass hangs on a light string and swings without air resistance. At the extreme position the speed is zero, and the tension $\overline{S}$ of the string cancels the component of the weight along the string. The symbol $S$ is used here for the tension, because $T$ is the period. The motion is driven by the component of the weight along the path, $G_\text{t} = mg\sin\alpha$, which points towards the equilibrium position.

      For small angles $\sin\alpha$ is approximately the angle in radians, and the arc length from the equilibrium position is $s = l\alpha$. The restoring force is then $\tfrac{mg}{l}s$, which is proportional to the displacement, and the pendulum is a simple harmonic oscillator with "spring constant" $\tfrac{mg}{l}$. When this is substituted into the formula for the spring, the mass cancels. For small swings the period of a simple pendulum is

      $$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$

      where $T$ is the period ($\text{s}$), $l$ the length of the pendulum, that is, the distance from the point of suspension of the string to the centre of mass of the body ($\text{m}$), and $g$ the acceleration of free fall ($9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$). The period does not depend on the mass of the body, nor, for small swings, on the amplitude.

      The formula is an approximation that holds for small swing angles. As the starting angle grows, $\sin\alpha$ remains smaller than the angle in radians, the restoring force remains smaller than the harmonic one and the period increases slightly. The table gives the true period $T$ relative to the value $T_0$ given by the formula:

      Starting angle $T : T_0$
      $10^\circ$ $1.002$
      $20^\circ$ $1.008$
      $40^\circ$ $1.031$
      $60^\circ$ $1.073$

      For swings of less than $10^\circ$ the difference is less than $0.2\,\%$, which is smaller than the uncertainty of timing by hand.

      Energy in oscillation

      When friction and air resistance are neglected, the mechanical energy of the oscillator is conserved. The energy of a mass on a spring is the sum of the kinetic energy and the elastic potential energy of the spring. At the extreme position the speed is zero and all the energy is potential energy, so

      $$\frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$$

      where $m$ is the mass ($\text{kg}$), $v$ the speed ($\text{m/s}$), $k$ the spring constant ($\text{N/m}$), $x$ the displacement from the equilibrium position ($\text{m}$) and $A$ the amplitude ($\text{m}$). The right-hand side is the energy of the oscillation, $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$. At the equilibrium position $x = 0$, so all the energy is kinetic energy and the speed is greatest. In a pendulum the energy changes in the same way between gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy.

        Optional Damping

        Damping

        In real oscillators air resistance and friction change some of the mechanical energy into internal energy. The energy $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$ then decreases, and the amplitude decreases from cycle to cycle. This is called damping. When the damping is small, the period stays almost the same as without damping, so a pendulum clock that keeps time by the period does not run fast or slow as the amplitude changes. In a pendulum clock a weight or a spring supplies energy to replace the damping, so the amplitude stays almost constant. The shock absorbers of a car, on the other hand, are made to damp the oscillation quickly, so that the car does not keep rocking after every pothole.

        Common misconception

        Many people think that a heavy pendulum bob swings faster, because a greater weight acts on it. A greater force does act on a heavier bob, but it is also harder to accelerate: the acceleration $a = \tfrac{F}{m}$ stays the same, as in free fall. That is why the period of a pendulum does not depend on the mass. For a mass on a spring, however, the mass does matter, because the force of the spring does not grow with the mass.

        Another mistake is to think that a bigger swing takes longer. For a simple harmonic oscillator the longer distance is covered at a greater speed, so the period stays the same; for a pendulum this holds for small swings. A third mistake is to confuse the cycle with half a cycle: one cycle is a motion back and forth that ends at the same extreme position where it started.

        Summary

        • A harmonic force $F = -kx$ makes a body perform simple harmonic motion: position as a function of time is a sine-shaped curve with amplitude $A$ and period $T$.
        • The period of a mass on a spring is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$: it increases as the mass increases and decreases as the spring gets stiffer, but it does not depend on the amplitude.
        • The period of a simple pendulum with small swings is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$: it depends on the length and the acceleration of free fall, not on the mass. At large angles the period is slightly longer.
        • The frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$. The period is measured from several cycles, so that the uncertainty of timing is divided among many cycles.
        • The energy of the oscillation $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$ is conserved when friction and air resistance are neglected. Damping reduces the energy and the amplitude.

        Worked example

        Problem

        A body of mass $0.400\,\text{kg}$ is attached to a spring and oscillates on a frictionless horizontal surface. A student measures with a stopwatch that ten complete oscillations take $8.00\,\text{s}$. What is the spring constant of the spring?

        Step 1/7

        Given and find. Mass $m = 0.400\,\text{kg}$, time of ten cycles $t = 8.00\,\text{s}$. We want the spring constant $k$.

        Step 2/7

        Principle. The only force along the motion acting on the body is the harmonic force of the spring, so the motion is simple harmonic motion and the period is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$. The period is obtained by dividing the time of ten cycles by ten.

        Step 3/7

        Formula solved. Square the formula for the period and solve $k$:

        $$\begin{aligned} T^2 &= \frac{4\pi^2 m}{k} \\ k &= \frac{4\pi^2 m}{T^2} \end{aligned}$$

        Step 4/7

        Unit conversions. None are needed: the mass is in kilograms and the time in seconds, and $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}^2} = \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$.

        Step 5/7

        Substitution. The period is

        $T$$= 8.00\,\text{s} : 10$
        $= 0.800\,\text{s}$

        so

        $$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{4\pi^2 m}{T^2} \\ &= \frac{4\pi^2 \cdot 0.400\,\text{kg}}{(0.800\,\text{s})^2} \\ &= 24.67\,\text{N/m} \end{aligned}$$

        Step 6/7

        Answer. The spring constant is about $24.7\,\text{N/m}$, to three significant figures as in the mass $0.400\,\text{kg}$ and the time $8.00\,\text{s}$.

        Step 7/7

        Reasonableness. Check the other way round: if the spring constant were exactly $25\,\text{N/m}$, the period would be $2\pi\sqrt{0.400\,\text{kg} : 25\,\text{N/m}} \approx 0.795\,\text{s}$, very close to the measured $0.800\,\text{s}$. A spring of about $25\,\text{N/m}$ stretches $1\,\text{cm}$ when pulled with a force of $0.25\,\text{N}$, which suits a light school spring. The frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{T} = 1.25\,\text{Hz}$, so there is a little over one oscillation per second, and they are easy to count.

        The mean of repeated measurements reduces the effect of random error. The time of ten cycles is ten times the period, and because the uncertainty of timing concerns only the starting and stopping moments, measuring ten cycles reduces the uncertainty of the period to one tenth.

        Formula

        $\overline{t}_{10}$$= \dfrac{t_1 + t_2 + t_3 + t_4 + t_5}{5}$

        $T = \dfrac{\overline{t}_{10}}{10}$, $\Delta T = \dfrac{\Delta t}{10}$

        The times are in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

        $\overline{t}_{10}$$= \dfrac{100.20\,\text{s}}{5}$
        $= 20.04\,\text{s}$
        $T$$= \dfrac{20.04\,\text{s}}{10}$
        $= 2.004\,\text{s}$
        $\Delta T$$= \dfrac{0.2\,\text{s}}{10}$
        $= 0.02\,\text{s}$

        $T = 2.004\,\text{s}$

        Reasonableness

        The small-swing formula gives a period of $2.006\,\text{s}$ for a pendulum one metre long, which is within the uncertainty interval of the measurement. The spread of the measurements is of the same order as the estimated uncertainty: half of the range is $\tfrac{20.21\,\text{s} - 19.88\,\text{s}}{2} \approx 0.17\,\text{s}$.

        Why ten cycles?

        Another student measures the time of one cycle of the same pendulum. Her timing uncertainty is also about $0.2\,\text{s}$.How large is the uncertainty of her period compared with the ten-cycle measurement?

        • The same person makes the same timing error of about $0.2\,\text{s}$ in both the one-cycle and the ten-cycle measurement. In the ten-cycle measurement the error is divided by ten, in the one-cycle measurement it is not, so the uncertainty of one cycle is about $0.2\,\text{s}$.

        • The resolution of the watch is $0.01\,\text{s}$, but in timing by hand the reaction time is a much greater source of uncertainty. The uncertainty can be greater than the smallest scale division: about $0.2\,\text{s}$ per measurement.

        • The uncertainty does not grow when fewer cycles are measured: in a one-cycle measurement the timing uncertainty of about $0.2\,\text{s}$ falls directly on the period. In a ten-cycle measurement it is reduced to one tenth per cycle.

        • Correct. The uncertainty of timing comes from starting and stopping the watch, not from the number of cycles. In a one-cycle measurement the whole $0.2\,\text{s}$ falls on one cycle, which is about $10\,\%$ of a period of two seconds. In a ten-cycle measurement it is divided among ten cycles.

        Optional 3. Explain with energy

        A body at the end of a spring oscillates without friction. Using conservation of energy, explain where the speed of the body is greatest, why doubling the amplitude doubles the greatest speed, and what this tells us about the period.

        Model answer

        In frictionless oscillation the mechanical energy is conserved: $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$. The elastic potential energy of the spring is zero at the equilibrium position $x = 0$, so there all the energy is kinetic energy and the speed is greatest. At the extreme positions the speed is zero. For the greatest speed we get $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_\text{max}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$, that is, $v_\text{max} = A\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{m}}$, so it is directly proportional to the amplitude. Doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy, and because the kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, the speed doubles. That is why double the distance is covered in the same period.

          1. The spring of a mass–spring system is replaced with a spring whose spring constant is four times as large. The mass stays the same. How does the period change?
            Answer

            The period halves. In the formula $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ the spring constant is in the denominator under the square root, and $\sqrt{4} = 2$: a stiffer spring pulls the body with a greater force, so it oscillates faster.

          2. Where is the acceleration of a body in simple harmonic motion greatest, and where is the speed greatest?
            Answer

            The acceleration is greatest at the extreme positions, where the displacement and therefore the harmonic force $F = -kx$ are greatest. The speed is greatest at the equilibrium position, where the force and the acceleration are zero and all the energy is kinetic energy.

            On Mars the restoring force is smaller, so the pendulum swings more slowly and the period increases. The acceleration of free fall is about a third, and $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.7$, so the period is about $1.6$ to $1.7$ times as large.

          1. A body of mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$ oscillates without friction on a spring. The spring constant of the spring is $200\,\text{N/m}$ and the amplitude of the oscillation is $0.100\,\text{m}$.

            What is the speed of the body when it is $0.0500\,\text{m}$ from the equilibrium position?

            m/s
            Answer

            $1.7\,\text{m/s}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            mass $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, spring constant $k = 200\,\text{N/m}$, amplitude $A = 0.100\,\text{m}$, displacement $x = 0.0500\,\text{m}$, frictionless oscillation

            Find

            the speed $v$ at the displacement $x$ in $\text{m/s}$

            Principle

            In frictionless oscillation the mechanical energy is conserved. At every point the sum of the kinetic energy and the elastic potential energy of the spring equals the energy at the extreme position, where the speed is zero. At the displacement $x$ some of the energy is still in the spring, so the speed is smaller than at the equilibrium position.

            Formula

            $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$$= \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$

            so $v^2 = \dfrac{k}{m}(A^2 - x^2)$ and $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}(A^2 - x^2)}$

            Conversions

            The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

            Substitution

            First

            $\dfrac{k}{m}$$= \dfrac{200\,\text{N/m}}{0.50\,\text{kg}}$
            $= 400\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$
            $A^2 - x^2$$= (0.100\,\text{m})^2 - (0.0500\,\text{m})^2$
            $= 0.00750\,\text{m}^2$

            so

            $v$$= \sqrt{400\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.00750\,\text{m}^2}$
            $= \sqrt{3.00\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
            $= 1.732\,\text{m/s}$

            Answer

            $1.7\,\text{m/s}$

            Two significant figures as in the mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$.

            Reasonableness

            At the equilibrium position the speed would be

            $A\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{m}}$$= 0.100\,\text{m} \cdot 20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
            $= 2.0\,\text{m/s}$

            At half the amplitude the spring holds only a quarter of the energy, because $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so three quarters of it is kinetic energy and the speed is $\sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.87$ times the greatest, that is, $1.7\,\text{m/s}$. So the speed is not half of the greatest.

          2. When a passenger of mass $80\,\text{kg}$ sits in a car of mass $1200\,\text{kg}$, the body of the car sinks $3.0\,\text{cm}$ lower on its suspension. Model the whole car with the passenger as one body on one spring and neglect the shock absorbers.

            What is the period of the vertical oscillation of the car body when the car drives into a pothole with the passenger on board?

            s
            Answer

            $1.4\,\text{s}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            mass of the passenger $m_\text{p} = 80\,\text{kg}$, mass of the car $1200\,\text{kg}$, sinking $\Delta x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; model: one body on one spring, no damping

            Find

            the period $T$ of the oscillation of the car body in $\text{s}$

            Principle

            Two ideas. First the spring constant: when the passenger has sat down and the car is at rest again, the extra force of the spring equals the weight of the passenger, so $k\Delta x = m_\text{p}g$. Then the period: in vertical oscillation the weight only shifts the equilibrium position, so the period is $2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$, where $m$ is the whole oscillating mass, the car and the passenger.

            Formula

            $k = \dfrac{m_\text{p}g}{\Delta x}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$, so

            $T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m\,\Delta x}{m_\text{p}g}}$

            Conversions

            $\Delta x$$= 3.0\,\text{cm}$
            $= 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
            $= 0.030\,\text{m}$

            the oscillating mass

            $m$$= 1200\,\text{kg} + 80\,\text{kg}$
            $= 1280\,\text{kg}$

            Substitution

            $k$$= \dfrac{80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.030\,\text{m}}$
            $= 26\,160\,\text{N/m}$
            $T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1280\,\text{kg}}{26\,160\,\text{N/m}}}$
            $= 2\pi\sqrt{0.04893\,\text{s}^2}$
            $= 2\pi \cdot 0.2212\,\text{s}$
            $= 1.390\,\text{s}$

            Answer

            $1.4\,\text{s}$

            Two significant figures as in the sinking $3.0\,\text{cm}$.

            Reasonableness

            Cars rock on their suspension with periods of about a second, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. In reality the shock absorbers damp the oscillation to one or two swings, and each wheel has its own spring, so the model gives only the order of magnitude.

          3. The pendulum of a pendulum clock is $0.994\,\text{m}$ long, and in spring the clock runs exactly right. In the heat of summer the rod of the pendulum lengthens by $0.50\,\text{mm}$ because of thermal expansion. The clock counts swings and assumes that every cycle is as long as in spring. The swings are small.

            By how many seconds per day does the clock run slow?

            s
            Answer

            $22\,\text{s}$

            Show the solution

            Given

            in spring the length of the pendulum $l = 0.994\,\text{m}$, in summer it is longer by $\Delta l = 0.50\,\text{mm}$, small swings, a day $t = 86\,400\,\text{s}$

            Find

            how much the clock runs slow in a day, $\Delta t$ in $\text{s}$

            Principle

            For small swings the period is $2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, so a longer pendulum swings more slowly. The clock counts cycles and adds the period $T$ it had in spring for each of them, although the real cycle lasts $T'$. In a day it manages to make only $\tfrac{t}{T'}$ of the longer cycles $T'$, so it shows the time $\tfrac{t}{T'}T$ and falls behind. The acceleration of free fall cancels in the ratio of the periods.

            Formula

            $\dfrac{T'}{T}$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{l + \Delta l}{l}}$

            $\Delta t = t - \dfrac{t}{T'}T$, so

            $\Delta t$$= t\left(1 - \dfrac{T}{T'}\right)$
            $= t\left(1 - \sqrt{\dfrac{l}{l + \Delta l}}\right)$

            Conversions

            $\Delta l$$= 0.50\,\text{mm}$
            $= 0.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
            $= 0.00050\,\text{m}$

            a day

            $t$$= 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
            $= 86\,400\,\text{s}$

            Substitution

            $\Delta t$$= 86\,400\,\text{s} \cdot \left(1 - \sqrt{\dfrac{0.994\,\text{m}}{0.9945\,\text{m}}}\right)$
            $= 86\,400\,\text{s} \cdot (1 - 0.999\,749)$
            $= 86\,400\,\text{s} \cdot 0.000\,251$
            $= 21.7\,\text{s}$

            The intermediate result must not be rounded: even rounding $1 - 0.9997 = 0.0003$ would give $26\,\text{s}$.

            Answer

            $22\,\text{s}$

            Two significant figures as in the lengthening $0.50\,\text{mm}$.

            Reasonableness

            The length increases by the relative amount $\tfrac{0.50\,\text{mm}}{994\,\text{mm}} \approx 0.050\,\%$, and because of the square root the period increases by about half of that, $0.025\,\%$. Of a day that is $0.00025 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s} \approx 22\,\text{s}$. That is why accurate pendulum clocks used pendulums that compensate for changes in temperature.
