Ideal gas law pV = nRT

Why is a helium balloon much lighter than an air balloon of the same size? Does it contain less gas?

In this lesson you learn

  • You can explain why all gases contain the same number of moles at the same pressure, volume and temperature.
  • You can calculate quantities describing the state of a gas with the equation of state of an ideal gas.
  • You can explain gas pressure as collisions of particles with the walls of the container.

1Think about it

At a party two balloons of the same size are filled: one from a helium cylinder, the other with a pump full of air. Both are at room temperature, and the pressure inside each is almost the same as the outside air. The helium balloon rises to the ceiling, and the air balloon falls to the floor. The gas is invisible, and you cannot count its particles one by one.

How could you compare the amounts of gas in the two balloons when you know only the pressure, volume and temperature?

The nozzle of a syringe is blocked when it contains 60 ml of air at a pressure of 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed until the volume of the air is 30 ml. The temperature does not change.What is the pressure of the air now?

  • The pressure is inversely proportional to the volume, not to its square. The volume is halved once, so the pressure doubles once: $200\,\text{kPa}$.

  • At constant temperature, pressure and volume change in opposite directions: Boyle's law $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$ means inverse proportionality. When the volume is halved, the pressure doubles.

  • Correct: Boyle's law $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$ gives $p_2 = 100\,\text{kPa} \cdot 60\,\text{ml} / 30\,\text{ml} = 200\,\text{kPa}$. Half the volume, twice the pressure.

  • The amount of air stays the same, but the pressure does not. By Boyle's law $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$, $p_2 = p_1V_1/V_2 = 100\,\text{kPa} \cdot 60\,\text{ml} / 30\,\text{ml} = 200\,\text{kPa}$.

Given

helium, from the table $m = 0.83\,\text{g}$ and $M = 4.00\,\text{g/mol}$

Find

the quotient $m/M$ in mol

Principle

The molar mass is the mass of one mole, so the mass of the gas divided by the molar mass tells how many moles of gas are in the flask. The calculation needs neither pressure nor temperature, because the mass and the molar mass determine the amount of substance directly.

Formula

$M = \dfrac{m}{n}$, so $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$

Conversions

No conversions are needed, because the mass and the molar mass are both in grams. The grams cancel, and the mole is left.

Substitution

$\dfrac{m}{M}$$= \dfrac{0.83\,\text{g}}{4.00\,\text{g/mol}}$
$= 0.2075\,\text{mol}$

Answer

$0.21\,\text{mol}$

Two significant figures. The limiting datum is the mass $0.83\,\text{g}$ (two digits); the molar mass $4.00\,\text{g/mol}$ has three digits. The intermediate result $0.2075\,\text{mol}$ of the substitution is kept with four digits for the next calculation.

Reasonableness

At the same pressure and temperature, $5.00\,\textsf{l}$ contains about $5.00 \cdot 0.041 \approx 0.21\,\text{mol}$ of any gas, so the result is believable. Checking backwards: $0.2075\,\text{mol} \cdot 4.00\,\text{g/mol} = 0.83\,\text{g}$.

How to use

Change the number of particles $N$, the temperature $T$ and the volume $V$ with the sliders. Reset measurement starts calculating the average of the pressure from the beginning. The colour tells the speed of a particle: hot gas is orange. A flash at the wall is one hit. Assumptions of the model

The box is a flat model in which area corresponds to volume. The particles collide only with the walls, not with each other. A change in volume does not change the speeds of the particles, so the temperature stays the same.

How the calculation works

The pressure is shown relative to the reference state ($N = 100$, $T = 300\,\text{K}$, $V = 100\,\%$), in which $p/p_\text{ref} = 1.00$ (dashed line). Show the solution

Given

temperature $T_2 = 600\,\text{K}$, that is, twice the temperature of the reference state $T_1 = 300\,\text{K}$; the volume $V = 100\,\%$ stays the same; in the reference state $N_1 = 100$ and $p/p_\text{ref} = 1.00$

Find

the number of particles $N_2$ for which $p/p_\text{ref}$ is again $1.00$

Principle

The volume is constant, so by the equation of state the pressure is proportional to the product of the number of particles and the temperature in kelvins: $p \propto NT$. Experiment 1 showed that $p \propto N$, and the row $N = 100$ shows the pressure doubling at 600 K, so $p \propto T$. The pressure stays the same when the product $NT$ stays the same.

Formula

$N_1 T_1 = N_2 T_2$, so $N_2 = N_1 \cdot \dfrac{T_1}{T_2}$

Conversions

The temperatures are already in kelvins and the units cancel in the ratio, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$N_2$$= 100 \cdot \dfrac{300\,\text{K}}{600\,\text{K}}$
$= 50$

Answer

$N_2 = 50$

Particles. The numbers of particles $N$ are exact counts and the temperature was doubled exactly ($300\,\text{K} \to 600\,\text{K}$), so the ratio $300/600 = 1/2$ is exact and does not limit the result; the answer is an exact number.

Reasonableness

The temperature doubled, so the pressure would double, and halving the number of particles brings it back: $\tfrac{50}{100} \cdot \tfrac{600\,\text{K}}{300\,\text{K}} = 1.00$. The number of particles must be reduced, not increased, because hotter particles hit the wall both more often and more forcefully.

4Theory summary

Key idea

The gas laws in the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law applied to a fixed amount of gas. Now we compare different amounts of gas and different gases. Fill a 5.00 litre balloon at a pressure of 101 kPa and a temperature of 20 °C, one after another, with helium, nitrogen, air and carbon dioxide. The masses of the gases are very different: 0.83 g, 5.78 g, 6.02 g and 9.10 g. But when you divide each mass by the molar mass of the gas, the result is about 0.207 mol every time. So at the same pressure and temperature, the same volume contains the same amount of gas, whatever the gas is. The helium balloon is lighter because helium particles are lighter, not because there is less gas.

Even a small amount of gas contains an enormous number of particles. So we do not count them one by one but in moles, in the same way as eggs are counted in dozens. One mole is $6.022 \cdot 10^{23}$ particles, and the number $N_\text{A} = 6.022 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{1/mol}$ is called the Avogadro constant. The amount of substance $n$ tells you how many moles of particles there are. The mass of one mole is the molar mass $M$ of the substance: 4.00 g/mol for helium, 29.0 g/mol for air on average and 44.0 g/mol for carbon dioxide. So you can get the amount of substance both from the number of particles and from the mass:

$$n = \frac{N}{N_\text{A}} = \frac{m}{M}$$

where $N$ is the number of particles, $m$ is the mass (g or kg) and $M$ is the molar mass (g/mol or kg/mol).

The ideal gas is a microscopic model. In it, the particles are thought of as points, and they interact with each other and with the walls of the container only when they collide. Pressure comes from collisions with the walls. It grows when there are more particles, when they move faster (that is, the temperature is higher) or when the volume is smaller. Real gases follow the model well in ordinary conditions. The model starts to fail at high pressures and low temperatures, when the particles are close together and the attraction between them condenses the gas into a liquid.

Equation of state of an ideal gas

The weighing result gives a number that is the same for all gases: pressure multiplied by volume and divided by the amount of substance and the temperature in kelvins. With the mean amount of substance of the four gases, 0.2071 mol, it is

$$\begin{aligned} R &= \frac{pV}{nT} \\ &= \frac{101\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.00500\,\text{m}^3}{0.2071\,\text{mol} \cdot 293.15\,\text{K}} \\ &\approx 8.3\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \end{aligned}$$

Precise measurements give the value $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$ for all gases, as long as the pressure is not very high and the temperature is not very low. This number is called the molar gas constant. From it we get the equation of state of an ideal gas

$$pV = nRT$$

where $p$ is the pressure (Pa), $V$ the volume (m³), $n$ the amount of substance (mol), $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$ the molar gas constant and $T$ the temperature in kelvins (K). The type of gas does not appear in the equation at all. A temperature $\theta$ given in degrees Celsius is converted to kelvins by adding 273.15 to the numerical value. The pressure is always the total pressure in pascals and the volume is in cubic metres, because the unit of $R$ contains the joule: 1 Pa · m³ = 1 N/m² · m³ = 1 N · m = 1 J.

When you solve the equation of state for pressure, you see that the pressure doubles when the number of particles or the temperature in kelvins doubles, or when the volume is halved. So the pressure is directly proportional to the number of particles and the temperature in kelvins and inversely proportional to the volume, that is, $p \propto NT/V$.

In the simulation you can investigate this at the particle level: change the number of particles, the temperature and the volume, and compare the pressure measured at the walls with the prediction of the equation of state, which you calculate yourself. Collisions between the particles do not change the pressure on average, so they have been left out of the simulation. In the simulation the pressure is shown relative to a reference state ($p/p_\text{ref}$), not in pascals. The reference state is $N = 100$ particles, $T = 300\,\text{K}$ and the full volume ($V = 100\,\%$); in it $p/p_\text{ref} = 1$, and the value 2 means twice the reference pressure. The number of particles $N$ represents the amount of gas.

When the amount of gas does not change, $nR$ is constant, and the equation of state gives the combined gas law $p_1V_1/T_1 = p_2V_2/T_2$ (subscripts 1 and 2 are the initial and final state). From it you get every gas law of the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law when one quantity is kept constant.

Common misconception

Three mistakes keep appearing in calculations with the equation of state:

  • Degrees Celsius. In the equation of state the temperature is always in kelvins. For example, 0 °C would give a pressure of zero, but a gas has a pressure at the freezing point too.
  • Mixed units. If the pressure is in kilopascals but the volume in cubic metres, or the pressure in pascals but the volume in litres, the result is wrong by a factor of a thousand. The safest way is to convert all quantities to base units: pressure to pascals, volume to cubic metres and temperature to kelvins.
  • Heavy gas. Many people think that fewer moles of a heavier gas fit in the same volume. But the amount of substance depends only on the pressure, the volume and the temperature, not on the type of gas. The mass, on the other hand, depends on the molar mass.

Summary

  • The equation of state of an ideal gas is $pV = nRT$, where $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $T$ is in kelvins.
  • The amount of substance $n$ tells the amount of gas in moles: $n = N/N_\text{A} = m/M$.
  • The pressure is proportional to the expression $NT/V$: the pressure doubles when the number of particles or the temperature in kelvins doubles, or when the volume is halved.
  • When the amount of gas stays the same, $p_1V_1/T_1 = p_2V_2/T_2$, and from it you get the separate gas laws.
  • At the same pressure, temperature and volume there is always the same number of moles of gas, whatever the type of gas; weighing gives the same $m/M$ for all gases.
  • The ideal gas model works well in ordinary conditions and fails at high pressures and low temperatures.

    Worked example

    Problem

    A party balloon is filled with helium until its volume is 5.0 l. In the balloon the pressure is 101 kPa and the temperature is 20 °C. How many moles of helium are in the balloon, and what is the mass of the helium? The molar mass of helium is 4.0 g/mol.

    Step 1/7

    Given and find. The volume of the balloon is $V = 5.0\,\textsf{l}$, the pressure is $p = 101\,\text{kPa}$, the temperature is $\theta = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the molar mass of helium is $M = 4.0\,\text{g/mol}$. We want the amount of substance $n$ and the mass $m$.

    Step 2/7

    Principle. The balloon contains a certain amount of helium in a certain volume, at a certain pressure and temperature, and helium behaves as an ideal gas at ordinary pressure and room temperature. So the equation of state $pV = nRT$ gives the amount of substance, and the molar mass converts the amount of substance into mass.

    Step 3/7

    Rearranged formula. Solve the amount of substance from the equation of state and the mass from the equation $n = m/M$:

    $$\begin{aligned} n &= \frac{pV}{RT} \\ m &= nM \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4/7

    Unit conversions. The equation of state needs base units:

    • volume
    $V$$= 5.0\,\textsf{l}$
    $= 0.0050\,\text{m}^3$
    • pressure
    $p$$= 101\,\text{kPa}$
    $= 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$
    • temperature
    $T$$= (20 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
    $= 293.15\,\text{K}$
    • the molar mass stays as $M = 4.0\,\text{g/mol}$, so the mass comes out in grams

    Step 5/7

    Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

    $$\begin{aligned} n &= \frac{pV}{RT} \\ &= \frac{101\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0050\,\text{m}^3}{8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 293.15\,\text{K}} \\ &= 0.2073\,\text{mol} \approx 0.21\,\text{mol} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 6/7

    Result. The mass of the helium is calculated with the more precise amount of substance:

    $$\begin{aligned} m &= nM \\ &= 0.2073\,\text{mol} \cdot 4.0\,\text{g/mol} \\ &\approx 0.83\,\text{g} \end{aligned}$$

    The balloon contains 0.21 mol of helium, and its mass is 0.83 g. The amount of substance is limited by the volume 5.0 l, and the mass by the molar mass 4.0 g/mol as well, each with two digits, so the results are given with two significant figures. The pressure 101 kPa and the temperature 293 K have three digits and do not limit the result.

    Step 7/7

    Reasonableness. One mole of gas takes up about 24 litres at room temperature and normal pressure, so 5.0 litres contain about 5.0/24 ≈ 0.21 mol of gas.     1. Concept

    The volume of a gas is halved, and the temperature and the number of particles stay the same. The pressure doubles.What is the reason according to the particle model?

    • Correct. The speed is the same, but the distance between the walls becomes shorter, so each particle hits the walls more often. Each hit pushes the wall as much as before, so the pressure grows with the collision rate.

    • The speed of the particles depends on the temperature, and the temperature stays the same. In the simulation too, a change in volume does not change the speeds. The pressure grows because the same particle gets from wall to wall in a shorter time.

    • The particles do not change: in the ideal gas model they are points, and each hit pushes the wall as much as before, because the speed is the same. The pressure grows because there are more hits.

    • In the ideal gas model, collisions between the particles do not change the pressure on average, and in the simulation there are none at all. Still, the pressure doubles, because the particles hit the walls more often.

      Three significant figures. All the data ($1.00\,\textsf{l}$, $1.15\,\text{g}$, $101\,\text{kPa}$ and $R = 8.31$) are given with three digits, and $295\,\text{K}$ (from the whole degrees $22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) also has three digits. The gas is nitrogen ($28\,\text{g/mol}$).

      Reasonableness

      The amount of substance is

      $\dfrac{101\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.00100\,\text{m}^3}{8.31 \cdot 295.15}$
      $= 0.0412\,\text{mol}$

      which fits the fact that a litre of gas at room temperature contains about $0.04\,\text{mol}$. With the same amount of substance the mass would have been $0.16\,\text{g}$ for helium, $1.32\,\text{g}$ for oxygen, $1.65\,\text{g}$ for argon and $1.81\,\text{g}$ for carbon dioxide, so only nitrogen ($1.15\,\text{g}$) fits.

    3. At the end of the intake stroke, the cylinder of a tractor's diesel engine contains 0.500 l of air. The pressure of the air is 101 kPa and the temperature is 30 °C. At the end of the compression stroke the volume of the air is 0.0300 l and the pressure is 4.60 MPa. The valves are closed, so no air can escape from the cylinder.

      What is the temperature of the air at the end of compression in degrees Celsius? Diesel fuel ignites by itself at a temperature of about 250 °C. Does the fuel injected into the cylinder ignite?

      °C
      Answer

      555 °C

      Show the solution

      Given

      air in a cylinder, initially $V_1 = 0.500\,\textsf{l}$, $p_1 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, $\theta_1 = 30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; finally $V_2 = 0.0300\,\textsf{l}$, $p_2 = 4.60\,\text{MPa}$

      Find

      final temperature $\theta_2$ in degrees Celsius, and whether the fuel ignites ($250\,{}^\circ\text{C}$)

      Principle

      The valves are closed, so the amount of air stays the same and $nR$ is constant. Then the equation of state gives the combined gas law between the initial and final state.

      Formula

      $\dfrac{p_1 V_1}{T_1}$$= \dfrac{p_2 V_2}{T_2}$

      so

      $T_2$$= T_1 \cdot \dfrac{p_2 V_2}{p_1 V_1}$

      and $\theta_2 = T_2 - 273.15\,\text{K}$

      Conversions

      $T_1$$= (30 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
      $= 303.15\,\text{K}$

      The pressures and volumes appear as ratios, so they cancel when they are in the same units: $4.60\,\text{MPa} = 4600\,\text{kPa}$ and the litres stay as litres.

      Substitution

      $T_2$$= 303.15\,\text{K} \cdot \dfrac{4600\,\text{kPa} \cdot 0.0300\,\textsf{l}}{101\,\text{kPa} \cdot 0.500\,\textsf{l}}$
      $= 828.4\,\text{K}$
      $\theta_2$$= 828.4 - 273.15$
      $= 555.3\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

      Answer

      $555\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

      Three significant figures. All the data ($0.500\,\textsf{l}$, $101\,\text{kPa}$, $0.0300\,\textsf{l}$, $4.60\,\text{MPa}$ and $303\,\text{K}$ from the whole degrees $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) have three digits, and subtracting $273.15\,\text{K}$ from the temperature $828\,\text{K}$ keeps the units. The fuel ignites, because $555\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is clearly above $250\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      Reasonableness

      The volume is about $17$ times smaller and the pressure about $46$ times greater ($0.500/0.0300 \approx 16.7$ and $4600/101 \approx 45.5$), so the product $pV$ and with it the temperature in kelvins grow by a factor of about $45.5 / 16.7 \approx 2.73$: $303\,\text{K} \cdot 2.73 \approx 828\,\text{K}$. The result agrees, and the temperature rises when a gas is compressed, so a diesel engine does not need a spark plug.

    4. In Lapland there is a frosty morning, when the temperature of the outdoor air is −30 °C and the atmospheric pressure is 102 kPa.

      What volume does 1.00 mol of air take up in these conditions? Use the value $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$. Give the answer in litres.

      l
      Answer

      19.8 l

      Show the solution

      Given

      air, $n = 1.00\,\text{mol}$, $\theta = -30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $p = 102\,\text{kPa}$; $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

      Find

      volume $V$ in litres

      Principle

      The amount of substance, the pressure and the temperature of the air are known, so the equation of state gives the volume. Even in freezing weather air behaves as an ideal gas, because it is far from liquefying.

      Formula

      $pV = nRT$, so $V = \dfrac{nRT}{p}$

      Conversions

      $T$$= (-30 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
      $= 243.15\,\text{K}$

      and

      $p$$= 102\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 102\,000\,\text{Pa}$

      Finally $1\,\text{m}^3 = 1000\,\textsf{l}$.

      Substitution

      $V$$= \dfrac{1.00\,\text{mol} \cdot 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 243.15\,\text{K}}{102\,000\,\text{Pa}}$
      $= 0.01981\,\text{m}^3$
      $= 19.81\,\textsf{l}$

      Answer

      $19.8\,\textsf{l}$

      Three significant figures. All the data ($1.00\,\text{mol}$, $102\,\text{kPa}$ and $R = 8.31$) are given with three digits, and $243\,\text{K}$ (from the whole degrees $-30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) also has three digits.

      Reasonableness

      At the same pressure, a mole of air at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ would take up

      $\dfrac{8.31 \cdot 293.15}{102\,000}$
      $= 0.0239\,\text{m}^3$
      $= 23.9\,\textsf{l}$

      The ratio of the temperatures in kelvins is $243.15 / 293.15 \approx 0.83$, and $0.83 \cdot 23.9\,\textsf{l} \approx 19.8\,\textsf{l}$. So cold air takes up about $17\,\%$ less space, as it should.

    1. At rest, a person breathes into the lungs with one inhalation air that takes up a volume of 0.55 l at a temperature of 37 °C. The pressure of the air in the lungs is 101 kPa.

      How many molecules are in one breath? The Avogadro constant is $6.022 \cdot 10^{23}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mol}}$ and $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$. Give the answer in the form $a \cdot 10^{22}$. Justify why the calculation does not need information about which gases the air consists of.

      · 10²²
      Answer

      1.3 · 10²²

      Show the solution

      Given

      air in the lungs, $V = 0.55\,\textsf{l}$, $\theta = 37\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $p = 101\,\text{kPa}$; $N_\text{A} = 6.022 \cdot 10^{23}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mol}}$, $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

      Find

      number of molecules $N$

      Principle

      The equation of state gives the amount of substance, and the Avogadro constant converts it into the number of molecules. Air is a mixture of gases, but the equation of state contains only the total amount of substance of all the particles and no molar mass. So you do not need to know which gases the air consists of.

      Formula

      $pV = nRT$ and $N = n N_\text{A}$, so $n = \dfrac{pV}{RT}$ and $N = \dfrac{pV N_\text{A}}{RT}$

      Conversions

      $V$$= 0.55\,\textsf{l}$
      $= 0.00055\,\text{m}^3$
      $p$$= 101\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$

      and

      $T$$= (37 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
      $= 310.15\,\text{K}$

      Substitution

      The numerator is

      $101\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.00055\,\text{m}^3 \cdot 6.022 \cdot 10^{23}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mol}}$
      $= 3.345 \cdot 10^{25}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}}$

      and the denominator is

      $8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 310.15\,\text{K}$
      $= 2577\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}}$

      so

      $$N = \dfrac{3.345 \cdot 10^{25}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}}}{2577\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}}} = 1.298 \cdot 10^{22}$$

      Answer

      $1.3 \cdot 10^{22}$

      Molecules, two significant figures. The limiting datum is the volume $0.55\,\textsf{l}$ (two digits); the pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$, the temperature $310\,\text{K}$ (from the whole degrees $37\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) and the constants have three or more digits.

      Reasonableness

      The amount of substance is

      $\dfrac{1.298 \cdot 10^{22}}{6.022 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{1/mol}}$
      $= 0.0216\,\text{mol}$

      which fits the fact that a litre of gas at room temperature is about $0.04\,\text{mol}$ and a little less at body temperature: $0.55 \cdot 0.04 \approx 0.022\,\text{mol}$. About $21\,\%$ of the molecules are oxygen, so one breath contains about $2.7 \cdot 10^{21}$ oxygen molecules.

    2. In new windows the space between the panes is often filled with argon, because argon conducts heat worse than air. The panes of an insulating glass unit are 1.20 m × 1.00 m. According to the manufacturer, an argon filling makes the unit 6.0 g heavier than an air filling when the gap is filled at a pressure of 101 kPa and a temperature of 20 °C.

      How thick is the layer of gas between the panes? The molar mass of argon is 40 g/mol and that of air is 29 g/mol, and $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$. Give the answer in millimetres.

      mm
      Answer

      11 mm

      Show the solution

      Given

      insulating glass unit, $A = 1.20\,\text{m}^2$, difference in mass $\Delta m = 6.0\,\text{g}$, $p = 101\,\text{kPa}$, $\theta = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $M_\text{Ar} = 40\,\text{g/mol}$, $M_\text{air} = 29\,\text{g/mol}$; $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

      Find

      thickness $d$ of the gas layer in millimetres

      Principle

      The gap is filled at the same pressure and temperature, so the volume is the same and there is the same number of moles of gas with either filling. The difference in mass is due only to the greater molar mass of argon, so you can get the amount of substance from the difference in mass and the difference in molar masses.

      Formula

      $\Delta m = n (M_\text{Ar} - M_\text{air})$, so

      $n$$= \dfrac{\Delta m}{M_\text{Ar} - M_\text{air}}$

      From the equation of state $V = \dfrac{nRT}{p}$ and $V = Ad$, so

      $d$$= \dfrac{nRT}{pA}$
      $= \dfrac{\Delta m\,RT}{pA(M_\text{Ar} - M_\text{air})}$

      Conversions

      $T$$= (20 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
      $= 293.15\,\text{K}$

      and

      $p$$= 101\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$

      The mass and the molar masses are in grams, so $n$ comes out directly in moles. Finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.

      Substitution

      $n$$= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{g}}{40\,\text{g/mol} - 29\,\text{g/mol}}$
      $= 0.5455\,\text{mol}$

      The numerator is

      $0.5455\,\text{mol} \cdot 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 293.15\,\text{K}$
      $= 1328.9\,\text{J}$

      and the denominator is $101\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 1.20\,\text{m}^2 = 121\,200\,\text{N}$, because $\text{Pa}\cdot\text{m}^2 = \text{N}$. So

      $$\begin{aligned} d &= \dfrac{1328.9\,\text{J}}{121\,200\,\text{N}} \\ &= 0.01096\,\text{m} \\ &= 10.96\,\text{mm} \end{aligned}$$

      Answer

      $11\,\text{mm}$

      Two significant figures. The limiting data are the difference in mass $6.0\,\text{g}$ and the difference in molar masses $40\,\text{g/mol} - 29\,\text{g/mol} = 11\,\text{g/mol}$, each with two digits; the dimensions $1.20\,\text{m}$ and $1.00\,\text{m}$, the pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$ and $293\,\text{K}$ have three digits.

      Reasonableness

      The gap in an insulating glass unit is usually $10\text{–}20\,\text{mm}$, so the result is believable. Check the masses: the argon is $0.5455\,\text{mol} \cdot 40\,\text{g/mol} = 21.8\,\text{g}$ and the air is $0.5455\,\text{mol} \cdot 29\,\text{g/mol} = 15.8\,\text{g}$, and the difference is $6.0\,\text{g}$.

    3. The compressed air tank of a car workshop's compressor has a volume of 200.0 l. In the evening the pressure gauge of the tank shows 10.0 bar, but in the morning only 7.0 bar, because air leaks from a joint. The gauge shows gauge pressure, that is, how much greater the pressure in the tank is than atmospheric pressure (1 bar = 100 kPa). The temperature of the workshop is 18 °C the whole time.

      How many kilograms of air leaked from the tank during the night? The molar mass of air is 29 g/mol, and $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$. Justify why in this calculation the readings of the gauge can be used without adding atmospheric pressure to them.

      kg
      Answer

      0.72 kg

      Show the solution

      Given

      compressed air tank, $V = 200.0\,\textsf{l}$, gauge pressure before $10.0\,\text{bar}$ and after $7.0\,\text{bar}$, $\theta = 18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $M = 29\,\text{g/mol}$; $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

      Find

      mass $\Delta m$ of the air that leaked out, in kilograms

      Principle

      The volume and the temperature of the tank stay the same, so the drop in pressure is due only to the decrease in the amount of substance: $\Delta p V = \Delta n RT$. The reading of the gauge is the total pressure minus atmospheric pressure, and atmospheric pressure is the same at the start and at the end. It cancels in the pressure difference, so the readings can be used as they are.

      Formula

      $\Delta n = \dfrac{\Delta p\,V}{RT}$ and $\Delta m = \Delta n\,M$, so $\Delta m = \dfrac{\Delta p\,V M}{RT}$

      Conversions

      $\Delta p$$= 10.0\,\text{bar} - 7.0\,\text{bar}$
      $= 3.0\,\text{bar}$
      $= 3.0 \cdot 10^5\,\text{Pa}$
      $V$$= 200.0\,\textsf{l}$
      $= 0.2000\,\text{m}^3$

      $M = 0.029\,\text{kg/mol}$ and

      $T$$= (18 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
      $= 291.15\,\text{K}$

      Substitution

      $\Delta n$$= \dfrac{3.0 \cdot 10^5\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.2000\,\text{m}^3}{8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 291.15\,\text{K}}$
      $= 24.80\,\text{mol}$
      $\Delta m$$= 24.80\,\text{mol} \cdot 0.029\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{mol}}$
      $= 0.7192\,\text{kg}$

      Answer

      $0.72\,\text{kg}$

      Two significant figures. The limiting datum is the pressure difference $10.0\,\text{bar} - 7.0\,\text{bar} = 3.0\,\text{bar}$: the difference of the readings has the decimals of the less precise reading, so it has two digits. The volume $200.0\,\textsf{l}$, the molar mass $29\,\text{g/mol}$ and $291\,\text{K}$ have at least two digits and do not limit the result.

      Reasonableness

      At the start the tank contained, at a total pressure of $11.0\,\text{bar}$, about $2.6\,\text{kg}$ of air, so the leaked $0.72\,\text{kg}$ is about $27\,\%$. The gauge pressure fell $10.0 \to 7.0\,\text{bar}$, which is $30\,\%$ of the gauge pressure and a little more than $27\,\%$ of the total pressure, because atmospheric pressure does not leak out. The amount of air that leaked was $24.8\,\text{mol}$, and that is a sensible amount for a drop in pressure of three bars.
