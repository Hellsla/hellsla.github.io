## fy5-11-L1-01
**Given:** a straight wall, angle of incidence $\alpha = 35^\circ$
**Find:** the angle of reflection $\beta$ in degrees
**Principle:** The wall is straight and reflects the wave, so the law of reflection holds: the angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence when both are measured from the normal of the wall. The angle of incidence is given already measured from the normal.
**Formula:** $\beta = \alpha$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the angle is in degrees.
**Substitution:** $\beta = 35^\circ$
**Answer:** $35^\circ$, in whole degrees like the angle of incidence $35^\circ$.
**Reasonableness:** The reflected ray is on the other side of the normal at the same angle, and the angle is between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, as an angle measured from the normal should be.

## fy5-11-L1-02
**Given:** the angle between the incoming ray and the surface of the wall $\gamma = 20^\circ$
**Find:** the angle of incidence $\alpha$ in degrees
**Principle:** The angle of incidence is measured from the normal of the surface, not from the surface. The normal is perpendicular to the surface, so the angle between the ray and the surface and the angle between the ray and the normal add up to $90^\circ$.
**Formula:** $\alpha + \gamma = 90^\circ$, so $\alpha = 90^\circ - \gamma$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the angles are in degrees.
**Substitution:** $\alpha = 90^\circ - 20^\circ = 70^\circ$
**Answer:** $70^\circ$, in whole degrees like the given angle $20^\circ$.
**Reasonableness:** The ray arrives at a shallow angle, almost along the wall, so its angle from the normal is large. As the angle measured from the surface gets small, the angle of incidence approaches $90^\circ$, as it should.

## fy5-11-L1-03
**Given:** frequency $f = 8.0\,\text{Hz}$, speed of the wave $v = 0.24\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the wavelength $\lambda$ in centimetres
**Principle:** In the time of one period a wave travels a distance of one wavelength, so the speed, frequency and wavelength are related by the basic equation of wave motion (chapter *Mechanical wave motion*).
**Formula:** $v = f\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$
**Conversions:** The result comes out in metres, and finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{0.24\,\text{m/s}}{8.0\,\text{Hz}} = 0.030\,\text{m} = 0.030 \cdot 100\,\text{cm} = 3.0\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the speed and the frequency.
**Reasonableness:** The crests of waves in a ripple tank are a few centimetres apart. Check: $8.0\,\text{Hz} \cdot 0.030\,\text{m} = 0.24\,\text{m/s}$, and the unit $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{1/s}} = \text{m}$.

## fy5-11-L1-04
**Find:** the direction of the displacement of the reflected pulse and the reason for it
**Principle:** A ring that slides without friction along a rod is the free end of the rope: nothing prevents it from moving vertically, and the rod cannot pull the rope up or down.
**Model answer:** The displacement of the reflected pulse points upwards, so the pulse returns not inverted. When the upward pulse arrives at the ring, the ring rises with the pulse and swings for a moment higher than usual. Nothing pulls it down, so the rope returns to its equilibrium position in the same way as it rose, and the pulse starts back with the same sign. If the end were tied to a wall, the wall would pull the rope down and the pulse would be inverted.

## fy5-11-L1-05
**Given:** delay of the echo $t = 1.4\,\text{s}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the distance $d$ to the cliff wall in metres
**Principle:** Sound travels at a steady speed to the wall, is reflected from it and returns. So during the delay of the echo the sound travels a distance of $2d$.
**Formula:** $2d = v t$, so $d = \dfrac{v t}{2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speed is in m/s and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $d = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.4\,\text{s}}{2} = \dfrac{480.2\,\text{m}}{2} = 240.1\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $240\,\text{m}$ or $2.4 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the delay $1.4\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Sound travels about a third of a kilometre per second, so in $1.4\,\text{s}$ about $0.48\,\text{km}$; half of that is about $0.24\,\text{km}$. Across a lake to the opposite shore that is a believable distance.

## fy5-11-L2-01
**Given:** angle of incidence $\alpha = 30^\circ$, distance between points $A$ and $B$ on the wall $d = 0.60\,\text{m}$, speed of the wave $v = 0.40\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the time $\Delta t$ that the front needs to go from $A$ to $B$
**Principle:** When the front touches point $A$, the point of the front that hits $B$ is still a distance $s$ from the wall along the ray. The front is perpendicular to the ray, so the distance $s$ is a leg of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $d$ and whose opposite angle is $\alpha$, as in the Huygens construction.
**Formula:** $s = d \sin\alpha$ and $s = v\,\Delta t$, so $\Delta t = \dfrac{d \sin\alpha}{v}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the distance is in metres and the speed in m/s.
**Substitution:** $\Delta t = \dfrac{0.60\,\text{m} \cdot \sin 30^\circ}{0.40\,\text{m/s}} = \dfrac{0.30\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{m/s}} = 0.75\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $0.75\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the distance $0.60\,\text{m}$ and the speed $0.40\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** At perpendicular incidence ($\alpha = 0^\circ$) the front would touch the whole wall at the same time, $\Delta t = 0$. If the wave travelled along the wall, $\Delta t = \tfrac{0.60\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{m/s}} = 1.5\,\text{s}$. The result lies between these.

## fy5-11-L2-02
**Given:** length of the rope $L = 8.0\,\text{m}$, time to return to the starting point $t = 3.2\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the pulse
**Principle:** The pulse travels at a steady speed along the rope to the tree, is reflected from the fixed end and returns along the same rope. Reflection inverts the pulse but does not change its speed, so in the time $t$ the pulse travels a distance of $2L$.
**Formula:** $2L = v t$, so $v = \dfrac{2L}{t}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the length is in metres and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{2 \cdot 8.0\,\text{m}}{3.2\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{16\,\text{m}}{3.2\,\text{s}} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $5.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the length and the time.
**Reasonableness:** If only $8.0\,\text{m}$ were taken as the distance, the speed would be half, $2.5\,\text{m/s}$. The pulse travels $8\,\text{m}$ in each direction in about one and a half seconds, which suits a taut rope.

## fy5-11-L2-03
**Given:** delay of the first echo $t_1 = 0.40\,\text{s}$, of the second $t_2 = 0.70\,\text{s}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the distance $D$ between the walls
**Principle:** Each echo is sound that has travelled to its own wall and been reflected back. The nearer wall gives the first echo. The distance to each wall is half of the distance travelled by the sound, and the walls are on opposite sides of the listener, so the distances are added.
**Formula:** $d_1 = \dfrac{v t_1}{2}$, $d_2 = \dfrac{v t_2}{2}$ and $D = d_1 + d_2 = \dfrac{v (t_1 + t_2)}{2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speed is in m/s and the times in seconds.
**Substitution:** $D = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s} \cdot (0.40\,\text{s} + 0.70\,\text{s})}{2} = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.10\,\text{s}}{2} = 188.65\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $190\,\text{m}$ or $1.9 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the delays $0.40\,\text{s}$ and $0.70\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The walls are $68.6\,\text{m}$ and $120\,\text{m}$ away; the farther one gives the later echo, as it should. Without halving the result would be about $380\,\text{m}$, which would be the whole round trip of the sound and not the distance between the walls.

## fy5-11-L2-04
**Given:** width of the doorway $b = 0.90\,\text{m}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the frequency $f$ at which the wavelength is $\lambda = b$
**Principle:** The speed, frequency and wavelength of sound are related by the equation $v = f\lambda$ (chapter *Mechanical wave motion*). Set the wavelength equal to the width of the opening and solve for the frequency.
**Formula:** $v = f\lambda$ and $\lambda = b$, so $f = \dfrac{v}{b}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speed is in m/s and the width in metres, so the result is in hertz.
**Substitution:** $f = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{0.90\,\text{m}} = 381.1\,\text{Hz}$
**Answer:** $380\,\text{Hz}$ or $3.8 \cdot 10^2\,\text{Hz}$, two significant figures like the width $0.90\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The frequency is in the range of the human voice, so the wavelength of speech is of the order of the doorway and speech bends from the door into the corridor. At lower frequencies than this the wavelength is longer than the opening and the bending is even stronger.

## fy5-11-L3-01
**Find:** why a low sound is heard behind the corner but a high one is not, explained with diffraction and wavelengths
**Principle:** Diffraction is strong when the width of the opening is of the order of the wavelength or smaller, and weak when the opening is many wavelengths wide. The wavelength is obtained from the equation $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$ (chapter *Mechanical wave motion*).
**Model answer:** The wavelength of the bass is $\lambda = \tfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{100\,\text{Hz}} = 3.43\,\text{m}$, so the window is only about a quarter of a wavelength ($b/\lambda = \tfrac{0.80\,\text{m}}{3.43\,\text{m}} \approx 0.23$). The window then acts like a single source of wavelets, and the sound spreads from the window in every direction, also behind the corner. The wavelength of the cymbal is $\lambda = \tfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{5000\,\text{Hz}} \approx 6.9\,\text{cm}$, so the window is about $12$ wavelengths wide. From it the sound continues as an almost straight beam, and bends only a little behind the corner. That is why mostly bass is heard behind the corner.

## fy5-11-L3-02
**Given:** distance between the marks $d = 0.40\,\text{m}$, delay $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{s}$, speed of the wave $v = 0.30\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the angle of incidence $\alpha$ in degrees
**Principle:** When the crest is at $A$, the point of it that hits $B$ is still a distance $s$ away along the ray, and it travels this distance during the delay. The front is perpendicular to the ray, so $s$, $d$ and the front form a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $d$ and in which $s$ is the leg opposite the angle of incidence.
**Formula:** $s = v\,\Delta t$ and $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{s}{d}$, so $\alpha = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{v\,\Delta t}{d}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the distances are in metres, the speed in m/s and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $s = 0.30\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.50\,\text{s} = 0.15\,\text{m}$ and $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{0.15\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{m}} = 0.375$, so $\alpha = 22.02^\circ$
**Answer:** $22^\circ$, two significant figures like all the data.
**Reasonableness:** If the wave arrived perpendicularly, the crest would reach both marks at the same time. If it travelled along the wall, the delay would be $\tfrac{0.40\,\text{m}}{0.30\,\text{m/s}} \approx 1.3\,\text{s}$. The measured $0.50\,\text{s}$ is less than half of this, so the wave arrives fairly steeply, at a small angle of incidence.

## fy5-11-L3-03
**Given:** length of the rope $L = 6.0\,\text{m}$, speed of the pulse $v = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, the pulse is at first upward; both ends are held in place
**Find:** the time $t$ until the pulse arrives at the post for the second time
**Principle:** The pulse travels at a steady speed. The post is a fixed end, and a hand holding the rope firmly in place is also a fixed end, so the pulse is inverted in both reflections. To reach the post for the second time the pulse has travelled three times the length of the rope: to the post, back to the hand and again to the post.
**Formula:** $3L = v t$, so $t = \dfrac{3L}{v}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the length is in metres and the speed in m/s.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{3 \cdot 6.0\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{m/s}} = \dfrac{18\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{m/s}} = 4.5\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $4.5\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the length and the speed. In addition, the displacement then points upwards: the post inverted the pulse downwards and the hand back upwards.
**Reasonableness:** The pulse travels the length of the rope in one direction in $1.5\,\text{s}$, so three trips take $4.5\,\text{s}$. After two inversions the pulse is again in the same direction as at the start, as two successive sign changes should give.
