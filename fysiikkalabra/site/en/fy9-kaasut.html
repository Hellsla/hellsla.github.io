Pressure, gases and the heat engine

Why must the pressure of a car's tyres be checked when a hard frost sets in, even though no air has leaked from the tyres?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve pressure, gas and heat engine problems with kelvin temperatures and total pressure
  • draw and interpret graphs of measured gas data and their extensions
  • evaluate the assumptions of gas models, measurement reports and inventors' claims with justification

Prior knowledge

  • Pressure is force divided by area, and in a liquid it increases with depthchapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure
  • At constant temperature the pressure of an enclosed gas is inversely proportional to its volumechapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law
  • The ideal gas law $pV = nRT$ links pressure, volume, amount of substance and kelvin temperaturechapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$
  • Absolute zero is estimated by extending the straight line of the measurements to zero pressurechapter Determining absolute zero
  • A heat engine turns part of the heat into work and gives the rest to a colder placechapter Work done by a gas and the heat engine

1Think about it

The car's tyres were pumped up in the evening in a warm garage at $+20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The tyre pressure gauge showed $230\,\text{kPa}$, as the car's manual advises. During the night the car stood in the yard in a frost of $-20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and no air leaked from the tyres. The air pressure is $101\,\text{kPa}$. In the matriculation exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the formulas are not.

What does the gauge show in the morning, and why does the reading change proportionally more than the kelvin temperature?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting point 1: snowshoes

A hiker stands in deep snow, first in ordinary boots and then on snowshoes whose sole area is about five times as large. In boots he sinks, on snowshoes he does not.Which explanation is correct?

  • Correct. The normal force is the same, but on five times the area the pressure is about a fifth, and the snow no longer gives way. If needed, review the chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.

  • The weight does not change, but pressure is force divided by area, $p = F/A$. Five times the area gives about a fifth of the pressure. Review the chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.

  • He stands still both times, so the normal force from the snow is equal to his weight. What changes is the area over which the same force is spread: the pressure $p = F/A$ decreases. Review the chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.

Starting point 2: narrow and wide

A narrow pipe and a wide pool are filled with water to the same height, $2.0\,\text{m}$. The pool holds a thousand times more water.Where is the water pressure at the bottom greater?

  • The water in the pool weighs more, but the weight is also spread over a bottom a thousand times larger. The pressure at the bottom is $p_0 + \rho g h$: it depends only on the depth and the density. Review the chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.

  • Correct. $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, and the depth and density are the same, so the pressure is the same whatever the shape of the container. If needed, review the chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.

  • Being cramped does not increase the pressure. The bottom bears the weight of the water column above it, and the height of the column is $2.0\,\text{m}$ in both. Review the chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure.

Starting point 3: compressed air

The air in a closed syringe is slowly compressed to a third of its original volume, and the temperature of the air stays the same.How does the pressure of the air change?

  • A constant temperature means that the particles move equally fast, but in a smaller space they hit the walls more often. According to Boyle's law $pV$ stays constant, not $p$. Review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

  • Correct. At constant temperature $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$, so at a third of the volume the pressure is three times as large. The same particles hit the walls three times as often. If needed, review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

  • Pressure and volume change in opposite directions: when the volume decreases, the same particles hit the walls more often and the pressure increases. $p_2 = p_1V_1/V_2 = 3p_1$. Review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

  • Pressure is inversely proportional to the volume, not to its square: $p_2 = p_1 \cdot V_1/V_2 = 3p_1$. Review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

Starting point 4: a heated bottle

The air in a closed, rigid glass bottle warms from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. How does the pressure of the air change?

  • Warm air is less dense only if it can expand. In a rigid bottle the volume stays the same, so the pressure increases with the kelvin temperature. Review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

  • Correct. $p_2/p_1 = T_2/T_1 = 313\,\text{K} : 293\,\text{K} \approx 1.07$. If needed, review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

  • There is as much air, but warm particles move faster and hit the walls more often and harder. At constant volume $p_1/T_1 = p_2/T_2$. Review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

  • The pressure is proportional to the kelvin temperature, and that rises only from $293\,\text{K}$ to $313\,\text{K}$, that is, by about $7\,\%$. The ratio of degrees Celsius tells nothing, because the zero of the scale is chosen by convention. Review the chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law.

Starting point 5: helium and carbon dioxide

Two balloons of the same size are filled at the same pressure and the same temperature, one with helium and the other with carbon dioxide.Which balloon contains more gas in moles?

  • The carbon dioxide balloon is heavier because its particles are heavier, not because there are more of them. $n = pV/(RT)$ does not depend on the kind of gas. Review the chapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$.

  • Correct. From the ideal gas law $n = \dfrac{pV}{RT}$, and the kind of gas does not appear in it. The masses differ, because the molar masses differ. If needed, review the chapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$.

  • In an ideal gas the size of the particles does not matter: they are far apart, and the amount of substance is $n = pV/(RT)$ for all gases. Review the chapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$.

Starting point 6: the perfect engine

An engineer says: “When all friction is removed from an engine, it turns all the heat it gets from the fuel into work.” Which assessment is correct?

  • Energy is never lost. The claim does not hold because part of the heat must be given to a colder environment, and this heat is still energy. Review the chapter Work done by a gas and the heat engine.

  • Conservation of energy would allow it, but it is not enough: a heat engine must give part of the heat to a colder environment so that it can repeat its cycle. Review the chapter Work done by a gas and the heat engine.

  • Correct. Even a frictionless heat engine must give the heat $Q_2$ to a colder place, so $W = Q_1 - Q_2 < Q_1$. If needed, review the chapter Work done by a gas and the heat engine.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

On the line of gas A at the temperature $200\,\text{K}$ the pressure is $50\,\text{kPa} = 50\,000\,\text{Pa}$, and $2.0\,\textsf{l} = 0.0020\,\text{m}^3$.

Show the solution

Given

from the graph, the point $T = 200\,\text{K}$, $p = 50\,\text{kPa}$ on the line of gas A (and $400\,\text{K}$, $100\,\text{kPa}$); volume of the container $V = 2.0\,\textsf{l}$; $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

Find

the amount of substance $n_\text{A}$ of the gas in container A, in moles

Principle

The container is closed and rigid, so the amount of gas and the volume stay the same, and the state of the gas follows the ideal gas law. Any point on the line gives the same amount of substance, because $p/T$ is constant along the line (chapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$).

Reading the graph

The line of gas A passes through the grid crossings $(200\,\text{K}$; $50\,\text{kPa})$ and $(400\,\text{K}$; $100\,\text{kPa})$. The first point is used.

Formula

$pV = nRT$, so $n_\text{A} = \dfrac{pV}{RT}$

Conversions

$50\,\text{kPa}$$= 50 \cdot 1000\,\text{Pa}$
$= 50\,000\,\text{Pa}$
$2.0\,\textsf{l}$$= 2.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}^3$
$= 0.0020\,\text{m}^3$

The temperature is already in kelvins.

Substitution

$n_\text{A}$$= \dfrac{50\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0020\,\text{m}^3}{8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 200\,\text{K}}$
$= \dfrac{100\,\text{J}}{1662\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}}}$
$= 0.0602\,\text{mol}$

$n_\text{A} \approx 0.060\,\text{mol}$

Two significant figures like the volume $2.0\,\textsf{l}$.

The other point gives the same:

$100\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0020\,\text{m}^3 : (8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 400\,\text{K})$
$= 0.0602\,\text{mol}$

The line of container B is less steep, $30\,\text{kPa}$ at the temperature $200\,\text{K}$, so it holds $\tfrac{30}{50}$ times as much gas, about $0.036\,\text{mol}$. The extension of each line passes through the origin, because $p = \tfrac{nR}{V}\,T$ is directly proportional to the kelvin temperature; a real gas would condense into a liquid before zero.

4Theory

Key idea

The problems of this lesson revolve around one quantity: pressure is the force perpendicular to a surface divided by the area. In a liquid, pressure comes from the weight of the liquid above, so it increases with depth. In a gas, it comes from the collisions of the particles with the walls of the container, so it depends on the amount, the volume and the temperature of the gas. When the pressure of a gas pushes a piston, the gas does work, and in this way a heat engine turns part of the heat into work.

The gas problems of the exam need two checks before the numbers. The temperature in kelvins, because the gas laws are proportionalities that start from absolute zero. The total pressure, because a pressure gauge usually shows only the gauge pressure, and the particles of the gas produce the whole pressure.

The opening question is answered with these two checks. In the warm garage ($+20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) the gauge showed a gauge pressure of $230\,\text{kPa}$, so the total pressure was $101\,\text{kPa} + 230\,\text{kPa} = 331\,\text{kPa}$. When the volume of the tyre stays almost the same, the total pressure is proportional to the kelvin temperature: after a night of frost ($-20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$) it is $331\,\text{kPa} \cdot 253\,\text{K} : 293\,\text{K} \approx 286\,\text{kPa}$. The gauge now shows only about $185\,\text{kPa}$, even though no air has leaked.

Pressure, gases and the heat engine

Pressure and pressure in a liquid

$$p = \frac{F}{A}$$

where $p$ is the pressure (Pa), $F$ the force perpendicular to the surface (N) and $A$ the area (m²). In a liquid at rest, at the depth $h$

$$p = p_0 + \rho g h$$

where $p_0$ is the pressure at the surface, usually the air pressure of about $101\,\text{kPa}$, $\rho$ the density of the liquid (kg/m³) and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The term $\rho g h$ is the gauge pressure, and it depends only on the depth and the density, not on the shape of the container. The force caused by pressure is always perpendicular to the surface.

The state of a gas

$$pV = nRT$$

where $V$ is the volume (m³), $n$ the amount of substance (mol), $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$ and $T$ the temperature in kelvins. The amount of substance is obtained from the mass, $n = m/M$, where $M$ is the molar mass. The equation holds for a dilute gas, when the pressure is not very high and the temperature not very low. When the amount of gas stays the same,

$$\frac{p_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 V_2}{T_2}$$

and from it follow the special cases: at constant temperature Boyle's law $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$, at constant pressure $V \propto T$ and at constant volume $p \propto T$. The kelvin temperature is

$$T = \theta + 273.15\,\text{K}$$

where $\theta$ is the numerical value of the Celsius temperature. In an experiment, absolute zero is obtained by extending the $(\theta, p)$ or $(\theta, V)$ line to zero. It is a prediction of the model: a real gas condenses into a liquid before that.

Work done by a gas and the heat engine

A gas expanding at constant pressure does the work

$$W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$$

where $\Delta V$ is the change in volume (m³); the work is the area under the ($V$, $p$) graph. By the first law of thermodynamics the internal energy of the gas changes by

$$\Delta U = Q - W_\text{g}$$

where $Q$ is the heat transferred to the gas (J) and $W_\text{g}$ the work done by the gas. In one cycle a heat engine takes the heat $Q_1$ from a hot source, does the work $W$ and gives the heat $Q_2$ to a colder environment. Energy is conserved, so

$$W = Q_1 - Q_2$$

and the efficiency is

$$\eta = \frac{W}{Q_1}$$

Part of the heat must always be given to a colder place, so the efficiency of a heat engine stays well below $100\,\%$. The smaller the temperature difference $T_1 - T_2$ is compared with the temperature $T_1$ of the hot source, the smaller the share of the heat the engine can turn into work. In a power plant the same is calculated from powers: the electrical power divided by the fuel power.

Common mistakes

  • Degrees Celsius in a ratio. The pressure of a gas heated from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ increases by only about $7\,\%$, it does not double: $313\,\text{K} : 293\,\text{K} \approx 1.07$.
  • Gauge pressure in a gas law. The gauge reading is not the pressure of the gas. Add the air pressure before calculating and subtract it only at the end, if the gauge reading is asked for.
  • Units mixed up. In the ideal gas law the pressure is in pascals and the volume in cubic metres. Kilopascals and litres work together, because $1\,\text{kPa} \cdot 1\,\textsf{l} = 1\,\text{J}$, but kilopascals and cubic metres give an error of a factor of a thousand.
  • Pressure and force mixed up. The same force on a small area gives a large pressure. Force is obtained from pressure only by multiplying by the area.
  • Suction pulls. A suction cup or a straw does not pull anything: the pressure of the outside air pushes when the pressure inside is lower.
  • A heat engine could turn all the heat into work. Removing friction does not help: the heat $Q_2$ cannot be avoided.

5Examples

First follow the worked example. Then practise the same yourself: the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: a warm bottle

A student calculated how high the pressure of the air in a closed glass bottle rises when the bottle warms in the sun from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $60\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.At the start the pressure was $100\,\text{kPa}$: “$p_2 = p_1 \cdot \tfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 100\,\text{kPa} \cdot \tfrac{60}{20} = 300\,\text{kPa}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • In a rigid bottle the pressure increases with the temperature, because the particles move faster. The formula is correct; the mistake is that the temperatures are in degrees Celsius.

  • Correct. $p_2 = 100\,\text{kPa} \cdot \tfrac{333\,\text{K}}{293\,\text{K}} \approx 114\,\text{kPa}$. The proportionality starts from absolute zero, so the ratio is calculated from kelvins.

  • The units cancel, but the ratio of degrees Celsius $60 : 20$ is not the real ratio of the temperatures, because the zero of the Celsius scale is not absolute zero. In kelvins the ratio is only $333 : 293$.

Which graph?

A student measures the pressure $p$ of air enclosed in a syringe at different volumes $V$ at constant temperature.Which way of drawing the graph shows directly that the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume?

  • A horizontal straight line would mean that the pressure does not change at all with the volume. In inverse proportionality $p$ increases in direct proportion to the variable $1/V$.

  • A falling straight line would mean that the pressure decreases by the same amount for each increase in volume. In inverse proportionality the $(V, p)$ points lie on a curve, and a straight line appears only with the variable $1/V$.

  • Inverse proportionality gives a falling curve, but so do many other relationships. From the curve you cannot see whether it is exactly of the form $p = \text{constant}/V$. Plot $p$ as a function of the variable $1/V$.

  • Correct. From Boyle's law $p = \text{constant} \cdot \tfrac{1}{V}$, so the $(1/V, p)$ points lie, within the measurement uncertainty, on a straight line through the origin whose slope is $pV$.

Justified claim: an empty gas canister

The gas canister of a camping stove has been used up, and its valve is open to the outside air. The pressure gauge attached to the canister shows $0\,\text{kPa}$.Which claim is justified?

  • The gauge shows the gauge pressure, that is, how much greater the pressure is than the air pressure. The reading $0\,\text{kPa}$ means that the pressure in the canister is the same as the air pressure, about $101\,\text{kPa}$, and there is gas in the canister.

  • Correct. The total pressure is $p = p_0 + \Delta p = 101\,\text{kPa} + 0\,\text{kPa}$. The total pressure is always substituted into the ideal gas law, so the canister holds gas $n = p_0V/(RT)$.

  • When the valve is open, gas flows until the pressures are equal. So the pressure in the canister is the air pressure, not lower; with a partial vacuum the gauge would show a negative reading.

Read the graph: work done by a gas

In the graph the pressure of the gas in a cylinder stays constant as the gas expands and pushes a piston.How much work does the gas do on the piston?

  • Correct. $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V = 250\,\text{kPa} \cdot 3.0\,\textsf{l} = 250\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0030\,\text{m}^3 = 750\,\text{J}$, the rectangle under the graph.

  • Constant pressure does not mean that no work is done. Work is done when the volume changes and the piston moves: $W_\text{g} = p\,\Delta V$.

  • You calculated $750$ correctly with kilopascals and litres, but the unit is the joule: $1\,\text{kPa} \cdot 1\,\textsf{l} = 1000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}^3 = 1\,\text{J}$.

  • You multiplied the pressure by the final volume $4.0\,\textsf{l}$. The work is the area under the graph over the expansion, that is, the pressure times the change in volume $3.0\,\textsf{l}$.

The service hatch of a diving pool

At the bottom of the diving pool of a swimming hall, at a depth of $5.0\,\text{m}$, there is a square service hatch with a side of $0.60\,\text{m}$. Under the hatch is a service room with the same air pressure as in the hall.

What is the total force exerted on the hatch by the water and the air? Answer in kilonewtons.

kN
Hint 1: a guiding question

The air pressure presses on the hatch from above through the water and from below from the service room. Which part of the pressure is left?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The gauge pressure at the depth $h$ is $\rho g h$, and the force is the pressure times the area.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The area of the hatch is $A = (0.60\,\text{m})^2$. First calculate the gauge pressure in pascals and then multiply it by the area.

Show the solution

Given

depth $h = 5.0\,\text{m}$, side of the hatch $a = 0.60\,\text{m}$, density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the same air pressure at the water surface and under the hatch

Find

the total force $F$ on the hatch in kilonewtons

Principle

The water is at rest, so the pressure on the upper surface of the hatch is $p_0 + \rho g h$. From below the air pressure $p_0$ acts on the hatch. The forces of the air pressure cancel each other out, so the total force comes from the gauge pressure $\rho g h$, and it is perpendicular to the hatch, that is, downwards (chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure).

Formula

$\Delta p = \rho g h$ and $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$, so $F = \rho g h\,a^2$

Conversions

All quantities are in base units. At the end $1\,\text{N} = 0.001\,\text{kN}$.

Substitution

$F$$= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{m} \cdot (0.60\,\text{m})^2$
$= 49\,050\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.36\,\text{m}^2$
$= 17\,658\,\text{N}$
$= 17.7\,\text{kN}$

Answer

$F \approx 18\,\text{kN}$

Two significant figures like the depth $5.0\,\text{m}$ and the side $0.60\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

Above the hatch is a water column of $0.36\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 5.0\,\text{m} = 1.8\,\text{m}^3$, whose mass is $1800\,\text{kg}$ and weight about $18\,\text{kN}$: the same result. The force is about the weight of one large car, so the hatch must be made sturdy.

The gauge of a bicycle pump

The cylinder of a bicycle pump holds $0.30\,\textsf{l}$ of air at the air pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$. The nozzle of the pump is blocked, and the piston is pushed slowly so that the volume of the air decreases to $0.12\,\textsf{l}$. The temperature stays the same. The pump's pressure gauge shows the gauge pressure.

What does the gauge show? Answer in kilopascals.

kPa
Hint 1: a guiding question

The temperature and the amount of air stay the same. Which law applies?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Boyle's law uses the total pressure. The gauge shows the difference between the total pressure and the air pressure.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$p_2$$= \dfrac{101\,\text{kPa} \cdot 0.30\,\textsf{l}}{0.12\,\textsf{l}}$

and the gauge reading is $p_2 - 101\,\text{kPa}$.

Show the solution

Given

initial volume $V_1 = 0.30\,\textsf{l}$, initial pressure $p_1 = 101\,\text{kPa}$ (air pressure), final volume $V_2 = 0.12\,\textsf{l}$; constant temperature

Find

the gauge reading, that is, the gauge pressure $\Delta p$ in kilopascals

Principle

The nozzle is blocked, so the amount of air stays the same, and the slow compression keeps the temperature constant. Then Boyle's law applies, and the total pressure is substituted into it. The gauge shows the difference between the total pressure and the air pressure (chapter Changes of state of a gas and Boyle's law).

Formula

$p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$, so

$p_2$$= \dfrac{p_1V_1}{V_2}$
$\Delta p$$= p_2 - p_1$
$= \dfrac{p_1V_1}{V_2} - p_1$

Conversions

Not needed: the litres cancel, and the pressure comes out directly in kilopascals.

Substitution

$p_2$$= \dfrac{101\,\text{kPa} \cdot 0.30\,\textsf{l}}{0.12\,\textsf{l}}$
$= 252.5\,\text{kPa}$
$\Delta p$$= 252.5\,\text{kPa} - 101\,\text{kPa}$
$= 151.5\,\text{kPa}$

Answer

The gauge shows about $150\,\text{kPa}$, two significant figures like the volumes $0.30\,\textsf{l}$ and $0.12\,\textsf{l}$.

Reasonableness

The volume decreases by a factor of $2.5$, so the total pressure becomes $2.5$ times as large, about $250\,\text{kPa}$. The gauge pressure is smaller than this by the air pressure. The reading is of the usual order of magnitude for a bicycle tyre.

Hot-air balloon

The envelope of a hot-air balloon is open at the bottom, so inside the balloon the pressure is the same as outside, $100\,\text{kPa}$. The volume of the envelope is $2800\,\text{m}^3$. The air inside the envelope is heated from $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The molar mass of air is $29.0\,\text{g/mol}$.

How many kilograms of air leave the balloon during the heating?

kg
Hint 1: a guiding question

The pressure and the volume stay the same, but the amount of air changes. Calculate the mass of air in the envelope before and after the heating.

Hint 2: what is the connection

From the ideal gas law and the amount of substance $n = m/M$ you get $m = \dfrac{pVM}{RT}$, with $T$ in kelvins.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

Calculate the mass at the temperatures $T_1 = 283.15\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = 373.15\,\text{K}$. The mass that leaves is their difference; remember $M = 0.0290\,\text{kg/mol}$.

Show the solution

Given

pressure $p = 100\,\text{kPa}$ (the same before and after), volume $V = 2800\,\text{m}^3$, temperatures $\theta_1 = 10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $\theta_2 = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, molar mass $M = 29.0\,\text{g/mol}$, $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

Find

the mass $\Delta m$ of the air that leaves, in kilograms

Principle

The envelope is open, so inside there is always the pressure of the outside air, and the volume of the envelope stays the same. When the air warms, fewer particles fit into the same volume at the same pressure, and the surplus flows out at the bottom. The air is described as an ideal gas, which suits ordinary pressure (chapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$).

Formula

$pV = nRT$ and $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$, so

$m$$= \dfrac{pVM}{RT}$
$\Delta m$$= \dfrac{pVM}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_1} - \dfrac{1}{T_2}\right)$

Conversions

$p$$= 100 \cdot 1000\,\text{Pa}$
$= 100\,000\,\text{Pa}$
$M$$= 29.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg/mol}$
$= 0.0290\,\text{kg/mol}$
$T_1$$= (10 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
$= 283.15\,\text{K}$
$T_2$$= (100 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
$= 373.15\,\text{K}$

Substitution

$m_1$$= \dfrac{100\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 2800\,\text{m}^3 \cdot 0.0290\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{mol}}}{8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 283.15\,\text{K}}$
$= 3451\,\text{kg}$
$m_2$$= m_1 \cdot \dfrac{T_1}{T_2}$
$= 3451\,\text{kg} \cdot \dfrac{283.15\,\text{K}}{373.15\,\text{K}}$
$= 2619\,\text{kg}$

so

$\Delta m$$= 3451\,\text{kg} - 2619\,\text{kg}$
$= 832\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$\Delta m \approx 830\,\text{kg}$

The pressure $100\,\text{kPa}$, the volume $2800\,\text{m}^3$ and the temperature $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ are given to about two significant figures, so the result is given to two significant figures, even though the molar mass $29.0\,\text{g/mol}$ is more precise.

Reasonableness

About a quarter of the air leaves, because the kelvin temperature increases in the ratio $373 : 283 \approx 1.32$ and the mass decreases in the ratio $283 : 373 \approx 0.76$. The initial mass $3450\,\text{kg}$ corresponds to an air density of $3450\,\text{kg} : 2800\,\text{m}^3 \approx 1.2\,\text{kg/m}^3$, which is the usual density of air.

The piston rises

3. Explain

The water level of a cottage well is $12\,\text{m}$ below the ground. A suction pump on the ground cannot make the water rise all the way up the pipe, even though the pump is powerful and completely airtight. Explain why the water rises in the suction pipe and why it does not rise higher than about $10\,\text{m}$. Also tell how the water can be brought up.

Model answer

A suction pump does not pull water up. It removes air from the upper part of the pipe, so the pressure there decreases. The air pressure $p_0 \approx 101\,\text{kPa}$ acts on the water surface of the well, and it pushes water into the pipe. The water rises until the gauge pressure $\rho g h$ of the water column in the pipe and the pressure in the upper part of the pipe together equal the air pressure. The best pump can lower the pressure in the upper part of the pipe to almost zero, but not below it, so the greatest height of rise is $h = \dfrac{p_0}{\rho g} = \dfrac{101\,000\,\text{Pa}}{1000\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 10.3\,\text{m}$. In practice it is a little less, because the water starts to boil at low pressure. So a more powerful suction pump does not help in a $12\,\text{m}$ well. The water is brought up with a submersible pump, which is in the well under the water and pushes the water up the pipe with a gauge pressure: a pushing pump has no such limit.

    a)

    Given

    diameter of the piston $d = 0.200\,\text{m}$, mass of the car and the platform $m = 1800\,\text{kg}$, pressure of the outside air $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, lift at a constant speed; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

    the gauge pressure $\Delta p$ in the cylinder, that is, the pressure difference between the air in the cylinder and the outside air, in kilopascals

    Principle

    The platform moves at a constant speed, so the forces acting on it are in balance (chapter Newton's second law). From below the piston the air in the cylinder pushes the piston up with the force $pA$, and from above the outside air pushes it down with the force $p_0A$. Their difference $\Delta p\,A$ supports the weight $mg$. The force of pressure is perpendicular to the surface of the piston, that is, vertical (chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure).

    $\Delta p\,A$$= mg$
    $A$$= \pi r^2$
    $= \pi \left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2$

    so $\Delta p = \dfrac{mg}{\pi (d/2)^2}$

    Conversions

    Radius

    $r$$= 0.200\,\text{m} : 2$
    $= 0.100\,\text{m}$

    b)

    Given

    $m = 1800\,\text{kg}$, lifting height $h = 1.20\,\text{m}$, from part a $\Delta p = 562.1\,\text{kPa}$ and $A = 0.031\,42\,\text{m}^2$, pressure of the outside air $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$

    $= \Delta p\,\Delta V$

    $p_2$$= p_1 - \dfrac{p_\text{c}\,\Delta V}{V_\text{s}}$

    The total pressure at first

    $= 1001\,\text{kPa}$

    One lift takes $250\,\text{kPa}$, that is, a quarter, of the tank's pressure, because the $pV$ of the air moved into the cylinder is $663 \cdot 37.7 \approx 25\,000\,\text{kPa}\cdot\textsf{l}$ and the original $pV$ of the tank is about $100\,000\,\text{kPa}\cdot\textsf{l}$.

    • In part a the area is calculated from the diameter instead of the radius, so the gauge pressure comes out as a quarter.

    Principle

    The law of conservation of energy allows only changes in which energy is neither created nor lost (chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy).     Model answer

The engine still cannot work. A heat engine does work only when heat flows from hot to colder and part of it is given to a colder environment. The brochure's engine has only one temperature, the sea at $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and nothing colder where the heat could go.     Exercise 2.3-5 The molar mass of air by weighing 15 points

    Made-up example data · The report and its measurement results are invented examples.

    A group of students wanted to determine the molar mass of air by weighing. An extract from the group's report:

    We weighed a $1.5$-litre soft-drink bottle with a bicycle valve fitted in its cap. The bottle held room air at air pressure, and its mass was $52.36\,\text{g}$. We pumped air into the bottle with a bicycle pump until the pump's gauge showed $210\,\text{kPa}$. We detached the pump and weighed the bottle immediately: $55.64\,\text{g}$. The room temperature was $21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. We calculated the amount of substance of the added air from the ideal gas law and got a molar mass of $25\,\text{g/mol}$. So the table value $29.0\,\text{g/mol}$ is too large.

    The resolution of the scale is $0.01\,\text{g}$.

    1. a) Show how the group got a molar mass of $25\,\text{g/mol}$. 4 p
    2. b) Assess whether the group's conclusion is justified. Name the most important sources of error in the measurement and tell in which direction each changes the result. 6 p
    3. c) Design an improved experiment that gives the molar mass of air more reliably. 5 p

    Skills from the chapters: Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$, Measuring and the reliability of a measurement, Graphing and fitting a straight line

    Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    from the report the mass before $m_1 = 52.36\,\text{g}$ and after $m_2 = 55.64\,\text{g}$, gauge pressure on the pump's gauge $\Delta p = 210\,\text{kPa}$, volume of the bottle $V = 1.5\,\textsf{l}$, temperature $\theta = 21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; $R = 8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}}$

    Find

    the molar mass $M$ calculated by the group

    Principle

    In the group's assumption the volume and the temperature of the bottle are the same before and after the pumping. By the ideal gas law the amount of substance is then proportional to the pressure, so the amount of substance of the added air is obtained from the increase in pressure, which is the gauge pressure shown by the gauge (chapter Ideal gas law $pV = nRT$). The molar mass is the mass of the added air divided by its amount of substance.

    Formula

    $\Delta n$$= n_2 - n_1$
    $= \dfrac{(p_0 + \Delta p)V}{RT} - \dfrac{p_0V}{RT}$
    $= \dfrac{\Delta p\,V}{RT}$

    $M = \dfrac{m_2 - m_1}{\Delta n}$

    Conversions

    $\Delta p$$= 210 \cdot 1000\,\text{Pa}$
    $= 210\,000\,\text{Pa}$
    $V$$= 1.5 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}^3$
    $= 0.0015\,\text{m}^3$
    $T$$= (21 + 273.15)\,\text{K}$
    $= 294.15\,\text{K}$

    Substitution

    $\Delta n$$= \dfrac{210\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.0015\,\text{m}^3}{8.31\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 294.15\,\text{K}}$
    $= 0.1289\,\text{mol}$
    $M$$= \dfrac{55.64\,\text{g} - 52.36\,\text{g}}{0.1289\,\text{mol}}$
    $= \dfrac{3.28\,\text{g}}{0.1289\,\text{mol}}$
    $= 25.4\,\text{g/mol}$

    Answer

    $M \approx 25\,\text{g/mol}$

    Two significant figures like the volume $1.5\,\textsf{l}$. The group's number is calculated correctly.

    Reasonableness

    If the molar mass were the table value $29.0\,\text{g/mol}$,

    $29.0\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}} \cdot 0.1289\,\text{mol}$
    $\approx 3.74\,\text{g}$

    of air should have entered the bottle. The weighed $3.28\,\text{g}$ is about $12\,\%$ less.

    b)

    Find

    whether the conclusion is justified, the most important sources of error and the direction of their effect

    Principle

    The conclusion is justified only if the difference from the table value is larger than the uncertainty of the result (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement). The effect of a source of error is estimated by recalculating the result with a changed value. The molar mass is $\Delta m : \Delta n$, so a $\Delta n$ that is too large or a $\Delta m$ that is too small decreases the result.

    Model answer

    The conclusion is not justified. The result rests on a single measurement, and the most important sources of error push it in the same direction, downwards.
    - Warm air. The pumped air warms as it is compressed, and the gauge was read immediately. The pressure of warm air is higher than that of the same amount of air at room temperature, so $\Delta n$ was calculated too large and the result too small. If the air was at $30\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the result would be about $27\,\text{g/mol}$.
    - The pump's gauge. It measures the pressure in the hose during pumping and is made for rough use. If the real gauge pressure of the bottle was $10\,\text{kPa}$ lower, the result would be $27\,\text{g/mol}$; the error can go in either direction.
    - Escaping air. When the pump is detached, and through the valve, air escapes before the weighing, so $\Delta m$ is too small and the result too small.
    - The volume of the bottle. The marking $1.5\,\textsf{l}$ is the volume of the drink. The bottle is slightly larger and bulges under pressure, so the real $\Delta n$ is larger than calculated. This increases the calculated result, so it does not explain the value that is too small.

    The resolution of the scale, $0.01\,\text{g}$, is less than $1\,\%$ of the increase in mass $3.28\,\text{g}$, so it is not significant. The errors of the temperature and the gauge are each about $5$–$7\,\%$, so the uncertainty is of the same order of magnitude as the difference from the table value. The measurement does not show the table value to be wrong.

    c)

    Find

    an experimental plan that gives the molar mass of air more reliably

    Principle

    The quantities are measured as directly as possible and at a time when the assumptions hold (the same temperature before and after). The measurement is repeated with several values, and the result is determined from the slope of a graph, so the error of a single measurement weighs less (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line).

    Model answer

    • Volume. The volume of the bottle is measured by filling it with water and weighing the water ($1\,\text{g} \approx 1\,\text{ml}$).
    • Pressure and temperature. A precise pressure gauge is connected to the bottle, and the gauge pressure is read only when the bottle has stood for a few minutes and the air has cooled to room temperature. The room temperature is measured with a thermometer.
    • Mass. The bottle is weighed straight after the pressure reading, without touching the valve in between.
    • Repetitions and graph. The measurement is made at several gauge pressures, for example $50$, $100$, $150$, $200$ and $250\,\text{kPa}$, with the same bottle and valve. The results are presented as a $(\Delta p, \Delta m)$ graph. By the ideal gas law $\Delta m = \dfrac{MV}{RT}\,\Delta p$, so a straight line through the origin is fitted to the points, and the molar mass is obtained from the slope: $M = \dfrac{kRT}{V}$.

    If the points lie on a straight line but the line does not pass through the origin, this reveals a systematic error, for example air escaping every time on detaching.

    Common mistakes

    • In part a the amount of substance is calculated with the total pressure $311\,\text{kPa}$ and not with the increase in pressure, so the result is a third.
    • In part b it is only stated that “there were measurement errors” without naming the source of error or the direction of its effect.
    • In part b the bulging of the bottle is named as the cause of the result being too small, although it acts the opposite way.
    • In part c only “more precise instruments” are proposed without telling what is measured and how.

    Also accepted

    • In part b one can also mention that the bulging bottle displaces more air, so the buoyancy increases and the scale shows about $0.06\,\text{g}$ too little (decreases the result slightly), when the reasoning is correct.

    Exercise 2.3-6 Vacuum lifter and glass pane 15 points

    Made-up example data · The lifter and its numbers are an invented example; the coefficients of friction are indicative values.

    A glazier from a glass shop lifts a window pane with a vacuum lifter that hangs from the cable of a crane. The lifter's round rubber pad is placed against the glass, and a pump sucks air from under the pad so that the pressure under the pad is $60\,\text{kPa}$ lower than the pressure of the outside air. The inner diameter of the pad is $300\,\text{mm}$.

    The length of the glass pane is $2.40\,\text{m}$, its width $1.20\,\text{m}$ and its thickness $10\,\text{mm}$, and the density of the glass is $2500\,\text{kg/m}^3$. The coefficient of static friction between rubber and dry glass is $0.60$, and for wet glass $0.30$. The crane accelerates the lift with an acceleration of at most $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. According to the manufacturer's instructions, the holding force of the lifter must be at least twice what the lift requires.

    1. a) The pane is lifted from a pallet in a horizontal position, and the pad is in the middle of the upper surface of the pane. Draw the force diagram of the pane at the start of the lift, while the crane is accelerating. What is the minimum partial vacuum needed under the pad to keep the pane attached? 5 p
    2. b) For installation the pane is turned upright, and the pad is on the vertical surface of the pane. Draw the force diagram of the pane, and calculate the minimum partial vacuum now needed when the lift accelerates in the same way. 5 p
    3. c) Use the manufacturer's instructions to assess whether the glazier can lift the pane in the upright position also in the rain, when the glass is wet. 5 p

    Skills from the chapters: Pressure and hydrostatic pressure, Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces, Weight, friction and the inclined plane, Newton's second law

    Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    dimensions of the glass pane $2.40\,\text{m}$, $1.20\,\text{m}$ and $10\,\text{mm}$, density $\rho = 2500\,\text{kg/m}^3$, inner diameter of the pad $d = 300\,\text{mm}$, upward acceleration $a = 1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Find

    the force diagram of the pane and the minimum partial vacuum $\Delta p$ under the pad, in kilopascals

    Principle

    The outside air presses on the pane from all sides, but under the pad the pressure is lower by $\Delta p$. That is why, at the pad, the force of the outside air on the lower surface of the pane is $\Delta p\,A$ larger than the force of the air on the upper surface, and the net force $F_\text{p} = \Delta p\,A$ points perpendicular to the surface, that is, upwards (chapter Pressure and hydrostatic pressure). In addition, the weight and the normal force from the rim of the pad, which presses the pane down, act on the pane. At the minimum partial vacuum the normal force from the rim is zero: the pane is on the verge of coming loose. The pane accelerates upwards, so by Newton's second law the sum of the forces is $ma$ upwards (chapter Newton's second law).

    Formula

    $F_\text{p} - mg = ma$ and $F_\text{p} = \Delta p\,A$, where $m = \rho\,l\,b\,h$ and $A = \pi \left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2$, so

    $\Delta p$$= \dfrac{m(g + a)}{\pi (d/2)^2}$

    Conversions

    $10\,\text{mm}$$= 10 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
    $= 0.010\,\text{m}$
    $d/2$$= 300\,\text{mm} : 2$
    $= 150\,\text{mm}$
    $= 0.150\,\text{m}$

    Substitution

    $m$$= 2500\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 2.40\,\text{m} \cdot 1.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.010\,\text{m}$
    $= 72.0\,\text{kg}$
    $A$$= \pi \cdot (0.150\,\text{m})^2$
    $= 0.070\,69\,\text{m}^2$
    $\Delta p$$= \dfrac{72.0\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81 + 1.2)\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.070\,69\,\text{m}^2}$
    $= \dfrac{792.7\,\text{N}}{0.070\,69\,\text{m}^2}$
    $= 11\,210\,\text{Pa}$

    Answer

    $\Delta p \approx 11.2\,\text{kPa}$

    Three significant figures like the dimensions of the pane; the acceleration $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ is given to two figures, but it is only a small part of the sum $g + a$.

    Reasonableness

    Without the acceleration $706\,\text{N} : 0.0707\,\text{m}^2 \approx 10\,\text{kPa}$ would be needed, so the acceleration increases the need by about $12\,\%$, as the ratio $1.2 : 9.81$ tells. The lifter's $60\,\text{kPa}$ is more than five times as much.

    b)

    Given

    $m = 72.0\,\text{kg}$, $A = 0.070\,69\,\text{m}^2$, $a = 1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ upwards, coefficient of static friction of dry glass $\mu_0 = 0.60$

    Find

    the force diagram of the upright pane and the minimum partial vacuum $\Delta p$

    Principle

    The force of the pressure difference is perpendicular to the surface of the glass, and now the surface is vertical, so the force $F_\text{p} = \Delta p\,A$ is horizontal and presses the pane against the pad. The rim of the pad pushes the pane back with the normal force $N$. Horizontally the pane does not accelerate, so $N = F_\text{p}$. Against the weight and the accelerating force the pane is supported only by the static friction of the rim of the pad, which is at most $\mu_0 N$ (chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane). At the minimum partial vacuum the static friction is at its maximum.

    Formula

    horizontally $N - F_\text{p} = 0$ and vertically $F_{\mu 0} - mg = ma$, where

    $F_{\mu 0}$$\le \mu_0 N$
    $= \mu_0\,\Delta p\,A$

    so $\Delta p = \dfrac{m(g + a)}{\mu_0 A}$

    Conversions

    Not needed: the quantities of part a are in base units.

    Substitution

    $\Delta p$$= \dfrac{72.0\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81 + 1.2)\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.60 \cdot 0.070\,69\,\text{m}^2}$
    $= \dfrac{792.7\,\text{N}}{0.042\,41\,\text{m}^2}$
    $= 18\,690\,\text{Pa}$

    Answer

    $\Delta p \approx 18.7\,\text{kPa}$

    Three significant figures as in part a; the coefficient of friction is an indicative two-figure value, so about $19\,\text{kPa}$ is an equally good answer.

    Reasonableness

    The result is the result of part a divided by the coefficient of friction: $11.2\,\text{kPa} : 0.60 \approx 18.7\,\text{kPa}$. The upright position needs more, because the friction is smaller than the normal force that causes it.

    c)

    Find

    whether an upright lift with wet glass meets the manufacturer's instructions

    Principle

    The safety factor is the ratio of the lifter's partial vacuum to the partial vacuum the lift requires, and according to the instructions it must be at least $2$. When wet, the coefficient of static friction is $0.30$, so the result of part b doubles (chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane).

    Model answer

    With wet glass $\Delta p = \dfrac{792.7\,\text{N}}{0.30 \cdot 0.070\,69\,\text{m}^2} \approx 37\,\text{kPa}$ is needed. So the lifter's $60\,\text{kPa}$ is just enough to hold the pane, but the safety factor is only $60 : 37 \approx 1.6$, below the two that the instructions require. When dry, the upright lift meets the instructions, $60 : 18.7 \approx 3.2$, and the horizontal lift clearly, $60 : 11.2 \approx 5.4$. The upright position is more demanding, because the force of the pressure is perpendicular to the surface of the glass, that is, horizontal. It does not support the weight at all; the weight is carried by friction, which is at most $\mu_0$ times the normal force. The glazier must not lift a wet pane upright with this lifter: the glass must be dried, or two pads are used, which doubles the area.

    Common mistakes

    • In part a the area is calculated from the diameter instead of the radius, or the thickness of the pane is left in millimetres.
    • In part a the acceleration is forgotten and only the weight is calculated.
    • In part b the force of the pressure difference is drawn upwards to support the pane, although the force of pressure is always perpendicular to the surface.
    • In part b the friction is calculated from the weight, $\mu_0 mg$, and not from the normal force $N = \Delta p\,A$.
    • In part c the answer is that the lift succeeds because $60\,\text{kPa}$ is more than $37\,\text{kPa}$, and the safety factor is forgotten.

    Also accepted

    • In parts a and b the normal force from the rim of the pad may also be drawn in the force diagram, when the answer says that it is zero at the minimum partial vacuum (a) or equal to the force of the pressure difference (b).
