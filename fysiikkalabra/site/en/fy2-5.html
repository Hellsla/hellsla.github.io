Fuels and heat of combustion $E = Hm$

Why do a kilogram of petrol and a kilogram of air-dried firewood release different amounts of energy when they burn, even though both are just burning matter?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the energy released from a fuel using its heat of combustion and mass.
  • compare fuels and food by energy content and explain how food energy differs from the heat of combustion.
  • tell released energy apart from useful energy using efficiency.

Prior knowledge

  • Energy is neither created nor destroyed but transformed from one form to anotherchapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy
  • The unit of energy is the joule, and $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$chapter Energy transformations and the joule
  • Efficiency tells you what share of the energy taken in is usefulchapter Efficiency
  • Power is energy per unit of timechapter Power P = ΔE/Δt

1Think about it

At a petrol station a car is filled with petrol, and in a cottage fireplace firewood is burnt. On the side of a chocolate bar it says "energy $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per hundred grams". In all three, energy is stored in the substance, and it is released when the substance burns or when the body uses it.

Is the same amount of energy released from a kilogram of different substances, and how can you calculate the energy released?

Firewood is burning in a fireplace and the room warms up. Which description of the energy transformation is correct?

    4Theory

    Key idea

    In this chapter a fuel means a chemical fuel: a substance whose chemical energy is released by burning it. The uranium in a nuclear power plant is also called a fuel, but nuclear fuel does not burn: its energy is nuclear energy (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy). Chemical energy is energy stored in the structure of the atoms and molecules of a substance. When a fuel burns, the structure changes and energy is released as heat and light. Energy does not appear out of nothing: chemical energy is transformed into another form. The same is true of food: your body slowly "burns" food and releases energy from it.

    Different fuels contain different amounts of chemical energy for their mass. The quantity that tells you this is the heat of combustion $H$. It is the energy released when one kilogram of fuel burns, and its unit is, for example, $\text{MJ/kg}$. The heat of combustion of petrol is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$, so about $43\,\text{MJ}$ is released from one kilogram (about $43 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}$). The heat of combustion of air-dried firewood (moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) is only about $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$, so to get the same energy you need almost three times the mass of wood.

    Because the heat of combustion is energy per unit of mass, the energy released is directly proportional to the mass of fuel burnt: twice as much fuel releases twice as much energy. This gives the law used to calculate the energy released.

    Heat of combustion and released energy

    The energy released when a fuel burns is

    $$E = Hm$$

    where $E$ is the energy released ($\text{J}$ or $\text{MJ}$), $H$ is the heat of combustion of the fuel ($\text{MJ/kg}$) and $m$ is the mass of fuel burnt ($\text{kg}$). The mass must be in kilograms, because the heat of combustion is energy per kilogram. For example, $3.0\,\text{kg}$ of petrol releases $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 3.0\,\text{kg} = 129\,\text{MJ} \approx 130\,\text{MJ}$, because the mass is known to two figures.

    The table gives the heats of combustion of common fuels. They are net heats of combustion, meaning that the heat released when water vapour in the flue gas condenses is not counted, and energy statistics use them. The values are rounded: the real value varies from batch to batch of fuel.

    Fuel $H\ (\text{MJ/kg})$
    natural gas 48
    petrol 43
    diesel 43
    coal 26
    wood pellets 17
    firewood (air-dried) 15
    peat (milled peat) 10

    Sources: firewood ($13\text{–}15\,\text{GJ/t}$, moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) and milled peat ($9\text{–}11\,\text{GJ/t}$, moisture $40\text{–}50\,\%$): Statistics Finland, Polttoaineluokitus, määritelmät (2017). The values are rounded.

    Moisture and fuel quality

    Wet wood gives less energy per kilogram than dry wood, because part of its mass is water, which does not burn, and in addition part of the released energy is used to vaporise this water. This is why the firewood in the table is air-dried (moisture $20\text{–}25\,\%$) and the peat is milled peat with a moisture of $40\text{–}50\,\%$. Completely dry wood would have a heat of combustion of about $19\,\text{MJ/kg}$.

    The energy content of food and the heat of combustion

    A nutrition label gives energy per hundred grams, for example $2200\,\text{kJ}$ per $100\,\text{g}$. When you convert the energy to megajoules and the mass to kilograms, you get the energy content of the food per unit of mass, here $e = 22\,\text{MJ/kg}$. Both the energy content of food and the heat of combustion of a fuel can be given in the unit $\text{MJ/kg}$. The nutrition label describes the energy the body can use, whereas the heat of combustion of a fuel describes the energy released in burning. So they are not the same quantity: your body does not get all the energy from food that would be released if it burned, because, for example, part of the food is not absorbed from the intestine. The calculation is still the same as with the heat of combustion: the energy of food is $E = em$.

    Food $\text{kJ}$ / $100\,\text{g}$ $e$ ($\text{MJ/kg}$)
    potato, boiled 300 3
    rye bread 1000 10
    milk chocolate 2200 22
    butter 3000 30

    Common misconception

    • Heat of combustion is not temperature. Many people think the heat of combustion tells you how hot the fuel burns. It is actually energy per unit of mass.
    • Energy is not created in burning. The chemical energy of the fuel is transformed into heat and light, but energy does not appear out of nothing.
    • Released energy is not useful energy. In a boiler, a fireplace or an engine, part of the energy is wasted, and the useful share is calculated with efficiency (chapter Efficiency).

    Burning carbon-containing fuels, such as wood, peat and fossil fuels, also produces carbon dioxide, which is covered in the chapter Energy production, the environment and climate change.

    Summary

    • The chemical energy of a fuel is released as heat and light when it burns. Energy is not created but transformed.
    • The heat of combustion $H$ is the energy released per kilogram of fuel, with the unit $\text{MJ/kg}$.
    • The energy released is $E = Hm$ when the mass $m$ is in kilograms.
    • The heat of combustion of petrol and diesel is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$, of coal $26$, of air-dried wood $15$ and of peat $10\,\text{MJ/kg}$.
    • The $\text{kJ}$ per $100\,\text{g}$ on a food package can be converted to $\text{MJ/kg}$ like a heat of combustion, but it describes the energy the body can use, not the energy released in burning.
    • Released energy is not the same as useful energy: useful energy is found with efficiency.

      Worked example

      Problem

      In a fireplace, $6.0\,\text{kg}$ of air-dried firewood with a heat of combustion of $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ is burnt. How much energy is released from the wood? Give the answer in megajoules and in kilowatt-hours.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The mass of the firewood is $m = 6.0\,\text{kg}$ and from the table the heat of combustion is $H = 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. We want the energy released, $E$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The wood burns, so its chemical energy is transformed into heat. The heat of combustion tells you the energy per kilogram, so the energy released is found by multiplying the heat of combustion by the mass.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The quantity we want, $E$, is already alone on the left side of the equation:

      $$E = Hm$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The mass is already in kilograms, so you can calculate the megajoules directly. For kilowatt-hours we use $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$, from which $1\,\text{MJ} = 1\,\text{kWh} : 3.6$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} E &= Hm \\ &= 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{kg} \\ &= 90\,\text{MJ} \end{aligned}$$

      In kilowatt-hours

      $E$$= 90\,\text{MJ}$
      $= 90 \cdot (1\,\text{kWh} : 3.6)$
      $= 25\,\text{kWh}$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The wood releases $90\,\text{MJ}$ of energy, which is $25\,\text{kWh}$. The answer is given to two significant figures, because both the mass $6.0\,\text{kg}$ and the heat of combustion $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ are known to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. One kilogram of wood gives $15\,\text{MJ}$, which is about $4\,\text{kWh}$, so six kilograms give about six times as much: $6 \cdot 4\,\text{kWh} = 24\,\text{kWh}$, which matches the result. The energy equals that of a one-kilowatt ($1\,\text{kW}$) heater switched on for $25\,\text{h}$. However, you do not get all of it as heat for the room, because some of the heat leaves with the flue gases (chapter Efficiency).

      Which releases more energy, and how much? Also explain why released energy is not the same as the energy obtained as useful energy.

      Model answer

      The heat of combustion of pellets is about $17\,\text{MJ/kg}$ and that of petrol about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$. By the law $E = Hm$, the pellets release $17\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 10\,\text{kg} = 170\,\text{MJ}$ and the petrol $43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 10\,\text{kg} = 430\,\text{MJ}$, so about $2.5$ times as much energy is released from the petrol. Only part of the energy released is transformed into a useful form, because part of the heat leaves with the flue gases and the surroundings in the boiler or the engine. The share that is useful is calculated with efficiency.

          $18\,\text{kW}$

          Two significant figures like the heat of combustion $17\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          Reasonableness

          In a minute $0.065\,\text{kg}$ burns, which releases $17\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 0.065\,\text{kg} = 1.1\,\text{MJ}$. That is

          $1.1\,\text{MJ} : 60\,\text{s}$$= 0.018\,\text{MJ/s}$
          $= 18\,\text{kW}$

          which suits the fuel power of the pellet boiler of a detached house. The power is the power released, not the useful power received by the house.

        1. Exercise 5-16

          To heat a cottage, $120\,\text{MJ}$ of energy must be released from fuel. The energy can be obtained either from air-dried firewood ($15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$) or from light heating oil ($43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$). Calculate the mass of each fuel needed and say how many kilograms more wood than oil are needed. Also explain why more wood is needed. Give the answer in kilograms.

          kg
          Answer

          $5.2\,\text{kg}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the energy needed $E = 120\,\text{MJ}$, air-dried firewood $H_\text{wood} = 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ and heating oil $H_\text{oil} = 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$

          Find

          how many kilograms more wood than oil are needed, $\Delta m = m_\text{wood} - m_\text{oil}$

          Principle

          The same energy $E$ must be released from both fuels. By the law $E = Hm$ the mass is the energy divided by the heat of combustion, so a smaller heat of combustion needs a larger mass.

          Formula

          $E = Hm$, so $m = \dfrac{E}{H}$ for each fuel, and $\Delta m = m_\text{wood} - m_\text{oil}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the energy is in megajoules and the heats of combustion in megajoules per kilogram.

          Substitution

          $m_\text{wood}$$= 120\,\text{MJ} : 15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 8.0\,\text{kg}$
          $m_\text{oil}$$= 120\,\text{MJ} : 43\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
          $= 2.79\,\text{kg}$

          Difference:

          $\Delta m$$= 8.0\,\text{kg} - 2.79\,\text{kg}$
          $= 5.21\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $5.2\,\text{kg}$

          More wood is needed, two significant figures like the data $120\,\text{MJ}$ and $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. More wood is needed because one kilogram of wood contains less chemical energy than one kilogram of oil.

          Reasonableness

          The mass of wood is about $2.9$ times the mass of oil, since $43 : 15 = 2.87$. So $2.87 \cdot 2.79\,\text{kg} \approx 8.0\,\text{kg}$, and the difference is $2.87 - 1 = 1.87$ times the mass of oil: $1.87 \cdot 2.79\,\text{kg} \approx 5.2\,\text{kg}$, as it should be.

          A hiker carries food with an energy content of $18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ (example value). There is $2.4\,\text{kg}$ of food, and the hiker needs $12\,\text{MJ}$ of energy from food per day.

          How many daily rations does the food provide? Give the answer as a decimal number and justify your calculation.

          daily rations
          Answer

          $3.6$

          Show the solution

          Given

          energy content of the food $e = 18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, mass $m = 2.4\,\text{kg}$, daily consumption $12\,\text{MJ}$

          Find

          the number of daily rations $N$ that the food provides (as a decimal number)

          Principle

          The total energy obtainable from the food is found by multiplying the energy content by the mass, as with a fuel by the law $E = Hm$. The number of rations is how many times the daily consumption fits into the total energy, so $N = E : \text{daily consumption}$.

          Formula

          $E = em$ and the number of days $N = \dfrac{E}{12\,\text{MJ}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the mass is in kilograms and the energy in megajoules.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 2.4\,\text{kg}$
          $= 43.2\,\text{MJ}$
          $N$$= 43.2\,\text{MJ} : 12\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 3.6$

          Answer

          $3.6$

          Daily rations, two significant figures like the data $2.4\,\text{kg}$ and $18\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$.

          Reasonableness

          The need for three rations is $36\,\text{MJ}$ and for four $48\,\text{MJ}$. There is $43.2\,\text{MJ}$ of food, so the result lies between three and four: three full days and part of a fourth.

        3. Exercise 5-18

          A peat-fired power plant burns $36\,\text{t}$ of milled peat per hour, with a heat of combustion of $10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$. The efficiency of the plant in electricity production is $35\,\%$.

          What is the electric power of the power plant? Give the answer in megawatts. Remember: $1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s}$ and one hour is $3600\,\text{s}$.

          MW
          Answer

          $35\,\text{MW}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          peat $m = 36\,\text{t}$ per hour, $H = 10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$, efficiency of electricity production $\eta = 35\,\% = 0.35$, time $t = 1\,\text{h}$

          Find

          the electric power $P$ in megawatts

          Principle

          In one hour the peat releases the energy $E_\text{in} = Hm$. By the efficiency, only the share $\eta$ of it becomes electricity (chapter Efficiency). Power is energy per unit of time, so power = energy : time (chapter Power: $P = \Delta E/\Delta t$).

          Formula

          $E_\text{in} = Hm$, $E_\text{useful} = \eta\, E_\text{in}$ and $P = \dfrac{E_\text{useful}}{t}$, together $P = \dfrac{\eta H m}{t}$

          Conversions

          $36\,\text{t}$$= 36 \cdot 1000\,\text{kg}$
          $= 36\,000\,\text{kg}$

          $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$. Since $1\,\text{MJ/s} = 1\,\text{MW}$, the unit of power comes out directly in megawatts.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{in}$$= 10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 36\,000\,\text{kg}$
          $= 360\,000\,\text{MJ}$
          $E_\text{useful}$$= 0.35 \cdot 360\,000\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 126\,000\,\text{MJ}$
          $P$$= 126\,000\,\text{MJ} : 3600\,\text{s}$
          $= 35\,\text{MJ/s}$
          $= 35\,\text{MW}$

          Answer

          $35\,\text{MW}$

          Two significant figures like the data $10\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$ and $35\,\%$.

          Reasonableness

          If all the energy of the peat became electricity, the power would be $360\,000\,\text{MJ} : 3600\,\text{s} = 100\,\text{MW}$. With an efficiency of $35\,\%$ we get $35\,\%$ of that, $35\,\text{MW}$, which is a typical power for a small power plant.
