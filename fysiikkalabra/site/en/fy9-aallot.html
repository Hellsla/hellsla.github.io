Oscillation, waves and sound

Why can the lifeguard's whistle also be heard under water, and does the sound change on its way from air into water?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve oscillation, wave and sound problems by choosing a suitable model
  • draw and interpret graphs of oscillation and wave data
  • assess claims and measurements about waves and sound with justification

Prior knowledge

  • Frequency is the reciprocal of the period, $f = \tfrac{1}{T}$chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude
  • The period of a spring and of a pendulum depends on the properties of the oscillatorchapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum
  • The speed of a wave is $v = f\lambda$, and the source determines the frequencychapter How waves form and travel
  • The path difference decides whether waves reinforce or weaken each otherchapter When waves combine: superposition and interference
  • The loudness of a sound is given as an intensity level in decibelschapter Intensity and the decibel scale

1Think about it

At a swimming pool the lifeguard blows a whistle whose frequency is $3.0\,\text{kHz}$. One of the swimmers has just dived to the bottom of the pool and hears the whistle under water. The speed of sound is about $350\,\text{m/s}$ in the warm air of the hall and about $1500\,\text{m/s}$ in water. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the formula is not.

Does the diver hear the whistle at a different pitch than at the surface, and how long is the wavelength of the sound in air and in water?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting level 1: a heavier swinger

First a $20\,\text{kg}$ child and then a $40\,\text{kg}$ child sits on a garden swing. The chains of the swing are equally long, and the swings are small.How does the period of the swing change?

  • The greater weight also accelerates a greater mass, so the acceleration is the same as for the lighter child. The period depends only on the length and the acceleration of free fall. Review in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

  • A heavier child is harder to accelerate, but twice the weight also acts on them. The acceleration stays the same, so the period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$ does not depend on the mass. Review in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

  • Correct. For small swings $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, and the mass does not appear in the expression. A small difference can arise only if the child's centre of gravity is at a different height, because that changes the length of the pendulum. Review if needed in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

Starting level 2: twice the amplitude

A mass oscillating on a spring is pulled the second time twice as far from the equilibrium position before it is released. There is no friction.How do the period and the energy of the oscillation change?

  • Correct. The period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ does not depend on the amplitude, and the energy of the oscillation is $\tfrac12 kA^2$, so the factor is $2^2 = 4$. Review if needed in the chapter Spring force and spring energy.

  • The distance doubles, but so do the spring force and the acceleration, so the mass moves faster. The period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ does not depend on the amplitude. Review in the chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum.

  • The period stays the same, but the energy depends on the square of the amplitude, $E = \tfrac12 kA^2$. Twice the amplitude gives four times the energy. Review in the chapter Spring force and spring energy.

Starting level 3: sound from air into water

Sound passes from air into water, where it travels faster. Which quantity of the sound stays the same?

  • The wavelength changes, because $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$ and the speed grows. In water the wavelength is longer than in air. Review in the chapter How waves form and travel.

  • Correct. At the boundary each compression in the air creates one compression in the water, so the frequency stays the same. The speed grows, so the wavelength grows in the same ratio. Review if needed in the chapter How waves form and travel.

  • The speed of sound depends on the medium: in air it is about $340\,\text{m/s}$ and in water about $1500\,\text{m/s}$. When the medium changes, the frequency stays the same. Review in the chapter How waves form and travel.

Starting level 4: two loudspeakers

Two loudspeakers play the same pure tone in phase. A listener stands at a point that is half a wavelength farther from one loudspeaker than from the other.What does the listener hear?

  • Correct. The path difference $\Delta s = \tfrac12\lambda$ satisfies the condition for destructive interference $\Delta s = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\lambda$ with $n = 0$. Review if needed in the chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference.

  • Two waves come from the two loudspeakers, but they can cancel each other. With a path difference of half a wavelength a crest meets a trough, and the interference is destructive. Review in the chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference.

  • The waves pass through each other, but at the same point their displacements are added. At this point they cancel each other almost completely. Review in the chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference.

Starting level 5: two lawnmowers

One lawnmower gives an intensity level of $80\,\text{dB}$ at the listener. A second identical mower is started at the same distance. How large is the combined level?

  • Two sources bring the listener twice the power per unit area, so the intensity doubles and the level rises by about $3\,\text{dB}$. Review in the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale.

  • Correct. The intensities are added, and $L_2 - L_1 = 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg 2 \approx 3.0\,\text{dB}$. Review if needed in the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale.

  • Decibels are not added. The intensities are added, and twice the intensity raises the level by $10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg 2 \approx 3\,\text{dB}$. Review in the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale.

Starting level 6: an ambulance passes

An ambulance drives at a constant speed straight towards a listener standing at the roadside, passes them and moves away.How does the heard pitch of the siren change?

  • The sound of an approaching source is higher than the siren's own, but at a constant speed the pitch stays the same: it depends on the speed, not on the distance. What grows as it approaches is the loudness. Review in the chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect.

  • Correct. While approaching $f = f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v}{v - v_\text{s}}$ and while moving away $f = f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v}{v + v_\text{s}}$, and both are constant. The pitch drops at the moment of passing. Review if needed in the chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect.

  • The siren sounds at the same frequency, but in front of an approaching source the wavefronts crowd together and behind it they spread out. That is why the heard frequency is higher while approaching and lower while moving away. Review in the chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect.

3Investigate

Two cardboard tubes

Made-up example data · An invented example: the frequencies have been calculated from the natural frequencies of the tubes with the speed of sound $343\,\text{m/s}$, and variation like that of measurements has been added. No end correction has been made.

In a physics lesson two identical cardboard tubes of length $0.600\,\text{m}$ were studied. One tube stood with one end tightly against the table, the other was held in the air so that both its ends were open. Each tube was tapped, and a frequency analysis app on a phone showed four clear peaks in the spectrum of the sound. The temperature of the classroom was $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

Peak A: $f\ (\text{Hz})$ B: $f\ (\text{Hz})$
1 $284$ $142$
2 $573$ $430$
3 $857$ $713$
4 $1146$ $1001$

From the ratios of the frequencies, work out which tube stood against the table, and determine the speed of sound from the measurements of each tube. Answer below with the value from tube B. The model answer compares both tubes.

What speed of sound do the frequencies of tube B give?

m/s
Hint 1: a guiding question

What frequency ratios does tube B have? Divide each frequency by the smallest one.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The ratios are about $1 : 3 : 5 : 7$, so the tube is closed at one end: $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{4L}$ with $n = 1, 3, 5, 7$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$v = 4L \cdot \tfrac{f_n}{n}$. Calculate $\tfrac{f_n}{n}$ from each peak and use their mean.

Show the solution

Given

length of the tube $L = 0.600\,\text{m}$; peaks of tube B $142\,\text{Hz}$, $430\,\text{Hz}$, $713\,\text{Hz}$ and $1001\,\text{Hz}$; peaks of tube A $284\,\text{Hz}$, $573\,\text{Hz}$, $857\,\text{Hz}$ and $1146\,\text{Hz}$

Find

the speed of sound $v$ from the measurements of tube B in $\text{m/s}$

Principle

A standing wave forms in the air of the tube only at the natural frequencies. The frequencies of tube B are in the ratio $1 : 3.0 : 5.0 : 7.0$, so only the odd multiples of the fundamental frequency are present. That is why tube B stood against the table: at the closed end there is a displacement node and at the open end an antinode, so the length of the tube is an odd number of quarter wavelengths (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies). Each peak gives an estimate $\tfrac{f_n}{n}$ of the fundamental frequency, and their mean reduces the effect of the random variation in the readings.

Formula

$f_n = n \cdot \dfrac{v}{4L}$ with $n = 1, 3, 5, 7$, so $v = 4L \cdot \dfrac{f_n}{n}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$\tfrac{f_n}{n}$ is $142\,\text{Hz}$, $430\,\text{Hz} : 3 = 143.3\,\text{Hz}$, $713\,\text{Hz} : 5 = 142.6\,\text{Hz}$ and $1001\,\text{Hz} : 7 = 143.0\,\text{Hz}$, mean $142.7\,\text{Hz}$. So

$v$$= 4 \cdot 0.600\,\text{m} \cdot 142.7\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
$= 342.6\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v \approx 343\,\text{m/s}$

Three significant figures like the length of the tube $0.600\,\text{m}$ and the frequencies.

Reasonableness

The frequencies of tube A are in the ratio $1 : 2 : 3 : 4$, so it was open, and $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{2L}$. Its mean $\tfrac{f_n}{n} = 285.7\,\text{Hz}$ gives

$v$$= 2 \cdot 0.600\,\text{m} \cdot 285.7\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
$\approx 343\,\text{m/s}$

the same result. The fundamental frequency $142\,\text{Hz}$ of the closed tube is half the $284\,\text{Hz}$ of the open tube, as the model says it should be, and $343\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of sound in air at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

4Theory

Key idea

All the phenomena of the module start from oscillation: a body moves back and forth on both sides of its equilibrium position, and the motion is described by the period $T$, the frequency $f$ and the amplitude $A$. The period of a spring and of a pendulum is set by the oscillator itself. A wave is an oscillation that travels through a medium: the particles oscillate in place, and the wave carries energy but not matter. Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave. Its pitch is set by the frequency and its loudness by the intensity.

In the wave problems of the exam it pays to ask first what determines which quantity. The source determines the frequency, the medium the speed, and the wavelength follows from them. When a wave meets an obstacle, a gap or another wave, it reflects, diffracts or interferes, and in every case what decides is comparing distances with the wavelength.

This gives the answer to the opening question. The whistle vibrates at the frequency $3.0\,\text{kHz}$, and the same frequency is kept when the sound passes into water, so the pitch is the same. The speed of sound is about $350\,\text{m/s}$ in the warm air of the hall and about $1500\,\text{m/s}$ in water, so the wavelength grows from $0.12\,\text{m}$ to $0.50\,\text{m}$. Most of the sound is, however, reflected from the water surface, so under water the whistle sounds faint.

Oscillation, waves and sound

Oscillators

$$\begin{aligned} f &= \frac{1}{T} \\ F &= -kx \\ T &= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \\ T &= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} \end{aligned}$$

where $f$ is the frequency ($\text{Hz}$), $T$ the period ($\text{s}$), $F$ the spring force ($\text{N}$), $k$ the spring constant ($\text{N/m}$), $x$ the displacement from the equilibrium position ($\text{m}$), $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$) and $l$ the length of the pendulum ($\text{m}$). The spring force holds when the spring is not stretched too far. The period of the pendulum holds for small swings, and it does not depend on the mass. The elastic potential energy of a spring is $E_\text{p} = \tfrac12 kx^2$, and without friction the energy of the oscillation $\tfrac12 kA^2$ is conserved.

Waves

$$\begin{aligned} v &= f\lambda \\ \Delta s &= n\lambda \\ \Delta s &= \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\lambda \end{aligned}$$

where $v$ is the speed of the wave ($\text{m/s}$), $\lambda$ the wavelength ($\text{m}$) and $\Delta s$ the path difference from two sources oscillating in phase ($\text{m}$), $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ The first condition gives constructive and the second destructive interference. Diffraction is strong when the width of the gap or obstacle is about the wavelength or smaller. In reflection the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, when the angles are measured from the normal to the surface.

In a standing wave the distance between neighbouring nodes is $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$. A string and an open tube have the natural frequencies $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{2L}$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, and a tube closed at one end $f_n = n \cdot \tfrac{v}{4L}$, $n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$, where $L$ is the length of the string or the tube.

Sound

$$\begin{aligned} f_\text{h} &= |f_1 - f_2| \\ f &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v}{v \mp v_\text{s}} \\ f &= f_0 \cdot \frac{v \pm v_\text{h}}{v} \\ I &= \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} \\ L &= 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg \frac{I}{I_0} \end{aligned}$$

where $f_\text{h}$ is the beat frequency ($\text{Hz}$), $f_0$ the frequency of the source and $f$ the heard frequency ($\text{Hz}$), $v$ the speed of sound, $v_\text{s}$ the speed of the source and $v_\text{h}$ the speed of the listener relative to the air ($\text{m/s}$). In the Doppler formulas the upper sign is chosen when they approach, and the formulas hold when only one of them moves and the speed is smaller than the speed of sound. The expression for the intensity holds for a point source of power $P$ at the distance $r$ without reflections. $L$ is the intensity level and $I_0 = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{W/m}^2$. The distance from an echo is $d = \tfrac{vt}{2}$, because the pulse travels the distance twice.

Common mistakes

  • Amplitude from one extreme position to the other. The amplitude is measured from the equilibrium position, so it is half the distance between the extreme positions.
  • Half a period. Two successive passes through the equilibrium position are only $\tfrac{T}{2}$ apart; a period is the interval between two peaks.
  • The frequency changes in the medium. The frequency stays the same when a wave passes from one medium into another; the speed and the wavelength change.
  • A snapshot and the graph of one point mixed up. Read the horizontal axis first: from an $(x, y)$ graph you read $\lambda$, from a $(t, y)$ graph $T$.
  • Decibels are added. Two equally loud independent sources raise the level by about $3\,\text{dB}$, because the intensities are added.
  • An echo without dividing by two. An echo travels the distance there and back, so $d = \tfrac{vt}{2}$.
  • The sign in the Doppler formula. Check at the end that approaching gives $f > f_0$ and moving away $f < f_0$.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: the echo sounder

An angler reads from the boat's echo sounder that the ultrasound pulse reflected from the bottom arrives back $24\,\text{ms}$ after it was sent. The speed of sound in lake water is $1480\,\text{m/s}$.A student calculated the depth: "$d = vt = 1480\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 0.024\,\text{s} = 35.5\,\text{m}$." What is wrong in the solution?

  • The frequency affects the wavelength and so the sharpness of the image, but not the speed of sound. The depth follows from the speed and the travel time: $d = \tfrac{vt}{2}$. Review in the chapter Applied chapter: ultrasound imaging and earthquake waves.

  • The pulse travels in water from the sensor to the bottom and back, so the speed is that of water, $1480\,\text{m/s}$. The mistake is that the travel time includes both the way down and the way back. Review in the chapter Applied chapter: ultrasound imaging and earthquake waves.

  • Correct. The travel time is there and back, so $d = \tfrac{vt}{2} = \tfrac{1480\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.024\,\text{s}}{2} \approx 18\,\text{m}$.

Read the graph: one point of a rope

The graph shows the displacement of one point of a wave travelling along a rope as a function of time. The wave travels along the rope at $3.0\,\text{m/s}$.Which statement is correct?

  • Correct. The curve rises through the axis every $0.40\,\text{s}$, so $T = 0.40\,\text{s}$ and $f = \tfrac{1}{T} = 2.5\,\text{Hz}$. The wavelength is $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f} = \tfrac{3.0\,\text{m/s}}{2.5\,\text{Hz}} = 1.2\,\text{m}$.

  • Successive crossings of the axis are half a period apart, because the point moves in opposite directions at them. The period is the interval between two upward crossings, $0.40\,\text{s}$. Review in the chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude.

  • The horizontal axis shows time, so the interval $0.40\,\text{s}$ between the peaks is the period, not the wavelength. The wavelength follows from the speed: $\lambda = vT = 1.2\,\text{m}$. Review in the chapter How waves form and travel.

Justified claim: music from round the corner

From the neighbours' garden party mainly the thump of the bass drum can be heard round the corner of the house, although the music also has high sounds. The speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.Which explanation is correct?

  • In air the speed of sound practically does not depend on the frequency. The difference comes from the wavelength: the wavelength of a low sound is metres, and it bends round the corner. Review in the chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap.

  • Loudness does not explain why it is the low sounds that go round the corner. High sounds travel almost straight, but the wavelength of a $60\,\text{Hz}$ sound, about $5.7\,\text{m}$, is on the scale of buildings, so it bends. Review in the chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap.

  • Correct. For example, the wavelength of a $60\,\text{Hz}$ sound is $\tfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{60\,\text{Hz}} \approx 5.7\,\text{m}$, but that of an $8\,\text{kHz}$ sound only about $4\,\text{cm}$. Diffraction is strong when the obstacle or opening is of the order of the wavelength or smaller. Review if needed in the chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap.

Justified claim: two machines

On a building site one machine gives a level of $88\,\text{dB}$ at a worker's ear, and the permitted daily exposure time is then $4\,\text{h}$. A second identical machine is started next to it, at the same distance from the worker.Which statement is correct?

  • Correct. Twice the intensity raises the level by about $3\,\text{dB}$, and each rise of $3\,\text{dB}$ halves the permitted time: $4\,\text{h} : 2 = 2\,\text{h}$. Review if needed in the chapter Noise, acoustics and sound reproduction.

  • A change of about $6\,\text{dB}$ happens when the distance from a point source is halved. The second machine doubles the intensity, and the level rises by $3\,\text{dB}$, so the permitted time is $2\,\text{h}$. Review in the chapter Noise, acoustics and sound reproduction.

  • Decibels are not added. Two identical machines double the intensity, which raises the level by only about $3\,\text{dB}$, to $91\,\text{dB}$. Review in the chapter Intensity and the decibel scale.

Car suspension

The body of a car ($1200\,\text{kg}$) rests on four springs. When a $90\,\text{kg}$ driver gets into the car, the body sinks $8.0\,\text{mm}$ lower. For a test the shock absorbers are removed, and the body is pushed down once. Assume that the force of the springs is harmonic and that the tyres do not give.

At what frequency does the body oscillate with the driver on board?

Hz
Hint 1: a guiding question

Where do you get the combined spring constant of the springs? What extra force pushed the body down by $8.0\,\text{mm}$?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The driver's weight $mg$ compresses the springs further, so $k = \tfrac{mg}{x}$. The oscillating mass is the body and the driver together.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$f = \tfrac{1}{T}$, where $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m_\text{body} + m}{k}}$.

Show the solution

Given

mass of the body $m_\text{body} = 1200\,\text{kg}$, mass of the driver $m = 90\,\text{kg}$, extra compression $x = 8.0\,\text{mm}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the frequency $f$ of the oscillation of the body in $\text{Hz}$

Principle

The four springs carry the body together like one spring with spring constant $k$. When the body is at rest again, the driver's weight is the extra force on the springs, and in a harmonic spring the extra force is proportional to the extra compression (chapter Spring force and spring energy). When the body is pushed down and released, the weight and the harmonic force of the springs act on the body and the driver. The weight only shifts the equilibrium position, so the body and the driver oscillate with the period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ (chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum).

Formula

$mg = kx$, so $k = \dfrac{mg}{x}$, and

$f$$= \dfrac{1}{T}$
$= \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m_\text{body} + m}}$

Conversions

$8.0\,\text{mm}$$= 8.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
$= 0.0080\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{90\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}}{0.0080\,\text{m}}$
$= 110\,363\,\text{N/m}$

so

$f$$= \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{110\,363\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}}{1290\,\text{kg}}}$
$= \dfrac{1}{2\pi} \cdot 9.249\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
$= 1.472\,\text{Hz}$

Answer

$f \approx 1.5\,\text{Hz}$

Two significant figures like the compression $8.0\,\text{mm}$.

Reasonableness

The period is

$T$$= \tfrac{1}{f}$
$\approx 0.68\,\text{s}$

: the body bobs up and down a little more than once a second, which matches the way a car rocks after a pothole. If the whole $1290\,\text{kg}$ pressed on the springs, the compression would be

$\tfrac{1290 \cdot 9.81\,\text{N}}{110\,363\,\text{N/m}}$$\approx 0.11\,\text{m}$

which is an ordinary spring travel for a car.

Waves from a motorboat

Waves made by a motorboat reach a buoy anchored next to a jetty. The buoy rises and falls so that $8$ full oscillations take $14.4\,\text{s}$. From the jetty the distance between successive wave crests is estimated to be $3.1\,\text{m}$.

At what speed do the waves travel?

m/s
Hint 1: a guiding question

What does the oscillation of the buoy tell you about the wave: the period or the wavelength?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The buoy oscillates in step with the wave, so the period of the wave is $T = \tfrac{14.4\,\text{s}}{8}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

A wave travels one wavelength in one period: $v = \tfrac{\lambda}{T}$.

Show the solution

Given

number of oscillations $n = 8$, their duration $t_n = 14.4\,\text{s}$, distance between crests, that is the wavelength, $\lambda = 3.1\,\text{m}$

Find

the speed $v$ of the waves in $\text{m/s}$

Principle

The buoy is a point of the medium that oscillates in step with the wave, so its period is the period of the wave (chapter Describing oscillation: period, frequency and amplitude). The period is found most accurately from the duration of several oscillations. In one period the wave travels one wavelength, so the wave equation is used (chapter How waves form and travel).

Formula

$T$$= \dfrac{t_n}{n}$
$v$$= \dfrac{\lambda}{T}$
$= \dfrac{n\lambda}{t_n}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$T$$= \dfrac{14.4\,\text{s}}{8}$
$= 1.8\,\text{s}$

so

$v$$= \dfrac{3.1\,\text{m}}{1.8\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.722\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v \approx 1.7\,\text{m/s}$

Two significant figures like the wavelength $3.1\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

The wave travels about $6\,\text{km/h}$, a brisk walking pace, which suits water waves a few metres long. The frequency is

$f$$= \tfrac{1}{1.8\,\text{s}}$
$\approx 0.56\,\text{Hz}$

and

$f\lambda$$= 0.556\,\text{Hz} \cdot 3.1\,\text{m}$
$\approx 1.7\,\text{m/s}$

the same result.

A train passes the platform

A passenger standing on a platform hears the horn of a passing train at the frequency $562\,\text{Hz}$ while the train approaches and at the frequency $478\,\text{Hz}$ after the train has passed. The train runs at a constant speed, the air is still, and the speed of sound is $340\,\text{m/s}$.

What is the speed of the train?

m/s
Hint 1: a guiding question

Both heard frequencies involve the same unknown frequency $f_0$ of the horn. How can you remove it from the equations?

Hint 2: what is the connection

While approaching $f_1 = f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v}{v - v_\text{s}}$ and while moving away $f_2 = f_0 \cdot \tfrac{v}{v + v_\text{s}}$. Divide one equation by the other.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$\tfrac{f_1}{f_2} = \tfrac{v + v_\text{s}}{v - v_\text{s}}$, which gives $v_\text{s} = v \cdot \tfrac{f_1 - f_2}{f_1 + f_2}$.

Show the solution

Given

frequency heard while approaching $f_1 = 562\,\text{Hz}$, heard while moving away $f_2 = 478\,\text{Hz}$, speed of sound $v = 340\,\text{m/s}$; the listener is at rest

Find

the speed of the train $v_\text{s}$ in $\text{m/s}$

Principle

The source moves and the listener is at rest, so in front of the source the wavelength becomes shorter and behind it longer: the Doppler formula for a moving source is used, with the minus sign while approaching and the plus sign while moving away (chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect). The horn's own frequency $f_0$ is not known, but it is the same in both, so it cancels when one equation is divided by the other.

Formula

$f_1$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v}{v - v_\text{s}}$
$f_2$$= f_0 \cdot \dfrac{v}{v + v_\text{s}}$

so

$\dfrac{f_1}{f_2}$$= \dfrac{v + v_\text{s}}{v - v_\text{s}}$

which gives

$f_1 v - f_1 v_\text{s}$$= f_2 v + f_2 v_\text{s}$
$v_\text{s}$$= v \cdot \dfrac{f_1 - f_2}{f_1 + f_2}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed; finally the speed is compared in the unit $\text{km/h}$.

Substitution

$v_\text{s}$$= 340\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \dfrac{562\,\text{Hz} - 478\,\text{Hz}}{562\,\text{Hz} + 478\,\text{Hz}}$
$= 340\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \dfrac{84\,\text{Hz}}{1040\,\text{Hz}}$
$= 27.46\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v_\text{s} \approx 27\,\text{m/s}$

Two significant figures, because the result depends on the difference of the frequencies $84\,\text{Hz}$.

Reasonableness

$27.46\,\text{m/s}$$= 27.46 \cdot \tfrac{3600\,\text{s/h}}{1000\,\text{m/km}}$
$\approx 99\,\text{km/h}$

an ordinary speed for a train near a station. The horn's own frequency is

When the frequency rose and the beats became slower, the frequency difference became smaller, so the frequency of the string was below $110.0\,\text{Hz}$.

If the string had been at $111.5\,\text{Hz}$, tightening would have increased the difference and the beats would have become faster. The frequency difference $1.5\,\text{Hz}$ is below about $10\,\text{Hz}$, so the beats really can be heard.

d)

Find

why a string lightly touched at its midpoint sounds an octave higher

Principle

The natural frequencies of a string fixed at both ends are $f_n = n f_1$, and at the natural frequency $f_n$ the string has $n$ antinodes. The touch stops the motion of the string at that point, so a node forms there (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies).

Model answer

A freely sounding string vibrates at the fundamental frequency and at the overtones at the same time. At the fundamental frequency the midpoint is an antinode, so the finger damps the fundamental and all the odd natural frequencies, which have an antinode at the midpoint. What remains are the natural frequencies $n = 2, 4, 6, \ldots$, which have a node at the midpoint, because the finger does not stop their motion. The lowest of them is $f_2 = 2f_1 = 220\,\text{Hz}$: the string vibrates in two segments, with an antinode in each half, and twice the frequency sounds an octave higher. The finger can be lifted, and the string still sounds the same way, because the standing wave goes on.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the length of the string is taken as the wavelength, so that $v = f_1 L \approx 71\,\text{m/s}$; at the fundamental frequency $L = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}$.
  • In part a the wavelength in the string $1.30\,\text{m}$ is given as the wavelength in air: the frequency stays the same, the wavelength does not.
  • In part b the speed is assumed to change, or the length is calculated as directly proportional to the frequency.
  • In part c the beat frequency is taken as $6\,\text{Hz}$, or both alternatives are given without justification.

Also accepted

  • In part b with a ratio: $L' = L \cdot \dfrac{f_1}{f'} = 648\,\text{mm} \cdot \dfrac{110.0}{123.5} \approx 577\,\text{mm}$, because the speed is the same.

Principle

The decibel scale is logarithmic, so a decibel reading cannot be divided like an intensity. At the houses the distance is $20$ times as large, so the intensity is $\tfrac{1}{400}$ and the level is $106\,\text{dB} - 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg 400 \approx 106\,\text{dB} - 26\,\text{dB} = 80\,\text{dB}$, the level of a busy street. Principle

The observation is true, but the reasoning is wrong. Low sounds are heard behind the fence because they bend over the fence. For example, the wavelength of a $60\,\text{Hz}$ bass sound is $\tfrac{340\,\text{m/s}}{60\,\text{Hz}} \approx 5.7\,\text{m}$, much longer than the fence, so the wave bends behind the fence almost without weakening. The wavelength of a high $4\,\text{kHz}$ sound is only about $9\,\text{cm}$, so it is left with a sound shadow behind the $2\,\text{m}$ fence. The bass sounds are not necessarily louder: even if the low and high sounds were equally loud when they leave, the fence weakens the high ones. The rule of thumb is an estimate, not a guarantee: different people's hearing tolerates noise differently.

Model answer

a)

Given

unstretched length of the cord $L_0 = 18.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is neglected

Find

the speed $v$ when the cord starts to stretch

Principle

Before the cord becomes tight, only the weight acts on the jumper, so mechanical energy is conserved (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The zero level is chosen at the point where the cord starts to stretch.

Formula

$mgL_0 = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so the mass cancels and $v = \sqrt{2gL_0}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$v$$= \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 18.0\,\text{m}}$
$= \sqrt{353.2\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
$= 18.79\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v \approx 18.8\,\text{m/s}$

That is about $68\,\text{km/h}$, three significant figures like the unstretched length $18.0\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

In free fall $18\,\text{m}$ takes

$\sqrt{\tfrac{2 \cdot 18.0\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}}$$\approx 1.9\,\text{s}$

and the speed is $gt \approx 18.8\,\text{m/s}$, the same result. In reality air resistance makes the speed a little smaller.

b)

Given

$m = 72.0\,\text{kg}$, $L_0 = 18.0\,\text{m}$, spring constant $k = 120\,\text{N/m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the depth of the lowest point below the bridge $L_0 + x$, where $x$ is the greatest extension of the cord

Principle

The weight and the force of the cord, which is harmonic like a spring force, act on the jumper. Both are conservative, so mechanical energy is conserved during the whole fall (chapters Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle and Spring force and spring energy). At the lowest point the speed is zero, so the change in the gravitational potential energy over the whole distance $L_0 + x$ has turned into the elastic potential energy of the cord. The zero level is chosen at the lowest point.

Formula

$mg(L_0 + x) = \tfrac12 kx^2$, that is $\tfrac12 kx^2 - mgx - mgL_0 = 0$, whose positive solution is

$x$$= \dfrac{mg + \sqrt{(mg)^2 + 2kmgL_0}}{k}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$mg$$= 72.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$= 706.32\,\text{N}$
$(mg)^2$$= (706.32\,\text{N})^2$
$\approx 498\,888\,\text{N}^2$
$2kmgL_0$$= 2 \cdot 120\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot 706.32\,\text{N} \cdot 18.0\,\text{m}$
$\approx 3\,051\,302\,\text{N}^2$

so the square root is

$\sqrt{3\,550\,190\,\text{N}^2}$$\approx 1884.2\,\text{N}$
$x$$= \dfrac{706.32\,\text{N} + 1884.2\,\text{N}}{120\,\text{N/m}}$
$= 21.59\,\text{m}$

and the depth $18.0\,\text{m} + 21.59\,\text{m} = 39.59\,\text{m}$

Answer

The jumper falls about $39.6\,\text{m}$ below the bridge, three significant figures like the given data.

Reasonableness

The energy balance checks:

$mg(L_0 + x)$$= 706.32\,\text{N} \cdot 39.59\,\text{m}$
$\approx 27.96\,\text{kJ}$
$\tfrac12 kx^2$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 120\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot (21.59\,\text{m})^2$
$\approx 27.96\,\text{kJ}$

The cord stretches more than its unstretched length, which is usual for a bungee cord; the bridge must be clearly more than $40\,\text{m}$ high.

c)

Given

extension $x = 21.59\,\text{m}$, $k = 120\,\text{N/m}$, $m = 72.0\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the acceleration $a$ at the lowest point with its direction

Principle

At the lowest point the weight acts on the jumper downwards and the force of the cord $kx$ upwards. By Newton's second law the net force gives the acceleration (chapter Newton's second law). Upwards is chosen as the positive direction.

Formula

$kx - mg = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{kx}{m} - g$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$kx$$= 120\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot 21.59\,\text{m}$
$= 2590.5\,\text{N}$

so

$a$$= \dfrac{2590.5\,\text{N}}{72.0\,\text{kg}} - 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$= 35.98\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} - 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$= 26.17\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a \approx 26.2\,\text{m/s}^2$

Upwards, three significant figures like the given data.

Reasonableness

The acceleration is about $2.7g$, and the force of the cord is $2590\,\text{N} : 706\,\text{N} \approx 3.7$ times the weight. That is a strong but brief load, which the harness spreads over the ankles and legs. The acceleration points upwards although the jumper is momentarily at rest, as it should at an extreme position.

d)

Given

$m = 72.0\,\text{kg}$, $k = 120\,\text{N/m}$

Find

the period $T$ of small oscillations

Principle

The weight and the harmonic force of the cord act on the hanging jumper. The weight only shifts the equilibrium position to the point where the cord is stretched by $x_0 = \tfrac{mg}{k}$, and the displacement from there follows the law of the mass–spring oscillator (chapter The mass–spring oscillator and the pendulum).

Formula

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{72.0\,\text{kg}}{120\,\text{N/m}}}$
$= 2\pi \cdot 0.7746\,\text{s}$
$= 4.867\,\text{s}$

Answer

$T \approx 4.87\,\text{s}$

Three significant figures like the mass $72.0\,\text{kg}$. The result is an estimate, because the force of the rubber cord is not exactly harmonic and air resistance damps the oscillation.

Reasonableness

At the equilibrium position the cord is stretched by $\tfrac{706.32\,\text{N}}{120\,\text{N/m}} \approx 5.9\,\text{m}$. The period of a vertical spring is also

$2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{x_0}{g}}$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{5.886\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}}$
$\approx 4.87\,\text{s}$

the same result. A slow bobbing of about five seconds suits a long, slack cord.

Common mistakes

  • In part b only $mgL_0$ is taken as the gravitational potential energy, so that the extension comes out as $\sqrt{\tfrac{2mgL_0}{k}} \approx 14.6\,\text{m}$; the weight also does work while the cord stretches.
  • In part b the extension $21.6\,\text{m}$ is given as the answer instead of the depth below the bridge.
  • In part c the acceleration is calculated as $\tfrac{kx}{m} \approx 36\,\text{m/s}^2$, forgetting the weight, or the acceleration is said to be zero because the speed is zero.
  • In part d the pendulum formula is used with the length of the cord.

Also accepted

  • In part b the zero level at the bridge, when the signs of the potential energy are consistent.
  • In part c the answer in the form $a \approx 2.7g$ upwards.

Given

Principle

The P and S waves leave at the same moment and travel the same distance at constant speeds, presumably along the same route. $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{d}{v_\text{S}} - \dfrac{d}{v_\text{P}}$
$= d \cdot \dfrac{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}$

so

$d$$= \dfrac{v_\text{P} v_\text{S}}{v_\text{P} - v_\text{S}}\,\Delta t$

The speeds are in the unit $\text{km/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the distance comes out directly in kilometres.

4. Explanation task

In a laboratory there is a mass oscillating on a spring and a mass swinging on a string. In each, the mass is replaced by one twice as heavy, and the swings are kept small. The period of one grows, the period of the other does not change. Explain with forces and Newton's second law whose period grows and why.

Model answer

The period of the mass–spring oscillator grows, that of the pendulum does not. The spring force $F = -kx$ depends only on the spring and the displacement, not on the mass of the weight. When the mass doubles, the same force gives, by Newton's second law $a = \tfrac{F}{m}$, only half the acceleration at the same point. The mass moves more slowly, and the period grows: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ grows by the factor $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.4$. In a pendulum the mass is pulled back by the component of the weight along the path, $mg\sin\alpha$. It doubles with the mass, so the acceleration $g\sin\alpha$ stays the same, as in free fall. That is why the period of the pendulum $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$ does not depend on the mass. The formula $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$ holds for small swings, for which the restoring force of the pendulum is harmonic.
