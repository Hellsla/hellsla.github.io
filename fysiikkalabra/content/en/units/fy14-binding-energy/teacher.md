## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–6 min, Think about it.** The hot-tea hook and the review question on helium-4. Ask: "Has anyone ever weighed energy?"
- **6–11 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, each with a short written reason.
- **11–33 min, Investigate.** Individually: the mass difference of helium-4 from the AME2020 table. Then pairs on one device: the Binding Energy Explorer (one operates, one writes down $B/A$ values and the two Q-values), and the pair conclusion with the word bank.
- **33–48 min, Theory summary.** Read the formula and the key idea, then the curve and *Fission and fusion*; the vocabulary match; back to the predictions.
- **48–62 min, Apply.** The worked example (binding energy of helium-4) step by step, then the D–T Q-value individually.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks and the explanation task individually; the summary; the French corner in pairs.

## Organisation and preparation
- A calculator that shows at least seven digits: the mass defect is a small difference of large numbers. Remind students not to round the masses before subtracting.
- Pairs share one device for the explorer. Ask them to write the $B/A$ values of $^{2}\text{H}$, $^{4}\text{He}$, $^{56}\text{Fe}$ and $^{235}\text{U}$ on paper: they are needed in the conclusion and in the checks.
- Fast pairs: the optional error-spotting task (proton mass against atomic mass) and the biography of Lise Meitner; then ask them to calculate the energy from $1\ \text{kg}$ of D–T fuel ($3.4 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J}$) and compare it with $1\ \text{kg}$ of coal ($3.3 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{J}$).
- Running out of time: do the worked example together on the board and give the D–T task as homework; it is repeated in the next chapter, so it can also be checked there.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade: the conclusion places the peak near iron or nickel, describes fusion and fission as moving towards the peak and uses at least one number from the explorer; the explanation task says that fusing iron would lower $B/A$ and therefore need energy.
- Calculation: the mass defect from atomic masses with the right sign and the conversion with $931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$ (or with $c^2$ and joules, correctly converted).
- Language: correct use of at least three key words (mass defect, binding energy, binding energy per nucleon, fusion, fission, Q-value) and one sentence starter.

## Common difficulties
- Thinking that a large binding energy is a store of energy that comes out. The misconception section and the first check address this; ask "Which needs more energy to take apart?"
- Mixing nuclear and atomic masses (the error-spotting task): use atomic masses throughout, with $m_\text{H}$ for the protons.
- Rounding too early: $0.03\ \text{u}$ instead of $0.030377\ \text{u}$ changes the answer by $1\ \%$; rounding the masses to three decimals ruins it.
- The explorer shows $Q = 173\ \text{MeV}$ for one particular split of uranium-235; the "about $200\ \text{MeV}$" in the text includes the later decays of the fragments and the other energy released. Both numbers are right for what they describe.

## Extension ideas
- Calculate $Q$ for $^{2}\text{H} + {}^{3}\text{He} \to {}^{4}\text{He} + {}^{1}\text{H}$ with the AME2020 atomic masses ($2.014102\ \text{u}$, $3.016029\ \text{u}$, $4.002603\ \text{u}$, $1.007825\ \text{u}$): $Q \approx 18.4\ \text{MeV}$, or $3.7\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon, slightly more than D–T's $3.5\ \text{MeV}$. Then discuss why D–T is still the choice for reactors (the next chapter answers this: the Coulomb barrier of helium-3, with $Z = 2$, is higher, and helium-3 is rare on Earth).
- Estimate how much mass the Sun loses every second from its power output (next unit, *The puzzle of the Sun's energy*).
- Discuss the history: why did it take until the end of 1938 to understand fission, when the binding energy curve was already known from Aston's measurements in the 1920s?
