Energy, power and efficiency

Why does charging an electric car from a household socket take all night, even though the battery holds less energy than ten litres of petrol?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve energy, power and efficiency problems, substituting the units
  • draw and interpret energy flow diagrams, energy chains and graphs of consumption data
  • evaluate energy production methods and claims with reasons

Prior knowledge

  • Energy is conserved: it is transformed and transferred but does not disappearchapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy
  • Power is energy per unit time, and $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$chapter Power
  • Efficiency is the fraction of the energy taken in that is usefulchapter Efficiency
  • The energy released from a fuel is $E = Hm$chapter Fuels and heat of combustion
  • The efficiencies of the stages of a power plant are multipliedchapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants

1Think about it

The battery of an electric car holds $40\,\text{kWh}$ of energy. Ten litres of petrol weigh about $7.5\,\text{kg}$, and the heat of combustion of petrol is about $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$. At home the car is charged from an ordinary socket at a power of $2.3\,\text{kW}$, at a fast charger at a power of $100\,\text{kW}$. In the matriculation exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the formulas are not.

Which holds more energy, a full battery or ten litres of petrol, and why does charging at home still take all night?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting level 1: the sledge stops

A sledge slides down a hill and stops on level snow. What has finally happened to the sledge's potential energy?

  • Energy is not used up and does not disappear. The kinetic energy has been transformed by friction into thermal energy, which spreads into the snow and the air. Review the chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy.

  • Correct. Friction and air resistance transform mechanical energy into thermal energy, and the total energy is conserved. Review if needed in the chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy.

  • The sledge is now lower than at the start, so its potential energy is smaller. The difference has become thermal energy by way of the motion. Review the chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy.

Starting level 2: power or energy

A hair dryer has a power of $1800\,\text{W}$, and it is on for $10\,\text{min}$ a day. A fridge has an average power of $40\,\text{W}$, and it is plugged in all day and night.Which uses more electrical energy in a day?

  • Correct. The fridge uses about $0.96\,\text{kWh}$ and the dryer $0.30\,\text{kWh}$. A small power for a long time can transform more energy than a large power for a moment. Review if needed in the chapter Power.

  • Power only tells how fast energy is transformed. Energy is power multiplied by time: the dryer $1.8\,\text{kW} \cdot \tfrac{1}{6}\,\text{h} = 0.30\,\text{kWh}$, the fridge $0.040\,\text{kW} \cdot 24\,\text{h} \approx 0.96\,\text{kWh}$. Review the chapter Power.

  • The same grid does not mean the same energy. The energy depends on the power of the device and how long it is used, $E = P\,t$, and these are very different for these devices. Review the chapter Power.

Starting level 3: the kilowatt-hour

How many joules are there in one kilowatt-hour?

  • Correct. $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3\,600\,000\,\text{J} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$. Review if needed in the chapter Power.

  • An hour has $60$ minutes, but a watt is a joule per second. Substitute the hour in seconds: $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$. Review the chapter Power.

  • The prefix kilo means a thousand, but a kilowatt-hour also contains an hour. A watt is a joule per second, so the time must be converted to seconds: $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$. Review the chapter Power.

  • An hour is $3600\,\text{s}$, but a kilowatt is $1000\,\text{W}$. Substitute both: $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$. Review the chapter Power.

Starting level 4: three stages

Energy passes through three successive stages, and the efficiency of each stage is $90\,\%$. What is the efficiency of the whole chain?

  • Each stage takes the useful energy of the previous stage and wastes part of that too, so a chain is always weaker than a single stage. Review the chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants.

  • Correct. $0.90 \cdot 0.90 \cdot 0.90 = 0.729 \approx 73\,\%$. Review if needed in the chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants.

  • The second stage wastes $10\,\%$ of what it receives, not of the original energy. The efficiencies are multiplied: $0.90 \cdot 0.90 \cdot 0.90 \approx 0.73$. Review the chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants.

Starting level 5: wood and petrol

The heat of combustion of air-dried firewood is $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$ and that of petrol $43\,\text{MJ/kg}$. Which releases more energy when it burns: $2.0\,\text{kg}$ of wood or $1.0\,\text{kg}$ of petrol?

  • The mass is double, but the heat of combustion is less than half. The energy released is $E = Hm$: wood $30\,\text{MJ}$, petrol $43\,\text{MJ}$. Review the chapter Fuels and heat of combustion.

  • Burning completely does not make the energies equal: the energy depends on both the heat of combustion and the mass, $E = Hm$. Review the chapter Fuels and heat of combustion.

  • Correct. The wood releases $15\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{kg} = 30\,\text{MJ}$, the petrol $43\,\text{MJ}$. Review if needed in the chapter Fuels and heat of combustion.

Starting level 6: a wind turbine's year

A wind turbine has a rated power of $5.0\,\text{MW}$, and a year has $8760\,\text{h}$. Which claim about the turbine's annual production is correct?

  • This would be the production if the turbine ran at its rated power all year. The wind varies, so the average power is smaller than the rated power. Review the chapter Ways of producing energy and how they compare.

  • Correct. The rated power is the maximum power. The year's energy is the average power multiplied by $8760\,\text{h}$, and the average power is much smaller than the rated power. Review if needed in the chapter Ways of producing energy and how they compare.

  • The rated power is the maximum power at which the turbine operates; in a strong wind the power is limited. So the year's energy cannot exceed $5.0\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h}$. Review the chapter Ways of producing energy and how they compare.

4Theory

Key idea

All the problems of this lesson start from the same principle: energy is conserved. It is transformed from one form to another and transferred from one place to another, but it is neither created nor destroyed. When a device “consumes” energy, it transforms it, and most of it finally ends up as heat in the surroundings.

Three questions branch off from the conservation law, and the energy problems of the exam come back to them. How fast is energy transformed? Power tells that. How large a share is useful? Efficiency tells that. Where does the energy come from? From the heat of combustion of a fuel, from the work done by a force, or from flowing water, the wind and the sun.

In the exam a solution starts with recognising which branch the problem is about and writing its law before the numbers. Energy and power are different quantities: the kilowatt tells the rate, the kilowatt-hour the amount.

This also answers the opening question. Ten litres of petrol contain about $7.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 43\,\text{MJ/kg} \approx 320\,\text{MJ} \approx 90\,\text{kWh}$ of energy, more than a $40\,\text{kWh}$ battery. Charging still takes a long time, because the power of the socket is small: $40\,\text{kWh} : 2.3\,\text{kW} \approx 17\,\text{h}$.

Energy, power and efficiency

Conservation and power

In an isolated system, which no energy enters or leaves, the total energy stays the same:

$$E_\text{initial} = E_\text{final}$$

where each side is the sum of all forms of energy, thermal energy included. Power is the energy transformed or transferred per unit time:

$$P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}$$

where $P$ is the power (W), $\Delta E$ the energy (J) and $\Delta t$ the time (s). For uneven use the formula gives the average power; at constant power $E = P\,t$. The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, and it is converted by substitution:

$$1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$$

Efficiency and the energy chain

$$\eta = \frac{E_\text{a}}{E_\text{o}} = \frac{P_\text{a}}{P_\text{o}}$$

where $\eta$ is the efficiency, $E_\text{a}$ and $P_\text{a}$ the useful energy and power given, and $E_\text{o}$ and $P_\text{o}$ the energy and power taken in. The ratio of the powers can be used when the device runs steadily. Energy is conserved, so $E_\text{o} = E_\text{a} + E_\text{h}$, where $E_\text{h}$ is the losses. The efficiency is at most $100\,\%$; it depends on what is defined as useful.

In successive stages the useful power of each stage is the power taken in by the next, so the efficiencies are multiplied:

$$\eta = \eta_1\,\eta_2\,\eta_3$$

In the example plant in the figure $\eta = 0.90 \cdot 0.45 \cdot 0.98 \approx 0.40$: of the fuel power of $500\,\text{MW}$, $198\,\text{MW}$ becomes electricity. The widths of the bands are to scale, and the outgoing bands are together as wide as the incoming one.

Sources of energy

The energy released from a fuel and the work done by a force are

$$\begin{aligned} E &= Hm \\ W &= Fs \end{aligned}$$

where $H$ is the heat of combustion ($\text{MJ/kg}$) and $m$ the mass burnt (kg); $E = Hm$ holds when the fuel burns completely and $H$ is the heat of combustion for its actual moisture content. In the work, $F$ is a constant force in the direction of motion (N) and $s$ the distance (m). In steady lifting $W = mgh$, where $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $h$ is the lifting height.

Common mistakes

  • Power and energy mixed up. “The power of the panel is $1.2\,\text{kWh}$” is a mistake: power is in kilowatts, energy in kilowatt-hours. A high-power device does not use much energy if it is on only for a moment.
  • Efficiencies added or subtracted. In a chain they are multiplied, so the efficiency of the whole chain is smaller than that of the weakest stage.
  • The wrong denominator. The loss percentage and the efficiency are always calculated from the energy taken in, not from the energy given.
  • Minutes as hours. $30\,\text{min} = 30 \cdot \tfrac{1}{60}\,\text{h} = 0.50\,\text{h}$; the product of power and time is in kilowatt-hours only when the time is in hours.
  • Losses “disappear”. Losses are energy that has been transformed into a useless form, usually heat.
  • The overall efficiency of combined heat and power compared with an electrical efficiency. Electricity and district heat are not equally valuable, so the numbers tell different things.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: the radiator

A student calculated how much energy a $2.0\,\text{kW}$ electric radiator uses in $30\,\text{min}$: “$E = P\,t = 2.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 30\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{kWh}$.” What is wrong with the solution?

  • Correct. The product of power and time is in kilowatt-hours only when the time is in hours: $30\,\text{min} = 0.50\,\text{h}$, so $E = 2.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 0.50\,\text{h} = 1.0\,\text{kWh}$.

  • Power is energy divided by time, $P = \Delta E : \Delta t$, so energy is power multiplied by time. The formula is correct; the mistake is in the unit: the minutes must be converted to hours.

  • The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, and energy is what is asked here. The mistake is in the time: $30\,\text{min} = 0.50\,\text{h}$, so the correct energy is $1.0\,\text{kWh}$.

Read the energy flow diagram

The energy flow diagram in the figure is drawn to scale on squared paper. What is the efficiency of the electric motor?

  • Correct. The useful arrow is $2$ squares wide and the incoming arrow $8$ squares, so $\eta = 2 : 8 = 0.25$. The kinetic energy is therefore $0.25 \cdot 240\,\text{J} = 60\,\text{J}$.

  • This is the fraction lost, $6 : 8$. The efficiency is the fraction of the incoming arrow that is useful, that is, kinetic energy.

  • You compared the useful arrow with the loss arrow, $2 : 6$. The denominator of the efficiency is the energy taken in, that is, the width of the incoming arrow, $8$ squares.

  • You read the useful energy ($60\,\text{J}$) as a percentage. The efficiency is a ratio: $60\,\text{J} : 240\,\text{J} = 0.25$.

A reasoned claim: the heat pump

An air-source heat pump takes an electrical power of $1.0\,\text{kW}$ from the grid and gives a heat power of $3.5\,\text{kW}$ to the room. Which claim is justified?

  • Energy is not created out of nothing. The pump transfers heat from the outdoor air into the room, about $2.5\,\text{kW}$, and the electricity brings the remaining $1.0\,\text{kW}$. That is why the number $3.5$ is not called an efficiency but the coefficient of performance.

  • Correct. The room receives the $1.0\,\text{kW}$ of electricity and the $2.5\,\text{kW}$ transferred from the outdoor air, $3.5\,\text{kW}$ in total. Energy is conserved when all the energies entering the pump are counted.

  • The output power cannot be greater than all the power taken in. Besides electricity the pump takes heat from the outdoor air, so the electrical power is not its whole input power.

Power and energy in charging

The $0.60\,\text{kWh}$ battery of an electric bicycle is charged from empty to full, once with an ordinary charger at a power of $120\,\text{W}$ and once with a fast charger at a power of $600\,\text{W}$. The efficiency is the same in both charges.Which claim is correct?

  • Correct. The energy is the same, and the time is $t = E : P$: with the ordinary charger $600\,\text{Wh} : 120\,\text{W} = 5.0\,\text{h}$, with the fast charger $1.0\,\text{h}$, if the power stays the same during the whole charge. In reality charging slows down as the battery fills up.

  • A longer time at a small power and a short time at a large power give the same energy, $E = P\,t$. Both times the battery holds the same $0.60\,\text{kWh}$.

  • A full battery holds the same $0.60\,\text{kWh}$ whatever the charging power. Power only tells how fast the energy is transferred.

From fuel to wheels

On a highway the motor of an electric car gives a mechanical power of $15\,\text{kW}$. The electricity is produced in a condensing power plant (efficiency $38\,\%$), the efficiency of transmission in the grid is $96\,\%$, that of charging and the battery $90\,\%$ and that of the motor $90\,\%$.

How large a fuel power is needed at the power plant to keep the car going? Answer in kilowatts.

kW
Hint 1: a guiding question

The useful power of each stage is the power taken in by the next stage. What is the efficiency of the whole chain?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The efficiency of the chain is the product of the efficiencies of the stages: $\eta = \eta_1\,\eta_2\,\eta_3\,\eta_4$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$P_0$$= \dfrac{P_\text{a}}{\eta}$

Show the solution

Given

power given by the motor $P_\text{a} = 15\,\text{kW}$; efficiencies $\eta_1 = 38\,\%$ (power plant), $\eta_2 = 96\,\%$ (transmission), $\eta_3 = 90\,\%$ (charging and battery) and $\eta_4 = 90\,\%$ (motor)

the fuel power $P_0$ of the power plant in kilowatts

Principle

The stages are in series: the useful power of each stage is the power taken in by the next one. So the efficiency of the whole chain is the product of the efficiencies of the stages, and the power taken in is obtained by dividing the useful power by it (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants).

Formula

$\eta$$= \eta_1\,\eta_2\,\eta_3\,\eta_4$
$= \dfrac{P_\text{a}}{P_0}$

so

$P_0$$= \dfrac{P_\text{a}}{\eta_1\,\eta_2\,\eta_3\,\eta_4}$

Conversions

Percentages to decimals: $38\,\% = 0.38$, $96\,\% = 0.96$ and $90\,\% = 0.90$. The power can stay in kilowatts, because the answer is asked in kilowatts.

Substitution

$P_0$$= \dfrac{15\,\text{kW}}{0.38 \cdot 0.96 \cdot 0.90 \cdot 0.90}$
$= \dfrac{15\,\text{kW}}{0.2955}$
$= 50.76\,\text{kW}$

Answer

$51\,\text{kW}$

Two significant figures like the power $15\,\text{kW}$ and the efficiencies.

Reasonableness

The efficiency of the chain, $0.30$, is smaller than that of the weakest stage, $0.38$, as it must be in a multiplication. About three times the motor's power is needed as fuel power, and the check $0.2955 \cdot 50.76\,\text{kW} = 15.0\,\text{kW}$ agrees.

The goods lift

A goods lift raises an $800\,\text{kg}$ load at a constant speed $24\,\text{m}$ up in $30\,\text{s}$. The efficiency of the lift's motor and machinery is $75\,\%$.

How large an electrical power does the motor take? Answer in kilowatts.

kW
Hint 1: a guiding question

The lift does work to raise the load. What is the work of lifting, and how large a power follows from it?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The work of lifting is $W = mgh$ and the useful power $P_\text{a} = W : t$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The power taken by the motor is $P_\text{o} = P_\text{a} : \eta$.

Show the solution

Given

mass $m = 800\,\text{kg}$, lifting height $h = 24\,\text{m}$, time $t = 30\,\text{s}$, efficiency $\eta = 75\,\%$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the power $P_\text{o}$ taken by the motor in kilowatts

Principle

The load rises at a constant speed, so the lifting force is equal to the weight $mg$ and the work of lifting is $mgh$ (chapter Work and energy). The useful power is the work divided by the time, and the motor takes a power of which only the share $\eta$ is useful.

Formula

$P_\text{a} = \dfrac{mgh}{t}$ and $\eta = \dfrac{P_\text{a}}{P_\text{o}}$, so $P_\text{o} = \dfrac{mgh}{\eta\,t}$

Conversions

$75\,\% = 0.75$ and finally $1\,\text{W} = 0.001\,\text{kW}$. The other quantities are in base units.

Substitution

$P_\text{o}$$= \dfrac{800\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 24\,\text{m}}{0.75 \cdot 30\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{188\,352\,\text{J}}{22.5\,\text{s}}$
$= 8371\,\text{W}$
$= 8.371\,\text{kW}$

Answer

$8.4\,\text{kW}$

Two significant figures like the height $24\,\text{m}$, the time $30\,\text{s}$ and the efficiency $75\,\%$.

Reasonableness

The useful power is $188\,352\,\text{J} : 30\,\text{s} \approx 6.3\,\text{kW}$, and the motor takes four thirds of that, about $8.4\,\text{kW}$. The power is about that of an electric sauna stove, which suits the motor of a goods lift.

Model answer

a)

Given

average power of the fridge $P_1 = 45\,\text{W}$ for the whole day, $t_1 = 24\,\text{h}$; six LED lamps, each $7.0\,\text{W}$, time $t_2 = 5.0\,\text{h}$; coffee maker $P_3 = 1.0\,\text{kW}$, time $t_3 = 15\,\text{min}$

Find

the electrical energy $E_\text{need}$ used by the devices in a day, in kilowatt-hours

Principle

Each device transforms energy at its own power, so its energy is power multiplied by the time of use (chapter Power). The power of the fridge is an average power, so it too is multiplied by the whole day. The energies are added up in the same unit.

Formula

$E = P\,t$ for each device, so $E_\text{need} = P_1 t_1 + 6\,P_2 t_2 + P_3 t_3$

Conversions

For kilowatt-hours the powers to kilowatts and the times to hours:

$45\,\text{W}$$= 45 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kW}$
$= 0.045\,\text{kW}$
$7.0\,\text{W}$$= 0.0070\,\text{kW}$
$15\,\text{min}$$= 15 \cdot \tfrac{1}{60}\,\text{h}$

$E_\text{need}$$= 0.045\,\text{kW} \cdot 24\,\text{h} + 6 \cdot 0.0070\,\text{kW} \cdot 5.0\,\text{h} + 1.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 0.25\,\text{h}$

That is, $1.54 \cdot 3.6\,\text{MJ} \approx 5.5\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures like the power of the fridge $45\,\text{W}$ and the power of the coffee maker $1.0\,\text{kW}$.

Reasonableness

The fridge uses almost two thirds of the energy, even though its power is the second smallest of the devices: it is on all the time. The large power of the coffee maker matters little, because it is on for only a quarter of an hour. One and a half kilowatt-hours a day is a usual consumption for a small summer cottage.

b)

Given

from part a $E_\text{need} = 1.54\,\text{kWh}$; efficiency of the battery $\eta_\text{batt} = 90\,\%$, of the inverter $\eta_\text{inv} = 93\,\%$; production on a cloudy day $1.5\,\text{kWh}$

Principle

Energy travels from the panels through the battery and the inverter to the devices. The stages are in series: the energy given by the battery is the energy taken in by the inverter. So the efficiency of the chain is the product of the efficiencies of the stages, and the panels must produce more than the devices use (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants).

Formula

$\eta$$= \eta_\text{batt}\,\eta_\text{inv}$
$= \dfrac{E_\text{need}}{E_\text{panels}}$

so

$E_\text{panels}$$= \dfrac{E_\text{need}}{\eta_\text{batt}\,\eta_\text{inv}}$

Conversions

$90\,\% = 0.90$ and $93\,\% = 0.93$. The energies stay in kilowatt-hours.

Substitution

$E_\text{panels}$$= \dfrac{1.54\,\text{kWh}}{0.90 \cdot 0.93}$
$= \dfrac{1.54\,\text{kWh}}{0.837}$
$= 1.84\,\text{kWh}$

On a cloudy day $0.837 \cdot 1.5\,\text{kWh} = 1.26\,\text{kWh}$ reaches the devices.

Answer

The panels must produce about $1.8\,\text{kWh}$ a day (two significant figures like the need in part a). The $1.5\,\text{kWh}$ of a cloudy day is not enough: only about $1.3\,\text{kWh}$ of it reaches the devices, so the missing part is taken from the battery if it holds a surplus from a sunny day.

Reasonableness

The efficiency of the chain, $0.84$, is smaller than that of either stage, as it must be in a multiplication, and the need grows by about a fifth. On a sunny day $0.837 \cdot 5.4\,\text{kWh} \approx 4.5\,\text{kWh}$ reaches the devices, which is enough for almost three days.

c)

Principle

Power tells how fast energy is transformed, and energy is power multiplied by time (chapter Power). The rated power of a panel is the maximum power it gives in bright sunshine.

Model answer

The kilowatt is a unit of power and the kilowatt-hour a unit of energy: $1.2\,\text{kW}$ means that the panels can produce at most $1.2\,\text{kWh}$ per hour. The day's energy is the average power multiplied by the time, and it depends on how long and how strongly the sun shines. According to the data, on a sunny day the panels produced $5.4\,\text{kWh}$, which corresponds to $5.4\,\text{kWh} : 1.2\,\text{kW} = 4.5$ hours at full power. On a cloudy day the production of $1.5\,\text{kWh}$ corresponded to only about one hour. The neighbour's number happens to be close to the cloudy day, but the reasoning is wrong.

  • The average power of the fridge is multiplied by only a few hours, because the compressor does not run all the time; the average power already takes this into account.
  • $15\,\text{min}$ is written as $0.15\,\text{h}$; correctly $15 \cdot \tfrac{1}{60}\,\text{h} = 0.25\,\text{h}$.
  • In part b the need is multiplied by the efficiencies, which gives a panel production smaller than the need.
  • The losses of the efficiencies are added up ($10\,\% + 7\,\% = 17\,\%$); here the result is close to the correct one, but the principle is wrong.

Given

Find

the fuel power $P_0$ in megawatts

Principle

The energy released from the chips is the heat of combustion multiplied by the mass burnt, because the heat of combustion is energy per kilogram (chapter Fuels and heat of combustion). The fuel power is this energy divided by the time in which it is released (chapter Power).

Formula

$E = Hm$ and $P_0 = \dfrac{E}{t}$, so $P_0 = \dfrac{Hm}{t}$

Conversions

$42\,\text{t}$$= 42 \cdot 1000\,\text{kg}$
$= 42\,000\,\text{kg}$

Substitution

$P_0$$= \dfrac{9.5 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}/\text{kg} \cdot 42\,000\,\text{kg}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{3.99 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{J}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.108 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{W}$
$= 110.8\,\text{MW}$

$P_0 \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{MW}$

Two significant figures like the heat of combustion $9.5\,\text{MJ/kg}$ and the mass $42\,\text{t}$. The later parts use the unrounded value $110.8\,\text{MW}$.

The fuel power is a little greater than the sum of the electrical and district heating powers, $100\,\text{MW}$, as conservation of energy requires: the remaining $11\,\text{MW}$ or so is losses. With round numbers

$10\,\text{MJ/kg} \cdot 40\,000\,\text{kg} : 3600\,\text{s}$
$\approx 110\,\text{MW}$

Model answer

If the electrical power stays at $30\,\text{MW}$, about the same amount of fuel is still used, but less of it is useful, and the overall efficiency falls towards the electrical efficiency. That is why a combined heat and power plant is run according to the heat demand: in summer at a lower power or not at all, and the electricity is produced elsewhere.

The group's conclusion is not justified, because the uncertainty of the result is several percentage points and the measurement was made only once.
- Power from the rating plate. The power on the rating plate is a nominal value, not a measured one, and the actual power depends on the mains voltage. The late reading of the final temperature certainly makes the result smaller. d)

Find

whether the claim “the plant produces $216\,\text{MWh}$ of electricity every day” holds, and why the plant can still be useful

Principle

Energy is conserved: as electricity the plant can give at most the energy stored in it by pumping, and because of the losses less (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy). A store shifts energy in time (chapter Transferring energy: electricity and heat).

Model answer

The claim is misleading. A pumped-storage plant does not produce energy but stores it: in each filling and emptying it takes about $289\,\text{MWh}$ from the grid and gives back about $216\,\text{MWh}$, so about $73\,\text{MWh}$ becomes heat. The plant is still useful, because it shifts energy in time. It is filled when electricity is plentiful and cheap, for example on a windy night, and emptied at the time of peak consumption or in calm weather, when electricity is scarce. As hydropower it is also quickly controllable power, which weather-dependent wind power needs alongside it. A correct wording would be: “The plant gives $216\,\text{MWh}$ to the grid from the $289\,\text{MWh}$ it took, later, when electricity is needed.”

Common mistakes

  • In part a the mass is calculated directly from the volume without the density, or the kilometres are not converted to metres.
  • The joules are converted to megawatt-hours by dividing by $3600$ or by $10^{6}$, but not by both.
  • In part c the potential energy is multiplied by the efficiency of the pump, so the energy taken from the grid would be smaller than the energy stored in the water.
  • The overall efficiency is calculated as the mean $(85\,\% + 88\,\%) : 2$ or as a sum and not as a product.

Also accepted

  • The energy of part c to three significant figures, $289\,\text{MWh}$, and the overall efficiency $74.8\,\%$.

Hint 2: what is the connection

Show the solution

Given

energy going into the battery $E_\text{a} = 15\,\text{Wh}$, efficiency $\eta = 80\,\%$

Find

the energy $E_\text{o}$ taken from the grid, in kilojoules

Principle

The efficiency is the fraction of the energy taken from the grid that is stored in the battery. Energy is conserved, and the rest becomes heat in the charger and the battery, so more is taken from the grid than the battery holds.

Formula

$\eta = \dfrac{E_\text{a}}{E_\text{o}}$, so $E_\text{o} = \dfrac{E_\text{a}}{\eta}$

$E_\text{o}$$= \dfrac{54\,000\,\text{J}}{0.80}$
$= 67\,500\,\text{J}$
$= 67.5\,\text{kJ}$

4. Explanation task

The town's combined heat and power plant has an overall efficiency of $88\,\%$ and an electrical efficiency of $30\,\%$. A councillor says: “The efficiency of a condensing power plant is only $40\,\%$, so combined heat and power is more than twice as good a way to produce electricity.” Evaluate the claim. Explain what causes the difference between the efficiencies and why the numbers cannot be compared directly.

Model answer

The claim does not hold. The $88\,\%$ of combined heat and power is an overall efficiency: both electricity ($30\,\%$ of the fuel power) and district heat ($58\,\%$) count as useful. The $40\,\%$ of the condensing power plant is an electrical efficiency alone. The difference comes from the heat of the condenser: in a condensing power plant it is transferred to the sea or a lake as waste heat, in combined heat and power it heats district heating water. From the same fuel combined heat and power produces even less electricity, because the steam is condensed at a higher temperature. Electricity and heat are not equally valuable either: electricity can be used for anything and can be transmitted far, while district heat is good only for heating nearby. Combined heat and power is therefore an efficient way to produce electricity and heat when heat is needed, but not a producer of electricity that is twice as good.
