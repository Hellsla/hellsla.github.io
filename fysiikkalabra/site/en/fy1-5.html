Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs

Why can the position of the Voyager 2 probe be calculated in advance for any day, even though it is more than 20 billion kilometres from the Sun?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the distance, time or speed in uniform motion with the formula $s = s_0 + vt$.
  • read the speed and the initial position from a $(t, s)$ graph.
  • explain when the distance is directly proportional to time and when the position is.

Prior knowledge

  • A straight line is fitted to measurement points, and its slope tells you how much $y$ changes when $x$ increases by onechapter Graphs and fitting a straight line
  • In the equation of a straight line, the constant term is where the line crosses the $y$-axischapter Graphs and fitting a straight line

1Think about it

The Voyager 2 probe left the Earth in 1977. In November 2018 it crossed the heliopause, the boundary beyond which the Sun's solar wind no longer dominates space. According to NASA's JPL, the probe was then 119.7 AU from the Sun, and it is still moving away at a speed of $15.341\,\text{km/s}$. The astronomical unit AU is roughly the distance between the Earth and the Sun. You cannot hold a ruler next to the probe, but you can still give its distance for any day.

What information do you need to calculate the distance of the probe from the Sun, say a year from now, and what do you assume about its speed?

Optional Recap: the slope of a line
The measurement points (time, position) lie on a straight line in the $(t, s)$ coordinate system. What does the slope of the line tell you?

  • That is the starting point of the line, not the slope (chapter Graphs and fitting a straight line). The slope tells you how steep the line is: by how many units $s$ changes when $t$ increases by one.

  • The length of the line does not matter, because the line continues in both directions. The slope is the ratio of two changes, $\Delta s : \Delta t$ (chapter Graphs and fitting a straight line).

  • Correct. The slope is $\Delta s : \Delta t$ (chapter Graphs and fitting a straight line). In motion it is the speed, and you apply it today.

You can also drag the Moment slider. On the track, the orange vertical lines are the positions of the trolley at whole seconds, the green flag is the initial position and the arrow shows the speed. Speed (replay) changes only the pace of the replay.

Assumptions of the model

The trolley moves along a straight track at constant speed: no friction and no acceleration, and the full speed is reached right at the start. The positions on the track and the values in the graph are values calculated by the model, not measurements. The trolley moves in one direction only, so the speed is positive.

How the calculation works

The position is $s = s_0 + vt$ and the distance travelled is $\Delta s = s - s_0 = vt$. The slope triangle in the graph goes from the moment $t = 2\,\text{s}$ to the moment $t = 6\,\text{s}$, so $\Delta t = 4.0\,\text{s}$, and the slope $\Delta s : \Delta t$ is the speed $v$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Uniform motion is motion along a straight line at constant speed: in equal intervals of time, the object travels equal distances. Speed tells you how far the object travels in one second, so its unit is metres per second (m/s). If a trolley moves two metres every second, its speed is $2\,\text{m/s}$.

    Direct proportionality

    Two quantities are directly proportional if their ratio is always the same. When uniform motion starts from the zero point, the distance travelled $s$ and the time $t$ are directly proportional: double the time gives double the distance, and the ratio $s : t$ is the speed $v$. In a (t, s) graph this shows as a straight line through the origin.

    Linear model

    Uniform motion is a model, not reality. A cyclist pedals unevenly, and Voyager 2 slows down slightly because of the Sun's gravity. The model is still good when the speed changes little compared with the time being considered. Its graph is a straight line, and a straight line is described by a linear model: the position changes by the same amount every second. Values calculated with the model are model values, which are compared with measurements.

    Uniform motion

    Suppose that at the start of the observation ($t = 0$) an object is at position $s_0$ and moves along a straight line at constant speed $v$. Its position at time $t$ is

    $$s = s_0 + v t$$

    where $s$ is the position (m) measured from the chosen zero point, $s_0$ the initial position (m), $v$ the speed (m/s) and $t$ the time from the start (s). The distance travelled by the object is $\Delta s = s - s_0 = vt$. In this chapter the object moves in one direction, so the speed is positive.

    Speed from a graph

    The (t, s) graph is a straight line, and the slope of the line is the speed:

    $$v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$$

    where $\Delta s$ is the change in position (m) and $\Delta t$ is its time interval (s). The line crosses the $s$-axis at the initial position $s_0$. If $s_0 = 0$, the position is directly proportional to time: $s = vt$. If $s_0 \neq 0$, the line is equally steep but shifted up or down. Then only the distance $\Delta s$, not the position $s$, is directly proportional to time.

    Units

    Always convert to compatible units (usually SI units) before substituting: substitute the value of the unit in its place. For example $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$, so $72\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{72\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20\,\text{m/s}$. In the same way $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$. In astronomy, long distances are given in astronomical units: $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$ is roughly the mean distance between the Earth and the Sun.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that in uniform motion the position doubles when the time doubles. This happens only if the motion starts from the zero point. When a trolley starts at $s_0 = 6\,\text{m}$ and moves at $2.0\,\text{m/s}$, it is at $10\,\text{m}$ at $2.0\,\text{s}$ and at $14\,\text{m}$ at $4.0\,\text{s}$, not at $20\,\text{m}$. The distance, however, does double: $4\,\text{m}$ becomes $8\,\text{m}$. Another mistake is to read the value of the position as the speed instead of the change in position per unit of time: the speed is $\Delta s : \Delta t$, not $s : t$, when $s_0 \neq 0$.

    Summary

    • In uniform motion the speed is constant, and the object travels equal distances in equal intervals of time: $s = s_0 + vt$.
    • The distance travelled $\Delta s = vt$ is directly proportional to time. The position $s$ is proportional to time only when $s_0 = 0$.
    • The (t, s) graph is a straight line. Its slope $\Delta s : \Delta t$ is the speed, and the intercept on the $s$-axis is the initial position $s_0$.
    • Convert units before calculating by substitution, for example $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.
    • Uniform motion is a linear model. The values it gives are model values, which can be compared with measurements, such as the distance of Voyager 2.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      Voyager 2 crossed the heliopause on 5 November 2018 at a distance of $s_0 = 119.7\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun (NASA/JPL). The speed of the probe relative to the Sun is $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$ (JPL Voyager mission status). Assume the speed is constant. According to the model, how far from the Sun is the probe on 5 November 2026?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $s_0 = 119.7\,\text{AU}$ at time $t = 0$ (5 November 2018), $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$ and $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$. The time interval from 5 November 2018 to 5 November 2026 is 8 years, including the leap days of 2020 and 2024: $8 \cdot 365 + 2 = 2922$ days. The quantity to find is the distance $s$ from the Sun.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. When the speed is assumed constant, the probe moves uniformly and its position follows the linear model. The result is a model value, not a measurement: the Sun's gravity slows the probe slightly.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. $$s = s_0 + v t$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The time is already in days, $t = 2922\,\text{days}$, so the speed is converted to AU/day:

      $v$$= 15.341\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 86\,400\,\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{day}}$
      $= 1\,325\,462\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{day}}$

      and dividing by $149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km/AU}$ gives $v = 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} s &= s_0 + v t \\ &= 119.7\,\text{AU} \\ &\quad + 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}} \cdot 2922\,\text{days} \\ &= 119.7\,\text{AU} + 25.89\,\text{AU} \\ &= 145.59\,\text{AU} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. According to the model, the probe is $s \approx 145.6\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun, about $2.18 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{km}$. The initial distance is given to the nearest tenth, so the result is rounded to the nearest tenth.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. A speed of $15\,\text{km/s}$ covers in a year about

      $15\,\text{km/s} \cdot 3.16 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$
      $\approx 4.7 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{km}$
      $\approx 3.2\,\text{AU}$

      so in 8 years the probe advances about 26 AU and $119.7 + 26 \approx 146\,\text{AU}$. For 15 February 2026 the same model gives $143.3\,\text{AU}$, and JPL reports $143.05\,\text{AU}$ for February 2026. The difference is about $0.2\,\text{AU}$, less than $0.2\,\%$ of the distance, so the constant-speed model works well here. The difference is partly because the Sun's gravity slows the probe very slightly and the initial distance is given only to the nearest tenth. So the model is accurate to a fraction of a percent.

      Find the error

      A student solves a problem: a trolley starts at $s_0 = 5.0\,\text{m}$ and moves at $3.0\,\text{m/s}$.Where is it at $t = 6.0\,\text{s}$? The student calculates: “At $2.0\,\text{s}$ the position is $5.0\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.0\,\text{s} = 11\,\text{m}$. The time $6.0\,\text{s}$ is three times as much, so the position is $3 \cdot 11\,\text{m} = 33\,\text{m}$.” Where is the error?

      • Correct. Only the distance $\Delta s = vt$ triples ($6.0\,\text{m}$ becomes $18\,\text{m}$), and the initial position is added once: $5.0 + 18 = 23\,\text{m}$. The error could have been noticed by reasoning: the trolley cannot be more than $30\,\text{m}$ away, because in $6.0\,\text{s}$ it travels only $18\,\text{m}$.

      • No conversion is needed: the speed is already in m/s and the time in seconds, so the units are compatible. The error is in the reasoning, where the position is multiplied by three.

      • The speed does not change, because the motion is uniform: it is $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ all the time. The time triples, and so the distance triples, but the initial position does not.

      • When the initial position is not zero, the distance is proportional to time, but the position is not. Check by substituting $t = 6.0\,\text{s}$ directly: $5.0 + 3.0 \cdot 6.0 = 23\,\text{m}$, not $33\,\text{m}$.

      Apply to a new situation

      At time $0\,\text{s}$ a cyclist is already $120\,\text{m}$ from the starting line and rides away from it uniformly at $21.6\,\text{km/h}$.

      How far from the starting line is the cyclist $45\,\text{s}$ later? Give the answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the initial position, and what is the speed? Are the units compatible?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Use the formula $s = s_0 + vt$. First convert the speed to m/s by substituting $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $v$$= 21.6\,\text{km/h}$
      $= \dfrac{21.6\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
      $= 6.0\,\text{m/s}$

      and $s = 120\,\text{m} + 6.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 45\,\text{s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      initial position $s_0 = 120\,\text{m}$, speed $v = 21.6\,\text{km/h}$, time $t = 45\,\text{s}$

      Find

      position $s$ from the starting line in metres

      Principle

      The speed of the cyclist is constant, so the motion is uniform, and the position follows the linear model. The initial position is not zero, so it must be added to the distance travelled.

      Formula

      $s = s_0 + vt$

      Conversions

      Substitute $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$:

      $v$$= 21.6\,\text{km/h}$
      $= \dfrac{21.6\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
      $= 6.0\,\text{m/s}$

      The time and the initial position are already in SI units.

      Substitution

      $s$$= 120\,\text{m} + 6.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 45\,\text{s}$
      $= 120\,\text{m} + 270\,\text{m}$
      $= 390\,\text{m}$

      Answer

      $390\,\text{m}$

      Two significant figures as in the time $45\,\text{s}$.

      Reasonableness

      The speed $6\,\text{m/s}$ is about $22\,\text{km/h}$, and $45\,\text{s}$ is three quarters of a minute, so the distance travelled is about $6 \cdot 45 \approx 270\,\text{m}$. The initial position $120\,\text{m}$ is added to this, and the position is greater than the initial position, as it should be when the cyclist moves away.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      An object moves uniformly, and its $(t, s)$ graph is a straight line that does not pass through the origin. Which statement is correct?

      • The mark of uniform motion is a straight line, not that the line passes through the origin. A line that does not pass through the origin describes uniform motion that starts from $s_0$: $s = s_0 + vt$.

      • Correct. The line is equally steep everywhere, so $\Delta s = vt$ and the ratio $\Delta s : t = v$ is constant. When the initial position $s_0$ is subtracted from the position $s$, the remaining distance is proportional to time.

      • The graph of a directly proportional quantity passes through the origin, because the ratio $s : t$ is constant. Here the line starts from $s_0 \neq 0$, so the ratio $s : t$ changes with time.

      2. Interpreting a graph

      The $(t, s)$ graph of an object is a horizontal line at $s = 4\,\text{m}$. What is the object doing?

      • The $(t, s)$ graph of uniform motion is a straight line, but a straight line alone does not tell whether the object moves. The speed is the slope of the line, $\Delta s : \Delta t$, and in a horizontal line $\Delta s = 0$, so the speed is zero.

      • Correct. The position does not change as time passes, so $\Delta s = 0$ and the speed is $v = \Delta s : \Delta t = 0$. The slope of a horizontal line is zero.

      • This is tempting, because a horizontal line in a $(t, v)$ graph means constant speed. Here, however, the vertical axis shows the position $s$, not the speed. The speed is the slope of the $(t, s)$ graph, and the slope of a horizontal line is zero.

      3. Reading a graph

      The line of a $(t, s)$ graph of uniform motion passes through the points $(1.0\,\text{s}$; $7.0\,\text{m})$ and $(5.0\,\text{s}$; $19\,\text{m})$.

      What is the initial position $s_0$ of the motion? Give the answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      The initial position is the position at time $t = 0$, that is, the point where the line crosses the $s$-axis. What is the slope of the line?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The slope is $v = \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$. You get the initial position by going back in time from the point $(t_1, s_1)$ to the start: $s_0 = s_1 - v t_1$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $v$$= \dfrac{19\,\text{m} - 7.0\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s} - 1.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= 3.0\,\text{m/s}$

      and $s_0 = 7.0\,\text{m} - 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      points $(t_1, s_1) = (1.0\,\text{s}$; $7.0\,\text{m})$ and $(t_2, s_2) = (5.0\,\text{s}$; $19\,\text{m})$ on the same line

      Find

      initial position $s_0$, that is, the position at time $t = 0$, in metres

      Principle

      The motion is uniform, so the line of the $(t, s)$ graph follows the equation $s = s_0 + vt$. The slope of the line is the speed, and the point where the line crosses the $s$-axis is the initial position.

      Reading the graph

      The two given points are enough. The change in position between them is divided by the time interval, which gives the slope. Then we go back from the first point to the moment $t = 0$: in one second the position decreases by $v \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$.

      Formula

      $v = \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$ and $s_1 = s_0 + v t_1$, so $s_0 = s_1 - v t_1$

      Conversions

      None needed: the positions are in metres and the times in seconds.

      Substitution

      $v$$= \dfrac{19\,\text{m} - 7.0\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s} - 1.0\,\text{s}}$
      $= 3.0\,\text{m/s}$

      and

      $s_0$$= 7.0\,\text{m} - 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{m}$

      Answer

      $4.0\,\text{m}$

      Two significant figures as in the positions $7.0\,\text{m}$ and $19\,\text{m}$.

      Reasonableness

      Check with the second point:

      $s$$= 4.0\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 19\,\text{m}$

      which is the given value. The initial position is smaller than $7.0\,\text{m}$, as it should be, because the object moves forwards.

        One formula or one step of reasoning. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          A cyclist rides uniformly at $5.5\,\text{m/s}$. How far does the cyclist travel in 20 seconds?

          m
          Answer

          $110\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed $v = 5.5\,\text{m/s}$, time $t = 20\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the zero point, so $s_0 = 0$

          Find

          distance travelled $s$ in metres

          Principle

          The speed is constant, so the motion is uniform, and the distance is directly proportional to time.

          Formula

          $s = s_0 + vt$, and since $s_0 = 0$, $s = vt$

          Conversions

          None needed: the speed is in m/s and the time in seconds, so the product is in metres.

          Substitution

          $s$$= 5.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 20\,\text{s}$
          $= 110\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $110\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures as in the speed $5.5\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          About $5\,\text{m}$ every second for twenty seconds is about $5 \cdot 20 = 100\,\text{m}$, and the unit $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m}$ is the unit of distance.

        2. Core

          A runner covers $360\,\text{m}$ in $45\,\text{s}$ at a steady pace. What is the runner's speed?

          m/s
          Answer

          $8.0\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance $s = 360\,\text{m}$, time $t = 45\,\text{s}$; the motion is uniform and starts from the zero point

          Find

          speed $v$ in m/s

          Principle

          In uniform motion, the speed is the distance travelled divided by the time it took.

          Formula

          $s = vt$, so $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \dfrac{360\,\text{m}}{45\,\text{s}}$
          $= 8.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

          Answer

          $8.0\,\text{m/s}$

          Two significant figures as in the time $45\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          Check backwards: $8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 45\,\text{s} = 360\,\text{m}$. The speed corresponds to about $29\,\text{km/h}$, which matches the pace of a good runner in a 400-metre race.

        3. The graph shows the position of a walker as a function of time. The walker keeps moving uniformly in the same direction also beyond the graph.

          How many seconds pass from the start until the walker has travelled $150\,\text{m}$?

          s
          Answer

          $60\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          a graph in which the position $s$ of a walker as a function of time $t$ is a straight line from the origin; the motion continues uniformly beyond the graph; distance $s = 150\,\text{m}$

          Find

          time $t$ in seconds when the distance is $150\,\text{m}$

          Principle

          A straight line from the origin means uniform motion, and the slope of the line is the speed. When the speed is known, the time beyond the graph is obtained by dividing the distance by the speed.

          Reading the graph

          The line passes through the grid crossing where $t_1 = 0\,\text{s}$ and $s_1 = 0\,\text{m}$ (the origin), and through the crossing where $t_2 = 8\,\text{s}$ and $s_2 = 20\,\text{m}$. Other crossings are for example $(4\,\text{s}$; $10\,\text{m})$ and $(10\,\text{s}$; $25\,\text{m})$. The reading is exact from the grid, so an answer that deviates by at most $3\,\%$ is accepted.

          Formula

          $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$ and $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{8\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 2.5\,\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

          and

          $t$$= \dfrac{150\,\text{m}}{2.5\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}$
          $= 60\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $60\,\text{s}$

          That is one minute; the speed was read with two significant figures, so the answer is given with the same precision.

          Reasonableness

          The speed $2.5\,\text{m/s}$ is about $9\,\text{km/h}$, a brisk walk or a slow jog. In the graph $10\,\text{s}$ takes $25\,\text{m}$, so $150\,\text{m}$, a distance six times as long, takes six times as long, $60\,\text{s}$.

        4. An object moves uniformly from its starting point and travels $18\,\text{m}$ in $4.0\,\text{s}$. How far does it travel in $10\,\text{s}$?

          m
          Answer

          $45\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $s_1 = 18\,\text{m}$ in time $t_1 = 4.0\,\text{s}$; the time asked about is $t_2 = 10\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the starting point, so $s_0 = 0$

          Find

          distance $s_2$ in time $t_2$ in metres

          Principle

          The motion is uniform and starts from the zero point, so the distance is directly proportional to time: the ratio $s : t$ is the same for both times.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{s_2}{t_2} = \dfrac{s_1}{t_1}$, so $s_2 = s_1\,\dfrac{t_2}{t_1}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the times are in seconds, and the seconds cancel.

          Substitution

          $s_2$$= 18\,\text{m} \cdot \dfrac{10\,\text{s}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 45\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $45\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures as in the distance $18\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The time $10\,\text{s}$ is $2.5$ times the time $4.0\,\text{s}$, so the distance must also be $2.5$ times as large: $2.5 \cdot 18\,\text{m} = 45\,\text{m}$. The speed is $18\,\text{m} : 4.0\,\text{s} = 4.5\,\text{m/s}$, and $4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 10\,\text{s} = 45\,\text{m}$.

        5. The position of a uniformly moving trolley, measured from the starting point, is $7.0\,\text{m}$ at $2.0\,\text{s}$ and $21\,\text{m}$ at $6.0\,\text{s}$. Calculate the speed of the trolley using the change in position and the time interval.

          m/s
          Answer

          $3.5\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          at time $t_1 = 2.0\,\text{s}$ the position is $s_1 = 7.0\,\text{m}$, and at time $t_2 = 6.0\,\text{s}$ the position is $s_2 = 21\,\text{m}$; the motion is uniform

          Find

          speed $v$ in m/s

          Principle

          In uniform motion the speed is constant, so it equals the change in position divided by the time interval. The initial position does not matter, because it cancels in the difference.

          Formula

          $v$$= \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$
          $= \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the positions are in metres and the times in seconds.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \dfrac{21\,\text{m} - 7.0\,\text{m}}{6.0\,\text{s} - 2.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= \dfrac{14\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 3.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

          Answer

          $3.5\,\text{m/s}$

          Two significant figures as in the positions $7.0\,\text{m}$ and the times $2.0\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The quotients $7.0\,\text{m} : 2.0\,\text{s}$ and $21\,\text{m} : 6.0\,\text{s}$ are both $3.5\,\text{m/s}$, so the position is proportional to time and the initial position is zero. The speed $3.5\,\text{m/s}$ corresponds to slow cycling, about $13\,\text{km/h}$.

        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          A car drives at a constant speed of $54\,\text{km/h}$. How far does it travel in 14 seconds? Give the answer in metres.

          m
          Answer

          $210\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed $v = 54\,\text{km/h}$, time $t = 14\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the zero point

          Find

          distance $s$ in metres

          Principle

          The car drives at constant speed, so the motion is uniform and $s = vt$. The speed and the time must be in compatible units.

          Formula

          $s = vt$

          Conversions

          substitute $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$:

          $v$$= 54\,\text{km/h}$
          $= \dfrac{54\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $= 15\,\text{m/s}$

          Substitution

          $s$$= 15\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 14\,\text{s}$
          $= 210\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $210\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures as in the speed $54\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $14\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The speed $54\,\text{km/h}$ is $0.9\,\text{km}$ per minute, and $14\,\text{s}$ is about $0.23\,\text{min}$, so the distance is about

          $0.9 \cdot 0.23\,\text{km}$$\approx 0.21\,\text{km}$
          $= 210\,\text{m}$
        2. Core

          A bus drives uniformly at $36\,\text{km/h}$. How many seconds does a distance of $4.5\,\text{km}$ take?

          s
          Answer

          $450\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed $v = 36\,\text{km/h}$, distance $s = 4.5\,\text{km}$; the motion is uniform and starts from the zero point

          Find

          time $t$ in seconds

          Principle

          The bus drives at constant speed, so the distance is proportional to time and the time is the distance divided by the speed.

          Formula

          $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$

          Conversions

          $v$$= 36\,\text{km/h}$
          $= \dfrac{36\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $= 10\,\text{m/s}$

          and $s = 4.5\,\text{km} = 4500\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $t$$= \dfrac{4500\,\text{m}}{10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}$
          $= 450\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $450\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures as in the data $36\,\text{km/h}$ and $4.5\,\text{km}$.

          Reasonableness

          In kilometres and hours:

          $4.5\,\text{km} : 36\,\text{km/h}$
          $= 0.125\,\text{h}$
          $= 7.5\,\text{min}$
          $= 450\,\text{s}$

          which is the same result.

        3. A hiker is at a point $1.5\,\text{km}$ from a cabin. The hiker walks along a straight path away from the cabin, uniformly at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$.

          How far from the cabin is the hiker after 10 minutes? Give the answer in kilometres.

          km
          Answer

          $2.4\,\text{km}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          initial position $s_0 = 1.5\,\text{km}$ from the cabin, speed $v = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$ away from the cabin, time $t = 10\,\text{min}$

          Find

          distance from the cabin $s$ in kilometres

          Principle

          The hiker walks at constant speed, so the motion is uniform. The initial position is not zero, so the position is the initial position plus the distance travelled.

          Formula

          $s = s_0 + vt$

          Conversions

          $s_0 = 1.5\,\text{km} = 1500\,\text{m}$ and $t = 10\,\text{min} = 600\,\text{s}$. Finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $s$$= 1500\,\text{m} + 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 600\,\text{s}$
          $= 1500\,\text{m} + 900\,\text{m}$
          $= 2400\,\text{m}$
          $= 2.4\,\text{km}$

          Answer

          $2.4\,\text{km}$

          Two significant figures as in the data $1.5\,\text{km}$ and $1.5\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The speed $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ is about $5.4\,\text{km/h}$, so in ten minutes, which is one sixth of an hour, the hiker advances about $0.9\,\text{km}$. Added to the initial position, $1.5\,\text{km} + 0.9\,\text{km} = 2.4\,\text{km}$, and the result is greater than the initial position, as it should be.

        4. After a flash of lightning, the rumble of thunder is heard $5.0\,\text{s}$ later. Sound travels in air uniformly at $343\,\text{m/s}$, and the travel time of light is so small that it is ignored.

          How far away did the lightning strike? Give the answer in kilometres.

          km
          Answer

          $1.7\,\text{km}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          time $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$; the travel time of light is ignored

          Find

          distance of the lightning $s$ in kilometres

          Principle

          Sound travels at constant speed, so the distance it travels is $s = vt$. Light arrives practically at once, so the delay of the thunder is the travel time of sound over the whole distance.

          Formula

          $s = vt$

          Conversions

          The distance is calculated in metres, and finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $s$$= 343\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
          $= 1715\,\text{m}$
          $= 1.7\,\text{km}$

          Answer

          $1.7\,\text{km}$

          Two significant figures as in the time $5.0\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          Rule of thumb: three seconds corresponds to about a kilometre, so five seconds corresponds to about $1.7\,\text{km}$. Light would travel this distance in $1715\,\text{m} : (3.0 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}) \approx 6\,\mu\text{s}$, which is negligible compared with the delay $5.0\,\text{s}$.

        Level 3 · challenging 3 exercises

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. The Voyager 2 probe crossed the heliopause on 5 November 2018 at a distance of $119.7\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun (NASA/JPL), and its speed relative to the Sun is $15.341\,\text{km/s}$ (JPL). Assume the speed is constant.

          How many days after the heliopause crossing is the probe, according to the model, $150\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun? Use the values $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$ and $1\,\text{day} = 86\,400\,\text{s}$. The result is a model value.

          vrk
          Answer

          $3420\,\text{days}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $s_0 = 119.7\,\text{AU}$ at time $t = 0$ (5 November 2018), $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$, $s = 150\,\text{AU}$, $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$, $1\,\text{day} = 86\,400\,\text{s}$

          Find

          time $t$ in days when the probe is at a distance of $150\,\text{AU}$

          Principle

          The speed is assumed constant, so the uniform motion model $s = s_0 + vt$ applies. The result is a model value, because the Sun's gravity slows the probe slightly and the real time is therefore a little longer.

          Formula

          $s = s_0 + vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s - s_0}{v}$

          Conversions

          The speed in astronomical units per day:

          $v$$= 15.341\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 86\,400\,\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{day}}$
          $= 1\,325\,462\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{day}}$

          and

          $\dfrac{1\,325\,462\,\text{km/day}}{149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km/AU}}$
          $= 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}}$

          Substitution

          $t$$= \dfrac{150\,\text{AU} - 119.7\,\text{AU}}{0.0088602\,\text{AU/day}}$
          $= \dfrac{30.3\,\text{AU}}{0.0088602\,\text{AU/day}}$
          $= 3419.8\,\text{days}$

          Answer

          $3420\,\text{days}$

          Three significant figures, when $150\,\text{AU}$ is interpreted as accurate to three figures and the difference is $30.3\,\text{AU}$.

          Reasonableness

          The rate is about $3.2\,\text{AU}$ per year, so $30.3\,\text{AU}$ takes about $30.3 : 3.2 \approx 9.5$ years. The result $3420\,\text{days} \approx 9.4$ years fits this and falls in March 2028. The model differs from the JPL value by about 0.2 AU, which corresponds to an error of about 20 days in the date.

        2. According to the model, Voyager 2 is at a distance of $150\,\text{AU}$ from the Earth. A radio signal travels in vacuum uniformly at $299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}$.

          How many hours pass from the moment a command leaves the Earth to the moment the reply of the probe reaches the Earth? The motion of the probe and its processing time for the reply are ignored. Use the value $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$. Justify why the motion can be ignored.

          h
          Answer

          $41.6\,\text{h}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance $d = 150\,\text{AU}$, speed of the signal $c = 299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$

          Find

          round-trip travel time $t$ in hours

          Principle

          A radio signal travels in vacuum at constant speed, so the distance it travels is $s = ct$. The command and the reply each travel the distance $d$, so the total distance is $2d$. During this time the probe moves about $15\,\text{km/s} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{km} \approx 0.015\,\text{AU}$, which is only $0.01\,\%$ of the distance $150\,\text{AU}$. In the same time the Earth moves about $30\,\text{km/s} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} \approx 0.03\,\text{AU}$, only about $0.02\,\%$, so neither motion needs to be taken into account.

          Formula

          $2d = ct$, so $t = \dfrac{2d}{c}$

          Conversions

          $d$$= 150 \cdot 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$
          $= 2.2440 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{km}$
          $= 2.2440 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}$

          Finally $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.

          Substitution

          $t$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 2.2440 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}}{299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= 1.497 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s}$
          $= \dfrac{1.497 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s}}{3600\,\text{s/h}}$
          $= 41.58\,\text{h}$

          Answer

          $41.6\,\text{h}$

          Three significant figures, when the distance $150\,\text{AU}$ is interpreted as accurate to three figures.

          Reasonableness

          Light travels one AU in about $499\,\text{s} \approx 8.3\,\text{min}$, so $150\,\text{AU}$ takes about

          $150 \cdot 8.3\,\text{min}$$\approx 1250\,\text{min}$
          $\approx 20.8\,\text{h}$

          and there and back $41.6\,\text{h}$. A delay of more than a day is reasonable, because the probe is tens of billions of kilometres away.

        3. Two friends set off from the school door along the same straight street in the same direction. The first walks uniformly at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$. The second leaves the door $40\,\text{s}$ later on a bicycle and rides uniformly at $4.5\,\text{m/s}$.

          How far from the door does the cyclist catch up with the walker?

          m
          Answer

          $90\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed of the walker $v_1 = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$, speed of the cyclist $v_2 = 4.5\,\text{m/s}$, delay of the cyclist $t_0 = 40\,\text{s}$; both start from the door, so $s_0 = 0$

          Find

          distance from the door $s$ at the point where the cyclist catches up with the walker

          Principle

          Both move uniformly. They are at the same position when their distances from the door are equal. The time $t$ is measured from the start of the walker, so the cyclist has been moving for the time $t - t_0$.

          Formula

          $s_1 = v_1 t$ and $s_2 = v_2 (t - t_0)$. From the condition $s_1 = s_2$ we get $v_1 t = v_2 (t - t_0)$, so $t = \dfrac{v_2 t_0}{v_2 - v_1}$ and $s = v_1 t$.

          Conversions

          None needed: the speeds are in m/s and the delay in seconds.

          Substitution

          $t$$= \dfrac{4.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 40\,\text{s}}{4.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}}$
          $= 60\,\text{s}$

          and

          $s$$= 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
          $= 90\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $90\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures as in the speeds.

          Reasonableness

          The cyclist rides for $60\,\text{s} - 40\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{s}$, and $4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 90\,\text{m}$, the same result. Another check: the walker's lead after $40\,\text{s}$ is $1.5 \cdot 40 = 60\,\text{m}$, and the cyclist closes it at the speed difference $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ in $60 : 3.0 = 20\,\text{s}$.
