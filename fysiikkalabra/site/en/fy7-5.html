Electromagnetic induction

Why does a bicycle dynamo light the lamp only while the wheel is turning, even though the dynamo contains no battery or accumulator?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the magnetic flux and the induced voltage in a coil using Faraday's law.
  • deduce the induced voltage from the slope of the magnetic flux graph.
  • explain the induced voltage of a moving rod using the magnetic force on the electrons.

Prior knowledge

  • A magnetic field exerts a force $F = qvB$ on a moving charged particle when the velocity is perpendicular to the fieldchapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields
  • A current-carrying wire and a coil produce a magnetic field around themchapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

The hub dynamo of a bicycle has no battery or accumulator. When the bicycle stands still, the lamp is dark. As soon as the wheel begins to turn, the lamp lights up, and the faster you ride, the brighter it shines. Inside the dynamo there is a permanent magnet and a wire coil. In 1831 Michael Faraday made a similar observation in his laboratory: a magnet beside a coil produced no current, but a moving magnet did.

What decides whether a voltage appears in the coil in the situation of the magnet and the coil, and what does the size of the voltage depend on?

Optional Review: the magnetic force on a charge
An electron is at rest in a uniform magnetic field whose magnetic flux density is $0.50\,\text{T}$. What force does the field exert on the electron?

  • The force is perpendicular to the field only when the charge moves, and its magnitude is $F = qvB$. No force acts on an electron at rest (chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields).

  • A compass needle is a magnetic dipole, but the field acts on an electron only when it moves: $F = qvB$, and at zero velocity the force is zero (chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields).

  • Correct. A magnetic field acts only on a moving charge (chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields). Today this explains why the electrons of a metal rod moving in a field shift to one end of the rod, but the electrons of a rod at rest do not.

A square coil moves at a steady speed through a region of magnetic field. Start the motion and follow how the magnetic flux and the induced voltage change with time.

Simulation

How to use

Choose the speed $v$ of the coil, the magnetic flux density $B$ and the number of turns $N$, and press Start. The coil starts from the left of the field and passes through the region. The green crosses show the magnetic field, which points into the paper, and the blue arrow shows the velocity of the coil. The graphs are drawn during the motion: the upper one is the magnetic flux $\Phi$ through one turn and the lower one the induced voltage $e$ of the coil. When the coil has passed through the field, the readout line shows the voltage on entry, its duration and the voltage inside the field. The Speed menu changes only the pace of the animation, not the speed of the coil. If you change the controls during the motion, the coil returns to the start.

Assumptions of the model

The coil is a square of side $l = 0.10\,\text{m}$. The field is uniform in a region $0.20\,\text{m}$ wide and zero outside it, and the edge of the field is sharp. The vertical sides of the coil are always within the height of the field region. The coil moves at a steady speed, and nothing slows it down. The voltmeter draws no current, so no current flows in the coil. The readouts are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

When the leading side of the coil is a distance $x$ inside the field, the flux through one turn is $\Phi = B \cdot l \cdot x$, until the whole coil is in the field. During entry $x$ grows steadily, so the induced voltage is

$$e = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = -NBlv$$

Inside the field the flux is constant, $\Phi = Bl^2$, so $e = 0$. During exit the flux decreases as fast as it grew, so $e = +NBlv$. The entry lasts a time $\Delta t = l : v$. The meaning of the minus sign becomes clear in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Faraday's observation

    In the chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil, an electric current produced a magnetic field. Michael Faraday spent years looking for the reverse effect: could a magnetic field produce an electric current? In 1831 he noticed that a magnet at rest beside a coil does nothing, however strong the magnet is. When the magnet is pushed into the coil, the needle of a voltmeter connected to the coil deflects. When the magnet is at rest inside the coil, the needle returns to zero. When the magnet is pulled out, the needle deflects the other way. The faster the magnet is moved and the more turns the coil has, the greater the voltage. The same happens if the magnet is at rest and the coil is moved.

    A change produces a voltage

    The phenomenon is called electromagnetic induction, and the voltage produced between the ends of the coil is called the induced voltage. When the ends of the coil are joined by a wire or a lamp, an induced current flows in the circuit. What all of Faraday's experiments have in common is that the magnetic field passing through the coil changes. A coil at rest in a strong but unchanging field gets no voltage. The change can come about in many ways: the magnet or the coil moves, the coil turns in the field, the area of the loop changes, or the current in a nearby electromagnet is switched on or off. In the hub dynamo of a bicycle, a magnet turns in the middle of the coils when the wheel turns. When the wheel stands still, nothing changes and the lamp stays dark.

    Magnetic flux

    The size of the change is measured with the magnetic flux. It tells how much magnetic field passes through a loop. When a uniform field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop, the magnetic flux is

    $$\Phi = BA$$

    where $\Phi$ is the magnetic flux, $B$ the magnetic flux density (T) and $A$ the area of the loop (m²). The unit of flux is the weber: $1\,\text{Wb} = 1\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}^2$. The flux is greater, the stronger the field and the larger the loop. If the loop is tilted, fewer field lines pass through it and the flux decreases. When the plane of the loop is parallel to the field, no field line passes through it and the flux is zero.

    Faraday's law of induction

    Faraday's observations are summarised in Faraday's law of induction: the induced voltage in a coil is proportional to the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the coil and to the number of turns of the coil:

    $$e = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$$

    where $e$ is the induced voltage (V), $N$ the number of turns of the coil, $\Delta\Phi$ the change in the magnetic flux through one turn (Wb) and $\Delta t$ the time (s) in which the change takes place. The turns are in series, so each turn adds the same amount to the voltage. The units fit together: $1\,\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}} = 1\,\text{V}$. The minus sign gives the direction of the induced voltage: it opposes the change that produces it. The direction is discussed in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents; the magnitude of the voltage is calculated from the equation $|e| = N \cdot \Delta\Phi : \Delta t$.

    The flux graph and the voltage

    The quotient $\Delta\Phi : \Delta t$ is the slope of the flux graph $(t, \Phi)$. The induced voltage therefore does not depend on how large the flux is but on how steeply it changes. In the graph below, a coil of ten turns moves at a steady speed through a region of magnetic field. When the coil enters the field, the flux grows steadily and the voltage is constant. When the coil is completely inside the field, the flux is at its greatest but does not change, so the voltage is zero. When the coil leaves the field, the flux decreases and the voltage is equal in size but opposite in sign.

    The induced voltage of a moving conductor

    When a straight metal rod moves with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $B$, the conduction electrons of the rod move along with it. The chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields showed that a magnetic field exerts a force $F_\text{m} = evB$ on a moving charge. The force points along the rod, so the electrons move to one end of the rod. A deficit of electrons is left at the other end, and an electric field $E$ appears between the ends, pulling the electrons back. The shift stops when the electric force $F_\text{s} = eE$ cancels the magnetic force. Then $eE = evB$, that is, $E = vB$. In a uniform field the voltage is the product of the field and the length (chapter Uniform electric field, potential and potential energy), so the induced voltage between the ends of the rod is

    $$e = vBl$$

    where $v$ is the velocity of the rod (m/s), $B$ the magnetic flux density (T) and $l$ the length of the rod in the field (m), when the velocity, the rod and the field are mutually perpendicular.

    The same result follows from Faraday's law. When the rod slides along rails, the area of the loop bounded by the rails and the rod grows in the time $\Delta t$ by $\Delta A = l \cdot v\Delta t$, so the flux grows by $\Delta\Phi = Bl \cdot v\Delta t$, and the magnitude of the voltage is $\Delta\Phi : \Delta t = vBl$. When the ends of the rails are joined by a resistor, a current flows in the circuit, and the magnetic field exerts a force $F = IlB$ on the current-carrying rod, as in the chapter Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker. The direction of this force and the conservation of energy are studied in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the magnetic field itself produces a voltage and that a strong field gives a large voltage. However, an induced voltage arises only from a change in flux: a coil at rest beside a strong magnet gets no voltage, and a coil moving steadily inside a field gets no voltage if the flux through it does not change. The flux and its rate of change are different things, in the same way as position and velocity: a large flux can stay unchanged, and zero flux can change quickly. Another common mistake is to forget the number of turns $N$ or to convert the area wrongly: $1\,\text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$, not $10^{-2}\,\text{m}^2$.

    Summary

    • In electromagnetic induction, an induced voltage appears in a coil when the magnetic flux through it changes. If the flux does not change, no voltage appears.
    • The magnetic flux is $\Phi = BA$ when the field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop; the unit is the weber, $1\,\text{Wb} = 1\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}^2$.
    • Faraday's law: $e = -N \cdot \Delta\Phi : \Delta t$. The voltage is the slope of the flux graph multiplied by the number of turns; the minus sign gives the direction.
    • The voltage between the ends of a rod moving perpendicular to the field is $e = vBl$, because the magnetic force moves the electrons of the rod to one end.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A coil has $250$ turns and a cross-sectional area of $4.0\,\text{cm}^2$. The coil is between the poles of an electromagnet so that the uniform field is along the axis of the coil. When the current of the electromagnet is increased, the magnetic flux density of the field grows steadily from $0.10\,\text{T}$ to $0.50\,\text{T}$ in $0.20\,\text{s}$. How large is the induced voltage between the ends of the coil?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Number of turns $N = 250$, area $A = 4.0\,\text{cm}^2$, magnetic flux density at first $B_1 = 0.10\,\text{T}$ and at the end $B_2 = 0.50\,\text{T}$, time $\Delta t = 0.20\,\text{s}$. The magnitude of the induced voltage $|e|$ is asked.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The magnetic flux through the coil changes because the field grows stronger, so an induced voltage appears in the coil according to Faraday's law. The field is along the axis, that is, perpendicular to the plane of the turns, so the flux through one turn is $\Phi = BA$. The area does not change, so the change in flux is $\Delta\Phi = (B_2 - B_1)A$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} |e| &= N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} \\ |e| &= \frac{N(B_2 - B_1)A}{\Delta t} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The area in square metres:

      $A$$= 4.0\,\text{cm}^2$
      $= 4.0 \cdot (0.01\,\text{m})^2$
      $= 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$

      The other quantities are in SI units.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} &|e| = \frac{N(B_2 - B_1)A}{\Delta t} \\ &= \frac{250 \cdot 0.40\,\text{T} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2}{0.20\,\text{s}} \\ &= 0.20\,\text{V} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. An induced voltage of $0.20\,\text{V}$ appears between the ends of the coil for as long as the field keeps growing. The result is given to two significant figures, like the area $4.0\,\text{cm}^2$ and the time $0.20\,\text{s}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The units fit:

      $\tfrac{\text{T}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{s}}$$= \tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}}$
      $= \text{V}$

      One turn would give only $1.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{Wb} : 0.20\,\text{s} = 0.80\,\text{mV}$, and $250$ turns give $250$ times that voltage. When the field stops growing, the flux no longer changes and the voltage drops to zero, even though the field is then at its strongest.

      Optional Find the error
      A coil has $200$ turns and a cross-sectional area of $6.0\,\text{cm}^2$. The field along the axis of the coil grows steadily from zero to $0.30\,\text{T}$ in $0.050\,\text{s}$.A student calculates

      $$\begin{aligned} &A = 6.0\,\text{cm}^2 = 0.060\,\text{m}^2 \\ &|e| = \frac{N \Delta B \cdot A}{\Delta t} \\ &= \frac{200 \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot 0.060\,\text{m}^2}{0.050\,\text{s}} \\ &= 72\,\text{V} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • Correct. A square centimetre is $(0.01\,\text{m})^2 = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$, so $A = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$ and $|e| = 200 \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2 : 0.050\,\text{s} = 0.72\,\text{V}$. The student multiplied the centimetres by $0.01$ only once, although in an area the centimetre is squared.

      • The turns are in series, and the same voltage appears in each, so the voltages add up: $N$ belongs in the formula. In experiment 2 the voltage doubled when the number of turns doubled. The error is in the conversion of the area: $6.0\,\text{cm}^2 = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$.

      • The minus sign only gives the direction of the voltage, and here the magnitude $|e|$ is calculated, so its absence is not an error. The result $72\,\text{V}$ is, however, a hundred times too large: $6.0\,\text{cm}^2 = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$, so $|e| = 0.72\,\text{V}$.

      Apply to a new situation: the shake-powered torch

      A shake-powered torch has no battery. When the torch is shaken, a magnet inside it slides through a coil of $600$ turns. When the magnet enters the coil, the magnetic flux through one turn grows by $0.12\,\text{mWb}$ in $15\,\text{ms}$.

      How large is the induced voltage in the coil on average during this time? Give the answer in volts.

      V
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What changes in the flux through the coil, and how fast? How many turns are in series?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The magnitude of the induced voltage is $|e| = N \cdot \Delta\Phi : \Delta t$. First convert the flux to webers and the time to seconds.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $\Delta\Phi = 0.12 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}$ and $\Delta t = 15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$, so

      $|e|$$= 600 \cdot 0.12 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb} : (15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s})$
      Show the solution

      Given

      number of turns $N = 600$, change in flux through one turn $\Delta\Phi = 0.12\,\text{mWb}$, time $\Delta t = 15\,\text{ms}$

      Find

      average magnitude of the induced voltage $|e|$ in volts

      Principle

      When the magnet slides into the coil, the magnetic flux through the coil grows, so an induced voltage appears in the coil according to Faraday's law. The flux does not necessarily grow steadily, so the quotient of the total change in flux and the time gives the average of the voltage over this interval. The turns are in series, so the voltage is $N$ times the voltage of one turn.

      Formula

      $|e| = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$

      Conversions

      $\Delta\Phi$$= 0.12\,\text{mWb}$
      $= 0.12 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}$
      $\Delta t$$= 15\,\text{ms}$
      $= 15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$
      $= 0.015\,\text{s}$

      Substitution

      $|e|$$= 600 \cdot \dfrac{0.12 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}}{0.015\,\text{s}}$
      $= 600 \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{V}$
      $= 4.8\,\text{V}$

      Answer

      $4.8\,\text{V}$

      Two significant figures like the change in flux $0.12\,\text{mWb}$ and the time $15\,\text{ms}$.

      Reasonableness

      One turn would give only $8.0\,\text{mV}$, which would not be enough for an LED. An LED needs a voltage of about $2$–$3\,\text{V}$, so a coil of hundreds of turns and fast shaking are essential. If shaken more slowly, the same change in flux takes longer and the voltage stays smaller.

      Optional 3. Explain: the voltage in a moving rod

      A metal rod moves perpendicular to a magnetic field. Explain why a voltage appears between the ends of the rod, and why the voltage does not grow without limit but settles at the value $e = vBl$.

      Model answer

      The conduction electrons of the rod move along with it. A magnetic field exerts on a moving charge a force $F_\text{m} = evB$, which is perpendicular to the velocity and the field, that is, along the rod. The electrons shift to one end of the rod, which becomes negative, and positive charge is left at the other end. An electric field $E$ appears inside the rod between the charges and exerts on the electrons an opposite force $F_\text{s} = eE$. Electrons shift until the forces are equal: $eE = evB$, that is, $E = vB$. In a uniform field the voltage is $El$, so $e = vBl$. At a greater speed the magnetic force is greater, so a greater electric field is needed for balance and the voltage is greater. In a rod at rest no voltage appears, because the magnetic force is zero.

        1. Exercise 5-1Core

          A square wire loop of side $0.12\,\text{m}$ is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. The magnetic flux density of the field is $0.35\,\text{T}$.

          How large is the magnetic flux through the loop? Give the answer in milliwebers.

          mWb
          Answer

          $5.0\,\text{mWb}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          side of the square $a = 0.12\,\text{m}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.35\,\text{T}$, field perpendicular to the plane of the loop

          Find

          magnetic flux $\Phi$ through the loop in milliwebers

          Principle

          When a uniform field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop, the magnetic flux is the product of the field and the area. The loop is a square, so its area is the square of the side.

          Formula

          $\Phi = BA$ and $A = a^2$, so $\Phi = B a^2$

          Conversions

          The given values are in SI units. The answer is converted to milliwebers: $1\,\text{Wb} = 1000\,\text{mWb}$.

          Substitution

          $\Phi$$= 0.35\,\text{T} \cdot (0.12\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 0.35\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0144\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 0.00504\,\text{Wb}$
          $= 5.04\,\text{mWb}$

          Answer

          $5.0\,\text{mWb}$

          Two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          The area of the loop is a little under $0.015\,\text{m}^2$, about the size of an A6 sheet (a postcard), and the field is of the order of tenths of a tesla, so the flux is of the order of thousandths of a weber. Doubling the side would quadruple the flux.

        2. Exercise 5-2Core

          A coil has $400$ turns. When a magnet is pushed into the coil, the magnetic flux through one turn grows steadily by $1.5\,\text{mWb}$ in $0.20\,\text{s}$.

          How large is the induced voltage in the coil?

          V
          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 400$, change in flux through one turn $\Delta\Phi = 1.5\,\text{mWb}$, time $\Delta t = 0.20\,\text{s}$

          Find

          magnitude of the induced voltage $|e|$ in the coil in volts

          Principle

          The magnetic flux through the coil changes when the magnet is pushed into it, so an induced voltage appears in the coil according to Faraday's law. The flux grows steadily, so the voltage is constant throughout. The turns are in series, so the voltage is $N$ times the voltage of one turn.

          Formula

          $|e| = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta\Phi$$= 1.5\,\text{mWb}$
          $= 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}$

          Substitution

          $|e|$$= 400 \cdot \dfrac{1.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}}{0.20\,\text{s}}$
          $= 400 \cdot 7.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{V}$
          $= 3.0\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the change in flux and the time.

          Reasonableness

          The units fit: $\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}} = \text{V}$. One turn would give only $7.5\,\text{mV}$; with a coil of hundreds of turns the voltage rises to volts, which matches experiments done with school coils.

        3. Exercise 5-3

          A metal rod of length $0.25\,\text{m}$ moves at a speed of $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ in a uniform magnetic field of $0.40\,\text{T}$. The rod, its velocity and the field are mutually perpendicular.

          How large is the induced voltage between the ends of the rod?

          V
          Answer

          $0.20\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          length of the rod $l = 0.25\,\text{m}$, speed $v = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.40\,\text{T}$; the rod, the velocity and the field are mutually perpendicular

          Find

          induced voltage $e$ between the ends of the rod in volts

          Principle

          The electrons that move with the rod experience a magnetic force $evB$ along the rod, so electrons collect at one end of the rod. The shift stops when the electric field $E = vB$ that appears cancels the magnetic force, and the voltage between the ends is $El$.

          Formula

          $e = vBl$

          Conversions

          None needed: all quantities are in SI units, and $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{T} \cdot \text{m} = \text{V}$.

          Substitution

          $e$$= 2.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.40\,\text{T} \cdot 0.25\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.20\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $0.20\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          Every second the rod sweeps an area of $0.25\,\text{m} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} = 0.50\,\text{m}^2$, so the flux grows at the rate

          $0.40\,\text{T} \cdot 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}}$
          $= 0.20\,\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}}$

          : Faraday's law gives the same voltage.

        4. Exercise 5-4

          A coil has $150$ turns. When a magnet is pulled away from the coil, an average induced voltage of $0.45\,\text{V}$ appears between the ends of the coil for $0.12\,\text{s}$.

          By how much did the magnetic flux through one turn change? Give the answer in milliwebers.

          mWb
          Answer

          $0.36\,\text{mWb}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 150$, average induced voltage $|e| = 0.45\,\text{V}$, time $\Delta t = 0.12\,\text{s}$

          Find

          change in the magnetic flux through one turn $\Delta\Phi$ in milliwebers

          Principle

          The voltage appears because the flux decreases as the magnet is pulled away. According to Faraday's law, the average voltage is the change in flux divided by the time, multiplied by the number of turns, so when the voltage, the time and the number of turns are known, the change in flux can be solved.

          Formula

          $|e| = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta\Phi = \dfrac{|e|\,\Delta t}{N}$

          Conversions

          The given values are in SI units, and $\text{V}\cdot\text{s} = \text{Wb}$. The answer is converted to milliwebers: $1\,\text{Wb} = 1000\,\text{mWb}$.

          Substitution

          $\Delta\Phi$$= \dfrac{0.45\,\text{V} \cdot 0.12\,\text{s}}{150}$
          $= \dfrac{0.054\,\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{150}$
          $= 3.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{Wb}$
          $= 0.36\,\text{mWb}$

          Answer

          $0.36\,\text{mWb}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage and the time.

          Reasonableness

          Check: $150 \cdot 3.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{Wb} : 0.12\,\text{s} = 0.45\,\text{V}$. If the area of the coil is about $10\,\text{cm}^2$, the flux corresponds to a field of about $0.4\,\text{T}$ inside the coil, which suits a strong permanent magnet.

        5. Exercise 5-5

          An induced voltage of $12\,\text{mV}$ is measured between the ends of a metal rod sliding on two rails. The distance between the rails is $0.30\,\text{m}$, and the magnetic flux density of the field perpendicular to the plane of the rails is $0.080\,\text{T}$. The rod moves perpendicular to itself.

          How large is the speed of the rod?

          m/s
          Answer

          $0.50\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          induced voltage $e = 12\,\text{mV}$, length of the rod in the field $l = 0.30\,\text{m}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.080\,\text{T}$, velocity perpendicular to the rod and the field

          Find

          speed of the rod $v$ in metres per second

          Principle

          The magnetic force on the electrons of the moving rod moves them to one end of the rod, and a voltage $e = vBl$ appears between the ends. The voltage is proportional to the speed, so the speed can be solved from the measured voltage.

          Formula

          $e = vBl$, so $v = \dfrac{e}{Bl}$

          Conversions

          $e$$= 12\,\text{mV}$
          $= 12 \cdot 0.001\,\text{V}$
          $= 0.012\,\text{V}$

          Substitution

          $v$$= \dfrac{0.012\,\text{V}}{0.080\,\text{T} \cdot 0.30\,\text{m}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.012\,\text{V}}{0.024\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= 0.50\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $0.50\,\text{m/s}$

          Two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          Check:

          $0.50\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.080\,\text{T} \cdot 0.30\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.012\,\text{V}$

          Half a metre per second is slower than a slow walk, which suits a rail experiment done on a table.

        Two steps, working with measured data, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Exercise 5-6Core

          A flexible wire loop is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of $0.80\,\text{T}$. The loop is squeezed so that its area decreases steadily from $200\,\text{cm}^2$ to $50\,\text{cm}^2$ in $0.25\,\text{s}$.

          How large is the induced voltage in the loop? Give the answer in millivolts.

          mV
          Answer

          $48\,\text{mV}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          magnetic flux density $B = 0.80\,\text{T}$ perpendicular to the plane of the loop, area at first $A_1 = 200\,\text{cm}^2$ and at the end $A_2 = 50\,\text{cm}^2$, time $\Delta t = 0.25\,\text{s}$, one turn $N = 1$

          Find

          magnitude of the induced voltage $|e|$ in the loop in millivolts

          Principle

          The field stays the same, but the area of the loop decreases, so the flux $\Phi = BA$ through the loop decreases. The change in flux produces a voltage according to Faraday's law. The area decreases steadily, so the voltage is constant.

          Formula

          $\Delta\Phi = B(A_1 - A_2)$ and $|e| = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$, so $|e| = \dfrac{B(A_1 - A_2)}{\Delta t}$ when $N = 1$

          Conversions

          $A_1$$= 200\,\text{cm}^2$
          $= 200 \cdot (0.01\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 0.0200\,\text{m}^2$
          $A_2$$= 50\,\text{cm}^2$
          $= 50 \cdot (0.01\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 0.0050\,\text{m}^2$

          Substitution

          $A_1 - A_2$$= 0.0200\,\text{m}^2 - 0.0050\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 0.0150\,\text{m}^2$
          $|e|$$= \dfrac{0.80\,\text{T} \cdot 0.0150\,\text{m}^2}{0.25\,\text{s}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.012\,\text{Wb}}{0.25\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.048\,\text{V}$
          $= 48\,\text{mV}$

          Answer

          $48\,\text{mV}$

          Two significant figures like the field $0.80\,\text{T}$ and the time $0.25\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          One turn gives a voltage of only tens of millivolts even though the field is strong; this fits the fact that practical devices need many turns. If the loop were squeezed twice as fast, the voltage would be twice as great.

        2. Exercise 5-7Core

          A coil has $50$ turns. The graph shows the magnetic flux $\Phi$ through one turn as a function of time $t$.

          What is the greatest absolute value of the induced voltage of the coil in the interval $0$–$0.70\,\text{s}$? Read the necessary values from the graph.

          V
          Answer

          $2.0\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 50$; the graph shows the magnetic flux $\Phi$ through one turn as a function of time $t$

          Find

          greatest absolute value of the induced voltage $|e|$ in volts

          Principle

          According to Faraday's law, the voltage is proportional to the rate of change of the flux $\Delta\Phi : \Delta t$, which is the slope of the flux graph. The voltage is therefore greatest where the graph is steepest, not where the flux is greatest.

          Reading the graph

          The graph consists of straight parts. In the interval $0$–$0.20\,\text{s}$ the flux grows from zero to $4.0\,\text{mWb}$, so the absolute value of the slope is $4.0\,\text{mWb} : 0.20\,\text{s} = 20\,\tfrac{\text{mWb}}{\text{s}}$. In the interval $0.20$–$0.50\,\text{s}$ the graph is horizontal, so the voltage is zero. In the interval $0.50$–$0.60\,\text{s}$ the flux decreases from $4.0\,\text{mWb}$ to zero, so the absolute value of the slope is $4.0\,\text{mWb} : 0.10\,\text{s} = 40\,\tfrac{\text{mWb}}{\text{s}}$. The steepest part is therefore $0.50$–$0.60\,\text{s}$, where $\Delta\Phi = 4.0\,\text{mWb}$ and $\Delta t = 0.10\,\text{s}$.

          Formula

          $|e| = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta\Phi$$= 4.0\,\text{mWb}$
          $= 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}$

          Substitution

          $|e|$$= 50 \cdot \dfrac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}}{0.10\,\text{s}}$
          $= 50 \cdot 0.040\,\text{V}$
          $= 2.0\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $2.0\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the flux $4.0\,\text{mWb}$ read from the graph.

          Reasonableness

          In the rising part the voltage is $50 \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb} : 0.20\,\text{s} = 1.0\,\text{V}$, half of the greatest, because the same change in flux takes twice as long. In the falling part the sign of the voltage is opposite to that in the rising part, because the flux decreases.

        3. Exercise 5-8

          Two horizontal metal rails are $0.40\,\text{m}$ apart, and their ends are joined by a $2.0\,\Omega$ resistor. A transverse metal rod slides on the rails at a speed of $3.0\,\text{m/s}$. The magnetic flux density of the vertical magnetic field is $0.50\,\text{T}$. The resistance of the rails and the rod is very small.

          How large is the current through the resistor?

          A
          Answer

          $0.30\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance between the rails, that is, the length of the rod in the field, $l = 0.40\,\text{m}$, speed $v = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.50\,\text{T}$, resistance $R = 2.0\,\Omega$; the resistance of the rails and the rod is very small

          Find

          current $I$ through the resistor in amperes

          Principle

          An induced voltage $e = vBl$ appears between the ends of the moving rod, because a magnetic force acts on the electrons of the rod. The rod, the rails and the resistor form a closed circuit in which the rod acts as the voltage source. The resistance of the rest of the circuit is small, so the whole voltage is across the resistor, and the current follows from Ohm's law.

          Formula

          $e = vBl$ and $I = \dfrac{e}{R}$, so $I = \dfrac{vBl}{R}$

          Conversions

          None needed: all quantities are in SI units.

          Substitution

          $e$$= 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.50\,\text{T} \cdot 0.40\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.60\,\text{V}$
          $I$$= \dfrac{0.60\,\text{V}}{2.0\,\Omega}$
          $= 0.30\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $0.30\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          The flux grows at the rate

          $0.50\,\text{T} \cdot 0.40\,\text{m} \cdot 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
          $= 0.60\,\tfrac{\text{Wb}}{\text{s}}$

          which gives the same voltage. When a current flows in the rod, the field exerts a force $IlB = 0.060\,\text{N}$ on it; its direction is found in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

        4. Exercise 5-9

          A square coil of side $5.0\,\text{cm}$ with $20$ turns is pulled at a steady speed of $0.25\,\text{m/s}$ into a region of uniform magnetic field. The field of $0.30\,\text{T}$ is perpendicular to the plane of the coil, and the leading side of the coil is parallel to the edge of the field.

          How large is the induced voltage in the coil during the time in which the coil is entering the field? Give the answer in millivolts.

          mV
          Answer

          $75\,\text{mV}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          side of the coil $l = 5.0\,\text{cm}$, number of turns $N = 20$, speed $v = 0.25\,\text{m/s}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.30\,\text{T}$ perpendicular to the plane of the coil

          Find

          induced voltage $|e|$ in millivolts while the coil is entering the field

          Principle

          When the coil enters the field, the part of its area that is in the field grows steadily, so the flux through each turn grows steadily and the voltage is constant. In the time $\Delta t$ the coil moves a distance $v\Delta t$, so the flux grows by $\Delta\Phi = Bl \cdot v\Delta t$.

          Formula

          $|e|$$= N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$
          $= N\dfrac{Bl \cdot v\Delta t}{\Delta t}$

          so $|e| = NBlv$

          Conversions

          $l$$= 5.0\,\text{cm}$
          $= 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.050\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $|e|$$= 20 \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot 0.050\,\text{m} \cdot 0.25\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 0.075\,\text{V}$
          $= 75\,\text{mV}$

          Answer

          $75\,\text{mV}$

          Two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          Check using the whole entry: the flux grows from zero to

          $BA$$= 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot (0.050\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 7.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{Wb}$

          in the time $0.050\,\text{m} : 0.25\,\text{m/s} = 0.20\,\text{s}$, so

          $|e|$$= 20 \cdot 7.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{Wb} : 0.20\,\text{s}$
          $= 0.075\,\text{V}$

        1. Exercise 5-10

          A train travels at a speed of $200\,\text{km/h}$ on a horizontal track. The track gauge of the Finnish railways is $1524\,\text{mm}$ (Finnish Transport Infrastructure Agency), and the metal axle of a pair of wheels extends from rail to rail. The vertical component of the Earth's magnetic field in southern Finland is about $50\,\mu\text{T}$ (WMM2025 model, NOAA/BGS).

          How large is the induced voltage between the ends of the axle at the rails? Give the answer in millivolts.

          mV
          Answer

          $4.2\,\text{mV}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed of the train $v = 200\,\text{km/h}$, length of the axle from rail to rail $l = 1524\,\text{mm}$, vertical component of the Earth's magnetic field $B_\text{v} = 50\,\mu\text{T}$

          Find

          induced voltage $e$ between the ends of the axle in millivolts

          Principle

          The axle is a metal rod that moves horizontally, perpendicular to itself. The magnetic force on the electrons is perpendicular to the velocity and the field; a component along the axle arises only from the vertical component of the field, because the horizontal component is either parallel to the velocity or gives a vertical force. The voltage is therefore given by $e = vB_\text{v}l$.

          Formula

          $e = vB_\text{v}l$

          Conversions

          $v$$= 200\,\text{km/h}$
          $= 200 \cdot \dfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $= 55.56\,\text{m/s}$
          $l$$= 1524\,\text{mm}$
          $= 1524 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
          $= 1.524\,\text{m}$
          $B_\text{v}$$= 50\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= 50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$

          Substitution

          $e$$= 55.56\,\text{m/s} \cdot 50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot 1.524\,\text{m}$
          $= 4.233 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{V}$
          $= 4.2\,\text{mV}$

          Answer

          $4.2\,\text{mV}$

          Two significant figures like the field $50\,\mu\text{T}$.

          Reasonableness

          The Earth's field is about one ten-thousandth of the field of a laboratory magnet, so despite the high speed the voltage stays in the millivolt range. At a cyclist's speed of $20\,\text{km/h}$ the voltage would be a tenth of this, about $0.4\,\text{mV}$.

        2. Exercise 5-11

          The Earth's magnetic field is measured with a flip coil. The coil has $100$ turns and an area of $0.020\,\text{m}^2$. The coil is at first in a position in which its plane is perpendicular to the Earth's magnetic field. The coil is turned through half a turn in $0.10\,\text{s}$, and an average induced voltage of $2.0\,\text{mV}$ appears in the coil during this time.

          How large is the magnetic flux density of the Earth's magnetic field? Give the answer in microteslas.

          µT
          Answer

          $50\,\mu\text{T}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 100$, area $A = 0.020\,\text{m}^2$, plane of the coil at first perpendicular to the field, half-turn in the time $\Delta t = 0.10\,\text{s}$, average voltage $|e| = 2.0\,\text{mV}$

          Find

          magnetic flux density $B$ of the Earth's magnetic field in microteslas

          Principle

          At first the flux through one turn is $\Phi_1 = BA$. After a half-turn the same field passes through the turn from the opposite direction, so the flux is $\Phi_2 = -BA$. The magnitude of the change in flux is therefore $\Delta\Phi = 2BA$, and according to Faraday's law the average voltage is $N \cdot 2BA : \Delta t$.

          Formula

          $|e| = N\dfrac{2BA}{\Delta t}$, so $B = \dfrac{|e|\,\Delta t}{2NA}$

          Conversions

          $|e|$$= 2.0\,\text{mV}$
          $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{V}$

          the answer is converted to microteslas: $1\,\text{T} = 10^{6}\,\mu\text{T}$

          Substitution

          $B$$= \dfrac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{V} \cdot 0.10\,\text{s}}{2 \cdot 100 \cdot 0.020\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{4.0\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= 5.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$
          $= 50\,\mu\text{T}$

          Answer

          $50\,\mu\text{T}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage $2.0\,\text{mV}$.

          Reasonableness

          The Earth's magnetic field in Finland is about $50\,\mu\text{T}$, so the result is believable. If the change in flux had wrongly been taken as $BA$, the result would be twice as great, $100\,\mu\text{T}$, which is too large.

        3. Exercise 5-12

          A bar magnet is dropped through a vertical coil of $300$ turns, and the voltage of the coil is measured with a data logger. When the magnet enters the coil, the measurement shows a voltage pulse that is roughly triangular: the voltage rises from zero to a peak value of $0.90\,\text{V}$ and falls back to zero, and the pulse lasts $40\,\text{ms}$ in all. Before the drop the magnet is far from the coil.

          How large is the magnetic flux through one turn on average when the pulse has ended and the magnet is in the middle of the coil? Give the answer in milliwebers.

          mWb
          Answer

          $0.060\,\text{mWb}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          number of turns $N = 300$, triangular voltage pulse, peak value $e_0 = 0.90\,\text{V}$, duration $\Delta t = 40\,\text{ms}$; at first the magnet is far away, so the flux is zero

          Find

          average magnetic flux $\Phi$ through one turn in milliwebers when the magnet is in the middle of the coil

          Principle

          Faraday's law implies that the product of the voltage and the duration of a short interval is $N$ times the change in flux. When the intervals of the pulse are added up, the area between the voltage graph $(t, e)$ and the time axis is $N\Delta\Phi$. At the end of the pulse the voltage is zero, because the flux is at its greatest and no longer growing; the magnet is then in the middle of the coil. The flux has grown from zero to this value, so $\Delta\Phi = \Phi$.

          Formula

          the area of the triangle is $N\Phi = \tfrac{1}{2} e_0 \Delta t$, so

          $\Phi$$= \dfrac{e_0 \Delta t}{2 \cdot N}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta t$$= 40\,\text{ms}$
          $= 40 \cdot 0.001\,\text{s}$
          $= 0.040\,\text{s}$

          the answer is converted to milliwebers: $1\,\text{Wb} = 1000\,\text{mWb}$

          Substitution

          $\Phi$$= \dfrac{0.90\,\text{V} \cdot 0.040\,\text{s}}{2 \cdot 300}$
          $= \dfrac{0.036\,\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{600}$
          $= 6.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{Wb}$
          $= 0.060\,\text{mWb}$

          Answer

          $0.060\,\text{mWb}$

          Two significant figures like the peak value $0.90\,\text{V}$ and the duration $40\,\text{ms}$.

          Reasonableness

          If the cross-sectional area of the coil is about $2\,\text{cm}^2 = 2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$, the average field through a turn is $6.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{Wb} : 2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2 = 0.3\,\text{T}$, which suits a small strong magnet. When the magnet leaves the coil, a pulse of opposite sign appears, with an equal area.
