## fy6-11-L1-01
**Given:** charge of the ball $q = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$, electric force $F = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the electric field strength $E$ in the unit $\text{N/C}$
**Principle:** The electric field strength is the force on a charge brought to the point, divided by that charge. The ball is small, so it is assumed to measure the field without changing it.
**Formula:** $F = qE$, so $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the force is in newtons and the charge in coulombs.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}}{3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$
**Answer:** $4.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$, two significant figures like the force and the charge.
**Reasonableness:** Check by multiplying: $3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$, which is the given force. The field is of the same order of magnitude as a voltage of a few kilovolts across a plate gap of a few centimetres, which is a typical field in a school experiment.

## fy6-11-L1-02
**Given:** electric field strength $E = 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$, charge of the ball $q = 5.0\,\text{nC}$
**Find:** the electric force $F$ on the ball in micronewtons
**Principle:** The electric field strength tells the force per coulomb, so the force on a charge is the charge times the electric field strength. The field is uniform, so the force is the same everywhere in the field.
**Formula:** $F = qE$
**Conversions:** $q = 5.0\,\text{nC} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$; finally $1\,\text{N} = 10^{6}\,\mu\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $F = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N} = 100\,\mu\text{N}$
**Answer:** $1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mu\text{N}$ or $100\,\mu\text{N}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** The force is about $0.1\,\text{mN}$, which corresponds to the weight of a body of about $10\,\text{mg}$. It is small, but a light ball moves clearly under its effect, as is seen in electrostatic experiments.

## fy6-11-L1-03
**Given:** voltage between the plates $U = 600\,\text{V}$, plate distance $d = 0.040\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the electric field strength $E$ in the unit $\text{V/m}$
**Principle:** Between parallel plates the field is uniform. When a charge moves from one plate to the other, the field does the work $qEd$, and according to voltage the same work is $qU$, so the electric field strength is the voltage divided by the plate distance.
**Formula:** $qEd = qU$, so $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the distance is in metres.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{600\,\text{V}}{0.040\,\text{m}} = 15\,000\,\text{V/m}$
**Answer:** $1.5 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{V/m}$, two significant figures like the distance $0.040\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The voltage is divided evenly over the plate gap: for every centimetre there is $600\,\text{V} : 4 = 150\,\text{V}$, and for every metre $15\,000\,\text{V}$. The unit $\text{V/m}$ is the same as $\text{N/C}$.

## fy6-11-L1-04
**Given:** electric field strength $E = 1.0 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{V/m}$, thickness of the membrane $d = 7.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the voltage $U$ across the membrane in millivolts
**Principle:** The surfaces of the membrane have charges of opposite signs like the plates, and inside the membrane the field is approximately uniform. Then the relation $E = U : d$ between the electric field strength and the voltage holds, and the voltage is obtained by solving it.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$, so $U = Ed$
**Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{V} = 1000\,\text{mV}$.
**Substitution:** $U = 1.0 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{V/m} \cdot 7.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m} = 0.070\,\text{V} = 70\,\text{mV}$
**Answer:** $70\,\text{mV}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** The voltage is small because the membrane is very thin, although the field is strong: $10^{7}\,\text{V/m}$ means only $0.01\,\text{V}$ per nanometre. The resting voltage of a nerve cell is indeed about $70\,\text{mV}$.

## fy6-11-L1-05
**Given:** the measurement points of the graph and a straight line fitted to them through the origin; the ball is at the same place in every measurement
**Find:** the electric field strength $E$ in the unit $\text{N/C}$
**Principle:** At the same place the field is the same, so the force is directly proportional to the charge, $F = qE$. The $(q, F)$ graph is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the electric field strength. The slope is read from the fitted line, not from a single measurement point.
**Reading the graph:** The line goes from the origin to the crossing of grid lines $(4.0\,\text{nC};\ 120\,\mu\text{N})$. So the charge grows by $4.0\,\text{nC}$ and the force by $120\,\mu\text{N}$.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{\Delta F}{\Delta q}$
**Conversions:** $120\,\mu\text{N} = 120 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{N}$ and $4.0\,\text{nC} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{120 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{N}}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$
**Answer:** $3.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$, two significant figures, because a crossing of the grid is read from the graph, where the force is $120\,\mu\text{N}$ with two significant figures.
**Reasonableness:** The measurement point $(3.0\,\text{nC};\ 91\,\mu\text{N})$ gives almost the same value: $91\,\mu\text{N} : 3.0\,\text{nC} \approx 3.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$. Because the line goes through the origin, an uncharged ball would feel no force, although the field exists.

## fy6-11-L2-01
**Given:** voltage between the plates $U = 4.5\,\text{kV}$, plate distance $d = 9.0\,\text{mm}$, charge of the ball $q = 4.0\,\text{nC}$
**Find:** the electric force $F$ on the ball in millinewtons
**Principle:** Between the plates the field is uniform, so its strength is obtained from the voltage and the distance. The force on a charge is the charge times the electric field strength, and it does not depend on where between the plates the ball is.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$ and $F = qE$, so $F = \dfrac{qU}{d}$
**Conversions:** $U = 4.5\,\text{kV} = 4.5 \cdot 1000\,\text{V} = 4500\,\text{V}$, $d = 9.0\,\text{mm} = 9.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$, $q = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$; finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{4500\,\text{V}}{9.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m}$ and $F = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N} = 2.0\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** Units: $\text{C} \cdot \text{V/m} = \text{J/m} = \text{N}$. The field $5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m}$ is strong but clearly smaller than the roughly $3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}$ needed for the breakdown of air, so the arrangement is possible.

## fy6-11-L2-02
**Given:** plate distance $d = 5.0\,\text{cm}$, charge of the ball $q = 8.0\,\text{nC}$, electric force $F = 2.4\,\text{mN}$
**Find:** the voltage $U$ between the plates in kilovolts
**Principle:** The electric field strength is obtained from the force and the charge, and in the uniform field between the plates the voltage is the electric field strength times the plate distance.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$ and $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$, so $U = \dfrac{Fd}{q}$
**Conversions:** $F = 2.4\,\text{mN} = 2.4 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$, $d = 5.0\,\text{cm} = 0.050\,\text{m}$, $q = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$; finally $1\,\text{kV} = 1000\,\text{V}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{2.4 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}}{8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{N/C}$ and $U = Ed = 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{N/C} \cdot 0.050\,\text{m} = 15\,000\,\text{V} = 15\,\text{kV}$
**Answer:** $15\,\text{kV}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** Check by multiplying: $8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 15\,000\,\text{V} : 0.050\,\text{m} = 2.4 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$, which is the given force. Units: $\text{N} \cdot \text{m} : \text{C} = \text{J/C} = \text{V}$.

## fy6-11-L2-03
**Given:** voltage $U = 3.0\,\text{kV}$, plate distance $d = 6.0\,\text{mm}$, charge of the particle $q = 1.6 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{C}$ and mass $m = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-14}\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the ratio of the electric force to the weight $F : G$
**Principle:** Between the plates the field is uniform, $E = U : d$, and the electric force $qE$ acts on the particle. Gravity is $mg$. The ratio tells which force determines the motion of the particle.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{F}{G} = \dfrac{qE}{mg} = \dfrac{qU}{mgd}$
**Conversions:** $U = 3.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{V} = 3000\,\text{V}$, $d = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $F = \dfrac{1.6 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{C} \cdot 3000\,\text{V}}{6.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{N}$, $G = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-14}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 3.924 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{N}$ and $\dfrac{F}{G} = \dfrac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{N}}{3.924 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{N}} = 20.4$
**Answer:** about $20$ times, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** The ratio has no unit, because both are forces. The electric force is much larger than the weight, so the particle drifts towards a plate and does not just fall, which is a condition for the purifier to work.

## fy6-11-L2-04
**Given:** at first the force $F_1 = 0.15\,\text{mN}$ and the distance $d_1 = 6.0\,\text{cm}$, at the end the distance $d_2 = 2.0\,\text{cm}$; the voltage and the charge of the ball stay the same
**Find:** the new force $F_2$ in millinewtons
**Principle:** The ball is far from the ends of the plates, so the field is uniform and $E = U : d$. The force is $F = qE = qU : d$, and when $q$ and $U$ stay the same, the force is inversely proportional to the plate distance.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{F_2}{F_1} = \dfrac{qU : d_2}{qU : d_1} = \dfrac{d_1}{d_2}$, so $F_2 = F_1 \cdot \dfrac{d_1}{d_2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the distances are in the same unit, so the unit cancels.
**Substitution:** $F_2 = 0.15\,\text{mN} \cdot \dfrac{6.0\,\text{cm}}{2.0\,\text{cm}} = 0.15\,\text{mN} \cdot 3.0 = 0.45\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $0.45\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** A distance reduced to one third triples the field, because the same voltage is spread over one third of the distance. The force does not grow ninefold, as it would by Coulomb's law for point charges, because the field of plates does not depend on the distance from a plate.

## fy6-11-L3-01
**Given:** at first the voltage $U_1 = 450\,\text{V}$ and the charge $q_1 = 3e$, at the end the charge $q_2 = 4e$; the mass and the plate distance stay the same
**Find:** the voltage $U_2$ at which the droplet hovers
**Principle:** A hovering droplet is at rest, so the electric force up and the weight down are equal: $qE = mg$. The field of the plates is uniform, $E = U : d$. When $m$, $g$ and $d$ stay the same, the product $qU$ must stay the same: a larger charge needs a smaller voltage.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{q_1 U_1}{d} = mg = \dfrac{q_2 U_2}{d}$, so $U_2 = U_1 \cdot \dfrac{q_1}{q_2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the elementary charge $e$ cancels.
**Substitution:** $U_2 = 450\,\text{V} \cdot \dfrac{3e}{4e} = 450\,\text{V} \cdot 0.75 = 337.5\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $3.4 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{V}$ ($337.5\,\text{V}$), rounded to two significant figures according to the precision of the voltage $450\,\text{V}$; the numbers of elementary charges are exact.
**Reasonableness:** The charge grows by one third, so the same electric force is obtained with a weaker field, and the voltage decreases by one quarter. If the voltage were kept at $450\,\text{V}$, the electric force would be $\tfrac{4}{3}$ of the weight, and the droplet would start to rise towards the positive plate.

## fy6-11-L3-02
**Given:** voltage $U = 50\,\text{V}$, plate distance $d = 1.0\,\text{cm}$, mass of the electron $m = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$ and charge $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$; initial speed $v_0 = 0$
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the electron at the positive plate in kilometres per second
**Principle:** In a uniform field a constant force $eE$ acts on the electron towards the positive plate, and the plates are in vacuum, so there are no other forces (the weight is negligibly small). By Newton's second law the acceleration $a = eE : m$ is constant, so the motion is uniformly accelerated, and for a body that has travelled the distance $d$ from rest $v^2 = 2ad$ holds.
**Formula:** $a = \dfrac{eE}{m} = \dfrac{eU}{md}$ and $v^2 = 2ad$, so $v = \sqrt{2ad} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m}}$
**Conversions:** $d = 1.0\,\text{cm} = 0.010\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{km/s} = 1000\,\text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $E = \dfrac{50\,\text{V}}{0.010\,\text{m}} = 5000\,\text{V/m}$, $a = \dfrac{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 5000\,\text{V/m}}{9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}} = 8.794 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 8.794 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.010\,\text{m}} = 4.19 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** about $4200\,\text{km/s}$ or $4.2 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the voltage and the distance.
**Reasonableness:** The speed is about $1.4\,\%$ of the speed of light, so classical mechanics is enough. The final result $\sqrt{2eU : m}$ does not depend on the plate distance: the shorter distance is compensated by the stronger field.

## fy6-11-L3-03
**Given:** mass of the ball $m = 2.0\,\text{g}$, electric field strength $E = 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{N/C}$ horizontal, angle of the thread from the vertical $\alpha = 10^\circ$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the charge $q$ of the ball in nanocoulombs
**Principle:** The forces on the ball are the weight $mg$ down, the electric force $qE$ horizontally and the tension of the thread along the thread. The ball is at rest, so the sum of the forces is zero. In the vertical direction the vertical component of the tension, $T\cos\alpha$, cancels the weight, and in the horizontal direction its horizontal component, $T\sin\alpha$, cancels the electric force.
**Formula:** $T\sin\alpha = qE$ and $T\cos\alpha = mg$; dividing gives $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{qE}{mg}$, so $q = \dfrac{mg\tan\alpha}{E}$
**Conversions:** $m = 2.0\,\text{g} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$; finally $1\,\text{C} = 10^{9}\,\text{nC}$.
**Substitution:** $mg\tan\alpha = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot \tan 10^\circ = 3.459 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$ and $q = \dfrac{3.459 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}}{1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{N/C}} = 2.306 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{C} = 23\,\text{nC}$
**Answer:** $23\,\text{nC}$, two significant figures like the mass and the electric field strength.
**Reasonableness:** The electric force is $mg\tan 10^\circ \approx 0.18 \cdot 0.020\,\text{N} = 3.5\,\text{mN}$, about one fifth of the weight, which fits a small deflection. A charge of tens of nanocoulombs is typical for a plastic ball charged by rubbing.
