Heat and changes of state

Why does it take so long to melt snow into drinking water over a campfire, although the snow is only a little below freezing? Which takes more energy: melting it or heating the meltwater to boiling?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve problems on the quantity of heat, changes of state and thermal balance using conservation of energy
  • draw and interpret heating curves and graphs of measured data
  • assess the results, sources of error and claims of heat experiments with justification

Prior knowledge

  • A temperature difference is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsiuschapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature
  • Heat is the transfer of energy because of a temperature difference, and $\Delta U = Q + W$chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy
  • The quantity of heat is $Q = cm\Delta T$ when the state does not changechapter Heating and cooling of matter
  • Melting and vaporisation need the energy $Q = sm$ and $Q = rm$ at constant temperaturechapter Changes of state
  • The power conducted through a building element is $P = UA\Delta T$chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change

1Think about it

Hikers are at a lean-to shelter in Lapland, and there is no liquid water nearby. They fill a sooty pot with snow and hang it over the campfire. The temperature of the snow is $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. First the pot holds only snow, then slush, then water that finally boils. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the situation is described, but the formulas are not.

Which stage takes more of the campfire's energy: turning a kilogram of snow into water, or heating the same water from $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to boiling? Why?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting point 1: change in temperature

In the morning the temperature of the air in a room rises from $18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $23\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. By how many kelvins does the temperature rise?

  • $296\,\text{K}$ is the final temperature, not the change. The change is the difference between the final and initial temperatures, and it is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius: $5\,\text{K}$. Review the chapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature.

  • The number $273$ is added when one temperature is converted to kelvins, not to a temperature difference. The addition to both readings cancels in the difference: $296\,\text{K} - 291\,\text{K} = 5\,\text{K}$. Review the chapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature.

  • Correct. The scales have different zero points but the same size of degree, so the temperature difference is the same: $23\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 18\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 5\,\text{K}$. If needed, review the chapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature.

Starting point 2: tea and a lake

A cup holds $0.2\,\text{kg}$ of tea at $80\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. In winter the water of a lake is at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ near the bottom. Which statement is correct?

  • Correct. Internal energy is the sum of the energies of all the particles, so the huge amount of water in the lake contains much more internal energy than a cup of tea. Temperature only tells how vigorous the thermal motion is. If needed, review the chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy.

  • A body does not contain heat: heat is energy that is transferred because of a temperature difference. The energy stored is internal energy, and it also depends on the amount of substance. Review the chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy.

  • The same substance and state do not make the internal energy equal. Internal energy depends both on the temperature and on the amount of substance, and the lake holds millions of times more water. Review the chapter Internal energy, heat and conservation of energy.

Starting point 3: sand and water

The Sun gives the same quantity of heat to a kilogram of sand ($c = 800\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$) and to a kilogram of water ($c = 4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$). What happens to the temperatures?

  • A large $c$ means that a lot of energy is needed to raise the temperature by one degree. With the same quantity of heat the water therefore warms up less: $\Delta T = \tfrac{Q}{cm}$. Review the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

  • Correct. $\Delta T = \tfrac{Q}{cm}$, so with the same $Q$ and $m$ the rise in temperature is inversely proportional to $c$: $4190 : 800 \approx 5.2$. If needed, review the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

  • The change in temperature depends on the substance as well as the mass: $\Delta T = \tfrac{Q}{cm}$. The $c$ of sand is about one fifth of the value for water. Review the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

Starting point 4: the hob on full

Water is boiling in a pan at normal pressure. The power of the hob is doubled. What happens?

  • During boiling the extra energy vaporises water and does not raise the temperature. At normal pressure the water stays at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ until it has boiled away. Review the chapter Changes of state.

  • Correct. During a change of state at constant pressure the temperature stays constant. A larger power vaporises water faster: $\tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = \tfrac{P}{r}$ when the losses are small. If needed, review the chapter Changes of state.

  • The temperature stays the same, but the energy does not disappear: the extra power goes into vaporisation, so the water boils away faster. Review the chapter Changes of state.

Starting point 5: ice and water

An insulated container holds $0.10\,\text{kg}$ of ice at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $0.10\,\text{kg}$ of water at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What is the final state?

  • The heat is not enough to melt all the ice: the water gives about $8.4\,\text{kJ}$, and melting would need $33.4\,\text{kJ}$. Some ice is left. Review the chapter Thermal balance during a change of state.

  • Correct. The $8.4\,\text{kJ}$ from the water melts only about $25\,\text{g}$ of ice. When the container holds both ice and water, the temperature is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. If needed, review the chapter Thermal balance during a change of state.

  • The ice is already at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so it cannot cool the water below $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ or freeze it. Heat flows from the water to the ice, and some of the ice melts. Review the chapter Thermal balance during a change of state.

  • The average forgets the latent heat of fusion. When the water cools to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ it gives only $cm\Delta T \approx 8.4\,\text{kJ}$, but melting the ice needs $sm = 33.4\,\text{kJ}$. Review the chapter Thermal balance during a change of state.

Starting point 6: a hole in a plate

A steel plate has a round hole in it. The plate is heated evenly to $200\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. What happens to the hole?

  • A body expands in all directions in the same proportion, like an enlarged photograph. The spacing of the particles at the edge of the hole grows, so the circumference of the hole gets longer and the hole gets bigger. Review the chapter Thermal expansion.

  • There is no material in the hole, but there is at its edge. The steel of the edge lengthens like the rest of the plate, so the diameter of the hole grows: $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$. Review the chapter Thermal expansion.

  • Correct. Thermal expansion enlarges all dimensions in the same proportion, including the diameter of the hole. This is used in shrink fitting. If needed, review the chapter Thermal expansion.

4Theory

Key idea

In thermal physics three concepts must be kept apart. Temperature describes how vigorous the thermal motion of the particles is. Internal energy is the sum of the kinetic energy of the particles and the energy of their interactions, so it also depends on the amount of substance and on its state. Heat is the transfer of energy because of a temperature difference: a body has a temperature and internal energy, but it has no heat.

Every problem of the lesson comes back to conservation of energy. Heat transferred to a substance either raises its temperature or changes its state, and as the temperature rises the substance also expands. In an isolated system the heat released by the hot part equals the heat received by the cold part. The temperature of a house stays constant when the power coming in equals the power going out.

In the exam, follow the energy first: where it comes from, where it goes and whether the state changes on the way. Only then choose the formula.

Quantity of heat and energy balance

Temperature and internal energy

$$T = \theta + 273.15$$

where $T$ is the temperature in kelvins ($\text{K}$) and $\theta$ in degrees Celsius (${}^\circ\text{C}$). A temperature difference is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius, but ratios of temperatures are calculated in kelvins. Internal energy is changed by heat and work:

$$\Delta U = Q + W$$

where $\Delta U$ is the change in internal energy, $Q$ the quantity of heat transferred to the system and $W$ the work done on the system, all in joules. Energy coming into the system is positive. The equation holds when the kinetic and potential energy of the system do not change.

Heating and change of state

$$Q = cm\Delta T$$

where $c$ is the specific heat capacity ($\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$), $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$) and $\Delta T$ the change in temperature ($\text{K}$). The formula holds only when the state does not change. In melting and vaporisation

$$Q = sm \qquad Q = rm$$

where $s$ is the specific latent heat of fusion and $r$ the specific latent heat of vaporisation ($\text{J/kg}$). At constant pressure the temperature of a pure substance stays constant during the change, because the energy increases the interaction energy of the particles. The same energy is released in solidification and condensation. For water $c = 4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, for ice $c = 2100\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, $s = 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$ and $r = 2260\,\text{kJ/kg}$.

This gives the answer to the opening question. Warming a kilogram of snow from $-10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ takes only $21\,\text{kJ}$, but melting it takes $334\,\text{kJ}$, almost as much as heating the meltwater to boiling, $419\,\text{kJ}$.

Thermal balance

In an insulated container energy is conserved:

$$Q_\text{released} = Q_\text{received}$$

When ice and water are mixed, first compare whether the heat the water releases as it cools to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is enough to warm the ice to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and melt it. If it is, all the ice melts and the final temperature is at least $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. If it is not, some ice is left and the temperature is exactly $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Very cold ice can also freeze some or all of the water.

Expansion and heat transfer

$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T \qquad \Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$$

where $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are the coefficients of linear and volume expansion ($1/\text{K}$) and $L_0$ and $V_0$ the original length and volume. For solids the formulas hold up to a change in temperature of about $100\,\text{K}$, and the $\gamma$ of a liquid is usable only near the temperature given in the table. Water is an exception: its density is greatest at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

Heat is transferred by conduction, convection and radiation, with a net flow from the warmer body to the colder one. The power conducted through a building element is

$$P = UA\Delta T$$

where $U$ is the U-value ($\text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$), $A$ the area ($\text{m}^2$) and $\Delta T$ the difference between the indoor and outdoor temperatures ($\text{K}$). The formula holds when the temperatures stay constant.

Common mistakes

  • Heat and temperature mixed up. "The coffee contains a lot of heat" means internal energy in physics. Heat is energy that is transferred.
  • $Q = cm\Delta T$ in a change of state. During melting the temperature does not change, but the energy $sm$ is used.
  • The final temperature as an average. The initial temperatures are weighted by the heat capacities $cm$, and ice also needs the latent heat of fusion.
  • Kelvins in the wrong place. The number $273$ is not added to a temperature difference, but ratios of temperatures need kelvins.
  • A hole shrinks when heated. A body expands like an enlarged photograph, so a hole gets bigger too.
  • Cold "flows" in. Energy flows from warm to cold. Cold is neither a substance nor energy.
  • Losses are forgotten. In a measurement some of the energy is transferred to the surroundings. Always think about which way a loss changes the result.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the error: melting ice

A student calculated how much energy is needed to melt a $0.50\,\text{kg}$ block of ice that is already at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$: "$Q = cm\Delta T = 2100\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 0\,\text{K} = 0\,\text{J}$." What is wrong in the solution?

  • If ice melted without energy, it would not stay ice in a room at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Melting increases the interaction energy between the particles, and that needs $Q = sm$.

  • A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius, and during melting the temperature does not change at all. The error is in the formula: melting takes the energy $Q = sm$.

  • Correct. $Q = cm\Delta T$ holds only when the state does not change. Melting needs energy although the temperature stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$: $Q = 334\,\text{kJ/kg} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} = 167\,\text{kJ}$.

Justified claim: the railing and the bench

On a frosty morning a park has a metal railing and a wooden bench that have both been outdoors all night. The railing feels much colder to a bare hand than the bench.Which explanation is correct?

  • Correct. Both have the same temperature, but in metal the free electrons carry energy away from under the hand efficiently. Wood is a poor conductor, so its surface warms up under the hand and energy flows slowly.

  • Cold is not transferred: the net flow of energy is from the warmer to the colder body, that is, from the hand to the railing. The hand feels cold when energy leaves it quickly.

  • During the night both have reached thermal equilibrium with the outdoor air, so their temperature is the same. The difference is in how fast they conduct energy away from the hand.

Justified claim: the stuck lid

The metal lid of a glass jar is stuck. Hot water is run over the lid for a moment, and then the lid opens more easily.What is the best explanation?

  • Friction hardly depends on temperature in this range. What matters is that the metal lid expands and its thread comes loose from the glass.

  • Glass softens only at temperatures of hundreds of degrees. The hot water mainly warms the lid, and metal expands more than glass.

  • Correct. The coefficient of linear expansion of metal is larger than that of glass (for example steel $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, glass $9 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$), and the water warms the lid first. The diameter of the lid grows more than the mouth of the jar.

Which graph: iced water on the table

A glass holds water and a lot of ice cubes, and the temperature of the mixture is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at first. The glass is left in a room at $22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the temperature is followed for two hours.Which description of the graph of temperature against time is correct?

  • Correct. The temperature of a mixture of ice and water is $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ as long as there is ice. After that the water warms up, and the rise slows down as the temperature difference to the room gets smaller.

  • The ice and the water are already at the same temperature $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so no heat flows between them. The energy from the room melts ice, and the temperature cannot fall.

  • As long as there is ice in the glass, the energy from the room melts ice and does not raise the temperature. The graph is horizontal at first.

Bath water

A bath holds $80.0\,\text{kg}$ of water at $42.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. It is too hot, so cold water at $8.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is added.

How much cold water is needed for the final temperature to be $37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$? Heat losses are ignored. Give the answer in kilograms.

kg
Hint 1: a guiding question

The hot water cools and the cold water warms to the same final temperature. Which one gives out heat, and which one receives it?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Energy is conserved: $c m_1 (T_1 - T) = c m_2 (T - T_2)$. Which factor cancels?

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$m_2$$= \dfrac{m_1 (T_1 - T)}{T - T_2}$
$= \dfrac{80.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 5.0\,\text{K}}{29.0\,\text{K}}$
Show the solution

Given

hot water $m_1 = 80.0\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 42.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; cold water $T_2 = 8.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; final temperature $T = 37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Find

the mass of the cold water $m_2$ in kilograms

Principle

Energy is conserved when there are no losses: the quantity of heat given out by the hot water equals the quantity received by the cold water. Each only cools or warms, so $Q = cm\Delta T$ holds, and $c$ is the same for both (chapter Heating and cooling of matter).

Formula

$c m_1 (T_1 - T) = c m_2 (T - T_2)$, so $m_2 = \dfrac{m_1 (T_1 - T)}{T - T_2}$

Conversions

None needed: the specific heat capacity cancels, and the temperature differences are the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius.

Substitution

$m_2$$= \dfrac{80.0\,\text{kg} \cdot (42.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C})}{37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 8.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}$
$= \dfrac{80.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 5.0\,\text{K}}{29.0\,\text{K}}$
$= 13.79\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$14\,\text{kg}$

Two significant figures, because the cooling of the hot water $42.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 37.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 5.0\,\text{K}$ is, as a difference, known to only two figures.

Reasonableness

The quantities of heat are equal: $c \cdot 80.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 5.0\,\text{K}$ and $c \cdot 13.79\,\text{kg} \cdot 29.0\,\text{K}$, because $80.0 \cdot 5.0 = 400$ and $13.79 \cdot 29.0 \approx 400$. Little cold water is needed, because it warms almost six times more than the hot water cools.

Ice for the drinks

For a party, $12.0\,\text{kg}$ of drinks are cooled from $22.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $6.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ with ice cubes from a freezer at $-18.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

How much ice is needed when all the ice melts and the meltwater warms to $6.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$? The specific heat capacity of the drinks is the same as that of water, and heat losses are ignored. Give the answer in kilograms.

kg
Hint 1: a guiding question

In which three stages does the ice use the energy it gets from the drinks?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The ice warms to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($c_\text{i} m \Delta T$), melts ($sm$) and the meltwater warms to $6.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ ($c_\text{w} m \Delta T$).

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$m_\text{i}$$= \dfrac{c_\text{w} m_\text{w} \cdot 16.0\,\text{K}}{c_\text{i} \cdot 18.0\,\text{K} + s + c_\text{w} \cdot 6.0\,\text{K}}$
Show the solution

Given

drinks $m_\text{w} = 12.0\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{w} = 22.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; ice $T_\text{i} = -18.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; final temperature $T = 6.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c_\text{w} = 4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, $c_\text{i} = 2100\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and $s = 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$

Find

the mass of the ice $m_\text{i}$ in kilograms

Principle

Energy is conserved: the heat given out by the drinks goes into warming the ice to $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, melting it at constant temperature and warming the meltwater to $6.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. For the warming stages $Q = cm\Delta T$ holds, for the melting $Q = sm$ (chapter Thermal balance during a change of state).

Formula

$c_\text{w} m_\text{w} (T_\text{w} - T)$$= m_\text{i}\,(c_\text{i} \Delta T_\text{i} + s + c_\text{w} \Delta T_\text{m})$

so

$m_\text{i}$$= \dfrac{c_\text{w} m_\text{w} (T_\text{w} - T)}{c_\text{i} \Delta T_\text{i} + s + c_\text{w} \Delta T_\text{m}}$

where $\Delta T_\text{i}$ is the warming of the ice and $\Delta T_\text{m}$ that of the meltwater

Conversions

$s$$= 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$
$= 334\,000\,\text{J/kg}$

The temperature differences are the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius: $T_\text{w} - T = 16.0\,\text{K}$, $\Delta T_\text{i} = 18.0\,\text{K}$ and $\Delta T_\text{m} = 6.0\,\text{K}$.

Substitution

$m_\text{i}$$= \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 12.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 16.0\,\text{K}}{(37\,800 + 334\,000 + 25\,140)\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}}$
$= \dfrac{804\,480\,\text{J}}{396\,940\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}}$
$= 2.027\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$2.03\,\text{kg}$

Three significant figures like the mass $12.0\,\text{kg}$ and the cooling of the drinks $16.0\,\text{K}$; the two-figure $6.0\,\text{K}$ belongs to a small term that is only about $6\,\%$ of the denominator.

Reasonableness

Each kilogram of ice takes about $397\,\text{kJ}$, of which melting is $334\,\text{kJ}$. With water at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ alone the same cooling would need

$804\,480\,\text{J} : 25\,140\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
$\approx 32\,\text{kg}$

more than fifteen times the mass, so ice is an efficient coolant.

Radio mast

A steel radio mast is $300\,\text{m}$ tall on a frosty night when its temperature is $-25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. On a summer day the temperature of the mast is $+25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the coefficient of linear expansion of steel is $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$.

How much taller is the mast in summer than in winter? Give the answer in millimetres.

mm
Hint 1: a guiding question

What is the change in the temperature of the mast from winter to summer?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The change in temperature is the difference between the final and initial temperatures: $25\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-25\,{}^\circ\text{C})$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$\Delta L$$= \alpha L_0 \Delta T$
$= 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 300\,\text{m} \cdot 50\,\text{K}$
Show the solution

Given

length of the mast $L_0 = 300\,\text{m}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$; temperatures $T_1 = -25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $T_2 = +25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Find

the change in height $\Delta L$ in millimetres

Principle

As steel warms, the vibration of its particles becomes stronger and their average distance grows, so the mast lengthens. The steel stays solid, and the change $50\,\text{K}$ is less than a hundred kelvins, so $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$ is a good model (chapter Thermal expansion).

Formula

$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_2 - T_1$

Conversions

$\Delta T$$= 25\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-25\,{}^\circ\text{C})$
$= 50\,\text{K}$

the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius. Finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.

Substitution

$\Delta L$$= 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 300\,\text{m} \cdot 50\,\text{K}$
$= 0.18\,\text{m}$
$= 0.18 \cdot 1000\,\text{mm}$
$= 180\,\text{mm}$

Answer

$180\,\text{mm}$

That is $18\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the coefficient of expansion $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ and the temperatures.

Reasonableness

Steel lengthens by about one hundred-thousandth of its length per kelvin: for a $300\,\text{m}$ mast that is $3.6\,\text{mm}$ per kelvin and $180\,\text{mm}$ for $50\,\text{K}$. The change is less than a thousandth of the height of the mast, but the anchorages of the guy wires that support the mast must still give way.

Power cut in the frost

The heating of a detached house stops because of a power cut. The house loses heat at $180\,\text{W}$ for every kelvin of difference between the indoor and outdoor temperatures. It is $21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ indoors and $-20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ outdoors. The combined heat capacity of the structures, furniture and air of the house is $30\,\text{MJ/K}$.

By how many kelvins does the indoor temperature fall during the first hour? Give the answer in kelvins.

K
Hint 1: a guiding question

At what power is heat conducted out of the house at the start of the power cut?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The power is $P = 180\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{K}} \cdot \Delta T$, and the energy $Q = Pt$ transferred in an hour lowers the temperature by $\tfrac{Q}{C}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$\Delta T_\text{in}$$= \dfrac{P\,t}{C}$
$= \dfrac{7380\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}}{30 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/K}}$
Show the solution

Given

heat loss $180\,\text{W}$ per kelvin, $T_\text{in} = 21\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{out} = -20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, heat capacity $C = 30\,\text{MJ/K}$, time $t = 1\,\text{h}$

Find

the fall in the indoor temperature $\Delta T_\text{in}$ in kelvins

Principle

Heat is conducted out at a power proportional to the temperature difference, like $P = UA\Delta T$. With no heating there is no energy balance: the energy leaving reduces the internal energy of the house, and the temperature falls according to $Q = C\,\Delta T$ (chapters Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change and Heating and cooling of matter). During the first hour the indoor temperature changes by less than a kelvin, so the power is almost constant.

Formula

$P = 180\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{K}} \cdot (T_\text{in} - T_\text{out})$ and $P\,t = C\,\Delta T_\text{in}$, so $\Delta T_\text{in} = \dfrac{P\,t}{C}$

Conversions

$1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$ and $30\,\text{MJ/K} = 30 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/K}$. The temperature difference is the same in kelvins: $21\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-20\,{}^\circ\text{C}) = 41\,\text{K}$.

Substitution

$P$$= 180\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{K}} \cdot 41\,\text{K}$
$= 7380\,\text{W}$
$\Delta T_\text{in}$$= \dfrac{7380\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}}{30 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/K}}$
$= 0.886\,\text{K}$

Answer

about $0.89\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the heat capacity $30\,\text{MJ/K}$ and the temperature difference $41\,\text{K}$.

Reasonableness

Less than a degree in an hour fits experience: a house cools slowly even in a hard frost, because its heat capacity is large. The fall slows down as the temperature difference gets smaller, so the result is only an estimate for the first hour. At the initial rate the temperature would fall by about $21\,\text{K}$ in a day, in reality less.

$= \dfrac{t}{r}$

Model answer

a)

Find

why a lake freezes from the surface and why the water at the bottom is at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

Principle

Most substances expand as they warm, but water contracts between $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so its density is greatest at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Denser water sinks and lighter water rises (chapter Thermal expansion).

Model answer

In autumn cold air cools the surface of the lake. As long as the surface water is above $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, it becomes denser as it cools and sinks to the bottom, and warmer water rises in its place. In this way the whole lake gradually cools to about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. When the surface cools below this, the water starts to expand as it cools: the colder water is now lighter than the water at $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at the bottom, so it stays at the surface and freezes there. The densest water, at about $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, stays at the bottom and does not rise to the surface to cool. In addition, ice is lighter than water and floats, so the lake freezes from the surface downwards and not from the bottom upwards.

b)

Find

where the energy of the freezing water goes and why the ice grows more slowly when it is thick

Principle

When water freezes it releases the latent heat of fusion $Q = sm$, although the temperature stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ (chapter Changes of state). Heat is conducted from warm to cold, and for the same temperature difference the conducted power is smaller the thicker the layer is (chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change).

Model answer

At the lower surface of the ice the water is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. For it to freeze, the latent heat of fusion must leave it, about $334\,\text{kJ}$ for every kilogram; a layer one centimetre thick on one square metre needs about $3\,\text{MJ}$. Energy is conserved, so this energy must be conducted through the ice to the cold air, and the ice grows only as fast as the energy is conducted away. The power conducted through the ice depends on the temperature difference between the frosty air and the lower surface at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and on the thickness of the layer. A thick layer insulates better, that is, its U-value is smaller, so in the same frost a smaller power is conducted through it. That is why thin autumn ice thickens quickly, but $40\,\text{cm}$ ice grows slowly in the same frost.

c)

Find

whether a snow cover strengthens the ice

Principle

Snow contains a lot of air, and air is a poor conductor of heat (chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change). For the ice to grow, the energy released in freezing must be conducted away (part b).

Model answer

The claim is wrong. Snow does not transfer "cold" to the ice: energy always flows from warmer to colder, that is, from the ice through the snow to the air. Snow is a poor conductor of heat, because it contains a lot of air. On top of the ice it acts as an insulator, like wool in a wall, so less energy is conducted away from the lower surface of the ice and less water freezes. Under a thick snow cover the ice therefore grows more slowly and stays thinner than bare ice. In addition, heavy snow can press the ice down, so that water rises through cracks on top of the ice and weak slush or snow ice forms. Snow weakens the ice rather than strengthening it.

Common mistakes

  • In part a it is only said that "ice floats", but it is not explained why the water at the bottom is at $4\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
  • It is claimed that cold water is always denser than warm water.
  • In part b it is forgotten that freezing releases energy, and the growth is explained by temperature alone.
  • In part c the idea of cold moving from the snow to the ice is accepted.

Given

mass of the car $m = 1600\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 90\,\text{km/h}$; mass of the discs $m_\text{discs} = 30\,\text{kg}$, $c = 450\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$; share transferred to the discs $85\,\%$

Find

the rise in temperature $\Delta T$ of the discs

Principle

On a level road braking turns the kinetic energy of the car, through friction, into internal energy of the brakes; energy is conserved (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). The share of air resistance in a short braking is small, so it is ignored. The discs stay solid, so the heat they receive raises their temperature according to $Q = cm\Delta T$.

Formula

$E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ and $0.85\,E_\text{k} = c\,m_\text{discs}\,\Delta T$, so

$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{0.85 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2}{c\,m_\text{discs}}$

Conversions

$90\,\text{km/h}$$= 90 \cdot \dfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 25\,\text{m/s}$

$85\,\% = 0.85$.

Substitution

$E_\text{k}$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1600\,\text{kg} \cdot (25\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}})^2$
$= 500\,000\,\text{J}$
$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{0.85 \cdot 500\,000\,\text{J}}{450\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 30\,\text{kg}}$
$= \dfrac{425\,000\,\text{J}}{13\,500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}}$
$= 31.5\,\text{K}$

Answer

about $31\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the mass of the discs $30\,\text{kg}$ and the speed $90\,\text{km/h}$.

Reasonableness

One stop heats the discs by a few tens of degrees, which fits experience: brake discs are hot after hard braking but do not glow. Air resistance would slightly reduce the energy reaching the discs, so the result is an upper limit.

b)

Given

drop $h = 250\,\text{m}$, distance $s = 4.0\,\text{km}$, driving resistance $F = 450\,\text{N}$, constant speed; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the data of part a

Find

the rise in temperature $\Delta T$ of the discs without cooling

Principle

The speed is constant, so the kinetic energy does not change. By the work–energy principle the decrease in potential energy $mgh$ goes into the work $Fs$ of the driving resistances and into the brakes (chapters Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy and Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle). Of the energy reaching the brakes, $85\,\%$ heats the discs.

Formula

$Q_\text{brakes} = mgh - Fs$ and $0.85\,Q_\text{brakes} = c\,m_\text{discs}\,\Delta T$, so

$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{0.85\,(mgh - Fs)}{c\,m_\text{discs}}$

Conversions

$4.0\,\text{km}$$= 4.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}$
$= 4000\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$mgh$$= 1600\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 250\,\text{m}$
$= 3\,924\,000\,\text{J}$
$Fs$$= 450\,\text{N} \cdot 4000\,\text{m}$
$= 1\,800\,000\,\text{J}$
$\Delta T$$= \dfrac{0.85 \cdot 2\,124\,000\,\text{J}}{13\,500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}}$
$= \dfrac{1\,805\,400\,\text{J}}{13\,500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}}$
$= 133.7\,\text{K}$

Answer

about $1.3 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{K}$, that is $130\,\text{K}$, two significant figures like the distance $4.0\,\text{km}$.

Reasonableness

The downhill releases about four times more energy to the brakes than one stop from $90\,\text{km/h}$ ($1.81\,\text{MJ}$ and $0.43\,\text{MJ}$), so the rise is about four times larger. Without cooling the discs would heat to about $150\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, which is possible but already near the upper end of the normal operating range of an ordinary brake.

c)

Given

the energy reaching the discs in part b $Q = 1.805\,\text{MJ}$; the temperature of the discs rose by $80\,\text{K}$; $c\,m_\text{discs} = 13\,500\,\text{J/K}$

Find

the share of the energy transferred to the air, as a percentage, and the modes of transfer

Principle

Energy is conserved: the energy reaching the discs either stayed in the discs and raised their temperature ($Q = cm\Delta T$) or was transferred away during the descent. A hot disc gives out heat by convection to the air flowing past, radiates and conducts heat to the hub and the rim (chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change).

Formula

$Q_\text{air} = Q - c\,m_\text{discs}\,\Delta T$ and share $= \dfrac{Q_\text{air}}{Q}$

Conversions

None needed: the energies are in joules, and the ratio is turned into a percentage by multiplying by $100\,\%$.

Substitution

$Q_\text{air}$$= 1\,805\,400\,\text{J} - 13\,500\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}} \cdot 80\,\text{K}$
$= 1\,805\,400\,\text{J} - 1\,080\,000\,\text{J}$
$= 725\,400\,\text{J}$

and share $= \dfrac{725\,400\,\text{J}}{1\,805\,400\,\text{J}} = 0.402 = 40.2\,\%$

Answer

about $40\,\%$, two significant figures like the result of part b. The energy was transferred mainly by convection to the air flowing past the disc, in addition by thermal radiation and by conduction through the hub and the rim.

Reasonableness

The descent takes $4000\,\text{m} : 16.7\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 240\,\text{s}$, and on average about $7.5\,\text{kW}$ reaches the discs. On average $725\,\text{kJ} : 240\,\text{s} \approx 3\,\text{kW}$ was transferred to the air, which is believable for four hot discs in the airflow.

d)

Find

why engine braking spares the brakes, in terms of energy

Principle

Energy is conserved, so the decrease in potential energy turns into internal energy somewhere. In engine braking the wheels turn the engine, whose friction opposes the motion (chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle).

Model answer

In engine braking the potential energy does not disappear, but turns into internal energy of the engine through the friction of the engine. The cooling system of the engine is made to transfer large thermal powers to the air, so the engine does not overheat. Then little energy reaches the brake discs, they stay cool, and the brakes work at full power when they are really needed. The advice is therefore justified: the energy does not decrease, but the body it heats changes from the brakes to the engine.

Common mistakes

  • The speed is not converted to metres per second, so the kinetic energy is more than ten times too large.
  • In part b all the potential energy is given to the brakes, and the result is about $250\,\text{K}$.
  • In part b the kinetic energy is included, although the speed is constant.
  • In part c it is said that the energy "was used up" or "disappeared", without saying where it was transferred.
  • In part d it is claimed that in engine braking the energy does not turn into heat at all.

Also accepted

  • In part a the answer $31.5\,\text{K}$ or $32\,\text{K}$, when justified by rounding.

  1. The work $200\,\text{J}$ is done on a gas, and at the same time $50\,\text{J}$ of heat flows from it to the surroundings. How much does the internal energy of the gas change?
    Answer

    $\Delta U = Q + W = -50\,\text{J} + 200\,\text{J} = 150\,\text{J}$: the internal energy grows by $150\,\text{J}$. Work done on the system is positive and heat leaving the system is negative.

  2. A steel measuring tape has been calibrated at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. It is used to measure a board in a frost of $-15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Does the tape show too large or too small a length?
    Answer

    Too large. Wood shrinks along the grain much less than steel, so the board's own change is small. In the frost the steel tape shortens, so the intervals of its scale are shorter than at calibration, and more of the scale fits along the length of the board. For example, over a distance of $10\,\text{m}$ the difference is $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 10\,\text{m} \cdot 35\,\text{K} \approx 4\,\text{mm}$.

In an insulated container, ice at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ melts into water at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Which statement is correct?

3. Calculation task

A gutter holds $12\,\text{m}$ of heating cable with a power of $30\,\text{W}$ per metre. The cable melts snow at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ that has collected in the gutter, and all its energy goes into melting.

How many kilograms of snow does the cable melt in an hour? Give the answer in kilograms.

kg
Hint 1: a guiding question

How much energy does the cable give in an hour?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The power of the cable is $P = 12\,\text{m} \cdot 30\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}}$ and the energy $Q = Pt$. The snow is already at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the energy goes into melting: $Q = sm$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$m$$= \dfrac{P\,t}{s}$
$= \dfrac{360\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}}{334\,000\,\text{J/kg}}$
Show the solution

Given

length of the cable $12\,\text{m}$ and power $30\,\text{W}$ per metre, time $t = 1\,\text{h}$; the snow is at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $s = 334\,\text{kJ/kg}$

Find

the mass $m$ of snow melted, in kilograms

Principle

The snow is already at its melting point, so the energy of the cable does not raise the temperature but melts snow at constant temperature: $Q = sm$. The power of the cable is constant, so the energy it gives is $Q = Pt$ (chapter Changes of state).

Formula

$P\,t = s m$, so $m = \dfrac{P\,t}{s}$

Conversions

$P$$= 12\,\text{m} \cdot 30\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}}$
$= 360\,\text{W}$

$1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$ and $334\,\text{kJ/kg} = 334\,000\,\text{J/kg}$.

Substitution

$m$$= \dfrac{360\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}}{334\,000\,\text{J/kg}}$
$= \dfrac{1\,296\,000\,\text{J}}{334\,000\,\text{J/kg}}$
$= 3.88\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$3.9\,\text{kg}$

Two significant figures like the length $12\,\text{m}$ and the power $30\,\text{W}$ per metre.

Reasonableness

In an hour the cable gives $0.36\,\text{kWh}$, that is about $1.3\,\text{MJ}$, and each kilogram of snow needs $0.334\,\text{MJ}$: just under four kilograms. In reality some of the energy warms the air and the gutter, so a little less melts.

4. Explanation task

Two identical glasses of juice are cooled: $50\,\text{g}$ of ice cubes at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is added to one, and $50\,\text{g}$ of water at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to the other.

Which juice cools more? Explain using conservation of energy, and estimate the difference with numbers.

Model answer

The juice cooled with ice cubes cools more. Energy is conserved, so the heat given out by the juice equals the heat received by the added substance. The water at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ only warms up, and each kelvin needs $4.19\,\text{kJ}$ per kilogram. The ice, on the other hand, first takes the latent heat of fusion while its temperature stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$: melting $50\,\text{g}$ takes $334\,\text{kJ/kg} \cdot 0.050\,\text{kg} \approx 17\,\text{kJ}$. The same energy would warm $50\,\text{g}$ of water by about $80\,\text{K}$. Only after melting does the meltwater warm up like the water at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. That is why the ice takes much more energy from the juice, and the temperature of the juice falls more, although the ice and the water were equally cold at first.
