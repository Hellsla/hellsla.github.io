The matriculation exam and answer technique

Why can two answers with the same final result get completely different points?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • write a complete solution with its justifications, units and reasonableness check.
  • assess answers with a marking scheme and find the steps missing from them.
  • solve the questions of the review test and use the results to choose the chapters you review.

Prior knowledge

  • A final result has as many significant figures as the least precise given valuechapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement

1Think about it

Aino and Eetu solve the same problem: how large an average force accelerates a bicycle and its rider from rest. Both end up with the number $71$. Aino's answer is two lines of numbers. Eetu writes what is known, which law applies and why, solves the formula, substitutes the values with their units and compares the result with the rider's weight. The examiner gives Eetu clearly more points.

What must a physics answer contain besides the right number, and why?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting point 1: the unit

A student calculates the speed of a car correctly and writes on the last line of the solution $v = 14$. Which statement about the answer is true?

  • The final result is the most important line of an answer, and it is read on its own. If it has no unit, it is not a quantity. Always write $v \approx 14\,\text{m/s}$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Without a unit the number could mean anything: $14\,\text{m/s}$ and $14\,\text{km/h}$ differ by a factor of almost four. The unit is part of the result, not a decoration. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Speed can be given in many units: $\text{m/s}$, $\text{km/h}$ or knots. That is exactly why the unit tells what the number means. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. A quantity is a number times a unit: $14\,\text{m/s}$ is about $50\,\text{km/h}$, but $14\,\text{km/h}$ is a cycling speed. Without the unit the reader does not know which you mean. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Starting point 2: the justification

In a problem a sledge slides down an icy hill. A student's solution starts straight away with the line $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, followed by the substitution and the answer.What is missing from the solution?

  • Correct. The energy equation $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ holds only if the weight is the only force doing work. Write that friction and air resistance are neglected, and why that is reasonable here: the ice is slippery and the speed is small. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • The law does not have to be derived, but using it has to be justified. One sentence is enough: which law applies and why it applies in exactly this situation. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • A formula does not say whether its conditions hold. If there were friction on the hill, the same line would give too large a speed. The principle and its condition are written out. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

  • A sketch of the situation helps, but it does not say why energy is conserved. The justification says that only the weight does work, because friction and air resistance are neglected. Review this in the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle.

Starting point 3: intermediate results

A calculation has three steps in a row. A student rounds every intermediate result to two significant figures, because the given values have two as well.What follows from this?

  • Rounding does not add precision; it removes information. Intermediate results are kept precise, and the precision is decided only in the final result, by the given values. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. Keep the intermediate results unrounded, or at least a couple of digits more precise, and round only the final result. For example $64 \cdot 1.1 \approx 70$, but $64 \cdot 1.111 \approx 71$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • It is worth writing the intermediate results out, because they show how the solution proceeds. They are just left unrounded, and the final result is rounded. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Every rounding shifts the result a little, and the shifts build up. For example $64 \cdot 1.1 \approx 70$, but with a more precise intermediate result $64 \cdot 1.111 \approx 71$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Starting point 4: precision

The mass of a body is $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and its acceleration $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. For the net force $F = ma$ the calculator shows the number $3$. How is the final result written?

  • The precision is right, but the final result has no unit. Write $F = 3.0\,\text{N}$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Correct. A final result has as many significant figures as the least precise given value. The calculator does not show the trailing zero, but $3.0\,\text{N}$ says that the result is known to a tenth. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • Four significant figures claim the result is more precise than the measurements are. The mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$ is known only to two figures, so the answer is $3.0\,\text{N}$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

  • A calculator leaves out trailing zeros. $3\,\text{N}$ shows a precision of one significant figure, although the given values have two. Write $3.0\,\text{N}$. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

Starting point 5: the slope

A problem asks you to determine the speed of a trolley graphically from a $(t, x)$ graph. There are seven measured points, and a straight line has been fitted to them.Where should the speed be read from?

  • The mean of the positions does not tell how fast the position changes. Speed is the change in position divided by the change in time, that is the slope of the fitted line. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • The division $x : t$ gives the speed only if the line passes through the origin. When the trolley does not start from the position $x = 0$, the result is wrong. The slope is read from the line. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Single points contain measurement error, and in the difference of two neighbouring points it is magnified. The fit smooths out the random variation, so the slope is read from the line. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. The fit gathers the information of all the points, and points far apart make the relative error of reading smaller. Show the points you read in your answer. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

Starting point 6: estimate

A problem asks you to estimate how much energy it takes to heat the water for one shower. The problem gives neither the amount of water nor the temperatures.What is expected of the answer?

  • This is exactly what an estimation task is about: the missing values are chosen from experience, and the choices are justified. Review this in the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

  • An estimate is a solution too: the assumptions and the calculation are shown so that the reader can check where the number comes from. Without them the number is a guess. Review this in the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

  • When the starting values are your own estimates, a precise result claims information that does not exist. Give the result roughly, for example about $5\,\text{MJ}$. Review this in the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

  • Correct. In an estimation task you choose sensible starting values yourself and state them, for example $40\,\text{kg}$ of water warming from $10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The result is given to one or two figures. Review this in the chapter Heating and cooling of matter.

3Investigate

Two answers, one problem

Made-up example data

Problem (6 p.) An electric scooter and its rider, $64\,\text{kg}$ together, accelerate uniformly from rest to the speed $20\,\text{km/h}$ in $5.0\,\text{s}$. How large is the average net force acting on them?

Marking scheme. Each item gives one point: the principle and its justification, the formula solved for the unknown, the unit conversion, the substitution with units (no point without units), the right answer with the right precision and unit, the reasonableness check. The items are the steps of a complete solution, reviewed in the Theory.

Answer A. $20 : 3.6 : 5 = 1.11$ and $64 \cdot 1.11 = 71.04$

Answer B.

  • The net force gives a body an acceleration according to Newton's second law, $F = ma$. The acceleration is uniform, so $a = \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ and $F = \tfrac{m\Delta v}{\Delta t}$.
  • Conversion: $20\,\text{km/h} = \tfrac{20}{3.6}\,\text{m/s} = 5.556\,\text{m/s}$
  • Substitution: $a = \tfrac{5.556\,\text{m/s}}{5.0\,\text{s}} = 1.111\,\text{m/s}^2$
  • $F = 64\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.111\,\text{m/s}^2 = 7.1\,\text{N}$
  • Answer: $F \approx 7.1\,\text{N}$

Mark both answers with the scheme. Which one gets more points, and what should each add or correct?

Model answer

Answer A gets one point: it has the conversion $20 : 3.6$. The principle and its justification are missing, the formula is not solved in symbols, the substitution has no units, and the final result $71.04$ has no unit and too many digits. There is no reasonableness check. Answer B gets four points: the principle is justified, the formula is solved, and the conversion and the substitution with units are right. The last multiplication, however, has a decimal-point error, since $64 \cdot 1.111 \approx 71$, so the point for the answer is lost. There is no reasonableness check, and that is exactly what would have revealed the error: a force of $7.1\,\text{N}$ would give a $64\,\text{kg}$ body an acceleration of only $0.11\,\text{m/s}^2$, not the calculated $1.1\,\text{m/s}^2$. So B gets more points, although A's number is more nearly right. A should write the principle and the steps with units and the answer $F \approx 71\,\text{N}$. B should check the result by comparing it with experience or by calculating the acceleration back.

Assess your own answer

Tick the points your own answer already contains.

    4Theory

    The exam and time management

    The digital exam

    The physics exam is taken on a computer. The answer is written as text, formulas with the formula editor, and a graph or a drawing can be attached to the answer as a screenshot. The exam lasts six hours. It usually has 11 questions in three parts, and you answer seven:

    • Part I is compulsory: one question, 20 points. It may contain multiple-choice items, statements and short open questions from different areas of physics.
    • Part II: choose 4 questions out of seven, 15 points each. The questions compare, assess and apply knowledge of physics.
    • Part III: choose 2 questions out of three, 20 points each. The questions use source material: you read, process and convert data and draw conclusions from it.

    The maximum score of the exam is 120 points. The permitted aids and programs are given in the regulations of the Matriculation Examination Board and in the exam's own instructions. Check the current versions before the exam and practise with the exam's programs in advance.

    Time management and choosing questions

    Read the exam instructions first: they say which questions are answered and how many. Then look through all the questions and mark the ones you can certainly do. In Parts II and III you choose the questions yourself: choose those whose phenomenon you recognise and all of whose parts you can answer. Divide the time roughly by the points of the questions. Six hours are $360\,\text{min}$. If you set aside about $50\,\text{min}$ in all for reading, choosing and checking, about $60\,\text{min}$ is left for Part I, about $35\,\text{min}$ for each question of Part II and about $55\,\text{min}$ for each question of Part III. If you get stuck, write what you can and come back to the question later.

    A complete solution

    A good answer shows the reader how the result was reached, not just the final result. That is why every calculation writes out the same seven steps that the examples and model answers of this book use. The justification says why exactly this law applies in exactly this situation: there is no friction, the state does not change, the volume stays constant.

    In the model answers of this book the steps have short headings: Given and Find are step 1, Principle step 2, Formula step 3, Conversions step 4, Substitution step 5, Answer step 6 and Reasonableness step 7. When you see these headings in a solution, you recognise the same seven steps.

    Solve in symbols first

    When the formula is solved for the unknown quantity before substituting, the reader sees what the result depends on, and the units can be checked. For example, in uniformly accelerated motion the acceleration of a body starting from rest is solved like this:

    $$ s = \frac{1}{2}at^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = \frac{2s}{t^2} $$

    where $s$ is the distance travelled, $t$ the time and $a$ the acceleration. The formula holds only when the acceleration is constant and the initial speed is zero.

    The precision of the answer

    Intermediate results are kept unrounded, and only the final result is rounded. A final result has as many significant figures as the least precise given value. A final result without a unit does not state a quantity, so it is not an answer.

    Command words

    Word What is expected of the answer
    calculate, determine a complete solution in seven steps
    explain a chain of cause and effect in the concepts of physics: phenomenon, law, conclusion
    justify a claim and, to support it, a law, a calculation or an observation
    determine graphically a graph of the data, a fit and the value read from the fit
    estimate justified assumptions, a rough calculation and a suitable precision
    plan the variables, the quantities kept constant, the measurements and the sources of error

    Graphs and formulas on the computer

    A graph has the quantities and units on its axes, all the measured points and a straight line or a curve fitted to them. The fit passes between the points, and the points are not joined into a zigzag line. The slope is read from the fit at two points far apart:

    $$ k = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} $$

    where $\Delta y$ and $\Delta x$ are the differences of the vertical and horizontal coordinates of two points of the line. The unit of the slope is the unit of the vertical axis divided by the unit of the horizontal axis.

    If you make the graph with a graphing program, such as Graphical Analysis, attach a screenshot to your answer showing the axes, the points and the equation of the fit. In addition, write in the text which coefficient of the fit corresponds to which quantity and what follows from it. A number printed by the program alone is not a justified answer.

    Common mistakes

    • The final result has no unit, or it has too many significant figures.
    • Intermediate results are rounded, and the error builds up into the final result.
    • A formula is written without justifying why it applies in the situation.
    • A unit conversion is forgotten: $\text{km/h}$ is not converted to the unit $\text{m/s}$ or degrees Celsius to kelvins.
    • The slope is calculated from two neighbouring measured points and not from the fit.
    • A formula is written as an explanation without a chain of cause and effect.
    • The result is not compared with experience, so a clear calculation error goes unnoticed.

    5Examples

    Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

    Example 1

    A bath is filled with $145\,\text{kg}$ of water, whose temperature rises in the hot-water tank from $10.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $38.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. How much energy in kilowatt-hours does heating the water take? The heat losses of the tank and the pipes are not taken into account.

    The stages of the example are the same as the headings of the model answers: Given and find is Given and Find, Rearranged formula is Formula, Unit conversions is Conversions and Result is Answer.

    Step 1/7

    Given and find. The mass of the water $m = 145\,\text{kg}$, the initial temperature $T_1 = 10.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, the final temperature $T_2 = 38.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and, from the table, the specific heat capacity of water $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$. The heat $Q$ in kilowatt-hours is asked for.

    Step 2/7

    Principle. The water warms up and its state does not change, so the heat transferred to the water is $Q = cm\Delta T$. Losses are not taken into account, so the energy given by the tank is the same as the heat the water receives.

    Step 3/7

    Rearranged formula. The unknown is already alone on the left-hand side:

    $$Q = cm(T_2 - T_1)$$

    Step 4/7

    Unit conversions. A temperature difference is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius: $T_2 - T_1 = 28.0\,\text{K}$. Finally the joules are converted to kilowatt-hours:

    $1\,\text{kWh}$$= 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
    $= 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}$

    Step 5/7

    Substitution. The values are substituted with their units and the result converted:

    $$\begin{aligned} Q &= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 145\,\text{kg} \cdot 28.0\,\text{K} \\ &= 17\,011\,400\,\text{J} \\ &= \frac{17\,011\,400\,\text{J}}{3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/kWh}} \\ &= 4.7254\,\text{kWh} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 6/7

    Result. Heating takes about $4.73\,\text{kWh}$ of energy. Three significant figures, like the mass $145\,\text{kg}$ and the temperature difference $28.0\,\text{K}$.

    Step 7/7

    Reasonableness. A rough estimate:

    $4000 \cdot 150 \cdot 30\,\text{J}$
    $\approx 18\,\text{MJ}$
    $= 5\,\text{kWh}$

    which is close to the result. So one full bath corresponds roughly to a $2\,\text{kW}$ electric heater being on for over two hours.

    Review test FY1 Example 2: 1. Kind of error
    A timekeeper measures with a stopwatch the time a ball takes to roll along a channel and starts the watch a little late every time. The measurement is repeated ten times, and the mean of the results is calculated.Which statement is correct?

    • The length of the delays may vary, but they always shorten the time. So the mean is too short as well. Repeating reduces only the random variation. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

    • A random error varies in both directions. Here the watch always starts late, so the error always has the same direction, that is it is systematic, and the mean does not remove it. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

    • Correct. A late start shortens every time in the same direction, so the mean is too small as well. Repeating reduces only the random error. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

    • A gross error is a single clear mistake, such as a misread reading. A late start every time is a systematic error, corrected by changing the method, for example with light gates. Review this in the chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement.

    Review test FY1 Example 3: 2. The fitted line
    A student fitted a straight line to eight measured points. The line passes between the points but through none of them. Which interpretation is best?

    • A small scatter of the points around the line is to be expected. A measurement is repeated only if a point differs clearly and a reason for it is found. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

    • A zigzag line repeats the measurement errors and is not a model. The fitted line shows the relationship, and its slope can be read from it. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

    • A good fit runs through the middle of the points, so that there are points on both sides of it. A line forced through two points may describe the whole data set worse. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

    • Correct. The fit describes the model, and the small scatter of the points on both sides of it is random variation of the measurement. The line may pass through a point, but it does not have to. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

    Review test FY1 Example 4: 3. Density

    A student measures the mass of a metal piece as $54.0\,\text{g}$ and, by immersion, its volume as $20.0\,\text{cm}^3$. Calculate the density of the piece in the unit $\text{kg/m}^3$.

    kg/m³
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What is the definition of density? Which is divided by which?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Convert first: $1\,\text{g} = 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$ and $1\,\text{cm}^3 = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^3$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step
    $\rho$$= \dfrac{54.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}}{20.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^3}$
    Show the solution

    Given

    mass $m = 54.0\,\text{g}$, volume $V = 20.0\,\text{cm}^3$

    Find

    the density $\rho$ in the unit $\text{kg/m}^3$

    Principle

    Density is the mass of a substance per unit volume. The piece is of one substance, so its density is its mass divided by its volume.

    Formula

    $m = \rho V$, so $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$

    Conversions

    $54.0\,\text{g}$$= 54.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$
    $20.0\,\text{cm}^3$$= 20.0 \cdot (10^{-2}\,\text{m})^3$
    $= 20.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^3$

    Substitution

    $\rho$$= \dfrac{54.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}}{20.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^3}$
    $= 2700\,\text{kg/m}^3$

    Answer

    $\rho \approx 2.70 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{kg/m}^3$

    Three significant figures like the mass and the volume.

    Reasonableness

    The result is $2.70\,\text{g/cm}^3$, about $2.7$ times the density of water, which fits a metal. According to the table it is the density of aluminium.

    Review test FY2 Example 5: 4. Power

    A washing machine uses $0.90\,\text{kWh}$ of electrical energy during one programme. The programme lasts $1\,\text{h}\ 30\,\text{min}$. Calculate the average power of the washing machine in kilowatts.

    kW
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Which quantity is energy divided by time?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    How many hours is $1\,\text{h}\ 30\,\text{min}$? Not $1.30\,\text{h}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step
    $P$$= \dfrac{0.90\,\text{kWh}}{1.5\,\text{h}}$
    Show the solution

    Given

    energy $E = 0.90\,\text{kWh}$, time $t = 1\,\text{h}\ 30\,\text{min}$

    Find

    the average power $P$ in kilowatts

    Principle

    Power tells how much energy is converted per unit time. The machine does not draw the same power all the time, so the energy divided by the whole time gives the average power.

    Formula

    $P = \dfrac{E}{t}$

    Conversions

    $30\,\text{min} = 0.5\,\text{h}$, so $t = 1.5\,\text{h}$. The energy is already in kilowatt-hours, so the result comes straight out in kilowatts.

    Substitution

    $P$$= \dfrac{0.90\,\text{kWh}}{1.5\,\text{h}}$
    $= 0.60\,\text{kW}$

    Answer

    $P \approx 0.60\,\text{kW}$

    Two significant figures like the energy $0.90\,\text{kWh}$.

    Reasonableness

    $0.60\,\text{kW}$ is $600\,\text{W}$. The heating element of a washing machine draws about $2\,\text{kW}$ for a while, but most of the time only the drum turns, so the average is much smaller. Check: $0.60\,\text{kW} \cdot 1.5\,\text{h} = 0.90\,\text{kWh}$.

    Review test FY2 Example 6: 5. The fireplace

    In a fireplace $2.5\,\text{kg}$ of birch logs are burnt, with a heating value of $15\,\text{MJ/kg}$. The efficiency of the fireplace is $70\,\%$: that is the share of the energy released in burning that heats the room.

    How much energy does the fireplace give to the room? Answer in megajoules.

    MJ
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    How much energy does the wood release when it burns?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    How large a share of it stays in the room? Multiply by the efficiency.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    $E_\text{useful} = 0.70 \cdot 15\,\text{MJ/kg} \cdot 2.5\,\text{kg}$

    Show the solution

    Given

    mass of the fuel $m = 2.5\,\text{kg}$, heating value $H = 15\,\text{MJ/kg}$, efficiency $\eta = 70\,\% = 0.70$

    Find

    the energy transferred to the room $E_\text{useful}$ in megajoules

    Principle

    When it burns, the wood releases the energy $E = Hm$. The efficiency tells how large a share of it goes to the useful purpose, here heating the room; the rest goes out with the flue gases.

    Formula

    $E = Hm$ and $\eta = \dfrac{E_\text{useful}}{E}$, so $E_\text{useful} = \eta H m$

    Conversions

    The percentage as a decimal: $70\,\% = 0.70$. The heating value is already in the unit $\text{MJ/kg}$, so the result comes out in megajoules.

    Substitution

    $E_\text{useful}$$= 0.70 \cdot 15\,\text{MJ/kg} \cdot 2.5\,\text{kg}$
    $= 26.25\,\text{MJ}$

    Answer

    $E_\text{useful} \approx 26\,\text{MJ}$

    Two significant figures like the given values.

    Reasonableness

    In all, the wood releases $15 \cdot 2.5\,\text{MJ} \approx 38\,\text{MJ}$, and the useful part is less, as it should be. $26\,\text{MJ}$ is about $7\,\text{kWh}$, which matches an electric heater switched on for a few hours.

    Review test FY2 Example 7: 6. Efficiency
    The efficiency of an LED lamp is $35\,\%$: that is the share of the electrical energy it takes in that becomes light.What happens to the rest of the energy?

    • Correct. Energy is always conserved. The efficiency only tells how large a share of the energy ends up in the wanted form; the rest heats the lamp and the room. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

    • Energy does not disappear in any device. An efficiency below one hundred per cent means that part of the energy turns into something other than the useful form, here heat. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

    • A lamp does not feed energy into the grid. The electrical energy it takes in becomes light and heat, and their sum equals the energy taken in. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

    • A lamp has no energy store. The energy that does not become light becomes heat at once in the lamp and its surroundings. Review this in the chapter Efficiency.

    Review test FY3 Example 8: 7. Water for tea

    In a kettle $1.50\,\text{kg}$ of water is heated from $12.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ for tea. The specific heat capacity of water is $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$.

    How much heat is transferred to the water? Answer in kilojoules.

    kJ
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Does the state of the water change? Which law applies then?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Calculate the change in temperature: $85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 12.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step
    $Q$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 73.0\,\text{K}$
    Show the solution

    Given

    $m = 1.50\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 12.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_2 = 85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$

    Find

    the heat $Q$ transferred to the water in kilojoules

    Principle

    The water warms up and its state does not change, because it does not boil. So the heat is proportional to the mass and to the change in temperature.

    Formula

    $Q = cm\Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_2 - T_1$

    Conversions

    A temperature difference is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius:

    $\Delta T$$= 85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 12.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
    $= 73.0\,\text{K}$

    Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

    Substitution

    $Q$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 73.0\,\text{K}$
    $= 458\,805\,\text{J}$
    $= 458.805\,\text{kJ}$

    Answer

    Model answer

    a)

    Find

    why the time of ten periods is measured and not of one

    Principle

    In hand timing the starting and stopping of the watch are late or early by the reaction time. The error arises at the start and at the end, not in every period.

    Model answer

    The uncertainty of hand timing is about $0.2\,\text{s}$, which is as much as $20\,\%$ of one period of about $1\,\text{s}$. When the time of ten periods is measured, the same uncertainty is shared by ten periods: the uncertainty of the period is about $0.02\,\text{s}$ and the relative uncertainty a tenth. In addition, in a long time it is easier to notice if the watch was started at the wrong moment.

    b)

    Find

    a graph whose points lie on a straight line, and a line fitted to it

    Graph

    For small displacements the period of a pendulum is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$, so $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\,l$: the square of the period is proportional to the length of the pendulum. So $T^2$ is chosen for the vertical axis and $L$ for the horizontal axis. From the table first $T = \tfrac{t_{10}}{10}$ and then $T^2$ are calculated: for example $L = 0.200\,\text{m}$, $T = 0.933\,\text{s}$, $T^2 = 0.870\,\text{s}^2$ and $L = 0.950\,\text{m}$, $T = 1.972\,\text{s}$, $T^2 = 3.889\,\text{s}^2$.

    Model answer

    The points lie on a straight line, so $T^2$ depends linearly on the length, as the pendulum model predicts. The line is fitted between the points and not forced through the origin, because it is not certain that the measured $L$ is the true length of the pendulum. The line crosses the vertical axis a little above the origin. With a graphing program the fit is $T^2 \approx 4.03\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}} \cdot L + 0.062\,\text{s}^2$.

    c)

    Given

    the line fitted in part b, $T^2$ as a function of the length $L$

    Find

    the free-fall acceleration $g$

    Principle

    By the pendulum model $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\,l$, so the slope of the line is $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$. The slope is read from the fitted line and not from single points, because the line gathers the information of all the measurements.

    Reading the graph

    Two points far apart are read from the line: $(0.100\,\text{m}$; $0.46\,\text{s}^2)$ and $(0.900\,\text{m}$; $3.68\,\text{s}^2)$. The slope is $k = \tfrac{\Delta (T^2)}{\Delta L}$.

    Formula

    $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$, so $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$

    Conversions

    None needed: the lengths are in metres and the times in seconds, so $k$ is in the unit $\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$ and $g$ in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$.

    Substitution

    $k$$= \dfrac{3.68\,\text{s}^2 - 0.46\,\text{s}^2}{0.900\,\text{m} - 0.100\,\text{m}}$
    $= \dfrac{3.22\,\text{s}^2}{0.800\,\text{m}}$
    $= 4.025\,\tfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}}$
    $g$$= \dfrac{4\pi^2}{4.025\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}}$
    $= 9.808\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Answer

    $g \approx 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Three significant figures like the measured lengths and times.

    Reasonableness

    The result is very close to the value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The uncertainty of reading the graph, about $\pm 0.05\,\text{s}^2$ at each point, changes the result by at most about $\pm 0.3\,\text{m/s}^2$.

    d)

    Made-up example data · The numbers are chosen to be realistic, but they are not a measurement.

    A vacuum flask has two glass walls, one inside the other. The air between the walls has been removed, and the surfaces of the walls facing each other are silvered like a mirror. The specific heat capacity of the tea is the same as that of water, $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$.

    2. b) The hiker notices that the tea cools more slowly in the afternoon than in the morning. Explain the observation. 3 p
    3. c) Calculate the average heat flow with which energy is transferred from the tea to the surroundings during the six hours. 3 p
    4. d) Does the vacuum flask also keep ice water cold? Justify your answer. 3 p

    Skills from the chapters: Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change, Heating and cooling of matter: $Q = cm\Delta T$, Changes of state

    Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

    Model answer

    a)

    Find

    how the structure of the flask slows down the cooling for each way of heat transfer

    Principle

    Heat is transferred by conduction, by convection and as thermal radiation. Because of the temperature difference there is a net transfer of energy from warmer to colder, so the flask has to slow down every way.

    Model answer

    Conduction. Energy is conducted when particles collide and give kinetic energy to their neighbours. Between the walls there is a vacuum with no particles, so energy is not conducted from the inner wall to the outer wall. Convection. In convection a warmed liquid or gas flows and carries energy with it. A vacuum has no air that could flow, and the stopper stops the flow of warm air and steam at the mouth of the flask. Thermal radiation. Thermal radiation also travels through a vacuum. The silvered surfaces reflect most of the radiation from the side of the tea back, and they also radiate little themselves. Evaporation. The stopper stops water vapour from escaping. Evaporation would take energy from the tea. There is still a net transfer of energy from the tea to the surroundings, but slowly: mainly through the mouth and the stopper and through the joint of the walls, where the glass conducts heat.

    b)

    Find

    why the tea cools more slowly in the afternoon

    Principle

    The heat flow from the tea to the surroundings grows when the temperature difference between the tea and the surroundings grows.

    Model answer

    In the morning the temperature difference between the tea and the outdoor air is about $65\,\text{K}$, in the afternoon only about $50\,\text{K}$. When the temperature difference decreases, a smaller heat flow passes through the walls and the stopper of the flask. The smaller the heat flow leaving the tea, the more slowly its temperature falls, because giving off the same heat takes longer. So the cooling slows down all the time, and the tea approaches the outdoor temperature ever more slowly.

    c)

    Given

    $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, the temperature falls from $85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, time $t = 6.0\,\text{h}$

    the average heat flow $P$ with which energy is transferred from the tea to the surroundings

    Principle

    The tea cools and its state does not change, so the heat it gives off is $Q = cm\Delta T$. The stopper is closed, so evaporation is not taken into account. The average power is the heat given off divided by the time.

    Formula

    $Q = cm\Delta T$ and $P = \dfrac{Q}{t}$, so $P = \dfrac{cm\Delta T}{t}$

    Conversions

    $\Delta T$$= 85.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 70.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
    $= 15.0\,\text{K}$
    $t$$= 6.0 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
    $= 21\,600\,\text{s}$

    Substitution

    $Q$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 15.0\,\text{K}$
    $= 31\,425\,\text{J}$
    $P$$= \dfrac{31\,425\,\text{J}}{21\,600\,\text{s}}$
    $= 1.4549\,\text{W}$

    Answer

    $P \approx 1.5\,\text{W}$

    Two significant figures like the mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$ and the time $6.0\,\text{h}$.

    Reasonableness

    The power is very small: in an open mug the same tea would cool as much in about ten minutes, which would be tens of watts. That is why the vacuum flask keeps the tea hot all day.

    d)

    whether the vacuum flask also keeps ice water cold

    Principle

    A vacuum flask produces neither cold nor heat; it slows down the transfer of energy in either direction.

    Model answer

    Yes. When the flask holds ice water, there is a net transfer of energy from the warm surroundings to the cold water. The vacuum stops conduction and convection in the same way as with the tea, and the mirror surfaces reflect the thermal radiation from the surroundings back outwards. If the temperature difference is about the same, energy is transferred about as slowly. As long as there is ice in the water, the energy transferred goes into melting the ice, and the water stays at $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

    Common mistakes

    • In part c the temperature difference used is between the tea and the outdoor air, not the change in the temperature of the tea.
    • In part b the only explanation is that "the tea has already cooled", without mentioning the connection between the temperature difference and the heat flow.

    Also accepted

    In an experiment one variable is changed, its effect is measured, and the other factors that affect the result are kept constant. The efficiency is the heat transferred to the water divided by the electrical energy used.

    Model answer

    Variables. The quantity changed is the mass of the water, for example $0.300$, $0.500$, $0.750$, $1.00$ and $1.50\,\text{kg}$. The quantity measured as the result is the efficiency $\eta = \tfrac{cm\Delta T}{E}$. Constants. The same kettle and the same initial temperature are used, for example tap water whose temperature has been measured, and the water is always heated to the same final temperature, for example to $90\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, before boiling, so that little water evaporates. The lid is closed, and the kettle is cooled to room temperature between the measurements so that it is not already warm. Measurements. The water is weighed in the kettle on the scales (the mass of the kettle subtracted), the initial temperature is measured before heating and the final temperature right after switching off, stirred. The electrical energy is read from the energy meter, which is reset before every measurement. Repeats. Every amount of water is measured three times, and the mean and the scatter of the efficiencies are calculated.

    b)

    Given

    $m = 0.500\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 15.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_2 = 95.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, electrical energy $E = 0.054\,\text{kWh}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$ (from the table)

    Find

    the efficiency $\eta$ of the kettle

    Principle

    The heat transferred to the water counts as useful. The water warms up without a change of state, so $Q = cm\Delta T$. The energy used is the reading of the energy meter.

    Formula

    $\eta$$= \dfrac{Q}{E}$
    $= \dfrac{cm(T_2 - T_1)}{E}$

    Conversions

    $E$$= 0.054\,\text{kWh}$
    $= 0.054 \cdot 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
    $= 194\,400\,\text{J}$

    $T_2 - T_1 = 80.0\,\text{K}$

    Substitution

    $Q$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.500\,\text{kg} \cdot 80.0\,\text{K}$
    $= 167\,600\,\text{J}$
    $\eta$$= \dfrac{167\,600\,\text{J}}{194\,400\,\text{J}}$
    $= 0.8621$

    Answer

    $\eta \approx 86\,\%$

    Two significant figures like the reading of the energy meter $0.054\,\text{kWh}$.

    Reasonableness

    The efficiency is below $100\,\%$, because part of the energy heats the body and the element of the kettle and goes to the surroundings. A check with the power: a $2.0\,\text{kW}$ kettle uses $194\,400\,\text{J}$ in about $97\,\text{s}$, which fits experience of boiling half a litre.

    c)

    Find

    the sources of error of the experiment and ways to reduce their effect

    Principle

    A systematic error changes every result in the same direction, and repeating does not remove it; random variation is reduced by repeating.

    Model answer

    Evaporation. Near the boiling point water evaporates, and evaporation takes up energy that is not counted as useful. The efficiency looks too small. Remedy: heat only to, for example, $90\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and keep the lid closed. The temperature of the kettle. If the kettle is warm from the previous measurement, less energy goes into heating its body and the efficiency looks larger. Remedy: cool the kettle and rinse it with cold water between the measurements. Measuring the temperature. The water has different temperatures in different places. Remedy: stir before the reading. The precision of the energy meter. The last digit of the reading, $0.001\,\text{kWh}$, is about $2\,\%$ of the reading $0.054\,\text{kWh}$, and an even larger share with a small amount of water. Remedy: a larger rise in temperature or repeats whose mean is calculated.

    d)

    Find

    how to present the results and a result that would show a dependence

    Principle

    A dependence shows in a graph with the quantity changed on the horizontal axis and the result on the vertical axis. A difference is real only if it is larger than the uncertainty of the measurements.

    Model answer

    The results are presented as a table and as an $(m, \eta)$ graph: the mass of the water $m\ (\text{kg})$ on the horizontal axis and the efficiency $\eta\ (\%)$ on the vertical axis. The scatter of the repeats is marked at every point as an error bar. If the points rise clearly as the amount of water grows and the rise is larger than the error bars, the efficiency depends on the amount of water. The physical explanation would be that the energy used to heat the body and the element of the kettle is a larger part of the total energy with a small amount of water. If the points lie on a horizontal line within the error bars, a dependence cannot be shown, but that does not prove there is none.

    Common mistakes

    • The energy used is calculated from the rated power of the kettle and the time, although the real power may differ from the rated one; the energy meter measures it directly.
    • The water is heated until it boils, and the final temperature is always written as $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ without measuring.
    • The plan changes the amount of water and the initial temperature at the same time, so their effects cannot be separated.
    • In part b the kilowatt-hours are not converted to joules, so the efficiency comes out in millions.
    • In part d the conclusion is drawn from one measurement with each amount of water, without repeats.

    Also accepted

    • In part a the electrical energy can also be found by measuring the time with a stopwatch, if the real power of the kettle has first been measured with the energy meter; the plan must then justify why the power is constant.
    • In part b the answer $0.86$ without a percent sign.

    A local train accelerates uniformly from rest at a station to the speed $72\,\text{km/h}$ in $45\,\text{s}$. Solve it completely in your notebook, and give the acceleration of the train in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$ with the right precision.

    m/s²
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What is the definition of acceleration when the acceleration is constant?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Convert the speed: $72\,\text{km/h} = \tfrac{72}{3.6}\,\text{m/s}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step
    $a$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{m/s}}{45\,\text{s}}$
    Show the solution

    Given

    initial speed $v_0 = 0$, final speed $v = 72\,\text{km/h}$, time $\Delta t = 45\,\text{s}$; uniform acceleration

    Find

    the acceleration $a$ in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$

    Principle

    The train accelerates uniformly, so its acceleration is constant and equal to the change in velocity divided by the time taken.

    Formula

    $a$$= \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$
    $= \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$

    Conversions

    $72\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{72 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
    $= 20\,\text{m/s}$

    Substitution

    $a$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{m/s} - 0}{45\,\text{s}}$
    $= 0.4444\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Answer

    $a \approx 0.44\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Two significant figures like the speed $72\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $45\,\text{s}$.

    Reasonableness

    The acceleration is less than $5\,\%$ of the free-fall acceleration, which fits a train with standing passengers. Check: $0.444\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 45\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{m/s}$.

    4. Explanation task

    A cyclist falls and hits their head on the street. Explain with the concepts of physics why a cycling helmet reduces the force on the head.

    Model answer

    In the impact the velocity of the head changes to zero in a short time, so its momentum changes by the same amount with and without a helmet. By the impulse principle the change in momentum equals the impulse of the force on the head: $F\Delta t = \Delta p$. The hard shell of the helmet spreads the impact, and the foam under it is compressed. Then the head takes longer to stop, that is $\Delta t$ grows. Since $\Delta p$ stays the same, the average force $F = \tfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$ decreases. A smaller force means a smaller deceleration and fewer injuries.
