Reflection and refraction of light

Why does a straw in a glass of water seem to break exactly at the surface of the water?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain the reflection and refraction of light at the boundary of two materials and the cause of refraction.
  • calculate angles of reflection and refraction, refractive indices and the speed of light in a material.
  • determine the refractive index of a material from measured angles as the slope of a graph.

Prior knowledge

  • Light is electromagnetic wave motion, and in a vacuum its speed is $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$chapter Electromagnetic radiation and the black-body spectrum
  • A wave is reflected so that the angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence, when the angles are measured from the normalchapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap

1Think about it

Put a straw in a glass of water and look at the glass obliquely from above: the straw seems to break at the surface of the water, and the part of it in the water seems to be shifted sideways. The bottom of a swimming pool seems to be closer than it is, and a still lake mirrors the trees on the shore. Spectacles, camera lenses and optical fibres are all based on what happens to light at the boundary of two materials.

3Investigate

A laser beam hits the boundary of two materials at the centre of a protractor disc. Choose the materials and the angle of incidence, and follow the reflected and the refracted ray.

Simulation

How to use

Choose the material above and the material below the boundary and adjust the angle of incidence $\alpha_1$. The laser is in the upper material, and the ray hits the boundary at the centre of the protractor disc. The dashed line is the normal of the boundary, and all angles are measured from it: $\alpha_1$ is the angle of incidence, $\beta$ the angle of reflection and $\alpha_2$ the angle of refraction. On the rim of the disc there is a mark every $10^\circ$ and a longer mark every $30^\circ$. The brighter and thicker the ray, the greater the share of the light that travels along it. In the readout you see the angles, their sines and the reflected share $R$.

Assumptions of the model

The model uses the refractive indices of yellow light (wavelength $589\,\text{nm}$): air $1.00$, water $1.33$, acrylic $1.49$, glass $1.52$ and diamond $2.42$. The boundary is flat and smooth, and the materials are uniform and transparent, so they do not absorb light. The reflected share is calculated for unpolarised light, and the rest of the light continues as the refracted ray. If the law of refraction gave $\sin\alpha_2 > 1$, there would be no refracted ray, and all the light is reflected (chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre). The readings are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

The angle of reflection is $\beta = \alpha_1$ according to the law of reflection. The angle of refraction is found from the law of refraction $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$:

$$\sin\alpha_2 = \frac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_2}$$

The reflected share $R$ is calculated from Fresnel's equations, which are not part of the upper secondary syllabus; for example, about $2\,\%$ of light arriving perpendicular from air to water is reflected. The refracted ray carries the share $100\,\% - R$.

    Show the solution

    Given

    slope of the fitted line $A = 1.489$, $\Delta A = 0.016$, constant term $B = -0.004$, refractive index of air $n_1 = 1.00$

    Find

    the refractive index of acrylic $n_2$ (no unit)

    Principle

    From the law of refraction $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$ we get $\sin\alpha_1 = \tfrac{n_2}{n_1} \sin\alpha_2$. When $\sin\alpha_1$ is plotted as a function of $\sin\alpha_2$, the points lie on a line through the origin whose slope is $n_2 : n_1$. The slope is read from the fitted line and not from individual points, because the line takes all the measurements into account. The constant term $B = -0.004$ is almost zero, as the model says it should be.

    Reading the graph

    The fitted line passes approximately through the origin and the point $(0.60$; $0.89)$, so the slope is about $0.89 : 0.60 \approx 1.48$; the exact value from the program is $A = 1.489$.

    Formula

    $A = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$, so $n_2 = A n_1$

    Conversions

    Not needed: the sines and the refractive indices have no unit.

    Substitution

    $n_2 = 1.489 \cdot 1.00 = 1.489$

    Answer

    $n_2 \approx 1.49$

    Three significant figures, because the uncertainty of the slope, $0.016$, is of the size of the second decimal: $n_2 = 1.49 \pm 0.02$.

    Reasonableness

    The result is the same as the table value for acrylic, $1.49$. The ratio of the sines calculated from individual rows varies, for example $\sin 10^\circ : \sin 7^\circ = 1.42$ and $\sin 50^\circ : \sin 31^\circ = 1.49$, because a reading error of a degree is relatively large at small angles. The slope, which takes all the points into account, is therefore more reliable.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Light travels as rays

    Light is electromagnetic wave motion (chapter Electromagnetic radiation and the black-body spectrum). In a uniform material such as air, water or glass, light travels in a straight line. The path of light is described by rays: a ray is a line that shows the direction in which light travels. When light meets the boundary between two materials, part of it is reflected back into the same material and part continues into the other material. Light that passes through the boundary usually changes direction: it is refracted. Angles are always measured from the normal of the boundary, the straight line that is perpendicular to the boundary at the point where the ray hits it. The angle between the incident ray and the normal is the angle of incidence $\alpha_1$, the angle of the reflected ray is the angle of reflection $\beta$, and the angle of the refracted ray is the angle of refraction $\alpha_2$.

    The law of reflection

    Light is reflected in the same way as the mechanical waves in the chapter A wave meets an obstacle or a gap: the angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence, and the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal lie in the same plane. A smooth surface, such as a mirror or the still surface of water, reflects parallel rays as parallel rays, so an image can be seen in it. On a rough surface, such as paper, the normal points in a different direction at every point. The surface therefore reflects light in all directions, and the paper can be seen from any direction. The law of reflection still holds at every small point of the surface.

    Light slows down in a material

    In a vacuum light travels at the speed $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. In a material light travels more slowly. The refractive index $n$ of a material tells how many times more slowly: in water the speed of light is about $2.26 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, so the refractive index of water is $1.33$. The refractive index of air is $1.0003$, so in air light travels almost as fast as in a vacuum. A material with a greater refractive index is optically denser. When light passes from one material to another, its frequency does not change, because the frequency is set by the source of the light. The speed does change, so the wavelength changes too according to the equation $v = f \lambda$: in water the wavelength is $1.33$ times shorter than in air.

    Why light is refracted

    Refraction is caused by the change in speed. In the figure, the wavefronts of a wave arriving obliquely at the boundary are parallel lines, and the ray is perpendicular to them. The left end of the front reaches the water first and slows down, while the right end still travels faster in the air. The front therefore turns, and in the water the wave travels in a new direction, closer to the normal. In the water the fronts are also closer together, because the wavelength is shorter. When light arrives perpendicular to the boundary, $\alpha_1 = 0^\circ$, every part of the front slows down at the same moment, and the direction does not change.

    Towards the normal or away from it

    When light passes into an optically denser material, for example from air into water, it is refracted towards the normal: the angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence. When light passes into an optically less dense material, for example from water into air, it is refracted away from the normal. The path of light is reversible: if a ray travels from air into water along a certain path, a ray coming from the water into the air can travel the same path back. The greater the difference between the refractive indices, the more the ray is refracted at the same angle of incidence. This is why diamond, with a refractive index of $2.42$, bends light much more than water does.

    Partial reflection

    At a boundary, light always divides into a reflected part and a transmitted part. When light arrives perpendicular to glass from air, about $4\,\%$ of the light is reflected from each surface of the glass, and about $2\,\%$ from the surface of water. The greater the angle of incidence, that is, the more the light grazes the surface, the greater the share that is reflected. This is why the surface of a lake mirrors the trees on the shore in the evening, although the bottom can be seen when you look straight down. In the evening you can see a reflection of your own room in a window, because little light comes from outside and the light from the room that hits the glass is partly reflected back. The reflected and transmitted parts together equal the incident light when the material does not absorb light.

    Apparent depth

    When you look at a coin at the bottom of a swimming pool, the rays from the coin are refracted away from the normal at the surface of the water. The eye and the brain interpret the light as having come along a straight line. When the rays that reach the eye are extended backwards into the water, they seem to start from a point that is higher than the coin. This is why the pool looks shallower than it is, and the coin seems to be closer to the surface. For the same reason the part of a straw that is in the water seems to be raised when the glass is looked at obliquely from above through the surface, and the straw seems to break at the surface. Because you look obliquely, the raised part also appears shifted sideways.

    Law of reflection and law of refraction

    The law of reflection: the angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence,

    $$\beta = \alpha_1$$

    where $\beta$ is the angle of reflection and $\alpha_1$ the angle of incidence, both measured from the normal of the boundary.

    The refractive index of a material is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in the material:

    $$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

    where $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of light in a vacuum and $v$ the speed of light in the material (m/s). The refractive index has no unit, and for materials transparent to visible light it is always greater than $1$.

    The law of refraction, or Snell's law

    When light passes from a material with refractive index $n_1$ into a material with refractive index $n_2$, the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction satisfy the equation

    $$n_1 \sin \alpha_1 = n_2 \sin \alpha_2$$

    where $\alpha_1$ is the angle of incidence and $\alpha_2$ the angle of refraction, both measured from the normal. The law follows from the turning of the wavefronts: the ratio of the sines of the angles is the same as the ratio of the speeds, $\sin\alpha_1 : \sin\alpha_2 = v_1 : v_2 = n_2 : n_1$. The law concerns the sines of the angles, not the angles: when the angle of incidence doubles, the angle of refraction does not double. In the graph the angle of refraction increases with the angle of incidence, but more and more slowly.

    Common misconception

    Many students measure the angle of incidence from the boundary instead of from the normal. If the ray and the surface of the water make an angle of $30^\circ$, the angle of incidence is $90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$. Another mistake is to think that the angle of refraction is proportional to the angle of incidence. The law of refraction concerns the sines of the angles: from air into water at an angle of incidence of $30^\circ$ the angle of refraction is $22.1^\circ$, but at an angle of incidence of $60^\circ$ it is $40.6^\circ$ and not $44.2^\circ$. A third mistake is to believe that light is always refracted towards the normal. The direction depends on which material is optically denser: a ray coming from water into air is refracted away from the normal. A fourth mistake is to think that light is refracted because it "collides" with the surface. Light is refracted because its speed changes.

    Summary

    • At a boundary part of the light is reflected and part is refracted. Angles are measured from the normal of the boundary.
    • The law of reflection: $\beta = \alpha_1$, and the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal lie in the same plane.
    • The refractive index $n = c : v$ tells how many times more slowly light travels in a material than in a vacuum.
    • The law of refraction: $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$. Into an optically denser material the ray is refracted towards the normal, into a less dense one away from the normal.
    • Objects in water seem to be closer to the surface than they are, because the rays from them are refracted at the surface of the water.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A laser beam arrives from the air at the surface of the water in a tank at an angle of incidence of $55.0^\circ$. What is the angle of refraction in the water, and at what speed does light travel in the water? The refractive index of air is $1.00$ and that of water $1.33$.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Angle of incidence $\alpha_1 = 55.0^\circ$, refractive index of air $n_1 = 1.00$, refractive index of water $n_2 = 1.33$ and speed of light in a vacuum $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. We are asked for the angle of refraction $\alpha_2$ and the speed of light in water $v$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Light passes from air into water, so it is refracted at the boundary according to the law of refraction. Water is optically denser, so the ray is refracted towards the normal. The speed of light in water follows from the definition of the refractive index, $n = c : v$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} n_1 \sin\alpha_1 &= n_2 \sin\alpha_2 \\ \sin\alpha_2 &= \frac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_2} \\[6pt] n_2 &= \frac{c}{v} \;\Rightarrow\; v = \frac{c}{n_2} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the refractive indices have no unit, and the angle is in degrees. The speed comes out in the same unit as $c$, metres per second.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} &\sin\alpha_2 = \frac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_2} \\ &= \frac{1.00 \cdot \sin 55.0^\circ}{1.33} \\ &= 0.6159 \\ &\alpha_2 = 38.02^\circ \\[6pt] &v = \frac{c}{n_2} \\ &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{1.33} \\ &= 2.256 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The angle of refraction is $\alpha_2 \approx 38.0^\circ$, and light travels in water at the speed $v \approx 2.26 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. The results are given to three significant figures, like the angle of incidence $55.0^\circ$ and the refractive indices.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The ray is refracted towards the normal, because $38.0^\circ < 55.0^\circ$, as it should be when light passes into an optically denser material. The speed is smaller than $c$, and the ratio of the sines is the same as the ratio of the speeds: $\sin 55.0^\circ : \sin 38.0^\circ \approx 1.33$ and $3.00 : 2.26 \approx 1.33$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      angle of incidence in water $\alpha_1 = 30.0^\circ$, refractive index of water $n_1 = 1.33$, of glass $n_2 = 1.52$, of air $n_3 = 1.00$, glass of uniform thickness

      Find

      the angle between the ray and the normal in air, $\alpha_3$, in degrees

      Principle

      The ray is refracted twice according to the law of refraction, from water into glass and from glass into air. The surfaces of the glass are parallel, so they have the same normal, and the ray in the glass makes the same angle $\alpha_2$ with the normal of both surfaces. Therefore $n \sin\alpha$ is the same in all three materials, and the refractive index of the glass cancels out.

      1. Concept: why light is refracted

      Why does light arriving obliquely from air into water change its direction at the surface of the water?

      • Correct. When one end of the front already travels more slowly in the water and the other still faster in the air, the front turns. This is why in the law of refraction the ratio of the sines is the same as the ratio of the speeds.

      • The surface does not push the light. The change of direction is caused by the change of speed: the part of the wavefront that reaches the water first slows down first, and the front turns. A mirror reflects light back into the same material.

      • The frequency does not change at the boundary, because it is set by the source of the light. The speed and the wavelength decrease, and the change of speed turns a wavefront arriving obliquely.

          The result is close to the refractive index of window glass in the table, $1.52$. The angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence, so the refractive index must be greater than $1$, as in the result.

        5. Exercise 10-13Core

          A group of students studied the refraction of light from air into a semicircular glass block and measured the angle of incidence $\alpha_1$ and the angle of refraction $\alpha_2$ with a protractor. The reading accuracy is $\pm 0.5^\circ$. The measurements are example data.

          Calculate $\sin\alpha_1$ and $\sin\alpha_2$ for each measurement point and draw a graph with $\sin\alpha_1$ on the vertical axis and $\sin\alpha_2$ on the horizontal axis. Fit a straight line through the origin to the points: the line passes through the origin, because at an angle of incidence of $0^\circ$ the angle of refraction is also $0^\circ$.

          What is the slope of the line? Use it to deduce the refractive index of the glass when the refractive index of air is $1.00$. Write the refractive index of the glass in the answer box.

          Refraction of light from air into glass (example data)
          $\alpha_1\,({}^\circ)$$\alpha_2\,({}^\circ)$
          10.06.5
          20.013.5
          30.019.0
          40.025.5
          50.030.5
          60.036.0
          70.038.5
          Answer

          $n_2 \approx 1.50$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the seven pairs of measurements $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ of the table, reading accuracy $\pm 0.5^\circ$, refractive index of air $n_1 = 1.00$

          Find

          the refractive index of the glass $n_2$ (no unit)

          Principle

          The law of refraction is $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$. The angles are not proportional to each other, but their sines are: when $\sin\alpha_1$ is plotted as a function of $\sin\alpha_2$, the points lie on a straight line whose slope is $n_2 : n_1$. The line passes through the origin, because at an angle of incidence of $0^\circ$ the angle of refraction is also $0^\circ$.

          Reading the graph

          The sines are: $\sin\alpha_1 = 0.174$; $0.342$; $0.500$; $0.643$; $0.766$; $0.866$; $0.940$ and $\sin\alpha_2 = 0.113$; $0.233$; $0.326$; $0.431$; $0.508$; $0.588$; $0.623$. In real series of measurements the points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty, so the line is drawn among the points. Draw the graph yourself and check from it that the points lie around a straight line through the origin. The slope is read from two points of the fitted line, not from two adjacent measurement points: the line passes through the points $(0$; $0)$ and $(0.60$; $0.90)$.

          Formula

          $\sin\alpha_1$$= \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} \sin\alpha_2$

          so the slope is

          $k$$= \dfrac{\Delta(\sin\alpha_1)}{\Delta(\sin\alpha_2)}$
          $= \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$

          $n_2 = k\, n_1$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the sines and the refractive indices have no unit.

          Substitution

          $k = \dfrac{0.90 - 0}{0.60 - 0} = 1.50$, so $n_2 = 1.50 \cdot 1.00 = 1.50$. The least-squares line through the origin gives the slope $1.498$.

          Answer

          $n_2 \approx 1.50$

          Three significant figures; the angles have been read with an accuracy of $0.5^\circ$, so the third figure is already uncertain.

          Reasonableness

          The slope is greater than $1$, so glass is optically denser than air, and at every point the angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence. The individual ratios of the sines $\sin\alpha_1 : \sin\alpha_2$ are between $1.47$ and $1.54$, so $1.50$ is in the middle of them. The value is typical for glass.

        6. Exercise 10-14

          A student solved the problem as follows: “Light passes from air into water at an angle of incidence of $50.0^\circ$. The refractive index of water is $1.33$ and that of air $1.00$. The light is refracted towards the normal, so the angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence. I divide the angle of incidence by the refractive index: $\alpha_2 = 50.0^\circ : 1.33 = 37.6^\circ$.”

          Explain what is wrong in the student's solution and calculate the correct angle of refraction. Write the correct angle of refraction in the answer box.

          °
          Answer

          $\alpha_2 \approx 35.2^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          angle of incidence $\alpha_1 = 50.0^\circ$, refractive index of water $n_2 = 1.33$, refractive index of air $n_1 = 1.00$; the student's result $37.6^\circ$

          Find

          the correct angle of refraction $\alpha_2$ in degrees

          Principle

          The student's reasoning about the direction of refraction is correct: when light passes into water, the ray is refracted towards the normal. The error is that the student divided the angle by the refractive index. The law of refraction concerns the sines of the angles, not the angles, so the angle of refraction is not the angle of incidence divided by the refractive index. An angle and its sine are not proportional: as the angle of incidence grows, the angle of refraction grows more and more slowly.

          Formula

          $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$, so

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_2}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the refractive indices have no unit, and the angle is in degrees.

          Substitution

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{1.00 \cdot \sin 50.0^\circ}{1.33}$
          $= \dfrac{0.7660}{1.33}$
          $= 0.5760$

          so $\alpha_2 = 35.17^\circ$

          Answer

          $\alpha_2 \approx 35.2^\circ$

          Three significant figures like the angle of incidence and the refractive indices.

          Reasonableness

          The student's result does not satisfy the law of refraction: $1.33 \cdot \sin 37.6^\circ = 0.811$, but $1.00 \cdot \sin 50.0^\circ = 0.766$. With the correct angle $1.33 \cdot \sin 35.2^\circ = 0.766$, so the sines match.

        7. Exercise 10-15

          A student measured the refraction of light from air into an acrylic plate with a laser. The angle of incidence was $45.0^\circ$ and the angle of refraction $28.0^\circ$. The uncertainty of reading each angle is $\pm 0.5^\circ$ with the protractor. The measurements are example data.

          First calculate the refractive index $n$ of the acrylic with the measured angles, and then the smallest and the largest refractive index that fit within the uncertainty limits of the angles. The refractive index of air is $1.00$. Estimate the uncertainty $\Delta n$ of the refractive index as half of the difference between the smallest and the largest value. Write the uncertainty $\Delta n$ in the answer box.

          Answer

          $\Delta n \approx 0.038$

          Show the solution

          Given

          angle of incidence $\alpha_1 = 45.0^\circ \pm 0.5^\circ$, angle of refraction $\alpha_2 = 28.0^\circ \pm 0.5^\circ$, refractive index of air $n_1 = 1.00$

          Find

          the uncertainty $\Delta n$ of the refractive index of the acrylic (no unit)

          Principle

          The refractive index is obtained from the law of refraction $n = \dfrac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{\sin\alpha_2}$. It is greatest when the angle of incidence is as large as possible and the angle of refraction as small as possible, and smallest in the opposite case. The uncertainty is estimated as half of the difference between the greatest and the smallest value. This is an estimate and not a guarantee: the true value probably lies within the limits.

          Formula

          $n$$= \dfrac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{\sin\alpha_2}$
          $\Delta n$$= \dfrac{n_\text{max} - n_\text{min}}{2}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the angles are in degrees and the refractive indices have no unit.

          Substitution

          $n$$= \dfrac{1.00 \cdot \sin 45.0^\circ}{\sin 28.0^\circ}$
          $= 1.506$
          $n_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{\sin 45.5^\circ}{\sin 27.5^\circ}$
          $= 1.545$
          $n_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{\sin 44.5^\circ}{\sin 28.5^\circ}$
          $= 1.469$
          $\Delta n$$= \dfrac{1.545 - 1.469}{2}$
          $= 0.038$

          Answer

          $\Delta n \approx 0.038$

          Two significant figures; an uncertainty is given with one or two significant figures, so the result is $n = 1.51 \pm 0.04$.

          Reasonableness

          The relative uncertainty is $0.038 : 1.506 = 0.025 = 2.5\,\%$. A reading of $0.5^\circ$ in the angle of refraction affects the relative value of the sine by about $1.6\,\%$ and in the angle of incidence by about $0.9\,\%$, in total about $2.5\,\%$, so the uncertainty is of the same order of magnitude. The tabulated refractive index of acrylic, $1.49$, is between $1.469$ and $1.545$.

        1. Exercise 10-16

          Two plane mirrors are perpendicular to each other. A ray of light hits the first mirror at an angle of incidence of $30.0^\circ$, is reflected and then hits the second mirror.

          What is the angle of incidence at the second mirror? The ray travels in the plane of the normals of the mirrors.

          °
          Answer

          $60.0^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mirrors perpendicular to each other, angle of incidence at the first mirror $30.0^\circ$, the ray in the plane of the normals of the mirrors

          Find

          the angle of incidence at the second mirror in degrees

          Principle

          At the first mirror, according to the law of reflection, the angle of reflection is $30.0^\circ$, so the reflected ray makes the angle $90^\circ - 30.0^\circ = 60.0^\circ$ with the surface of the first mirror. The second mirror is perpendicular to the first, so its normal is parallel to the surface of the first mirror. Therefore the angle between the ray and the normal of the second mirror is the same as the angle between the ray and the surface of the first mirror.

          Formula

          $\beta_1 = \alpha_1$ and $\alpha_\text{second} = 90^\circ - \beta_1$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the angles are in degrees.

          Substitution

          $\alpha_\text{second}$$= 90^\circ - 30.0^\circ$
          $= 60.0^\circ$

          Answer

          $60.0^\circ$

          Three significant figures like the angle of incidence $30.0^\circ$.

          Reasonableness

          The angles of incidence $30.0^\circ$ and $60.0^\circ$ add up to $90^\circ$, as follows from the angle between the normals of perpendicular mirrors. After two reflections the ray returns in the direction opposite to its original direction, and bicycle reflectors, for example, are based on the same principle.

        2. Exercise 10-17

          At the bottom of a swimming pool there is a lamp at a depth of $2.0\,\text{m}$. A ray from the lamp hits the surface of the water at a point $1.5\,\text{m}$ away horizontally from the point above the lamp, and continues into the air. The refractive index of water is $1.33$ and that of air $1.00$.

          What angle does the ray make with the normal of the surface in the air?

          °
          Answer

          $\alpha_2 \approx 53^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          depth of the lamp $h = 2.0\,\text{m}$, horizontal distance $x = 1.5\,\text{m}$, refractive index of water $n_1 = 1.33$, refractive index of air $n_2 = 1.00$

          Find

          the angle between the ray and the normal in air, $\alpha_2$, in degrees

          Principle

          In the water the ray travels straight from the lamp to the surface, so the angle of incidence is found from the right-angled triangle whose legs are the depth and the horizontal distance. The normal is vertical, so $\tan\alpha_1 = x : h$. At the surface the ray is refracted according to the law of refraction, away from the normal, because air is optically less dense.

          Formula

          $\tan\alpha_1 = \dfrac{x}{h}$ and $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$, so

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_2}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the distances are in metres, and in their ratio the units cancel.

          Substitution

          $\tan\alpha_1$$= \dfrac{1.5\,\text{m}}{2.0\,\text{m}}$
          $= 0.75$

          so $\alpha_1 = 36.87^\circ$;

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{1.33 \cdot \sin 36.87^\circ}{1.00}$
          $= 1.33 \cdot 0.600$
          $= 0.798$

          so $\alpha_2 = 52.94^\circ$

          Answer

          $\alpha_2 \approx 53^\circ$

          Two significant figures like the depth $2.0\,\text{m}$ and the distance $1.5\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The angle in air is greater than the angle in water, $36.9^\circ$, so the ray was refracted away from the normal. $\sin\alpha_2 = 0.798$ is less than $1$, so the ray gets out into the air.

        3. Exercise 10-18

          Light travels in material A at the speed $2.25 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ and in material B at the speed $1.97 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. A ray of light passes from material A into material B at an angle of incidence of $40.0^\circ$.

          What is the angle of refraction?

          °
          Answer

          $\alpha_2 \approx 34.2^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed of light in material A $v_1 = 2.25 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, in material B $v_2 = 1.97 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, angle of incidence $\alpha_1 = 40.0^\circ$

          Find

          the angle of refraction $\alpha_2$ in degrees

          Principle

          The law of refraction $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$ and the definition of the refractive index $n = c : v$ together give $\dfrac{\sin\alpha_1}{v_1} = \dfrac{\sin\alpha_2}{v_2}$: the ratio of the sines of the angles is the same as the ratio of the speeds. Light slows down when it passes into material B, so it is refracted towards the normal.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{c}{v_1}\sin\alpha_1$$= \dfrac{c}{v_2}\sin\alpha_2$

          so

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{v_2 \sin\alpha_1}{v_1}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the speeds are in the same unit, so they cancel.

          Substitution

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{1.97 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s} \cdot \sin 40.0^\circ}{2.25 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= 0.8756 \cdot 0.6428$
          $= 0.5628$

          so $\alpha_2 = 34.249^\circ$

          Answer

          $\alpha_2 \approx 34.2^\circ$

          Three significant figures like the speeds and the angle of incidence.

          Reasonableness

          The angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence, as it should be when light passes into a material where it travels more slowly. The refractive indices are $3.00 : 2.25 = 1.33$ (water) and $3.00 : 1.97 = 1.52$ (glass), and with them

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= 1.33 \cdot 0.6428 : 1.52$
          $= 0.563$

          the same result.

        4. Exercise 10-19

          A small object lies at the bottom of a pool at a depth of $d = 60.0\,\text{cm}$ below the surface of the water. A student looks at the object from the air at different angles $\alpha_2$ to the normal of the surface and measures, for each viewing angle, the apparent depth $h'$, that is, the depth at which the object appears to be. The measurements are example data.

          The ray arriving at the eye and the ray travelling from the object to the same point on the surface are in the same vertical plane.

          Assume that the object appears to be directly above its real position.

          The point on the surface is at a horizontal distance $x$ from the point above the object. In the water the angle between the ray and the normal is $\alpha_1$, and the extension drawn to the apparent position makes the angle $\alpha_2$ with the normal.

          a) Draw a picture of the situation. From this information derive an equation from which $\alpha_1$ can be calculated, and calculate $\alpha_1$ for each row of the table.

          b) Calculate the refractive index $n$ for each row from the law of refraction, when the refractive index of air is $1.00$. Determine the refractive index of water as the mean of these four values. It is worth calculating the sum separately.

          c) Justify why the apparent depth is smaller than the real depth and why it decreases as the viewing angle increases.

          Write the result of part b), that is, the refractive index of water, in the answer box.

          Apparent depth of the object at different viewing angles (example data)
          $\alpha_2\,({}^\circ)$$h'\,(\text{cm})$
          10.044.9
          20.043.8
          30.042.0
          40.039.6
          Answer

          $n \approx 1.33$

          Show the solution

          Given

          depth of the object $d = 60.0\,\text{cm}$, viewing angles $\alpha_2 = 10.0^\circ$; $20.0^\circ$; $30.0^\circ$; $40.0^\circ$ and apparent depths $h' = 44.9$; $43.8$; $42.0$; $39.6\,\text{cm}$, refractive index of air $n_2 = 1.00$

          Find

          the refractive index of water $n$ (no unit) as the mean of the four rows; also the justification for the apparent depth

          Principle

          The ray travelling from the object to a point on the surface and the ray arriving at the eye meet the surface at the same point. Its horizontal distance from the point above the object is $x = d\tan\alpha_1$ in the water and $x = h'\tan\alpha_2$ through the apparent position. Setting these equal gives $\alpha_1$. We assume that the object appears to be directly above its real position, so the extension drawn through the apparent position starts from the same vertical line as the object. After that the refractive index is obtained from the law of refraction at the boundary of water and air.

          Reading the table

          The rows of the table are read as pairs $(\alpha_2,\ h')$. For each row its own $\alpha_1$ and its own $n$ are calculated, and finally their mean is calculated, because the measurements scatter slightly.

          Formula

          $d\tan\alpha_1 = h'\tan\alpha_2$, so

          $\tan\alpha_1$$= \dfrac{h'\tan\alpha_2}{d}$

          from the law of refraction $n \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$ follows

          $n$$= \dfrac{n_2 \sin\alpha_2}{\sin\alpha_1}$

          and the mean

          $n_\text{mean}$$= \dfrac{n_{10} + n_{20} + n_{30} + n_{40}}{4}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: $h'$ and $d$ are in centimetres, so their ratio has no unit.

          Substitution

          In the row $10.0^\circ$:

          $\tan\alpha_1$$= \dfrac{44.9 \cdot \tan 10.0^\circ}{60.0}$
          $= 0.1320$
          $\alpha_1$$= 7.517^\circ$
          $n$$= \dfrac{\sin 10.0^\circ}{\sin 7.517^\circ}$
          $= 1.3274$

          In the row $20.0^\circ$: $\alpha_1 = 14.880^\circ$, $n = 1.3319$. In the row $30.0^\circ$: $\alpha_1 = 22.006^\circ$, $n = 1.3344$. In the row $40.0^\circ$: $\alpha_1 = 28.978^\circ$, $n = 1.3268$. The sum is $1.3274 + 1.3319 + 1.3344 + 1.3268 = 5.3205$, so

          $n_\text{mean}$$= \dfrac{5.3205}{4}$
          $= 1.3301$

          Answer

          $n \approx 1.33$

          Three significant figures like the depth and the values of the table. Justification: light is refracted from water into air away from the normal, so the ray arriving at the eye is less steep than the ray in the water, and its extension meets the vertical line through the object above the real depth, so $h' < d$. As the viewing angle grows, the difference between the rays grows, because the refraction is stronger the more oblique the ray is, and therefore $h'$ decreases according to the data from $44.9\,\text{cm}$ to $39.6\,\text{cm}$.

          Reasonableness

          The result corresponds to the tabulated refractive index of water, $1.33$. At a small angle the apparent depth approaches

          $d : n$$= 60.0\,\text{cm} : 1.33$
          $= 45.1\,\text{cm}$

          and the reading $44.9\,\text{cm}$ at the smallest angle $10.0^\circ$ is close to this. In every row $h' < 60.0\,\text{cm}$.

        5. Exercise 10-20

          A horizontal glass plate of thickness $6.0\,\text{mm}$ and refractive index $1.52$ lies on top of a layer of water. The thickness of the water layer is $20.0\,\text{mm}$ and the refractive index of water $1.33$.

          A laser beam arrives from the air at the upper surface of the glass plate at an angle of incidence of $55.0^\circ$. The refractive index of air is $1.00$, and all boundaries are horizontal.

          a) Calculate the angle of refraction in the glass.

          b) Calculate the angle between the ray and the normal in the water. Justify why it would be the same even if there were no glass plate.

          c) What horizontal distance does the ray travel when it passes through the glass plate and the water layer to the lower surface of the water layer? Write the result of part c) in millimetres in the answer box.

          mm
          Answer

          $x \approx 19.5\,\text{mm}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          angle of incidence $\alpha_1 = 55.0^\circ$, thickness of the glass plate $t_2 = 6.0\,\text{mm}$ and refractive index $n_2 = 1.52$, thickness of the water layer $t_3 = 20.0\,\text{mm}$ and refractive index $n_3 = 1.33$, refractive index of air $n_1 = 1.00$

          Find

          the horizontal distance $x$ through the glass and the water in millimetres

          Principle

          The ray is refracted at both boundaries according to the law of refraction. The boundaries are parallel, so the angle in the glass is the same with respect to the normal of the boundary between air and glass and the normal of the boundary between glass and water. Therefore $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2 = n_3 \sin\alpha_3$, and the refractive index of the glass cancels out when the angle in the water is calculated. In each layer the ray travels straight, so the horizontal distance is the thickness of the layer multiplied by the tangent of the angle.

          Formula

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_2}$
          $\sin\alpha_3$$= \dfrac{n_1 \sin\alpha_1}{n_3}$

          $x = t_2 \tan\alpha_2 + t_3 \tan\alpha_3$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the thicknesses are in millimetres, so $x$ is obtained in millimetres.

          Substitution

          a)

          $\sin\alpha_2$$= \dfrac{1.00 \cdot \sin 55.0^\circ}{1.52}$
          $= 0.5389$

          so $\alpha_2 = 32.61^\circ$. b)

          $\sin\alpha_3$$= \dfrac{1.00 \cdot \sin 55.0^\circ}{1.33}$
          $= 0.6159$

          so $\alpha_3 = 38.02^\circ$; without the glass plate the result would be the same, because for a ray passing directly from air into water $1.00 \cdot \sin 55.0^\circ = 1.33 \cdot \sin\alpha_3$. c)

          $x$$= 6.0\,\text{mm} \cdot \tan 32.61^\circ + 20.0\,\text{mm} \cdot \tan 38.02^\circ$
          $= 3.839\,\text{mm} + 15.636\,\text{mm}$
          $= 19.47\,\text{mm}$

          Answer

          $x \approx 19.5\,\text{mm}$

          Three significant figures like the angle of incidence and the refractive indices; the term of the glass ($3.8\,\text{mm}$) is small, so the $20.0\,\text{mm}$ water layer limits the accuracy of the result.

          Reasonableness

          If the ray travelled the whole distance of $26.0\,\text{mm}$ in the direction of the air, $55.0^\circ$, the horizontal distance would be $26.0\,\text{mm} \cdot \tan 55.0^\circ = 37.1\,\text{mm}$. In denser materials the ray is closer to the normal, so the distance is shorter than this, and $19.5\,\text{mm} < 37.1\,\text{mm}$. The angles $32.6^\circ < 38.0^\circ < 55.0^\circ$ are in the order of the refractive indices $1.52 > 1.33 > 1.00$.
