## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–7 min, Think about it.** The hook; each student writes three remembered events, then pairs compare.
- **7–11 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, committed, with a reason.
- **11–45 min, Investigate.** *How to use the timeline* together on the projector (4 min): show an era button, the theme menu, the search and one *Show on the map* link. Pairs then work through the exploration sheet: Task 1 themes by era (8 min), Task 2 claims and checks (8 min), Task 3 timeline to map (7 min), Task 4 turning points with another pair (7 min).
- **45–52 min, Theory summary.** *What the timeline shows* and the misconception; back to the predictions.
- **52–60 min, Apply.** The pattern matching (3 min) and the plan-or-fact error (2 min); the timeline card is optional.
- **60–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks (4 min), the chapter's big question in writing (8 min), the summary and the French corner (3 min).

**Fast pairs** write their own timeline card (the optional extension), or use the map unit's year filter to compare the facilities operating in 1960 and in 2026. **If time runs short**, divide Tasks 2 and 3 between the pairs of a group and let them report to each other, and make the big-question paragraph homework: it is the chapter's summary task and worth doing well.

## Organisation and preparation
- One device per pair; a projector for the demonstration. The timeline works offline from the page itself (its data is embedded); the map tiles need an internet connection, but the map's list of facilities works without them.
- The map links open the unit *Global Fusion Explorer* (chapter 8). If that unit is not yet published on your site, do Task 3 with the timeline cards only and come back to the map in chapter 8.
- A paper copy of the Task 1 list helps: students fill the 16 counts (four eras, four themes) by switching eras and themes.
- Photographs: the timeline cards will carry licensed photographs when the course's photo pass adds them; until then the cards are text.

## Assessment
- Self-assessment with the shown criteria. For a grade, the big-question paragraph is the main evidence: at least three events with correct years, one way the Cold War slowed or distorted research, one way it pushed it forward, and a conclusion.
- Source skills: the student distinguishes a checked fact with a date from a plan (the find-the-error task, check 3), and uses the map panel's status rather than guessing.
- Language: past tenses for events, linking words for cause and contrast (*because*, *after*, *on the other hand*).

## Common difficulties
- Counting: the eras share their boundary years (1938, 1958, 1985); the era buttons decide which era an event belongs to, so students should count with the buttons, not by year.
- A timeline looks like a complete list. Repeat that it is a selection; the extension task makes this concrete.
- Plans as facts: ITER's dates after 2026 are the ITER Organization's 2024 baseline (as of November 2026); check iter.org before the lesson.
- Search finds words in the text, so *ZETA* or *Geneva* also shows events that only mention them; students must read the cards.

## Extension ideas
- Which theme has the most events in the cooperation era, and why might the authors have chosen so many machine records? Discuss what a timeline's selection says about its authors.
- Make a class timeline on the wall with the students' own cards (the extension task) and the twelve events the class chose as turning points.
- Compare the timeline's events of the 1950s with the facilities on the map: which 1950s machines appear on both?
