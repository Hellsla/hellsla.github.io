## fy7-13-L1-01
**Given:** line density $N = 300$ lines per millimetre
**Find:** the grating spacing $d$ in micrometres
**Principle:** The grating spacing is the distance between adjacent lines. When $N$ evenly spaced lines fit into a millimetre, the spacing of the lines is a millimetre divided by the number of lines.
**Formula:** $d = \dfrac{1\,\text{mm}}{N}$
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{mm} = 1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$ and finally $1\,\mu\text{m} = 1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $d = \dfrac{1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}}{300} = 3.333 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} = 3.333\,\mu\text{m}$
**Answer:** $3.33\,\mu\text{m}$, three significant figures like the line density $300$.
**Reasonableness:** The grating spacing is a few wavelengths of visible light ($3.33\,\mu\text{m} = 3330\,\text{nm}$), so diffraction and interference are clearly visible. Check: $300 \cdot 3.333\,\mu\text{m} = 1000\,\mu\text{m} = 1\,\text{mm}$.

## fy7-13-L1-02
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 650\,\text{nm}$, grating spacing $d = 2.00\,\mu\text{m}$, order $k = 1$
**Find:** the angle $\theta$ of the first-order maximum in degrees
**Principle:** Light arrives perpendicular to the grating, so the directions of the maxima obey the grating equation: a maximum forms in the direction where the path difference $d\sin\theta$ of adjacent slits is a whole number of wavelengths.
**Formula:** $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so $\theta = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{k\lambda}{d}$
**Conversions:** $650\,\text{nm} = 650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $2.00\,\mu\text{m} = 2.00 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\theta = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{1 \cdot 650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{2.00 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = \sin^{-1} 0.325 = 18.97^\circ$
**Answer:** $19.0^\circ$, three significant figures like the grating spacing $2.00\,\mu\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength is about a third of the grating spacing, so $\sin\theta$ is about $0.33$ and the angle about $19^\circ$, clearly less than $90^\circ$. Check: $2.00\,\mu\text{m} \cdot \sin 18.97^\circ = 0.650\,\mu\text{m}$.

## fy7-13-L1-03
**Given:** grating spacing $d = 2.50\,\mu\text{m}$, order $k = 2$, angle of the maximum $\theta = 30.0^\circ$
**Find:** the wavelength $\lambda$ of the light in nanometres
**Principle:** Light arrives perpendicular to the grating, so the direction of the maximum obeys the grating equation. In the second-order maximum the path difference of adjacent slits is two wavelengths.
**Formula:** $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{d\sin\theta}{k}$
**Conversions:** $2.50\,\mu\text{m} = 2.50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$ and finally $1\,\text{nm} = 1 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{2.50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot \sin 30.0^\circ}{2} = \dfrac{2.50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot 0.500}{2} = 6.25 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m} = 625\,\text{nm}$
**Answer:** $625\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures like the grating spacing and the angle.
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength is in the range of visible light, $400$–$700\,\text{nm}$; $625\,\text{nm}$ is orange-red light. If the order had been forgotten, the result $1250\,\text{nm}$ would be infrared, so dividing by two is necessary. Check: $2 \cdot 625\,\text{nm} = 1250\,\text{nm} = 2500\,\text{nm} \cdot 0.500$.

## fy7-13-L1-04
**Given:** distance from the grating to the screen $L = 1.20\,\text{m}$, distance of the first maximum from the central maximum $x = 30.0\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** the angle $\theta$ of the maximum from the direction of the central maximum in degrees
**Principle:** The screen is perpendicular to the laser beam, so the grating, the central maximum and the maximum form a right-angled triangle whose legs are $L$ and $x$. The tangent of the angle is the ratio of the opposite leg to the adjacent leg.
**Formula:** $\tan\theta = \dfrac{x}{L}$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{x}{L}$
**Conversions:** $x = 30.0\,\text{cm} = 30.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.300\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{0.300\,\text{m}}{1.20\,\text{m}} = \tan^{-1} 0.250 = 14.04^\circ$
**Answer:** $14.0^\circ$, three significant figures like the distances $1.20\,\text{m}$ and $30.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The maximum is a quarter of the distance of the screen to the side, so the angle must be clearly less than $45^\circ$, at which $x$ would equal $L$. Check: $1.20\,\text{m} \cdot \tan 14.04^\circ = 0.300\,\text{m}$.

## fy7-13-L2-01
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 450\,\text{nm}$, line density $N = 600$ lines per millimetre
**Find:** the number of dots of light that form on the screen
**Principle:** Each maximum is one dot of light. According to the grating equation $\sin\theta = k\lambda : d$, and since the sine is at most $1$, a maximum forms only for orders for which $k\lambda < d$. On each side of the central maximum there are equally many maxima.
**Formula:** $d = \dfrac{1\,\text{mm}}{N}$ and $k < \dfrac{d}{\lambda}$; the number of dots is $2k_\text{max} + 1$, where $k_\text{max}$ is the largest whole number that satisfies the condition.
**Conversions:** $d = 1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} : 600 = 1.667 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$ and $450\,\text{nm} = 450 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{d}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1.667 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}}{450 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3.70$, so $k_\text{max} = 3$ and the number of dots is $2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 7$.
**Answer:** $7$ dots of light: the central maximum and the orders $\pm 1$, $\pm 2$ and $\pm 3$. The answer is a whole number, so no rounding is needed.
**Reasonableness:** In the third-order maximum $\sin\theta = 3 \cdot 0.450 : 1.667 = 0.81$, that is $\theta = 54^\circ$, which is possible. In the fourth $\sin\theta = 1.08$, which is impossible, so a fourth-order maximum does not form.

**Reasonableness:** The angle for a sparse grating is small, so $\tan\theta \approx \sin\theta = 0.065$ and $x \approx 2.50\,\text{m} \cdot 0.065 = 16\,\text{cm}$. The spacing of the dots is easy to measure with a tape measure.

## fy7-13-L3-01
**Given:** wavelength of the second order ($k = 2$) $\lambda_2 = 700\,\text{nm}$, third order $k = 3$, wavelengths of white light $400$–$700\,\text{nm}$
**Find:** the wavelength $\lambda_3$ whose third-order maximum is in the same direction, and what this tells about the spectra
**Principle:** In the same direction $\theta$ the left-hand side $d\sin\theta$ of the grating equation is the same for both wavelengths, so the right-hand sides are equal too. The grating spacing is not needed, because it cancels: the result holds for every grating.
**Formula:** $d\sin\theta = 2\lambda_2$ and $d\sin\theta = 3\lambda_3$, so $3\lambda_3 = 2\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3 = \dfrac{2\lambda_2}{3}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the wavelengths stay in nanometres, because the unit cancels in the ratio $2 : 3$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda_3 = \dfrac{2 \cdot 700\,\text{nm}}{3} = 466.7\,\text{nm}$
**Answer:** $467\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures like the wavelength $700\,\text{nm}$. Blue $467\,\text{nm}$ is present in white light, and the third-order spectrum begins from violet, $400\,\text{nm}$, at an even smaller angle. So the red end of the second-order spectrum and the violet and blue part of the third-order spectrum overlap.
**Reasonableness:** The first- and second-order spectra do not overlap, because the second-order violet, $2 \cdot 400\,\text{nm} = 800\,\text{nm}$, is more than the first-order red, $700\,\text{nm}$. Between the second and third orders $3 \cdot 400\,\text{nm} = 1200\,\text{nm}$ is less than $2 \cdot 700\,\text{nm} = 1400\,\text{nm}$, so overlap is to be expected.

## fy7-13-L1-06
**Find:** how the width of a diffraction pattern changes when the slit is made narrower
**Principle:** Diffraction is stronger the closer the slit width is to the wavelength. In a narrow slit the light bends at the edges of the slit into a wider range of angles, and the wavelength is determined by the light source and does not change in the slit.
**Model answer:** The pattern becomes wider, because in a narrow slit the light bends more strongly to the sides. The option that the pattern becomes narrower is not true, because even a narrow slit does not let through only the light that travels straight ahead. The option that the width of the pattern depends only on the wavelength ignores the slit width, although it is exactly what decides the bending. The option that the slit shortens the wavelength and moves the maxima further out is wrong, because the slit does not change the wavelength; the pattern becomes wider because the light bends more, not because the wavelength changes.

## fy7-13-L2-07
**Find:** which change reduces the relative error of the wavelength when the reading error $\Delta x$ is always the same
**Principle:** The wavelength is calculated from the distance $x$, and the relative error is approximately $\Delta x : x$. When $\Delta x$ stays the same, the error is a smaller part the larger $x$ is. So the measurement should be made so that $x$ is as large as possible.
**Model answer:** The relative error decreases when a more distant maximum is measured: the same reading error is then a smaller part of a larger distance. Moving the screen closer to the grating makes $x$ smaller, so the same $\Delta x$ is a larger part and the error grows. A sparser grating makes the angles and so $x$ smaller, so the relative error grows too, and the error is not divided among several maxima. A brighter laser does not change the positions of the maxima, because they are determined by the wavelength, the grating spacing and the distance $L$.

**Principle:** The direction of the maximum is determined by the grating equation $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, and the angle does not depend on the position of the screen. The change therefore has two opposite effects: the denser grating increases the angle, and the closer screen decreases the distance at the same angle. We calculate $x$ for both situations.
**Model answer:** In the initial situation the grating constant is $d_1 = 1\,\text{mm} : 100 = 1.00 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{m}$.

$$\begin{aligned}
&\sin\theta_1 = \frac{650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{1.00 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{m}} = 0.0650 \\
&\theta_1 = 3.727^\circ \\
&x_1 = 2.00\,\text{m} \cdot \tan 3.727^\circ \\
&x_1 = 0.130\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}$$

The result agrees with the given distance $13.0\,\text{cm}$. The grating constant of the new grating is $d_2 = 1\,\text{mm} : 300 = 3.333 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$.

$$\begin{aligned}
&\sin\theta_2 = \frac{650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{3.333 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}} = 0.195 \\
&\theta_2 = 11.24^\circ \\
&x_2 = 1.00\,\text{m} \cdot \tan 11.24^\circ \\
&x_2 = 0.199\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}$$

The ratio is $x_2 : x_1 = 19.9\,\text{cm} : 13.0\,\text{cm} \approx 1.5$. The distance therefore grows to about $1.5$ times the original, from $13.0\,\text{cm}$ to about $19.9\,\text{cm}$.

For small angles $\tan\theta \approx \sin\theta$, so $x \approx L\lambda : d$. The grating constant decreases to a third, which makes the distance about three times as large, and the distance of the screen halves, which halves the distance. Together the factor is $3 : 2 = 1.5$. The exact factor $1.53$ is slightly larger, because at the new angle $11.2^\circ$ the tangent is already clearly larger than the sine. If you took only the grating into account, you would get three times the distance, and from the move of the screen alone a half of it. The denser grating does not bend the light less but more, because when the line density grows, the grating constant decreases.
