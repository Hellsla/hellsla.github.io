Efficiency

Why does an incandescent lamp get so hot that it can burn your finger, even though its job is to give light, and why does an LED lamp shine just as brightly with much less electricity?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the efficiency of a device as a ratio of energies or powers.
  • explain why efficiency is at most $100\,\%$ and why in real devices it is often below that.
  • read an energy flow diagram and work out the shares of useful energy and losses from it.

Prior knowledge

  • Energy is neither created nor destroyed. It changes form, and part of it often becomes heatchapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy
  • The unit of energy is the joule, and when energy is transformed, part of it ends up as heatchapter Energy transformations and the joule
  • Power tells how fast energy is transferred, the unit is the watt, and at constant power energy is power multiplied by timechapter Power P = ΔE/Δt

Simulation

How to use

Choose a device from the menu. Adjust the power taken $P_\text{o}$ and the time $t$ with the sliders. The green arrow is the useful power $P_\text{a}$ and the orange arrow the loss power $P_\text{h}$. The readouts show the powers and the energies in the time $t$. The speed only changes how fast the stripes move in the arrows.

Assumptions of the model

In the model, each device has a constant efficiency. The values are typical rounded values, not measurements of any particular device: incandescent lamp $5\,\%$, LED lamp $30\,\%$, kettle $90\,\%$, electric motor $90\,\%$, petrol engine of a car $25\,\%$ and solar panel $20\,\%$. The efficiency of a real device depends on the load: strongly for a petrol engine, because at a small load it is clearly lower, and only a little for an electric motor when it runs close to the power it was designed for. So the model does not tell how efficiency depends on the power taken. The model assumes that the device runs at constant power. The losses are heat, and in a solar panel they also include reflected light. The readouts of the simulation are values of the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

The useful power is $P_\text{a} = \eta \cdot P_\text{o}$ and the loss power $P_\text{h} = P_\text{o} - P_\text{a}$. In the time $t$ the energies are $E_\text{o} = P_\text{o} \cdot t$, $E_\text{a} = \eta \cdot E_\text{o}$ and $E_\text{h} = E_\text{o} - E_\text{a}$. In the picture the incoming arrow is always $100\,\text{px}$ thick, the useful arrow $\eta \cdot 100\,\text{px}$ and the loss arrow $(1 - \eta) \cdot 100\,\text{px}$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Energy does not disappear, but not all the energy taken by a device becomes the form of energy that you want from the device. The job of an incandescent lamp is to give light, so its useful energy is light. However, most of the electrical energy that the lamp takes becomes heat, which warms the lamp and its surroundings but does not give light. This part is called losses. Losses are not lost energy but energy that has changed into a useless form, usually heat.

    The flow of energy through a device can be shown with an energy flow diagram, also called a Sankey diagram. In the diagram the thickness of an arrow is proportional to energy: the incoming arrow divides into a thin arrow of useful energy and a thick arrow of losses. When the lamp takes electrical energy of $100\,\text{J}$, only $5\,\text{J}$ becomes light and the remaining $95\,\text{J}$ is heat. The thicknesses of the outgoing arrows add up to the thickness of the incoming arrow, because energy is conserved.

    Losses depend on the purpose of the device

    In a kettle, heat is useful when it warms the water, and a loss when it escapes through the sides of the kettle into the room. The heat of an incandescent lamp, on the other hand, is counted as a loss, even if it warms a room in winter, because the benefit of the lamp has been defined as light. So what is useful depends on the purpose of the device, and the value of the efficiency depends on what is defined as useful and what is counted as part of the device.

    Efficiency tells the share of the useful part

    Efficiency is the share of useful energy in the energy taken by the device. Efficiency is at most $100\,\%$, because energy is conserved and you cannot get more useful energy out than you put in. In real devices it is often below $100\,\%$, because part of the energy taken does not end up in the defined useful purpose: for example, it becomes heat in friction or in conductors, and the heat spreads into the surroundings. When the purpose of a device is something other than heat, efficiency varies a lot: an electric motor reaches about $90\,\%$, but a lamp and a combustion engine stay clearly below $100\,\%$. In an electric heater the purpose is the heat itself: in practice all the electrical energy becomes heat that warms the room, so the efficiency is almost $100\,\%$. An ideal, lossless device would have an efficiency of $100\,\%$. The efficiencies of devices vary a lot:

    Device and its useful output typical $\eta$
    incandescent lamp: light $5\,\%$
    LED lamp: light $30\,\%$
    kettle: warming of water $90\,\%$
    electric motor: motion $90\,\%$
    petrol engine: motion of a car $25\,\%$
    solar panel: electricity $20\,\%$

    The values in the table are typical rounded values, not measurements of any particular device. The real efficiency depends on the model of the device and on the way it is used. The value for the petrol engine is typical for steady motorway driving; in city driving and at other small loads the efficiency is clearly lower. The efficiency of an electric motor changes little when it runs close to the power it was designed for.

    Efficiency

    Efficiency $\eta$ (eta) is the ratio of the useful energy to the energy taken by the device. When the device runs at constant power, the same ratio is also obtained from powers:

    $$\eta = \frac{E_\text{a}}{E_\text{o}} = \frac{P_\text{a}}{P_\text{o}}$$

    where $\eta$ is the efficiency (a ratio, often given as a percentage), $E_\text{a}$ the useful energy given by the device (J), $E_\text{o}$ the energy taken by the device (J), $P_\text{a}$ the useful power (W) and $P_\text{o}$ the power taken by the device (W). A ratio is converted to a percentage by multiplying it by $100\,\%$, for example $0.30 \cdot 100\,\% = 30\,\%$. The multiplication does not change the number, because $1\,\% = 0.01$ and so $100\,\% = 100 \cdot 0.01 = 1$.

    The ratio of the powers is the same as the ratio of the energies, because at constant power energy is power multiplied by time (chapter Power P = ΔE/Δt). While the device runs for a time $t$, the energies are $E_\text{a} = P_\text{a} \cdot t$ and $E_\text{o} = P_\text{o} \cdot t$, and the time cancels:

    $$\begin{aligned} \eta &= \frac{E_\text{a}}{E_\text{o}} \\ &= \frac{P_\text{a} \cdot t}{P_\text{o} \cdot t} \\ &= \frac{P_\text{a}}{P_\text{o}} \end{aligned}$$

    So efficiency does not depend on whether energy or power is measured, or on the running time.

    Conservation of energy and losses

    Conservation of energy divides the energy taken by the device into useful energy and losses $E_\text{h}$:

    $$E_\text{o} = E_\text{a} + E_\text{h}$$

    The same holds for powers, $P_\text{o} = P_\text{a} + P_\text{h}$. When the efficiency is known, the useful energy is $E_\text{a} = \eta \cdot E_\text{o}$ and the energy taken is $E_\text{o} = \tfrac{E_\text{a}}{\eta}$.

    Comparing two lamps

    A lamp with a low efficiency needs a higher power to get the same useful output. For a light power of $3\,\text{W}$ an incandescent lamp needs $60\,\text{W}$ but an LED lamp only $10\,\text{W}$, because the efficiencies are $5\,\%$ and $30\,\%$. In the diagram the thicknesses of the arrows are on the same scale: the green arrows of the light power are equally thick, but the incoming arrow of the incandescent lamp is six times thicker.

    In a diagram drawn to scale you can see the efficiency directly: it is the thickness of the useful arrow as a share of the thickness of the incoming arrow. If the incoming arrow is $5.0\,\text{cm}$ thick and the useful arrow $4.0\,\text{cm}$, the efficiency is $4.0 : 5.0 = 0.80$, that is, $80\,\%$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that energy disappears in the losses. However, energy is conserved in every device. The energy leaving an incandescent lamp is equal to the energy entering it: $3\,\text{W}$ of light and $57\,\text{W}$ of heat make $60\,\text{W}$ in total. A loss only means that part of the energy ended up in a form that is of no use.

    Another mistake is to think that a high-power device is efficient. Efficiency is a ratio, not an amount. A $100\,\text{W}$ incandescent lamp gives $5\,\text{W}$ of light and a $60\,\text{W}$ lamp $3\,\text{W}$, but both have an efficiency of $5\,\%$. A high-power device uses more energy, but power does not tell what share of the energy is useful.

    Summary

    • Efficiency is the share of useful energy in the energy taken by the device: $\eta = \tfrac{E_\text{a}}{E_\text{o}} = \tfrac{P_\text{a}}{P_\text{o}}$. It is often given as a percentage and is at most $100\,\%$. In real devices it is often below $100\,\%$, because part of the energy does not end up as the useful output; for an electric heater it is almost $100\,\%$.
    • Energy is conserved: $E_\text{o} = E_\text{a} + E_\text{h}$. Losses are usually heat that spreads into the surroundings.
    • In an energy flow diagram the thickness of an arrow is proportional to energy or power, and efficiency is the share of the useful arrow in the incoming arrow.
    • The efficiency of a device that runs steadily does not depend on running time, because the time cancels from the ratio of the energies. The way the device is used can change it: for example, the efficiency of a petrol engine is clearly lower at a small load.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      A kettle has an efficiency of $90\,\%$ and takes a power of $2.0\,\text{kW}$ from the electricity grid. The kettle is on for $3.0\,\text{min}$. How much electrical energy becomes losses, that is, heat that does not warm the water?

      Step 1/7

      Known and unknown. Efficiency $\eta = 90\,\%$, power taken $P_\text{o} = 2.0\,\text{kW}$ and running time $t = 3.0\,\text{min}$. We want the loss energy $E_\text{h}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The kettle runs at constant power, so the energy taken is power multiplied by time. Efficiency tells what share of the energy taken warms the water. Energy is conserved, so the rest is losses.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The energy taken, the useful energy and the losses are found in order:

      $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{o} &= P_\text{o} \cdot t \\ E_\text{a} &= \eta \cdot E_\text{o} \\ E_\text{h} &= E_\text{o} - E_\text{a} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Power to watts:

      $2.0\,\text{kW}$$= 2.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{W}$
      $= 2000\,\text{W}$

      Time to seconds:

      $3.0\,\text{min}$$= 3.0 \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
      $= 180\,\text{s}$

      Efficiency to a number: $90\,\% = 90 \cdot 0.01 = 0.90$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{o} &= P_\text{o} \cdot t \\ &= 2000\,\text{W} \cdot 180\,\text{s} \\ &= 360\,000\,\text{J} \\[6pt] E_\text{a} &= \eta \cdot E_\text{o} \\ &= 0.90 \cdot 360\,000\,\text{J} \\ &= 324\,000\,\text{J} \\[6pt] E_\text{h} &= E_\text{o} - E_\text{a} \\ &= 360\,000\,\text{J} - 324\,000\,\text{J} \\ &= 36\,000\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The energy that becomes losses is

      $36\,000\,\text{J}$$= 36 \cdot 1000\,\text{J}$
      $= 36\,\text{kJ}$

      The result is given with two significant figures, because the initial values $90\,\%$, $2.0\,\text{kW}$ and $3.0\,\text{min}$ are given to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The losses must be $100\,\% - 90\,\% = 10\,\%$ of the energy taken, and the energy taken is

      $360\,000\,\text{J}$$= 360 \cdot 1000\,\text{J}$
      $= 360\,\text{kJ}$

      so $0.10 \cdot 360\,\text{kJ} = 36\,\text{kJ}$. In addition, the useful energy and the losses add up to the energy taken: $324\,\text{kJ} + 36\,\text{kJ} = 360\,\text{kJ}$.

      Find the error

      A student calculates the efficiency of an LED lamp. The lamp takes a power of $8.0\,\text{W}$ and gives a light power of $2.4\,\text{W}$. The student calculates $\eta = 8.0\,\text{W} : 2.4\,\text{W} = 3.3 = 330\,\%$ and concludes that the lamp produces more energy than it takes.What is the error?

      • The heat is a loss of the lamp, not a benefit, because the benefit of a lamp has been defined as light. If the heat were counted in, the efficiency would be $(2.4 + 5.6) : 8.0 = 100\,\%$ and it would tell nothing about how good the lamp is.

      • Correct. Efficiency tells what share of the power taken is useful, so the power taken is the divisor. Efficiency cannot be over $100\,\%$, so the result $330\,\%$ should have raised suspicion straight away.

      • The conversion is not necessary. Efficiency can be calculated directly from the powers, because both are multiplied by the same time and the time cancels out. The error is turning the ratio the wrong way round.

      • No device produces extra energy, because energy is conserved: the sum of the useful energy and the losses is the energy taken. An efficiency of $330\,\%$ would break this, so the calculation is wrong.

      3. Calculation

      An electric motor takes electrical energy of $2.5\,\text{kJ}$, and its losses are $0.50\,\text{kJ}$. What is the efficiency of the motor? Give the answer as a percentage.

      %
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What you know: the energy taken and the losses. You need the useful energy for the efficiency. Where can you get it?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Energy is conserved, so $E_\text{o} = E_\text{a} + E_\text{h}$. Solve $E_\text{a}$ from it and then calculate $\eta = E_\text{a} : E_\text{o}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $E_\text{a}$$= 2.5\,\text{kJ} - 0.50\,\text{kJ}$
      $= 2.0\,\text{kJ}$

      so

      $\eta$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{kJ}}{2.5\,\text{kJ}}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      energy taken $E_\text{o} = 2.5\,\text{kJ}$ and losses $E_\text{h} = 0.50\,\text{kJ}$

      Find

      the efficiency $\eta$ of the motor as a percentage

      Principle

      Efficiency is the share of the useful energy in the energy taken. The useful energy was not given, but energy is conserved, so it is found as the difference between the energy taken and the losses.

      Formula

      $E_\text{a} = E_\text{o} - E_\text{h}$ and $\eta = \dfrac{E_\text{a}}{E_\text{o}}$, so

      $\eta$$= \dfrac{E_\text{o} - E_\text{h}}{E_\text{o}}$

      Conversions

      Not needed: all energies are in kilojoules, so the units cancel. The ratio is converted to a percentage by multiplying it by $100\,\%$.

      Substitution

      $E_\text{a}$$= 2.5\,\text{kJ} - 0.50\,\text{kJ}$
      $= 2.0\,\text{kJ}$

      so

      $\eta$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{kJ}}{2.5\,\text{kJ}}$
      $= 0.80$
      $= 0.80 \cdot 100\,\%$
      $= 80\,\%$

      Answer

      $80\,\%$

      Two significant figures, as in the given energies.

      Reasonableness

      The losses are $0.50 : 2.5 = 20\,\%$ of the energy taken, and $80\,\% + 20\,\% = 100\,\%$. The result is below $100\,\%$ and close to the typical $90\,\%$ of an electric motor.

      Optional 4. Explain

      The efficiency of an incandescent lamp stays clearly below $100\,\%$ even though energy does not vanish anywhere. Explain why.

      Model answer

      Energy is conserved, so the energy taken by an incandescent lamp divides into useful energy, that is, light, and losses. In the lamp an electric current heats the filament very hot, and only a small part of the energy leaves as light. For example, of a $60\,\text{W}$ lamp about $3\,\text{W}$ is light and the remaining $57\,\text{W}$ is heat that spreads into the surroundings. The heat is a loss here, because the purpose of the lamp is to give light. The efficiency $3\,\text{W} : 60\,\text{W} = 5\,\%$ is below $100\,\%$, because the useful energy is only a part of the energy taken. No energy is lost: $3\,\text{W} + 57\,\text{W} = 60\,\text{W}$.

        1. A device takes energy of $500\,\text{J}$ and gives useful energy of $350\,\text{J}$ from it. What is its efficiency, and where does the rest of the energy go?

        3. The figure shows the energy flow diagram of an electric motor on squared paper. The thicknesses of the arrows are drawn to scale, and the energy entering the motor is marked in the figure.

          How much energy becomes losses in the motor? Give the answer in joules.

          J
          Answer

          $180\,\text{J}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          from the figure, the electrical energy entering the motor $E_\text{o} = 240\,\text{J}$, and the thicknesses of the arrows in squares: incoming arrow $d_\text{o} = 8$ squares, useful energy arrow $d_\text{a} = 2$ squares and loss arrow $d_\text{h} = 6$ squares

          Find

          the energy that becomes losses, $E_\text{h}$, in joules

          Principle

          In an energy flow diagram drawn to scale, the thickness of an arrow is proportional to energy. So one square always corresponds to the same energy, and the thickness and the energy of the incoming arrow tell how much. Energy is conserved, so the thicknesses of the outgoing arrows add up to the thickness of the incoming arrow.

          Reading the graph

          The incoming arrow spans eight squares from top to bottom. The green arrow of useful energy on the right edge is two squares thick and the orange loss arrow is six squares thick; the thickness is read from the straight part of the arrow, before the head. Check: $2 + 6 = 8$ squares.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{E_\text{h}}{E_\text{o}}$$= \dfrac{d_\text{h}}{d_\text{o}}$

          so

          $E_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{d_\text{h}}{d_\text{o}} \cdot E_\text{o}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the thicknesses are in the same unit (squares), so they cancel, and the energy is in joules.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{6}{8} \cdot 240\,\text{J}$
          $= 0.75 \cdot 240\,\text{J}$
          $= 180\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $180\,\text{J}$

          Or $1.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{J}$, two significant figures, as in the incoming energy $240\,\text{J}$.

          Reasonableness

          One square corresponds to the energy $240\,\text{J} : 8 = 30\,\text{J}$, so the useful energy is $2 \cdot 30\,\text{J} = 60\,\text{J}$ and the losses $6 \cdot 30\,\text{J} = 180\,\text{J}$; together $240\,\text{J}$, as conservation of energy requires. The efficiency is $60\,\text{J} : 240\,\text{J} = 25\,\%$, which is possible for a small toy motor, for example.
