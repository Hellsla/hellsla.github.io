Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy

Why does a skateboarder who is twice as fast rise four times as high on a ramp?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy of an object relative to a chosen zero level.
  • explain why kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed and why only the change in potential energy matters.
  • identify conservative forces and explain why no potential energy is associated with friction.

Prior knowledge

  • An object thrown straight up at speed $v$ rises to a height $\tfrac{v^2}{2g}$ when air resistance is smallchapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion
  • The weight of an object is $G = m g$chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane
  • Energy changes from one form to another and its unit is the joulechapter Energy transformations and the joule

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 1.

1Think about it

A skateboarder rolls along level ground and goes up a straight ramp until she stops for a moment and starts back down. Friction and air resistance are small. When her speed at the foot of the ramp is $3.0\,\text{m/s}$, she rises to a height of about $0.46\,\text{m}$. When the speed is doubled, $6.0\,\text{m/s}$, she rises to about $1.8\,\text{m}$, that is, four times as high.

What grows so much in the skateboarder's motion that doubling the speed is enough for four times the rise?

Review from the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion

A ball is thrown straight up at a speed of $10\,\text{m/s}$, and air resistance is small. The acceleration of the ball is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downward all the time.How high above the throwing point does the ball rise?

  • You calculated $\tfrac{v}{2g}$, whose unit is the second, not the metre. The speed appears in the rise height to the second power: $h = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$.

  • The number $\tfrac{v}{g} \approx 1.0$ is the time of rise in seconds, not a height. The height is the average speed times the time of rise: $\tfrac{v}{2} \cdot \tfrac{v}{g} = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$.

  • You calculated $\tfrac{v^2}{g}$, that is, you multiplied the initial speed by the time of rise. The ball slows down all the way up, so its average speed is only half the initial speed: $h = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$.

  • Correct. The time of rise is $t = \tfrac{v}{g} \approx 1.02\,\text{s}$, and the average speed during the rise is half the initial speed, so $h = \tfrac{v}{2} \cdot \tfrac{v}{g} = \tfrac{v^2}{2g} = \tfrac{(10\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 5.1\,\text{m}$.

Simulation

How to use

Choose the initial speed $v_0$, the mass of the cart, the steepness of the ramp and the friction. Press Start and let the cart rise and return to the foot of the ramp; the run ends there. The blue arrow is the speed of the cart, and the dashed line marks the highest point $h_\text{max}$. On the right (on a phone, below) the orange bar is the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$, the green one the potential energy $E_\text{p}$ and the grey one their sum, the mechanical energy $E_\text{mech}$. The bars have a fixed scale, so the bars of different runs can be compared with each other. The Speed option changes only the pace of the animation.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation, not measurements. The cart is a point mass, and the ramp is straight: the angle of the gentle ramp is $25^\circ$ and that of the steep one $50^\circ$. The zero level is the foot of the ramp ($h = 0$), and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. There is no air resistance. When friction is selected, the motion is opposed by a friction force with coefficient of friction $\mu = 0.10$; otherwise there is no friction.

How the calculation works

On the ramp the acceleration of the cart is $g(\sin\alpha + \mu\cos\alpha)$ when going up and $g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha)$ when coming down, in the direction down along the ramp. The height is $h = s\sin\alpha$, where $s$ is the distance travelled along the surface of the ramp. The energies are calculated at every moment: $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$, $E_\text{p} = m g h$ and $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$. $h_\text{max}$ is the highest point and $v_\text{return}$ the speed when the cart has returned to the foot of the ramp.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Height stores energy

    In the chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy, potential energy and kinetic energy were forms of energy. Now we calculate how much of them there is. When an object is lifted higher, energy is transferred to it: a book lifted onto a shelf can gain speed when it falls, and water pumped uphill can turn a turbine. This energy tied to the position of an object is called potential energy. Gravitational potential energy is greater the greater the mass of the object and the higher it is. Lifting two identical books onto the same shelf takes twice as much energy as lifting one, and lifting by two metres takes twice as much as lifting by one metre. The height is measured from a chosen zero level, for example the floor.

    Kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed

    A moving object has kinetic energy. When a cart goes up a frictionless ramp, it slows down, and its kinetic energy changes into potential energy. The cart rises as high as a ball thrown straight up at the same speed in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion, that is, to the height $h = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$: on the ramp the acceleration is smaller, but the distance is correspondingly longer. At double the speed the cart therefore rises four times as high, and that takes four times as much energy. Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed. The mass does not change the rise height, but lifting a cart that is twice as heavy to the same height takes twice the energy. Kinetic energy is therefore also proportional to the mass.

    The square of the speed shows in traffic. When the speed of a car grows from $50\,\text{km/h}$ to $70\,\text{km/h}$, the kinetic energy grows by the factor $\left(\tfrac{70}{50}\right)^2 \approx 2.0$, that is, almost doubles. This is exactly the energy that braking or a collision has to change into other energy.

    A conservative force returns the energy

    When a frictionless cart has risen to its highest point on the ramp, it starts back down and is at the foot of the ramp as fast as when it started. Gravity returns as kinetic energy all the energy that changed into potential energy in the rise. In addition, a frictionless object that starts from rest at a height $h$ gets the same speed on the way down, whether it falls straight down or slides down a steep or a gentle ramp: the energy depends only on height, not on the path. A force with these properties is called a conservative force. Potential energy is always associated with a conservative force.

    Gravitational potential energy is not the only kind of potential energy. A stretched spring, a drawn bow and a compressed trampoline return the energy as kinetic energy: the spring force is conservative, and a stretched spring has spring potential energy. The force between electric charges is also conservative. Friction and air resistance, on the other hand, are not conservative. They change kinetic energy into the internal energy of the objects and the air, and this energy does not return as motion even if the object returns to its starting place. That is why no potential energy is associated with friction.

    Mechanical energy

    The kinetic energy and the potential energy of an object together are its mechanical energy. When a cart goes up a frictionless ramp, the kinetic energy decreases and the potential energy grows by the same amount, so the mechanical energy stays the same. When friction acts, part of the mechanical energy changes into internal energy, and the mechanical energy decreases. In the chapter Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle this becomes a tool for calculation.

    Kinetic energy and potential energy

    Gravitational potential energy is

    $$E_\text{p} = m g h$$

    where $E_\text{p}$ is the potential energy ($\text{J}$), $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ the acceleration of free fall and $h$ the height from the chosen zero level ($\text{m}$). The expression holds near the surface of the Earth, where $g$ is constant. Below the zero level $h < 0$, so the potential energy is negative. The unit is $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2 \cdot \text{m} = \text{N} \cdot \text{m} = \text{J}$.

    Kinetic energy is

    $$E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$$

    where $E_\text{k}$ is the kinetic energy ($\text{J}$), $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$) and $v$ the speed ($\text{m/s}$). The expression is obtained from the rise height on a ramp. A frictionless cart rises to the height $h = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$, and its potential energy then grows by $m g h = m g \cdot \tfrac{v^2}{2g} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$. That is exactly how much kinetic energy the cart had at the foot of the ramp. Kinetic energy is never negative, and it does not depend on the direction of motion.

    The zero level is chosen

    You may choose the zero level freely, as long as it stays the same throughout the calculation. When a $0.50\,\text{kg}$ book is on a shelf $1.80\,\text{m}$ above the floor, its potential energy is $8.8\,\text{J}$ relative to the floor but only $4.9\,\text{J}$ relative to the surface of a table $0.80\,\text{m}$ high. When the book is moved from the shelf to the table, the potential energy decreases by the same amount in both cases, $4.9\,\text{J}$. Only the change in potential energy has physical significance, and it does not depend on the zero level.

    Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy:

    $$E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$$

    where $E_\text{mech}$ is the mechanical energy ($\text{J}$). Because potential energy depends on the zero level, the value of the mechanical energy depends on it too. Always state in a calculation what the zero level is.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that double the speed means double the kinetic energy. Kinetic energy, however, grows with the square of the speed: double the speed gives four times and triple the speed nine times the kinetic energy. Another mistake is to treat potential energy as a property of the object itself with one correct value. Its value depends on the chosen zero level, and only the change in potential energy is the same whatever the zero level. A third mistake is to think that potential energy is associated only with gravity. Potential energy is also associated with the spring force, but not with friction, because friction does not return the energy as motion.

    Summary

    • Kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$ is proportional to the mass and the square of the speed: double the speed gives four times the kinetic energy.
    • Gravitational potential energy $E_\text{p} = m g h$ depends on the height above the chosen zero level. Below the zero level it is negative, and only its change has physical significance.
    • Potential energy is associated with a conservative force, such as gravity or the spring force, which returns the energy as kinetic energy and whose energy depends only on position, not on the path. Friction and air resistance are not conservative.
    • Mechanical energy is $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$. When there is no friction or air resistance and the object is not pushed or pulled, it stays the same; friction and air resistance change part of it into internal energy.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A snowboarder whose mass with equipment is $68\,\text{kg}$ goes down a slope. At a point $25\,\text{m}$ above the bottom of the slope her speed is $12\,\text{m/s}$. How great is the snowboarder's mechanical energy at this point when the bottom of the slope is chosen as the zero level?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Mass $m = 68\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 12\,\text{m/s}$, height above the zero level $h = 25\,\text{m}$ and acceleration of free fall $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We are asked for the mechanical energy $E_\text{mech}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy. Kinetic energy is calculated from the speed and potential energy from the height above the zero level, that is, the bottom of the slope. With such a small difference in height the acceleration of free fall is constant, so $E_\text{p} = m g h$ holds.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{mech} &= E_\text{k} + E_\text{p} \\ E_\text{mech} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2 + m g h \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. Both terms come in the unit $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = \text{J}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate each energy and then their sum:

      $$\begin{aligned} &E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2 \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 68\,\text{kg} \cdot (12\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &= 4896\,\text{J} \\[4pt] &E_\text{p} = m g h \\ &= 68\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 25\,\text{m} \\ &\approx 16\,677\,\text{J} \\[4pt] &E_\text{mech} = 4896\,\text{J} + 16\,677\,\text{J} \\ &\approx 21\,573\,\text{J} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The snowboarder's mechanical energy is about $22\,\text{kJ}$ relative to the bottom of the slope. Two significant figures, like the mass $68\,\text{kg}$, the speed $12\,\text{m/s}$ and the height $25\,\text{m}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. With rounded numbers

      $E_\text{k}$$\approx \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 70\,\text{kg} \cdot 150\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
      $\approx 5\,\text{kJ}$

      and

      $E_\text{p}$$\approx 70\,\text{kg} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 25\,\text{m}$
      $= 17.5\,\text{kJ}$

      together about $23\,\text{kJ}$, close to the result. Both energies are positive, because the snowboarder is moving and is above the zero level.

      Find the error

      A student calculates the kinetic energy of a $1000\,\text{kg}$ car when the speed of the car is $36\,\text{km/h}$. He writes $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 36^2 = 648\,000\,\text{J}$. Where is the error?

      • Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, so the square belongs in the calculation. The error is that the speed has not been converted to the unit $\text{m/s}$.

      • Correct. $36\,\text{km/h} = \tfrac{36 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 10\,\text{m/s}$, and $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot (10\,\text{m/s})^2 = 50\,000\,\text{J}$. The joule is $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so the speed must be in the unit $\text{m/s}$.

      • The factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ belongs in the expression for kinetic energy: it comes from the rise height $h = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$. The error is in the unit: the speed was substituted in the unit $\text{km/h}$.

      • The formula is correct, but you get the joule only from SI units. $36\,\text{km/h} = 10\,\text{m/s}$, so the correct result is $50\,\text{kJ}$, not $648\,\text{kJ}$.

      Apply to a new situation

      A road safety campaign compares a collision with a fall. A car drives at a speed of $50\,\text{km/h}$.

      How high above the zero level would the potential energy of the car be equal to its kinetic energy at this speed? Answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Write an equation where the potential energy is equal to the kinetic energy. What happens to the mass?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $m g h = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$, so the mass cancels and $h = \tfrac{v^2}{2g}$. First convert the speed to the unit $\text{m/s}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Show the solution

      Given

      speed $v = 50\,\text{km/h}$, acceleration of free fall $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the mass of the car is not given

      Principle

      Kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$ and potential energy $m g h$. Set them equal. The mass is a factor in both, so it cancels and is not needed. Near the surface of the Earth the height is small, so $g$ is constant.

      Formula

      $m g h = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$, so $h = \dfrac{v^2}{2g}$

      Conversions

      $\approx 13.89\,\text{m/s}$

      Substitution

      $h$$= \dfrac{(13.89\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
      $\approx 9.83\,\text{m}$

      $9.8\,\text{m}$

      Two significant figures like the speed $50\,\text{km/h}$.

      Reasonableness

      A storey of an apartment building is about $3\,\text{m}$, so a collision at $50\,\text{km/h}$ corresponds in energy to a fall from the height of about the third storey.       1. Concept

      A bow's string is drawn and an arrow is shot. In another situation a box sliding on the floor stops because of friction.With which force is potential energy associated?

      • Friction changes the kinetic energy into the internal energy of the floor and the box: the surfaces warm up slightly. This energy does not return as motion of the box, so it is not potential energy. Friction is not a conservative force.

      • Each force changes the kinetic energy of an object here, but potential energy is associated only with a force that returns the energy. The spring returns it, friction does not. So potential energy is associated only with the spring force.

      • Gravitational potential energy is not the only kind of potential energy. A stretched spring has the potential energy of a spring, because the spring force is conservative and returns the energy as kinetic energy.

      • Correct. The spring force is conservative: the energy of a stretched spring returns as kinetic energy, and it depends only on the stretch of the spring. Friction changes the kinetic energy of the box into internal energy, which does not return as motion, so no potential energy is associated with friction.

      2. Reading a graph

      The graph shows the kinetic energy of a ball as a function of its speed. What is the mass of the ball? Answer in kilograms.

      kg
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Choose a point on the curve that falls on the crossing of grid lines and read its speed and kinetic energy.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      For example, at the speed $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ the kinetic energy is $4.0\,\text{J}$. Solve the mass from the equation $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $m$$= \dfrac{2E_\text{k}}{v^2}$
      $= \dfrac{2 \cdot 4.0\,\text{J}}{(4.0\,\text{m/s})^2}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      from the graph the point $v = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, $E_\text{k} = 4.0\,\text{J}$; the graph is a parabola starting from the origin

      Find

      the mass $m$ of the ball in kilograms

      Principle

      Kinetic energy is $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$. Every point of the graph satisfies this equation with the same mass, so the mass can be found from one point that is easy to read.

      Reading the graph

      The point $(4.0\,\text{m/s}$; $4.0\,\text{J})$ is at the crossing of grid lines, so it is read exactly. The points $(2.0\,\text{m/s}$; $1.0\,\text{J})$ and $(6.0\,\text{m/s}$; $9.0\,\text{J})$ also fall on crossings.

      Formula

      $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$, so $m = \dfrac{2E_\text{k}}{v^2}$

      Conversions

      The energy is in joules and the speed in $\text{m/s}$, and $\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = \text{kg}$. No conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $m$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 4.0\,\text{J}}{(4.0\,\text{m/s})^2}$
      $= \dfrac{8.0\,\text{J}}{16\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
      $= 0.50\,\text{kg}$

      Answer

      $0.50\,\text{kg}$

      Two significant figures like the values read from the graph.

      Reasonableness

      Another point gives the same: $\tfrac{2 \cdot 9.0\,\text{J}}{(6.0\,\text{m/s})^2} = 0.50\,\text{kg}$. A ball of half a kilogram is about the size class of a football.

      3. Explain

      Explain the phenomenon at the beginning: why does a skateboarder who is twice as fast rise four times as high on the ramp? Use the concepts kinetic energy and potential energy.

      Model answer

      When friction and air resistance are small, the skateboarder's kinetic energy changes into potential energy on the ramp until she stops at the highest point. Kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$ is proportional to the square of the speed, so at the speed $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ the skateboarder has $2^2 = 4$ times as much kinetic energy as at the speed $3.0\,\text{m/s}$. Potential energy $E_\text{p} = m g h$ is proportional to the height, so four times the energy lifts her to four times the height: to about $1.8\,\text{m}$ instead of $0.46\,\text{m}$. The mass has no effect, because it is a factor in both energies.

        Finally, three quick questions. Answer first yourself and open the answer only after that.

        1. A book is in the basement, below the floor chosen as the zero level. Is its potential energy positive, zero or negative? Justify.
          Answer

          Negative. Potential energy is $E_\text{p} = m g h$, and below the zero level the height $h$ is negative. A negative value only means that the book has less potential energy than at the zero level.

        2. A cyclist's mass doubles because of a heavy load, and her speed is halved. How does her kinetic energy change?
          Answer

          The kinetic energy is halved. The mass doubles it, but half the speed reduces it to a quarter:

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2m \cdot \left(\tfrac{v}{2}\right)^2$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$
        3. Two students calculate the potential energy of the same book on a shelf. One gets $9.8\,\text{J}$, the other $4.9\,\text{J}$. Can both be right?
          Answer

          They can, if they have chosen different zero levels, for example the floor and the surface of a table. The value of potential energy depends on the zero level, but the change in potential energy when the book is moved is the same for both.

        1. Core

          The mass of a passenger car is $1200\,\text{kg}$, and it drives at a speed of $20.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          How great is the kinetic energy of the car? Answer in kilojoules.

          kJ
          Answer

          240.0 kJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 20.0\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ of the car in kilojoules

          Principle

          A moving object has kinetic energy, which is proportional to the mass and the square of the speed. The speed and the mass of the car are known, so the kinetic energy is obtained directly.

          Formula

          $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2$. The formula is already solved for the quantity asked.

          Conversions

          The mass and the speed are in SI units, so the result comes in joules. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{k}$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg} \cdot (20.0\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 600\,\text{kg} \cdot 400\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
          $= 240\,000\,\text{J}$
          $= 240\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $240\,\text{kJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $1200\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          With rounded numbers

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 400\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
          $= 200\,\text{kJ}$

          so the order of magnitude is right. The speed $20\,\text{m/s} = 72\,\text{km/h}$ is a normal speed on a country road, and the kinetic energy of a car is then hundreds of kilojoules.

        2. Core

          A flower pot with a mass of $2.50\,\text{kg}$ is on a balcony $8.00\,\text{m}$ above the ground.

          How great is the potential energy of the pot when the zero level is the ground?

          J
          Answer

          196.20000000000002 J

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 2.50\,\text{kg}$, height $h = 8.00\,\text{m}$ above the zero level, that is, the ground, acceleration of free fall $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the potential energy $E_\text{p}$ of the pot in joules

          Principle

          Gravitational potential energy depends on the mass and the height above the chosen zero level. The difference in height is small, so $g$ is constant and $E_\text{p} = m g h$ holds.

          Formula

          $E_\text{p} = m g h$. The formula is already solved for the quantity asked.

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, and $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2 \cdot \text{m} = \text{J}$. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{p}$$= 2.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 8.00\,\text{m}$
          $= 196.2\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $196\,\text{J}$

          Three significant figures like the mass and the height.

          Reasonableness

          With rounded numbers $2.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 8\,\text{m} = 200\,\text{J}$. The weight of the pot is about $25\,\text{N}$, and lifting it to eight metres takes an energy of a few hundred joules. The result is positive, because the pot is above the zero level.

        3. A gull with a mass of $1.20\,\text{kg}$ flies at a speed of $10.0\,\text{m/s}$ at a height of $20.0\,\text{m}$ above sea level.

          How great is the mechanical energy of the gull when the zero level is sea level?

          J
          Answer

          295.44 J

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 1.20\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 10.0\,\text{m/s}$, height $h = 20.0\,\text{m}$ above the zero level, that is, sea level, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the mechanical energy $E_\text{mech}$ of the gull in joules

          Principle

          Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy. Kinetic energy is obtained from the speed and potential energy from the height above sea level.

          Formula

          $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$, so $E_\text{mech} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2 + m g h$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so both terms come in joules. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{mech}$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.20\,\text{kg} \cdot (10.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + 1.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 20.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 60.0\,\text{J} + 235.4\,\text{J}$
          $\approx 295\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $295\,\text{J}$

          Three significant figures like the initial data.

          Reasonableness

          With rounded numbers

          $60\,\text{J} + 1.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 20\,\text{m}$
          $= 60\,\text{J} + 240\,\text{J}$
          $= 300\,\text{J}$

          The potential energy is greater than the kinetic energy, which suits a gull flying high but slowly.

        4. A climber whose mass with equipment is $72\,\text{kg}$ climbs $12\,\text{m}$ up a climbing wall. By how much does his potential energy increase? Answer in kilojoules.

          kJ
          Answer

          8.47584 kJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 72\,\text{kg}$, change in height $\Delta h = 12\,\text{m}$ upward, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the change in potential energy $\Delta E_\text{p}$ in kilojoules

          Principle

          Potential energy is proportional to height, so its change depends only on the change in height, not on the zero level. The climber goes up, so the potential energy increases.

          Formula

          $E_\text{p} = m g h$, so $\Delta E_\text{p} = m g h_2 - m g h_1$, that is, $\Delta E_\text{p} = m g\,\Delta h$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so the result comes in joules. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $\Delta E_\text{p}$$= 72\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 12\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 8476\,\text{J}$
          $\approx 8.48\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $8.5\,\text{kJ}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $72\,\text{kg}$ and the height $12\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The climber's weight is about $700\,\text{N}$, and $700\,\text{N} \cdot 12\,\text{m} \approx 8400\,\text{J}$. The result does not depend on the height from which the climber started, because only the change is asked.

        1. Core

          The mass of a delivery van is $1350\,\text{kg}$, and it drives on a motorway at a speed of $90\,\text{km/h}$.

          How great is the kinetic energy of the van? Answer in kilojoules.

          kJ
          Answer

          421.875 kJ

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 1350\,\text{kg}$, speed $v = 90\,\text{km/h}$

          Find

          the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ of the van in kilojoules

          Principle

          Kinetic energy is proportional to the mass and the square of the speed. The joule is $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so the speed must first be converted to the unit $\text{m/s}$.

          Formula

          $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2$. The formula is already solved for the quantity asked.

          Conversions

          $90\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{90 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
          $= 25\,\text{m/s}$

          and finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{k}$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1350\,\text{kg} \cdot (25\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 675\,\text{kg} \cdot 625\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
          $= 421\,875\,\text{J}$
          $\approx 422\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $422\,\text{kJ}$

          That is, to two significant figures $4.2 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{kJ}$, like the speed $90\,\text{km/h}$.

          Reasonableness

          With rounded numbers

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1400\,\text{kg} \cdot 600\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
          $= 420\,\text{kJ}$

          If the speed were substituted in kilometres per hour, the result would be almost $13$ times too large, because $3.6^2 \approx 13$.

        2. Core

          The mass of a tennis ball is $57\,\text{g}$. After a serve the kinetic energy of the ball is $82\,\text{J}$. What is the speed of the ball?

          m/s
          Answer

          53.639472242520625 m/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 57\,\text{g}$, kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = 82\,\text{J}$

          Find

          the speed $v$ of the ball in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          Kinetic energy depends on the mass and the speed. When the energy and the mass are known, the speed is solved from the expression for kinetic energy. The speed is positive, because we ask for its magnitude.

          Formula

          $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2$, so $v^2 = \dfrac{2E_\text{k}}{m}$ and $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_\text{k}}{m}}$

          Conversions

          $m$$= 57\,\text{g}$
          $= 57 : 1000\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.057\,\text{kg}$

          Substitution

          $v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 82\,\text{J}}{0.057\,\text{kg}}}$
          $= \sqrt{2877\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
          $\approx 53.6\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $54\,\text{m/s}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $57\,\text{g}$ and the energy $82\,\text{J}$.

          Reasonableness

          $54\,\text{m/s} \approx 190\,\text{km/h}$, which is the speed of a hard serve by a top player. Calculated back,

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.057\,\text{kg} \cdot (54\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $\approx 83\,\text{J}$

          close to the given energy.

        3. A book with a mass of $1.20\,\text{kg}$ is on a shelf $1.90\,\text{m}$ above the floor. In the room there is a table whose surface is $0.75\,\text{m}$ above the floor.

          How great is the potential energy of the book when the surface of the table is chosen as the zero level?

          J
          Answer

          13.537799999999999 J

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 1.20\,\text{kg}$, height of the shelf from the floor $1.90\,\text{m}$, height of the table surface from the floor $0.75\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the zero level is the surface of the table

          Find

          the potential energy $E_\text{p}$ of the book relative to the surface of the table in joules

          Principle

          In potential energy the height is measured from the chosen zero level. The zero level is the surface of the table, so the height is the difference between the heights of the shelf and the table, not the height of the shelf from the floor.

          Formula

          $E_\text{p} = m g h$, where $h = h_\text{shelf} - h_\text{table}$. So $E_\text{p} = m g\,(h_\text{shelf} - h_\text{table})$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{p}$$= 1.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (1.90 - 0.75)\,\text{m}$
          $= 1.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.15\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 13.5\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $13.5\,\text{J}$

          Three significant figures like the mass and the difference in height $1.15\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          Relative to the floor the potential energy would be

          $1.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.90\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 22.4\,\text{J}$

          Relative to the table it is smaller, because the book is lower relative to the surface of the table than relative to the floor. The difference $8.8\,\text{J}$ is exactly the share of the height of the table, $0.75\,\text{m}$.

        4. A remote-controlled model plane with a mass of $1.80\,\text{kg}$ flies $40.0\,\text{m}$ above the ground. Its mechanical energy relative to the ground is $905\,\text{J}$.

          What is the speed of the plane?

          m/s
          Answer

          14.857844916257388 m/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 1.80\,\text{kg}$, height $h = 40.0\,\text{m}$ above the ground, mechanical energy $E_\text{mech} = 905\,\text{J}$ relative to the ground, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the speed $v$ of the plane in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy. When the potential energy is calculated from the height and subtracted from the mechanical energy, the kinetic energy is left, and the speed is solved from it.

          Formula

          $E_\text{mech} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2 + m g h$, so $\tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2 = E_\text{mech} - m g h$ and

          $v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2\,(E_\text{mech} - m g h)}{m}}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $m g h$$= 1.80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 40.0\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 706.3\,\text{J}$

          so

          $v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot (905 - 706.3)\,\text{J}}{1.80\,\text{kg}}}$
          $= \sqrt{220.8\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
          $\approx 14.9\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $14.9\,\text{m/s}$

          Three significant figures like the initial data.

          Reasonableness

          Calculated back,

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.80\,\text{kg} \cdot (14.9\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $\approx 200\,\text{J}$

          and $200\,\text{J} + 706\,\text{J} \approx 905\,\text{J}$. The speed $15\,\text{m/s} \approx 54\,\text{km/h}$ is normal for a model plane.

        1. A lorry with a mass of $12.0\,\text{t}$ drives in a built-up area at a speed of $40.0\,\text{km/h}$.

          At what speed should a passenger car with a mass of $1.20\,\text{t}$ drive for its kinetic energy to be equal to that of the lorry? Answer in km/h.

          km/h
          Answer

          126.49110640673518 km/h

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the lorry $m_2 = 12.0\,\text{t}$ and speed $v_2 = 40.0\,\text{km/h}$, mass of the passenger car $m_1 = 1.20\,\text{t}$

          Find

          the speed $v_1$ of the passenger car in $\text{km/h}$ when the kinetic energies are equal

          Principle

          Kinetic energy is proportional to the mass and the square of the speed. The lighter car has to drive faster, but because the speed is squared, ten times the mass is made up for by only $\sqrt{10}$ times the speed.

          Formula

          $\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_1 v_1^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m_2 v_2^2$, so $v_1^2 = \dfrac{m_2}{m_1}\,v_2^2$ and $v_1 = v_2\sqrt{\dfrac{m_2}{m_1}}$

          Conversions

          The formula has only the ratio of the masses and the speed is multiplied by it, so the tonnes need not be converted to kilograms nor the speed to $\text{m/s}$: the units are preserved.

          Substitution

          $v_1$$= 40.0\,\text{km/h} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{12.0\,\text{t}}{1.20\,\text{t}}}$
          $= 40.0\,\text{km/h} \cdot \sqrt{10.0}$
          $\approx 126.5\,\text{km/h}$

          Answer

          $126\,\text{km/h}$

          Three significant figures like the initial data.

          Reasonableness

          Check in joules: $40.0\,\text{km/h} = 11.11\,\text{m/s}$ and $126.5\,\text{km/h} = 35.14\,\text{m/s}$, so

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 12\,000\,\text{kg} \cdot (11.11\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $\approx 741\,\text{kJ}$

          and

          $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg} \cdot (35.14\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $\approx 741\,\text{kJ}$

          A lorry at city speed thus carries as much kinetic energy as a passenger car well over the motorway speed.

        2. The bob of a pendulum, with a mass of $0.500\,\text{kg}$, hangs at the end of a string $1.20\,\text{m}$ long. The bob is pulled aside so that the taut string is at an angle of $40^\circ$ to the vertical.

          How great is the potential energy of the bob when its lowest point is chosen as the zero level?

          J
          Answer

          1.3770624078016955 J

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 0.500\,\text{kg}$, length of the string $L = 1.20\,\text{m}$, angle of the string to the vertical $\alpha = 40^\circ$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the zero level is the lowest point of the bob

          Find

          the potential energy $E_\text{p}$ of the bob in joules

          Principle

          For potential energy we need the height of the bob above its lowest point. The bob moves along a circular arc whose radius is the length of the string. Measured from the point of suspension, the bob is at its lowest a distance $L$ below it, and when pulled aside a distance $L\cos\alpha$ below it, so the height is the difference of these.

          Formula

          $h$$= L - L\cos\alpha$
          $= L\,(1 - \cos\alpha)$

          and $E_\text{p} = m g h$, so $E_\text{p} = m g L\,(1 - \cos\alpha)$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed. The calculator must be in degrees.

          Substitution

          $h$$= 1.20\,\text{m} \cdot (1 - \cos 40^\circ)$
          $\approx 1.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.2340$
          $\approx 0.2808\,\text{m}$

          so

          $E_\text{p}$$= 0.500\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.2808\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 1.377\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $1.38\,\text{J}$

          Three significant figures like the mass $0.500\,\text{kg}$ and the length of the string $1.20\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          The height $0.28\,\text{m}$ is clearly smaller than the length of the string, as it should be when the angle is under $90^\circ$; at the angle $90^\circ$ the height would be the whole $1.20\,\text{m}$. With rounded numbers $5\,\text{N} \cdot 0.28\,\text{m} \approx 1.4\,\text{J}$.

        3. A roller skater with a mass of $55.0\,\text{kg}$ arrives at the foot of a ramp at a speed of $7.00\,\text{m/s}$. She does not push for extra speed, and she stops for a moment at a height of $2.10\,\text{m}$ above the foot of the ramp.

          How much mechanical energy changed into other energy, such as internal energy, during the rise?

          J
          Answer

          214.4449999999997 J

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass $m = 55.0\,\text{kg}$, speed at the foot of the ramp $v = 7.00\,\text{m/s}$, height at the highest point $h = 2.10\,\text{m}$, where the speed is zero, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the zero level is the foot of the ramp

          Find

          the mechanical energy that changed into other energy, in joules

          Principle

          Without friction and air resistance the mechanical energy would stay the same. Friction and air resistance are not conservative, and they change part of the mechanical energy into internal energy. The amount changed is the decrease in mechanical energy from the foot to the highest point. At the foot there is only kinetic energy ($h = 0$), at the highest point only potential energy ($v = 0$).

          Formula

          $E_\text{other} = E_\text{mech,1} - E_\text{mech,2}$, where $E_\text{mech,1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2$ and $E_\text{mech,2} = m g h$. So $E_\text{other} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,mv^2 - m g h$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\tfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 55.0\,\text{kg} \cdot (7.00\,\text{m/s})^2$
          $= 1347.5\,\text{J}$

          and

          $m g h$$= 55.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 2.10\,\text{m}$
          $\approx 1133.1\,\text{J}$

          so

          $E_\text{other}$$= 1347.5\,\text{J} - 1133.1\,\text{J}$
          $\approx 214\,\text{J}$

          Answer

          $214\,\text{J}$

          Three significant figures like the initial data.

          Reasonableness

          Without friction the skater would rise to the height

          $\tfrac{v^2}{2g}$$= \tfrac{49\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{19.62\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $\approx 2.50\,\text{m}$

          She rises $2.10\,\text{m}$, about $16\,\%$ less, and the changed energy $214\,\text{J}$ is about $16\,\%$ of the original $1347.5\,\text{J}$. The result is positive, because friction only decreases the mechanical energy.
