Waves, probability and quantum tunnelling

Why can the Sun shine, when its protons are far too slow to reach each other?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain wave–particle duality with the double-slit experiment and probability.
  • calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron or a proton.
  • explain how quantum tunnelling makes fusion possible in the Sun.

Prior knowledge

  • Light consists of photons, which are detected one by one, $E = hf$chapter Light as particles: the photoelectric effect
  • Two positive nuclei repel each other, and a slower particle turns back further awaychapter From Rutherford's nucleus to Bohr's atom
  • Kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and momentum $p = mv$ (earlier physics courses)

1Think about it

The centre of the Sun is about $15.7$ million kelvins. That sounds extremely hot. But fusion needs two positive nuclei to come almost into contact, and at this temperature the protons are far too slow to push so close against their electric repulsion. By the rules of classical physics, fusion in the Sun should practically never happen; physicists met this puzzle in the 1920s. Yet the Sun has been shining for about $4.6$ billion years.

How can protons do something that classical physics says they cannot?

Review: how close can a nucleus come?

In the unit From Rutherford's nucleus to Bohr's atom, an alpha particle with $8.3\ \text{MeV}$ turned back about $27\ \text{fm}$ from a gold nucleus. What happens to this closest distance if the alpha particle has less kinetic energy?

  • The charges fix the force, but the distance depends on the energy too: $d = kq_1q_2 : \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Less energy gives a larger distance.

  • Right. All the kinetic energy turns into electric potential energy $kq_1q_2/d$ at the turning point, so a smaller energy means a larger $d$. Slow particles cannot come close.

  • At the turning point $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = kq_1q_2/d$. A smaller kinetic energy gives a larger $d$: a slow particle is turned back earlier.

Prediction 2: a wall that is too high

An electron moves towards a thin energy barrier. Its kinetic energy is less than the energy it would need to get over the top. The barrier is then made twice as wide. What do you predict?

  • The electron can pass, but the chance does not simply halve. It falls exponentially: from about $7.7\ \%$ at $0.25\ \text{nm}$ to $0.60\ \%$ at $0.50\ \text{nm}$, about $13$ times less.

  • This is the classical answer. For an electron and a thin barrier there is a real chance of passing: for a $1\ \text{eV}$ barrier $0.25\ \text{nm}$ wide, about $7.7\ \%$. This is quantum tunnelling.

  • The electron can pass, but the width matters a great deal: the chance falls from about $7.7\ \%$ at $0.25\ \text{nm}$ to $0.60\ \%$ at $0.50\ \text{nm}$.

  • Right. The electron's wave reaches through the barrier, so there is a chance of finding it on the other side. The chance falls very fast with width: about $7.7\ \%$ at $0.25\ \text{nm}$ but only $0.60\ \%$ at $0.50\ \text{nm}$, about $13$ times less.

3Investigate

Electrons one at a time

Light passing through two narrow slits makes a pattern of bright and dark bands on a screen. Thomas Young showed such interference of light around 1801–1803, and it became the classic proof that light is a wave. In 1909 Geoffrey Taylor repeated a two-slit experiment with light so dim that only a few photons were on their way at any moment, and the bands still appeared.

In 1924 the French physicist Louis de Broglie suggested that electrons might be waves too. In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer, and independently George Paget Thomson and Alexander Reid, saw electrons diffract from crystals, just like waves. Later, Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi (1974) sent electrons one at a time through an electron biprism, which acts like two slits. The figure shows what such an experiment looks like.

Each electron arrives at one single point, like a particle. But where it arrives is not decided like a bullet's path: after thousands of electrons, the dots form the bands of a wave pattern. At the Fotoni lab visit you can study a double-slit experiment repeated with single photons (unit Fotoni lab visit: modern physics experiments).

    Review the theory.

    Through the wall

    Roll a ball towards a hill. If its kinetic energy is less than the energy needed to reach the top, it always rolls back. In classical physics the region beyond the hill is simply forbidden. A small particle such as an electron behaves differently. Its wave does not stop at the edge of an energy barrier: inside the barrier the wave shrinks quickly, and if the barrier is thin, a small wave comes out on the other side. So the particle has a chance of appearing beyond the barrier. This is quantum tunnelling.

    How large is the chance? The table gives approximate model values for an electron whose energy is $1\ \text{eV}$ below the top of a barrier, for different barrier widths.

    Barrier width (nm) Chance of passing
    0.25 7.7 %
    0.50 0.60 %
    0.75 0.046 %
    1.00 0.0035 %

    Tunnelling is not just a theory. In 1928 George Gamow, and independently Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon, explained alpha decay with it: an alpha particle escapes from a nucleus by tunnelling through a barrier that it could never climb. The scanning tunnelling microscope of Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer (1981, Nobel Prize 1986) measures the tunnelling current between a sharp tip and a surface and can show single atoms. The flash memory in your phone stores data by moving electrons through thin barriers by tunnelling.

    Investigate: how fast does the chance fall?

    Values calculated by a model

    Use the table in Through the wall. Look at how the chance of passing changes each time the barrier becomes $0.25\ \text{nm}$ wider.

    By what factor does the chance of passing become smaller when the barrier width grows from $0.25\ \text{nm}$ to $0.50\ \text{nm}$? (For example, type $2$ if the chance is halved.)

    Hint 1: a guiding question

    A factor is found by dividing, not by subtracting.

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Divide the larger chance by the smaller one: $7.7\ \% : 0.60\ \%$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Check the next step too: $0.60\ \% : 0.046\ \%$. Is it about the same factor?

    Show the solution

    Given

    The chance is $7.7\ \%$ at $0.25\ \text{nm}$ and $0.60\ \%$ at $0.50\ \text{nm}$ (model values). Find the factor.

    Principle

    A factor of change is the ratio of the two values.

    Formula

    $\text{factor} = 7.7\ \% : 0.60\ \%$.

    Unit conversions

    None: both values are percentages.

    Substitution

    $7.7 : 0.60 = 12.8$.

    Answer

    The chance becomes about $13$ times smaller (two significant figures, like the table). The next steps give the same factor: $0.60 : 0.046 \approx 13$ and $0.046 : 0.0035 \approx 13$.

    Reasonableness

    The same factor at every step is the sign of exponential decrease: every extra $0.25\ \text{nm}$ makes passing about $13$ times rarer. Doubling the width does not halve the chance; here it makes it $13$ times smaller.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Light and matter are neither ordinary waves nor ordinary particles. This is called wave–particle duality. An electron, a proton or a photon is detected at one point, like a particle, but how it travels is described by a wave. In 1926 Max Born proposed what the wave means: the square of its amplitude at a place gives the probability of finding the particle there. Quantum physics cannot say where one electron will land, but it predicts the pattern of many very accurately.

    Every moving particle has a de Broglie wavelength. When the wavelength is similar to the size of an object in its way, such as the distance between atoms or the width of a barrier, the wave nature becomes important: diffraction, interference and tunnelling. For a ball or a car the wavelength is so tiny that we never notice it.

    The de Broglie wavelength

    A particle with the momentum $p = mv$ has the wavelength

    $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}$$

    where $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}$. If the kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ is known, the momentum is $p = \sqrt{2mE_\text{k}}$. The chance of tunnelling falls very fast, roughly exponentially, when the barrier becomes wider or higher, or when the particle is heavier.

    Words of the lesson

    Tunnelling and the Sun

    At the centre of the Sun the temperature is about $15.7$ million kelvins. A typical kinetic energy of a particle there is of the order of $k_\text{B}T \approx 1.35\ \text{keV}$. Two protons repel each other, because both are positive. To fuse, they must come within a few femtometres, where the strong nuclear force acts. At $2\ \text{fm}$ the electric potential energy of two protons is about $720\ \text{keV}$, more than $500$ times the typical energy. In classical physics a proton with $1.35\ \text{keV}$ turns back at about $1000\ \text{fm}$ and never gets close.

    Gamow's tunnelling (1928) solved the puzzle. A proton's de Broglie wavelength at these energies is hundreds of femtometres, so its wave reaches far into the barrier, and there is a tiny chance that two protons tunnel close enough to fuse. In 1929 Robert Atkinson and Fritz Houtermans used this idea to make the first calculation of fusion in stars. Each meeting has only a tiny chance, but the Sun's core contains an enormous number of protons, so fusion goes on all the time. But tunnelling only lets two protons get close. Two protons alone cannot stay together: fusion succeeds only if one of them turns into a neutron at that moment, $\text{p} + \text{p} \to \text{d} + \text{e}^+ + \nu$, and this change is a very slow process of the weak interaction. So an average proton in the Sun's core waits about $9$ billion years before it fuses, and this is why the Sun shines steadily for so long. The chapter The Energy of the Stars continues this story.

      People in this story

      Louis de Broglie (1892–1987)

      Louis de Broglie was born in Dieppe, in Normandy, into the noble de Broglie family. He first studied history and took a degree in it in 1910, but then turned to physics. In his doctoral thesis of 1924, Recherches sur la théorie des quanta, he proposed that every particle, such as an electron, also behaves like a wave. Three years later experiments with crystals confirmed it. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1929 "for his discovery of the wave nature of electrons". His idea is the basis of tunnelling.

      5Examples

      Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

      Example 1

      In an electron microscope, electrons are accelerated from rest through a voltage of $100\ \text{V}$. Calculate their de Broglie wavelength and compare it with the distance between atoms in a crystal, about $0.1\text{–}0.3\ \text{nm}$.

      Step 1/7

      Given: ${U = 100\ \text{V}}$, electron mass ${m = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}}$, charge ${e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{C}}$, ${h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}}$. Find the wavelength $\lambda$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle: The electric field does the work $eU$ on the electron, which becomes its kinetic energy (conservation of energy). The de Broglie wavelength follows from the momentum.

      Step 3/7

      Formula: Solved in symbols:

      $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{k} &= eU \\ p &= \sqrt{2mE_\text{k}} = \sqrt{2meU} \\ \lambda &= \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meU}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions: None are needed: all quantities are in SI units. The answer in metres is changed to nanometres with $1\ \text{nm} = 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution: $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{k} &= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{C} \\ &\quad \cdot 100\ \text{V} \\ &= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-17}\ \text{J} \\[6pt] 2mE_\text{k} &= 2 \cdot 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\ \text{kg} \\ &\quad \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-17}\ \text{J} \\ &= 2.919 \cdot 10^{-47}\ \text{kg}^2 \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \\[6pt] p &= \sqrt{2.919 \cdot 10^{-47}} \\ &\quad \cdot \sqrt{\text{kg}^2 \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} \\ &= 5.402 \cdot 10^{-24}\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} \\[6pt] \lambda &= \frac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}}{5.402 \cdot 10^{-24}\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}} \\ &= 1.227 \cdot 10^{-10}\ \text{m} \\ &= 0.123\ \text{nm} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer: The electrons have the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda \approx 0.123\ \text{nm}$, three significant figures like the constants. It is of the same size as the distance between atoms in a crystal, so the electrons diffract from crystals, as Davisson and Germer saw.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness: Check the units:

      $$\text{C} \cdot \text{V} = \text{J} = \text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$$

      So the square root gives $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$, a momentum, and $\text{J} \cdot \text{s}$ divided by $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ gives $\text{m}$. The speed is $p/m \approx 5.9 \cdot 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$, about $2\ \%$ of the speed of light, so classical momentum is good enough. The wavelength is much shorter than that of visible light ($400\text{–}750\ \text{nm}$), which is why electron microscopes can see much smaller details than light microscopes.

      Example 2: Apply: the wave of a proton in the Sun

      A proton (mass $1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$) at the centre of the Sun has the kinetic energy $1.35\ \text{keV}$. Calculate its de Broglie wavelength in femtometres ($1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$). Compare it with the classical turning distance, about $1000\ \text{fm}$.

      fm
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Change the energy to joules: $1\ \text{keV} = 1000\ \text{eV}$ and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $p = \sqrt{2mE_\text{k}}$ and $\lambda = h/p$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $E_\text{k} = 2.163 \cdot 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$ and $p = 8.507 \cdot 10^{-22}\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, $E_\text{k} = 1.35\ \text{keV}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}$. Find the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda$.

      Principle

      Every particle has the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$. The momentum follows from the kinetic energy, $E_\text{k} = p^2/(2m)$; at this speed classical momentum is accurate.

      Formula

      $p = \sqrt{2mE_\text{k}}$ and $\lambda = h/p = h/\sqrt{2mE_\text{k}}$.

      Unit conversions

      $E_\text{k}$$= 1.35 \cdot 10^{3}\ \text{eV} \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{J/eV}$
      $= 2.163 \cdot 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$

      and at the end $1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$.

      Substitution

      $$\begin{aligned} 2mE_\text{k} &= 2 \cdot 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} \\ &\quad \cdot 2.163 \cdot 10^{-16}\ \text{J} \\ &= 7.237 \cdot 10^{-43}\ \text{kg}^2 \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \\ p &= \sqrt{7.237 \cdot 10^{-43}\ \text{kg}^2 \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} \\ &= 8.507 \cdot 10^{-22}\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} \\ \lambda &= \frac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}}{8.507 \cdot 10^{-22}\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}} \\ &= 7.79 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Answer

      $\lambda \approx 7.79 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{m} = 779\ \text{fm}$

      Three significant figures like the energy. It is almost as long as the classical turning distance of about $1000\ \text{fm}$.

      Reasonableness

      The proton is much heavier than the electron of the worked example, but also has more energy; its wavelength, $779\ \text{fm}$, is shorter than the electron's $0.123\ \text{nm} = 123\,000\ \text{fm}$, as expected. Compare: $779\ \text{fm}$ is about the same size as the classical turning distance of about $1000\ \text{fm}$, so the proton's wave reaches far into the barrier, and tunnelling becomes possible.

      Optional Example 3: Think further: a tennis ball
      A tennis ball ($0.057\ \text{kg}$) flies at $50\ \text{m/s}$. Its de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda = h/(mv) = 2.3 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$. Why does nobody ever see a tennis ball diffract or tunnel through the net?

      • Quantum physics is valid for everything, also for protons and whole atoms. A tennis ball's wavelength is just far too small to notice.

      • Every moving object has a de Broglie wavelength, $\lambda = h/(mv)$. For the tennis ball it is $2.3 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$: real, but far too small to matter.

      • A net that stops a ball acts like a barrier. The point is that the ball's wavelength is so tiny that the chance of tunnelling through it is practically zero.

      • Right. $2.3 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$ is more than $10^{18}$ times smaller than a proton. Wave effects matter only when the wavelength is similar to the size of slits or barriers, and the tunnelling chance through a net is practically zero.

      Common misconception

      "In tunnelling the particle borrows energy to climb over the barrier" or "it finds a hole". Neither is true. The particle's wave reaches through the barrier, and there is a probability of finding the particle on the other side, with the same energy it had before. Most particles still bounce back.

      A second mistake is to think that tunnelling happens only in theory, or to big objects too. It is measured every day in microscopes, electronics and radioactive decay, but its chance falls so fast with mass and width that a ball never tunnels through a wall. A third: wave–particle duality does not mean that an electron is sometimes a wave and sometimes a particle. It is always the same quantum object; the wave tells us the probabilities.

      3. Concept task: The Sun

      Why does fusion happen in the Sun's core, although a typical proton there has only about $1.35\ \text{keV}$ and the barrier is hundreds of $\text{keV}$?

      • Protons do not collect energy for a later climb; they collide and exchange energy all the time. Fusion happens by tunnelling with the energy they have.

      • Gravity holds the Sun together and keeps the core hot and dense, but between two single protons it is extremely weak. They meet by tunnelling through their electric barrier.

      • A typical proton has about $1.35\ \text{keV}$, more than $500$ times too little to climb a $720\ \text{keV}$ barrier. Classical physics alone would allow almost no fusion.

      • Right. Each meeting of two protons has only a tiny chance to tunnel close enough, but the core contains so many protons that fusion goes on all the time. An average proton still waits about $9$ billion years, also because the first step, $\text{p} + \text{p} \to \text{d} + \text{e}^+ + \nu$, needs the weak interaction.
