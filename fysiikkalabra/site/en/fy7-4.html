Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker

Why does a loudspeaker cone vibrate and the blades of a desk fan turn, when nothing visible pushes either of them?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field and deduce its direction.
  • explain why parallel current-carrying wires attract or repel each other, and calculate the force between them.
  • explain how a direct-current motor and a loudspeaker work.

Prior knowledge

  • A magnetic field exerts on a moving charged particle a force perpendicular to the velocity and the fieldchapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields
  • The magnetic field of a straight current-carrying wire circles the wire, and its magnitude is $B = \mu_0 I : (2\pi r)$chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

When a loudspeaker plays a bass note, you can see its cone moving back and forth. The blades of a desk fan start to turn as soon as the plug is pushed into the wall. Nobody touches either of them, and neither has any burning fuel. Both, however, contain a magnet and a wire carrying an electric current.

How can a magnet make a wire move, if the wire is copper, which a magnet does not attract?

Optional Recall: the magnetic field of a straight wire
A current flows in a long straight wire. How large is the magnetic flux density at twice the distance from the wire compared with the original distance?

  • It is the electric field of a point charge that weakens with the square of the distance. The field of a long straight wire is $B = \mu_0 I : (2\pi r)$, so at twice the distance it is half (chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil).

  • Correct. The field of a straight wire circles the wire, and its magnitude is inversely proportional to the distance $r$ (chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil). Today this field is used to explain why two current-carrying wires attract or repel each other.

  • The current is the same, but the field weakens as the distance grows: $B = \mu_0 I : (2\pi r)$. At twice the distance the field is half (chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil).

3Investigate

In the current balance a magnet stands on the balance and a wire is between its poles. Change the current and the angle of the wire and follow the reading of the balance.

Simulation

How to use

Adjust the current $I$ and the angle $\alpha$ between the wire and the field, and choose the magnet and the length $l$ of wire in the field. A negative current means that the current flows in the opposite direction in the wire. The side view shows the balance, the magnet and the wire, and the force on the wire as a red arrow whose length shows the magnitude of the force. In the top view you see the poles of the magnet from above, the green field lines, the angle of the wire and the violet current arrow. The force points out of the page (a dot in a circle) or into the page (a cross in a circle). The reading of the balance is in the readings row and on the display of the balance.

Assumptions of the model

The field is uniform between the poles and zero outside them. Only the horizontal lower part of the wire is in the field; the forces on the vertical lead wires are horizontal and do not change the reading of the balance. The wire is rigidly fixed to the stand, so it does not move. The magnitudes of the fields of the magnets are not shown: they are determined from the measurements. The readings are values calculated by the model to a precision of $0.01\,\text{g}$, not measurements, and they contain no measurement error.

How the calculation works

The field exerts on the wire a vertical force $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. By Newton's third law the wire exerts on the magnet an equal and opposite force, so the balance shows the reading

$$m = m_0 + \frac{F}{g}$$

where $m_0 = 245.30\,\text{g}$ is the reading without a current and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. From the change in the reading $\Delta m = m - m_0$ we obtain the force $F = \Delta m \cdot g$ and from it the magnetic flux density $B = F : (I l)$, when the wire is perpendicular to the field.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    A magnetic field pushes a current-carrying wire

    In the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields, a magnetic field exerted on a moving charged particle a force perpendicular to both the velocity of the particle and the field. An electric current in a wire is a motion of charges: the conduction electrons of the metal move along the wire. The magnetic field exerts a force on every moving electron, and the electrons pass it on to the metal of the wire, so the force acts on the whole wire. When a light copper wire is hung between the poles of a horseshoe magnet and a current is switched on, the wire swings sideways. Copper is not ferromagnetic, and the magnet does not attract it: the force appears only when there is a current in the wire.

    The direction of the force

    The force is always perpendicular to both the direction of the current and the magnetic field. Its direction is given by the right-hand rule: when the thumb of an outstretched right hand points in the direction of the current and the other fingers in the direction of the magnetic field, the force points out of the palm. It is the same rule as for a positively charged particle, because the direction of the current is the direction in which positive charge moves. When the direction of the current or of the field is reversed, the direction of the force reverses. If the wire is parallel to the field, there is no force at all.

    The electric motor

    In a direct-current motor, a current loop that can turn about an axle sits between the poles of a magnet. In the two sides of the loop that cross the field, the current flows in opposite directions, so the forces on them are in opposite directions. The forces are not on the same line, so they have a turning effect: they turn the loop. When the loop has turned to be perpendicular to the field, the forces point away from the axle and no longer turn it, and the loop keeps moving because of its inertia. Exactly then the commutator, a split ring with the carbon brushes that touch it, reverses the direction of the current in the loop. In this way the forces turn the loop in the same direction every half turn. A real motor has many coils and an iron core, so it turns smoothly and strongly.

    The loudspeaker

    The magnet of a loudspeaker is built so that in the ring-shaped gap the field points radially outwards. In the gap is a light voice coil, and every part of it is perpendicular to the field, so an equal force acts on every part in the direction of the axis of the coil. A radial field is essential: in a uniform field the forces on opposite sides of a closed coil would be in opposite directions and would cancel, so nothing would push the coil. The coil is attached to the cone. The alternating current from the amplifier changes its direction in step with the sound signal, so the force pushes the cone alternately forwards and backwards. The cone vibrates at the frequency of the signal, compresses and rarefies the air and produces a sound wave. The larger the current, the larger the force and the louder the sound. Most of the electrical energy delivered to the loudspeaker is, however, transformed into internal energy of the coil and the magnet.

    Force on a current-carrying wire

    When a current $I$ flows in a straight wire and the wire is in a uniform magnetic field, the field exerts on the wire a force of magnitude

    $$F = I l B \sin\alpha$$

    where $F$ is the force (N), $I$ the current (A), $l$ the length of the part of the wire that is in the field (m), $B$ the magnetic flux density (T) and $\alpha$ the angle between the wire and the field. Only the component of the field perpendicular to the wire, $B \sin\alpha$, produces a force. The force is greatest when the wire is perpendicular to the field ($\sin 90^\circ = 1$) and zero when the wire is parallel to the field ($\sin 0^\circ = 0$). At an angle of $30^\circ$ the force is half of its greatest value, because $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$. The formula gives the unit of the tesla: $1\,\text{T} = 1\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$.

    The force between two current-carrying wires

    There is a force between two parallel current-carrying wires, because each is in the magnetic field of the other. In the chapter Magnetic field of a wire and a coil, the field of wire $1$ circles around it, and at a distance $r$ its magnitude is $B_1$. At wire $2$ the field is perpendicular to wire $2$, so a force $F$ acts on the part of wire $2$ whose length is $l$:

    $$\begin{aligned} &B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r} \\[6pt] &F = I_2 l B_1 \\ &\quad = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2 l}{2\pi r} \end{aligned}$$

    where $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the currents of the wires (A), $r$ is the distance between the wires (m), $l$ the length of the part that runs parallel (m) and $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$ the permeability of free space. By Newton's third law, an equal and opposite force acts on wire $1$. The right-hand rule shows that currents in the same direction attract each other and currents in opposite directions repel each other. Until 2019 the ampere was defined with this force: the current is $1\,\text{A}$ when the force between wires $1\,\text{m}$ apart is $2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{N}$ for every metre of wire.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a magnet attracts a wire and that the wire moves along the field lines towards the other pole. The force is, however, perpendicular to the field, and a copper or aluminium wire experiences a force only when it carries a current. When the direction of the current is reversed, the wire moves in the opposite direction, although the magnet is the same. Another common mistake is to conclude from the charges that currents in the same direction repel each other. The force between wires is magnetic, and currents in the same direction attract each other. A third mistake is to use the cosine in the formula: the angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the wire and the field, and the force is zero when the wire is parallel to the field.

    Summary

    • A magnetic field exerts on a current-carrying wire a force $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the wire and the field. The force is greatest when the wire is perpendicular to the field and zero when the wire is parallel to the field.
    • The force is perpendicular to both the current and the field; its direction is given by the right-hand rule, and it reverses when the direction of the current or of the field reverses.
    • Currents in the same direction attract each other, and currents in opposite directions repel each other. The force is $F = I_2 l B_1$, where $B_1 = \mu_0 I_1 : (2\pi r)$.
    • In a direct-current motor the forces turn a current loop, and the commutator reverses the current every half turn.
    • In a loudspeaker an alternating current in a radial field pushes the voice coil and the cone back and forth in step with the signal.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A loudspeaker voice coil has $60$ turns of thin wire, and the radius of the coil is $1.3\,\text{cm}$. The coil is in the gap of a magnet, where the magnetic flux density of the radial field is $0.95\,\text{T}$, and the field is perpendicular to the wire everywhere. How large is the force on the coil when the current is at its peak value of $1.5\,\text{A}$?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Number of turns $N = 60$, radius of the coil $r = 1.3\,\text{cm}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.95\,\text{T}$, current $I = 1.5\,\text{A}$ and angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 90^\circ$. We are asked for the force $F$ on the coil.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The field is radial, so every part of the wire of the coil is perpendicular to the field, and the force on every part points in the same direction, along the axis of the coil. The forces therefore add up, and in the formula $F = I l B \sin\alpha$ the length $l$ is the whole length of the wire of the coil: $N$ turns, each with the length of the circumference of a circle, $2\pi r$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} l &= N \cdot 2\pi r \\ F &= I l B \sin 90^\circ = I l B \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The radius in metres:

      $r$$= 1.3\,\text{cm}$
      $= 1.3 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.013\,\text{m}$

      The other quantities are in SI units.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the length of the wire and then the force:

      $$\begin{aligned} l &= N \cdot 2\pi r \\ &= 60 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 0.013\,\text{m} \\ &= 4.9009\,\text{m} \\[6pt] F &= I l B \\ &= 1.5\,\text{A} \cdot 4.9009\,\text{m} \cdot 0.95\,\text{T} \\ &= 6.984\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The force on the coil is $F \approx 7.0\,\text{N}$ along the axis of the coil. The result is given to two significant figures, like the radius $1.3\,\text{cm}$ and the current $1.5\,\text{A}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The units agree:

      $\text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{T}$$= \text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$
      $= \text{N}$

      If the mass of the cone and the coil is about $10\,\text{g}$, their weight is only about $0.1\,\text{N}$, so the force is about $70$ times the weight. This is plausible, because the cone has to accelerate back and forth hundreds of times a second.

      Optional Find the error
      In a uniform magnetic field $B = 0.30\,\text{T}$ there is $20\,\text{cm}$ of straight wire carrying a current of $5.0\,\text{A}$. The angle between the wire and the field is $30^\circ$.A student calculates

      $$\begin{aligned} F &= I l B \cos 30^\circ \\ &= 5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \\ &\quad \cdot 0.866 \\ &\approx 0.26\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • The angle affects the magnitude of the force: a wire parallel to the field experiences no force at all. The force $I l B = 0.30\,\text{N}$ is obtained only when the wire is perpendicular to the field. Here the angle is $30^\circ$, so the force is $0.30\,\text{N} \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 0.15\,\text{N}$.

      • Correct. The angle is between the wire and the field, and the component of the field perpendicular to the wire is $B \sin\alpha$. Therefore $F = 5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.30\,\text{T} \cdot 0.5 = 0.15\,\text{N}$. Check with a limiting case: for a wire parallel to the field ($\alpha = 0^\circ$) the force must be zero, but the cosine would give the greatest force.

      • The length has been correctly converted to metres: $20\,\text{cm} = 20 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.20\,\text{m}$. Since $\text{T} = \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$, the result is in newtons only if the length is in metres. The error is in the function of the angle.

      Apply to a new situation: jump leads

      A car with a flat battery is started with the help of the battery of another car, using jump leads. When the starter motor turns the engine, a current of $150\,\text{A}$ flows in one lead and an equal current back in the other. The leads run parallel for $2.0\,\text{m}$, with the distance between their centres $1.5\,\text{cm}$.

      How large is the force between the leads? Answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      In what field is the second lead? What is the magnetic field of the first lead at the second lead, and in which direction does it point relative to the lead?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The field of the first lead at the second is $B_1 = \mu_0 I_1 : (2\pi r)$, and it is perpendicular to the second lead, so $F = I_2 l B_1$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $B_1$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 150\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.015\,\text{m}}$
      $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$

      and $F = 150\,\text{A} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} \cdot B_1$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      currents of the leads $I_1 = I_2 = 150\,\text{A}$ in opposite directions, length of the parallel part $l = 2.0\,\text{m}$, distance $r = 1.5\,\text{cm}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

      Find

      force $F$ between the leads in newtons

      Principle

      Each lead is in the magnetic field of the other. The field of the first lead circles around it, so at the second lead it is perpendicular to the lead and equally strong along the whole parallel stretch. A force $F = I_2 l B_1$ therefore acts on the second lead. The currents are in opposite directions, so the leads repel each other.

      Formula

      $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ and $F = I_2 l B_1$

      Conversions

      $r$$= 1.5\,\text{cm}$
      $= 1.5 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
      $= 0.015\,\text{m}$

      Substitution

      $B_1$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 150\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.015\,\text{m}}$
      $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
      $F$$= 150\,\text{A} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
      $= 0.60\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $0.60\,\text{N}$

      Two significant figures like the distance $1.5\,\text{cm}$ and the length $2.0\,\text{m}$. The leads repel each other.

      Reasonableness

      The field at the second lead is $B_1 = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$, about $40$ times the Earth's field but small compared with a magnet. The force corresponds to the weight of an object of about $60\,\text{g}$, so the leads may jerk slightly when the motor starts, but they do not jump.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept: the commutator

      What is the function of the commutator in a direct-current motor?

      • The field is strengthened by an iron core or a stronger magnet. The commutator is a split ring through which the current enters the loop; it reverses the direction of the current in the loop every half turn.

      • Correct. Without a commutator, the forces on the sides of the loop would turn the loop back after half a turn, and the loop would end up swinging about the position in which it is perpendicular to the field. When the direction of the current reverses, the direction of the forces reverses too, and the rotation continues.

      • The motor feeds nothing into the mains: the power supply gives it direct current, and the commutator reverses the current only in the loop, at half-turn intervals. The purpose is to keep the turning effect of the forces on the loop in the same direction.

      2. Calculate: an oblique wire

      In a uniform magnetic field $B = 0.20\,\text{T}$ there is $0.50\,\text{m}$ of straight wire carrying a current of $4.0\,\text{A}$. The angle between the wire and the field is $30^\circ$.

      How large is the force on the wire? Answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which component of the field produces the force: the one along the wire or the one perpendicular to it?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the wire and the field.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $F$$= 4.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} \cdot 0.20\,\text{T} \cdot \sin 30^\circ$
      Show the solution

      Given

      $B = 0.20\,\text{T}$, $l = 0.50\,\text{m}$, $I = 4.0\,\text{A}$, angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 30^\circ$

      Find

      force $F$ on the wire in newtons

      Principle

      The field is uniform and the wire straight, so the force is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. The wire is oblique, so only the component of the field perpendicular to the wire, $B \sin\alpha$, produces a force.

      Formula

      $F = I l B \sin\alpha$

      Conversions

      None needed: the quantities are in SI units, and $\text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{T} = \text{N}$.

      $F$$= 4.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} \cdot 0.20\,\text{T} \cdot \sin 30^\circ$
      $= 0.40\,\text{N} \cdot 0.5$
      $= 0.20\,\text{N}$

      $0.20\,\text{N}$

      Two significant figures like the data.

      For a perpendicular wire the force would be $0.40\,\text{N}$. For an oblique wire it must be smaller, and at $30^\circ$ it is exactly half, because $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$.

      Optional 3. Explain: the loudspeaker

      Explain why the cone of a loudspeaker vibrates at the same frequency as the current from the amplifier. Also explain why the field of the loudspeaker magnet is made radial in the gap.

      Model answer

      The voice coil is in the gap of the magnet, and the field exerts on the wire of the coil a force $F = I l B$ along the axis of the coil. The direction of the force depends on the direction of the current. The alternating current from the amplifier changes direction in step with the signal, so the force pushes the coil, and the cone attached to it, alternately forwards and backwards at the same frequency. The cone compresses and rarefies the air, and a sound wave of the same frequency is produced. In a uniform field the forces on opposite sides of a closed coil would be in opposite directions and would cancel, so the coil would not move. In a radial field every part of the coil is perpendicular to the field, so the greatest possible force acts on every part and all the forces point in the same direction, along the axis. Then the coil moves straight back and forth and does not tilt.

        1. Exercise 4-1Core

          In a laboratory a straight wire is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. There is $0.40\,\text{m}$ of wire in the field, the current in the wire is $12\,\text{A}$ and the magnetic flux density is $0.050\,\text{T}$.

          How large is the force on the wire?

          N
          Answer

          $0.24\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          length of the wire in the field $l = 0.40\,\text{m}$, current $I = 12\,\text{A}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.050\,\text{T}$, wire perpendicular to the field, so $\alpha = 90^\circ$

          Find

          force $F$ on the wire in newtons

          Principle

          A magnetic field exerts a force on a current-carrying wire, because a magnetic force acts on the charges moving in the wire. The field is uniform and the wire is straight, so the formula $F = I l B \sin\alpha$ can be used for the whole piece of wire.

          Formula

          $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, and when $\alpha = 90^\circ$, $F = I l B$

          Conversions

          None needed: all quantities are in SI units, and $\text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{T} = \text{N}$.

          Substitution

          $F$$= 12\,\text{A} \cdot 0.40\,\text{m} \cdot 0.050\,\text{T}$
          $= 0.24\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $0.24\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          The force is about the weight of a $25\,\text{g}$ object, which suits a laboratory magnet: the wire swings clearly, but the force is small. Doubling the current would double the force.

        2. Exercise 4-2Core

          Between the poles of an electromagnet is a straight piece of wire $15\,\text{cm}$ long, at an angle of $40^\circ$ to the magnetic field. The current in the wire is $8.0\,\text{A}$ and the magnetic flux density is $0.25\,\text{T}$.

          How large is the force on the piece of wire?

          N
          Answer

          $0.19\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $l = 15\,\text{cm}$, $I = 8.0\,\text{A}$, $B = 0.25\,\text{T}$, angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 40^\circ$

          Find

          force $F$ on the piece of wire in newtons

          Principle

          The wire is oblique to the field, so only the component of the field perpendicular to the wire, $B \sin\alpha$, produces a force. The field is uniform, so the force is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$.

          Formula

          $F = I l B \sin\alpha$

          Conversions

          $l$$= 15\,\text{cm}$
          $= 15 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.15\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $F$$= 8.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.15\,\text{m} \cdot 0.25\,\text{T} \cdot \sin 40^\circ$
          $= 0.30\,\text{N} \cdot 0.6428$
          $= 0.1928\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $0.19\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          For a perpendicular wire the force would be $0.30\,\text{N}$. For an oblique wire it must be smaller, and $\sin 40^\circ \approx 0.64$ reduces it to about two thirds. With the cosine the result would wrongly be $0.23\,\text{N}$.

        3. Exercise 4-3

          The horizontal piece of wire in a current balance is $6.0\,\text{cm}$ long and perpendicular to the field of the magnet. When a current of $4.5\,\text{A}$ flows in the wire, a force of $27\,\text{mN}$ acts on the piece of wire.

          How large is the magnetic flux density between the poles of the magnet?

          T
          Answer

          $0.10\,\text{T}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $l = 6.0\,\text{cm}$, $I = 4.5\,\text{A}$, $F = 27\,\text{mN}$, wire perpendicular to the field, so $\alpha = 90^\circ$

          Find

          magnetic flux density $B$ in teslas

          Principle

          The force that a field exerts on a straight current-carrying wire is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. When the force, the current and the length are known, the magnitude of the field can be solved from the formula. A current balance is thus a way of measuring a magnetic field.

          Formula

          $F = I l B$, so $B = \dfrac{F}{I l}$

          Conversions

          $l$$= 6.0\,\text{cm}$
          $= 6.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.060\,\text{m}$
          $F$$= 27\,\text{mN}$
          $= 27 \cdot 0.001\,\text{N}$
          $= 0.027\,\text{N}$

          Substitution

          $B$$= \dfrac{0.027\,\text{N}}{4.5\,\text{A} \cdot 0.060\,\text{m}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.027\,\text{N}}{0.27\,\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= 0.10\,\text{T}$

          Answer

          $0.10\,\text{T}$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          Between the poles of a horseshoe magnet the field is typically from hundredths to tenths of a tesla, so $0.10\,\text{T}$ is plausible. Check: $4.5\,\text{A} \cdot 0.060\,\text{m} \cdot 0.10\,\text{T} = 0.027\,\text{N}$.

        4. Exercise 4-4

          A $100\,\text{m}$ section of power line between two pylons is horizontal and runs north–south as shown by a compass. A current of $200\,\text{A}$ flows in the wire. The magnetic flux density of the Earth's magnetic field in southern Finland is about $53\,\mu\text{T}$, and the field points towards magnetic north and downwards at an angle of $74^\circ$ to the horizontal (WMM2025 model, NOAA/BGS). The angle between the wire and the field is therefore $74^\circ$.

          How large is the force that the Earth's magnetic field exerts on the section of wire?

          N
          Answer

          $1.0\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $l = 100\,\text{m}$, $I = 200\,\text{A}$, the Earth's field $B = 53\,\mu\text{T}$, angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 74^\circ$

          Find

          force $F$ that the Earth's magnetic field exerts on the piece of wire, in newtons

          Principle

          Over a region the length of the wire the Earth's magnetic field is practically uniform, so the force is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. The wire runs north–south as shown by a compass, and the field points towards magnetic north at $74^\circ$ to the horizontal, so the angle between the wire and the field is $74^\circ$.

          Formula

          $F = I l B \sin\alpha$

          Conversions

          $B$$= 53\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$

          Substitution

          $F$$= 200\,\text{A} \cdot 100\,\text{m} \cdot 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot \sin 74^\circ$
          $= 1.06\,\text{N} \cdot 0.9613$
          $= 1.0189\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $1.0\,\text{N}$

          Two significant figures like the magnetic flux density $53\,\mu\text{T}$.

          Reasonableness

          The force is about the weight of a $100\,\text{g}$ object over the whole $100\,\text{m}$, which is negligible compared with the weight of the wire: a power line carrying $200\,\text{A}$ weighs about $1\,\text{kg}$ per metre, so $100\,\text{m}$ of wire weighs about $1000\,\text{N}$. The Earth's field is weak, so the result is as expected.

        5. Exercise 4-5

          Of the piece of wire in a current balance, $5.0\,\text{cm}$ is between the poles of a magnet, where the magnetic flux density is $0.20\,\text{T}$ and the field is perpendicular to the wire.

          How large is the current in the wire when a force of $0.030\,\text{N}$ acts on the piece of wire?

          A
          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $l = 5.0\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.20\,\text{T}$, $F = 0.030\,\text{N}$, wire perpendicular to the field, so $\alpha = 90^\circ$

          Find

          current $I$ in the wire in amperes

          Principle

          The field exerts on a current-carrying wire a force $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, which is directly proportional to the current. When the force, the length and the field are known, the current can be solved.

          Formula

          $F = I l B$, so $I = \dfrac{F}{l B}$

          Conversions

          $l$$= 5.0\,\text{cm}$
          $= 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.050\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $I$$= \dfrac{0.030\,\text{N}}{0.050\,\text{m} \cdot 0.20\,\text{T}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.030\,\text{N}}{0.010\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= 3.0\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          A current of a few amperes is usual for a laboratory power supply. Units:

          $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{T}\cdot\text{m}}$$= \tfrac{\text{N}}{\frac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= \text{A}$
        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Exercise 4-6Core

          In a laboratory two parallel wires run side by side for $0.80\,\text{m}$ at a distance of $5.0\,\text{mm}$ from each other. A current of $10\,\text{A}$ flows in the same direction in both wires.

          How large is the force on a wire in the magnetic field of the other wire? Give the answer in millinewtons.

          mN
          Answer

          $3.2\,\text{mN}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          currents of the wires $I_1 = I_2 = 10\,\text{A}$ in the same direction, distance $r = 5.0\,\text{mm}$, length of the parallel part $l = 0.80\,\text{m}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

          Find

          force $F$ between the wires in millinewtons

          Principle

          The magnetic field of wire $1$ circles around it, so at wire $2$ it is perpendicular to wire $2$. The field at wire $2$ is the same along the whole length, because the distance stays the same. A force $F = I_2 l B_1$ therefore acts on wire $2$. Currents in the same direction attract each other.

          Formula

          $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ and $F = I_2 l B_1$

          Conversions

          $r$$= 5.0\,\text{mm}$
          $= 5.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.0050\,\text{m}$

          Finally $1\,\text{mN} = 0.001\,\text{N}$.

          Substitution

          $B_1$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 10\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.0050\,\text{m}}$
          $= 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T}$
          $F$$= 10\,\text{A} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T}$
          $= 0.0032\,\text{N}$
          $= 3.2\,\text{mN}$

          Answer

          $3.2\,\text{mN}$

          Two significant figures like the distance and the length. The wires attract each other.

          Reasonableness

          The field at wire $2$ is $B_1 = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T}$, less than a tenth of the field of a fridge magnet, so the force is small, about the weight of a $0.3\,\text{g}$ object. The force grows if the wires are brought closer together.

        2. Exercise 4-7Core

          Students used a current balance to measure how much the reading of the balance changes when a current flows in the wire. There is $4.0\,\text{cm}$ of wire between the poles of the magnet, perpendicular to the field. The graph shows the change $\Delta m$ in the reading as a function of the current $I$, and a line fitted to the data points. Determine the magnetic flux density between the poles. Read the values you need from the line of the graph.

          T
          Answer

          $0.074\,\text{T}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          length of the wire in the field $l = 4.0\,\text{cm}$, wire perpendicular to the field; in the graph the change $\Delta m$ of the balance reading as a function of the current $I$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          magnetic flux density $B$ in teslas

          Principle

          The field exerts on the wire a force $F = I l B$, and by Newton's third law the wire exerts an equal and opposite force on the magnet. The reading of the balance therefore changes: the change $\Delta m$ corresponds to a force $F = \Delta m \cdot g$. The line passes through the origin, because without current there is no force.

          Reading the graph

          The data points deviate slightly from the line because of measurement uncertainty, so we use the line fitted to the points, which smooths out the deviations. The line passes through the origin and the grid point ($5.0\,\text{A}$; $1.50\,\text{g}$). We therefore read the current $I = 5.0\,\text{A}$ and the corresponding change $\Delta m = 1.50\,\text{g}$ from the line, not from a single data point: the last data point, $1.47\,\text{g}$, is slightly below the line.

          Formula

          $\Delta m \cdot g = I l B$, so $B = \dfrac{\Delta m \cdot g}{I l}$

          Conversions

          $\Delta m$$= 1.50\,\text{g}$
          $= 1.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.00150\,\text{kg}$
          $l$$= 4.0\,\text{cm}$
          $= 4.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.040\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $B$$= \dfrac{0.00150\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.040\,\text{m}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.014715\,\text{N}}{0.20\,\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= 0.0736\,\text{T}$

          Answer

          $0.074\,\text{T}$

          Two significant figures like the length $4.0\,\text{cm}$.

          Reasonableness

          The value is typical of a horseshoe magnet, of the order of a tenth of a tesla. Check with another point of the line: at $3.0\,\text{A}$ the line gives $0.90\,\text{g}$, and

          $0.00090\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 : (3.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.040\,\text{m})$
          $= 0.074\,\text{T}$
        3. Exercise 4-8

          In a uniform magnetic field with a magnetic flux density of $0.40\,\text{T}$ there is $0.50\,\text{m}$ of straight wire. When a current of $5.0\,\text{A}$ flows in the wire, a force of $0.50\,\text{N}$ acts on it.

          How large is the angle between the wire and the field? Give the angle in the range $0^\circ$–$90^\circ$.

          °
          Answer

          $30^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $B = 0.40\,\text{T}$, $l = 0.50\,\text{m}$, $I = 5.0\,\text{A}$, $F = 0.50\,\text{N}$

          Find

          angle $\alpha$ between the wire and the field in degrees

          Principle

          The force depends on the angle between the wire and the field: $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. The greatest possible force, $I l B$, would be obtained with a perpendicular wire, so the ratio of the measured force to the greatest force gives the sine of the angle.

          Formula

          $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, so $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{F}{I l B}$ and $\alpha = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{F}{I l B}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the quantities are in SI units, and the expression for the sine has no unit.

          Substitution

          $\sin\alpha$$= \dfrac{0.50\,\text{N}}{5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} \cdot 0.40\,\text{T}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.50\,\text{N}}{1.0\,\text{N}}$
          $= 0.50$

          so $\alpha = \sin^{-1} 0.50 = 30^\circ$

          Answer

          $30^\circ$

          To two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          For a perpendicular wire the force would be $1.0\,\text{N}$. The measured force is half of that, and half of the greatest force is obtained at the angle $30^\circ$, because $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$.

        4. Exercise 4-9

          Two horizontal metal rails are $8.0\,\text{cm}$ apart, and a metal rod of mass $20\,\text{g}$ lies across them. In the region of the rails there is a vertical magnetic field with a magnetic flux density of $0.30\,\text{T}$. A current of $15\,\text{A}$ flows along one rail to the rod, through the rod and back along the other rail.

          How large is the acceleration of the rod when friction is neglected?

          m/s²
          Answer

          $18\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance between the rails, that is the part of the rod in the path of the current, $l = 8.0\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.30\,\text{T}$ vertical, $I = 15\,\text{A}$, mass of the rod $m = 20\,\text{g}$, no friction

          Find

          acceleration $a$ of the rod in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          The current flows through the rod from one rail to the other, so the $l = 8.0\,\text{cm}$ part of the rod is a current-carrying wire perpendicular to the vertical field. The force $F = I l B$ is horizontal and along the rails. The weight and the normal force of the rails cancel each other and there is no friction, so by Newton's second law $F = m a$.

          Formula

          $I l B = m a$, so $a = \dfrac{I l B}{m}$

          Conversions

          $l$$= 8.0\,\text{cm}$
          $= 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.080\,\text{m}$
          $m$$= 20\,\text{g}$
          $= 20 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.020\,\text{kg}$

          Substitution

          $a$$= \dfrac{15\,\text{A} \cdot 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot 0.30\,\text{T}}{0.020\,\text{kg}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.36\,\text{N}}{0.020\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 18\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $18\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          The acceleration is almost twice the acceleration of free fall, which is plausible for a light rod acted on by a force equal to the weight of a $36\,\text{g}$ object. In reality friction would reduce the acceleration.

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. Exercise 4-10

          A horizontal copper rod of mass $12\,\text{g}$ and length $20\,\text{cm}$ hangs from two light conducting wires. The rod is entirely in a uniform horizontal magnetic field with a magnetic flux density of $0.25\,\text{T}$, and perpendicular to the field. A current is led to the rod through the wires.

          How large must the current be for the wires to go slack, that is, for the magnetic force to support the whole weight of the rod?

          A
          Answer

          $2.4\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the rod $m = 12\,\text{g}$, length in the field $l = 20\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.25\,\text{T}$, rod perpendicular to the horizontal field; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          current $I$ at which the wires go slack

          Principle

          The wires are slack when their tension is zero. Then only the weight, downwards, and the magnetic force act on the rod. The rod is at rest, so by Newton's first law the forces must cancel: the magnetic force must point upwards and equal the weight. The field is horizontal and the rod is perpendicular to it, so the force $I l B$ is vertical; the direction of the current is chosen so that the force points upwards.

          Formula

          $I l B = m g$, so $I = \dfrac{m g}{l B}$

          Conversions

          $m$$= 12\,\text{g}$
          $= 12 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.012\,\text{kg}$
          $l$$= 20\,\text{cm}$
          $= 20 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
          $= 0.20\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $I$$= \dfrac{0.012\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.25\,\text{T}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.11772\,\text{N}}{0.050\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= 2.354\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $2.4\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          A current of a few amperes is enough to support a light rod, which suits a laboratory experiment. If the direction of the current were reversed, the force would point downwards and the wires would tighten: the tension would then be twice the weight.

        2. Exercise 4-11

          Two parallel busbars at a substation are $0.30\,\text{m}$ apart. In a short circuit a current of $20\,\text{kA}$ flows momentarily in them in opposite directions.

          How large is the force on a $1.0\,\text{m}$ length of a busbar, and do the busbars attract or repel each other? Give the force as positive if the busbars attract each other and as negative if they repel each other.

          N
          Answer

          $-270\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance between the busbars $r = 0.30\,\text{m}$, currents $I_1 = I_2 = 20\,\text{kA}$ in opposite directions, length considered $l = 1.0\,\text{m}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$

          Find

          force $F$ on a $1.0\,\text{m}$ length of a busbar and its direction; the force is given as positive if the busbars attract each other and negative if they repel each other

          Principle

          Each busbar is in the magnetic field of the other. The field of one busbar at the other is perpendicular to it and the same along its whole length, so the force is $F = I_2 l B_1$. Currents in opposite directions repel each other, so the force pushes the busbars apart.

          Formula

          $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ and $F = I_2 l B_1$

          Conversions

          $I_1$$= I_2$
          $= 20\,\text{kA}$
          $= 20 \cdot 1000\,\text{A}$
          $= 20\,000\,\text{A}$

          Substitution

          $B_1$$= \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 20\,000\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.30\,\text{m}}$
          $= 0.013\,333\,\text{T}$
          $F$$= 20\,000\,\text{A} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} \cdot 0.013\,333\,\text{T}$
          $= 266.7\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          The magnitude of the force is $270\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the current and the distance. The busbars repel each other, so by the sign rule of the task the answer is $-270\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          The field at the other busbar is $B_1 = 0.013\,\text{T}$ and the current is very large, so the force per metre corresponds to the weight of a $27\,\text{kg}$ object. This is why the busbars of a substation are fixed firmly: a short circuit could otherwise bend them.

        3. Exercise 4-12

          A thin aluminium wire of length $1.0\,\text{m}$ and mass $2.7\,\text{g}$ is horizontal and runs east–west by the compass. The magnetic flux density of the Earth's magnetic field in southern Finland is about $53\,\mu\text{T}$, and the field points towards magnetic north and downwards at an angle of $74^\circ$ to the horizontal (WMM2025 model, NOAA/BGS).

          How large a current would have to flow in the wire for the Earth's magnetic field to support the weight of the wire? Give the answer in kiloamperes.

          kA
          Answer

          $1.8\,\text{kA}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          length of the wire $l = 1.0\,\text{m}$, mass $m = 2.7\,\text{g}$, wire horizontal and east–west by the compass; the Earth's field $B = 53\,\mu\text{T}$ towards magnetic north and downwards at $74^\circ$ to the horizontal; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          current $I$ in kiloamperes

          Principle

          Split the field into a horizontal and a vertical component. The wire runs east–west by the compass, so it is perpendicular to the horizontal component, which points to magnetic north, and also perpendicular to the vertical component. The vertical component exerts on the wire a horizontal force, which does not support the wire. The horizontal component $B_\text{horizontal} = B \cos 74^\circ$ points to magnetic north, and by the right-hand rule a current flowing east experiences from it a vertical force upwards. The wire stays in place when this force equals the weight.

          Formula

          $I l B \cos 74^\circ = m g$, so $I = \dfrac{m g}{l B \cos 74^\circ}$

          Conversions

          $m$$= 2.7\,\text{g}$
          $= 2.7 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
          $= 0.0027\,\text{kg}$
          $B$$= 53\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$

          Finally $1\,\text{kA} = 1000\,\text{A}$.

          Substitution

          $I$$= \dfrac{0.0027\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.0\,\text{m} \cdot 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot \cos 74^\circ}$
          $= \dfrac{0.026487\,\text{N}}{1.4609 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}}$
          $= 1813\,\text{A}$
          $= 1.813\,\text{kA}$

          Answer

          $1.8\,\text{kA}$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          The required current is enormous: a thin wire would melt at once. The result shows how weak the Earth's magnetic field is, about $0.05\,\text{mT}$, compared with a tenth of a tesla for a laboratory magnet. A compass needle still turns, because it is a magnet and not a current-carrying wire.
