Equation of motion for connected bodies

Why does a mass hanging over the edge of a table fall more slowly than a freely falling object when it pulls a cart along the table with a string?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the acceleration of connected bodies and the tension in the string from the equation of motion of each body.
  • draw a separate free-body diagram for each body.
  • explain why the tension for an accelerating hanging body is not its weight.

1Think about it

A cart stands on a table, and a string tied to it passes over a pulley at the edge of the table. A mass hangs from the other end of the string. When the cart is released, the mass starts to descend and pulls the cart along. The mass does not fall as fast as it would in free fall, though.

What pulls the cart, and how large is the force of the string compared with the weight of the mass?

Optional Review: force and reaction force
The string pulls the cart on the table to the right with the force $\overline{T}_1$. What is the reaction force of this force?

  • The normal force belongs to a different interaction, the one between the table and the cart. The reaction force always belongs to the same interaction as the force it pairs with: here the one between the string and the cart.

  • Correct. The reaction force belongs to the same interaction as the original force and acts on the other body: the cart pulls the string. It acts on the string, so it is not in the free-body diagram of the cart.

  • Weight is the interaction between the Earth and the cart. The reaction force of the string's force arises in the interaction between the string and the cart.

  • It is equally large as $\overline{T}_1$ when the string is light and the pulley is light and frictionless, but it acts on the hanging mass and belongs to the string–mass interaction. It is not the reaction force of $\overline{T}_1$.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation. The string is light and inextensible, the pulley is light and frictionless, and friction on the table and air resistance are neglected. So the accelerations of the bodies have the same magnitude and the string pulls both bodies with a force of the same magnitude $T$. The blue arrow for velocity and the orange arrow for acceleration are in the situation view because they are not forces. The arrows of the free-body diagrams are on a common scale.

How the calculation works

Cart and hanging mass: the equation of the cart $T = m_1 a$ and the equation of the hanging mass $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$ give $a = \dfrac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$ and $T = m_1 a$. Two hanging bodies, $m_2 > m_1$: $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$ and $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$ give $a = \dfrac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$ and $T = \dfrac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$. In the readings $\Sigma F_1$ and $\Sigma F_2$ are the net forces on the bodies along the motion, and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Conclusion

Using your measurements and the free-body diagrams, explain why the tension in the string is smaller than the weight of the hanging mass and why it approaches the weight when the cart is heavier.

Model answer

The hanging mass accelerates downward, so the net force $G_2 - T = m_2 a$ on it points down and $T$ is smaller than the weight $G_2 = 4.9\,\text{N}$. Only the string acts on the cart horizontally, so $T = m_1 a$. When the cart is heavier, the acceleration decreases and the net force $m_2 a$ on the hanging mass decreases. Then $T$ approaches the weight: in the model's values $T = 2.45\,\text{N}$ with cart mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$, $3.27\,\text{N}$ with $1.0\,\text{kg}$ and $3.68\,\text{N}$ with $1.5\,\text{kg}$. The tension would equal the weight only if the bodies did not accelerate, for example when the cart is held still.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Often two bodies move together because a string joins them. When the string is taut and does not stretch, the bodies travel the same distance in the same time, so their accelerations have the same magnitude $a$. The string passes a force from one body to the other: it pulls each body toward itself. In this chapter the string is light and inextensible, the pulley is light and frictionless, and friction on the table and air resistance are neglected.

    Connected bodies are treated one at a time. Each gets its own free-body diagram that shows only the forces acting on it, and each gets its own equation of motion. In the figure, the cart is acted on by its weight $\overline{G}_1$, the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the table and the tension $\overline{T}_1$ in the string. The hanging mass is acted on by its weight $\overline{G}_2$ and the tension $\overline{T}_2$ in the string.

    The string pulls the cart with the force $\overline{T}_1$ and the hanging mass with the force $\overline{T}_2$. They act on different bodies, so they appear in different free-body diagrams. A light string and a frictionless pulley only pass the force on and turn its direction, so the magnitudes are equal: $T_1 = T_2 = T$. The forces $\overline{T}_1$ and $\overline{T}_2$ are nevertheless not an action–reaction pair. When the string pulls the cart, the cart pulls the string back with an equally large force, and that force acts on the string and is not in the cart's free-body diagram.

    When the hanging mass accelerates downward, the tension in the string is smaller than its weight. The acceleration is downward, so the net force on the mass points down and $G_2 > T$. If $T$ were equal to $G_2$, the net force on the mass would be zero and it would not start to move. The mass therefore descends more slowly than a freely falling object: the acceleration $a = \tfrac{m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$ is smaller than $g$, because the weight of the hanging mass also accelerates the cart.

    Equations of motion for connected bodies

    Each body gets its own equation of motion $\Sigma F = ma$. The positive direction is chosen separately for each body along its acceleration: to the right for the cart and down for the hanging mass. Because the string does not stretch, both bodies have the same acceleration magnitude $a$. The equations of the cart and the hanging mass are

    $$\begin{aligned} T &= m_1 a \\ m_2 g - T &= m_2 a \end{aligned}$$

    where $m_1$ is the mass of the cart (kg), $m_2$ the mass of the hanging mass (kg), $T$ the tension in the string (N), $a$ the acceleration (m/s²) and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. There are two unknowns, $a$ and $T$, and two equations.

    When the equations are added side by side, $T$ cancels, because the string is an internal force between the bodies. This gives the acceleration and then the tension:

    $$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g \\ T &= m_1 a = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g \end{aligned}$$

    The tension is always smaller than the weight $m_2 g$ of the hanging mass, because $\tfrac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2} < 1$. The heavier the cart is compared with the hanging mass, the less the bodies accelerate and the closer $T$ is to the weight $m_2 g$.

    Two hanging bodies

    When a body hangs at each end of the string, the heavier one ($m_2$) goes down and the lighter one ($m_1$) goes up. The positive direction is chosen along the motion: up for the lighter body and down for the heavier one. Then

    $$\begin{aligned} T - m_1 g &= m_1 a \\ m_2 g - T &= m_2 a \end{aligned}$$

    $$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,g \\ T &= \frac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g \end{aligned}$$

    where $m_2 > m_1$. The tension lies between the weights $m_1 g$ and $m_2 g$ of the bodies. If the masses are equal, $a = 0$ and $T = mg$.

    Method for connected bodies

    1. Draw a free-body diagram for each body. The string pulls each body toward itself, and each body has its own tension arrow.
    2. Choose the positive direction for each body along its acceleration.
    3. Write the equation of motion $\Sigma F = ma$ for each body separately. The acceleration magnitude $a$ and the tension magnitude $T$ are the same in both. If a body is also pulled by an external force $\overline{F}$, that force belongs only in the free-body diagram and equation of motion of the body being pulled.
    4. Solve the two equations: add them, so that $T$ cancels, or substitute the $T$ from one into the other.
    5. Check the signs and compare: the tension is not equal to the weight of a hanging body when the bodies accelerate.

    Common misconception

    People often think that the cart is pulled by the weight of the hanging mass, so that the tension would be $m_2 g$. The cart is pulled by the string, however, and the force of the string is $T$. Only when the bodies do not accelerate, for example when the cart is held still, is $T = m_2 g$. When the hanging mass descends with increasing speed, its net force $G_2 - T = m_2 a$ must point down, so $T < m_2 g$.

    Another mistake is to treat the forces $\overline{T}_1$ and $\overline{T}_2$ as an action–reaction pair. They act on different bodies but belong to different interactions: one to the string–cart pair and the other to the string–mass pair. They are equal because the string is light and the pulley is light and frictionless, not because they would be the forces of one interaction pair. A third mistake is to think that the heavier body accelerates more. Bodies at the ends of an inextensible string have the same acceleration magnitude.

    Summary

    • Bodies connected by a string or over a pulley each have their own free-body diagram and their own equation of motion $\Sigma F = ma$, and the positive direction is chosen for each body along its acceleration.
    • A light, inextensible string and a light, frictionless pulley give the bodies the same acceleration magnitude $a$ and equal tensions $T_1 = T_2 = T$.
    • For a cart and a hanging mass, $a = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$ and $T = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$. For two hanging bodies, $a = \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$ and $T = \frac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$.
    • The tension for an accelerating hanging body is not its weight: for the cart and the hanging mass $T < m_2 g$, and for two hanging bodies $T$ lies between the weights.
    • The tensions $\overline{T}_1$ and $\overline{T}_2$ act on different bodies and are not an action–reaction pair.

      Problem

      Step 1/8

      Given and find. $m_1 = 1.5\,\text{kg}$, $m_2 = 0.60\,\text{kg}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the acceleration $a$ and the tension $T$ in the string.

      Step 2/8

      Principle. The cart and the hanging mass are two bodies, so each gets its own equation of motion. The string does not stretch, so the accelerations of the bodies have the same magnitude $a$. There is no friction, so in the horizontal direction only the string acts on the cart.

      Step 3/8

      Free-body diagram. Each body has its own free-body diagram. The positive direction is to the right for the cart and down for the hanging mass. The cart does not accelerate vertically, so $N = G_1$. In the horizontal direction the cart is pulled only by $\overline{T}_1$. The hanging mass is acted on by $\overline{G}_2$ downward and $\overline{T}_2$ upward.

      Step 4/8

      Rearranged formula. The two equations of motion are read from the diagrams and solved:

      $$\begin{aligned} &T = m_1 a \\ &m_2 g - T = m_2 a \\ &m_2 g = (m_1 + m_2) a \\ &a = \frac{m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2} \\ &T = m_1 a \end{aligned}$$

      Step 5/8

      Unit conversions. None are needed: the masses are in kilograms and $\tfrac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2}{\text{kg}} = \text{m/s}^2$.

      Step 6/8

      Substitution. The numerical values are substituted with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2} \\ \phantom{a} &= \frac{0.60\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.5\,\text{kg} + 0.60\,\text{kg}} \\ \phantom{a} &\approx 2.80\,\text{m/s}^2 \\[4pt] T &= m_1 a = 1.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 2.803\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ \phantom{T} &\approx 4.20\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 7/8

      Result. The acceleration of the cart is $a \approx 2.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the tension in the string is $T \approx 4.2\,\text{N}$, with two significant figures like the masses $1.5\,\text{kg}$ and $0.60\,\text{kg}$.

      Step 8/8

      Reasonableness. The weight of the hanging mass is $m_2 g \approx 5.9\,\text{N}$, and the tension $4.2\,\text{N}$ is smaller than that, as it should be. The net force on the hanging mass is $5.886\,\text{N} - 4.204\,\text{N} \approx 1.68\,\text{N}$, and this equals

      $m_2 a$$= 0.60\,\text{kg} \cdot 2.803\,\text{m/s}^2$
      $\approx 1.68\,\text{N}$

      The acceleration is smaller than $g$, because the hanging mass also accelerates the cart. Without the cart, the mass would fall with acceleration $g$.

          The lighter body as a rectangle, positive direction up: $\overline{T}_1$ up and $\overline{G}_1$ down, with $\overline{T}_1$ longer. The heavier body as a rectangle, positive direction down: $\overline{G}_2$ down and $\overline{T}_2$ up, with $\overline{G}_2$ longer.

          Formula

          $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$ and $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$. The first gives $T = m_1 (g + a)$, and the second gives $m_2 (g - a) = T$, so $m_2 = \dfrac{m_1 (g + a)}{g - a}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $m_2$$= \dfrac{0.60\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81 + 2.0)\,\text{m/s}^2}{(9.81 - 2.0)\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= \dfrac{7.086\,\text{N}}{7.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 0.9073\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $0.91\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures like the mass $0.60\,\text{kg}$ and the acceleration $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          The mass $0.91\,\text{kg}$ is greater than $0.60\,\text{kg}$, as it must be for the heavier body. Check with the formula

          $a$$= \dfrac{(m_2 - m_1)\,g}{m_1 + m_2}$
          $= \dfrac{0.3073 \cdot 9.81}{1.5073}\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $\approx 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

          and the tension $T = 7.09\,\text{N}$ lies between the weights $5.9\,\text{N}$ and $8.9\,\text{N}$.

        1. An elevator cabin of mass $800\,\text{kg}$ hangs from the end of a light, inextensible rope. The rope passes over a light, frictionless wheel, and at its other end is a counterweight of mass $1200\,\text{kg}$. The counterweight descends and the cabin rises. Friction and air resistance are neglected. Draw the free-body diagrams of the cabin and the counterweight.

          What is the force that the rope exerts on the axle of the wheel, when the wheel is light and the axle holds it in place?

          N
          Answer

          $19000\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the cabin $m_1 = 800\,\text{kg}$, mass of the counterweight $m_2 = 1200\,\text{kg}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the rope is light and inextensible, the wheel is light and frictionless, air resistance is neglected

          Find

          the force $F_\text{axle}$ that the rope exerts on the axle of the wheel, in $\text{N}$

          Principle

          The cabin and the counterweight are two bodies, and each gets its own equation of motion. The rope is inextensible, so $a$ is the same for both, and the light rope pulls both with the force $T$. The wheel is light and does not accelerate, so its net force is zero.

          Free-body diagram

          The cabin as a rectangle, positive direction up: $\overline{T}_1$ up and $\overline{G}_1$ down. The counterweight as a rectangle, positive direction down: $\overline{G}_2$ down and $\overline{T}_2$ up. The arrows $\overline{T}_1$ and $\overline{T}_2$ are equally long. On the wheel: two forces $T$ down and $F_\text{axle}$ up.

          Formula

          $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$ and $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$, so

          $T$$= \dfrac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\cdot g$

          From the equilibrium of the wheel, $F_\text{axle} - 2T = 0$, so $F_\text{axle} = 2T$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $T$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 800\,\text{kg} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg}}{800\,\text{kg} + 1200\,\text{kg}} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 960\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 9418\,\text{N}$

          and

          $F_\text{axle}$$= 2 \cdot 9418\,\text{N}$
          $= 18\,835\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $19000\,\text{N}$

          That is $19\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures, because the masses are given to the nearest hundred kilograms.

          Reasonableness

          The weights are $m_1 g \approx 7850\,\text{N}$ and $m_2 g \approx 11\,770\,\text{N}$, and $T = 9418\,\text{N}$ lies between them. The force on the axle, $18\,835\,\text{N}$, is smaller than the sum of the weights, $19\,620\,\text{N}$, because the counterweight accelerates downward.

        2. A cart of mass $1.0\,\text{kg}$ is on a frictionless table and is tied by a light, inextensible string over a light, frictionless pulley to a hanging mass. The string breaks if its tension exceeds $3.0\,\text{N}$.

          What is the greatest mass of the hanging mass for which the string still holds while the bodies are moving? First draw the free-body diagram of each body. Answer in kilograms.

          kg
          Answer

          $0.44\,\text{kg}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          cart $m_1 = 1.0\,\text{kg}$, greatest allowed tension $T_{\max} = 3.0\,\text{N}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; friction is neglected, the string and the pulley are light

          Find

          the greatest mass $m_2$ of the hanging mass in $\text{kg}$

          Principle

          The bodies get their own equations of motion, and from them the tension is obtained as a function of the masses. The tension increases as the mass of the hanging mass increases, so the limiting value $T = T_{\max}$ gives the greatest mass.

          Free-body diagram

          The cart as a rectangle, positive direction to the right: horizontally only $\overline{T}_1$. The hanging mass as a rectangle, positive direction down: $\overline{G}_2$ down and $\overline{T}_2$ up.

          Formula

          $T = m_1 a$ and $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$, so $T = \dfrac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$. When $T = T_{\max}$: $T_{\max} (m_1 + m_2) = m_1 m_2 g$, so

          $m_2$$= \dfrac{m_1\,T_{\max}}{g\,m_1 - T_{\max}}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $m_2$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{N} \cdot 1.0\,\text{kg}}{1.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 3.0\,\text{N}}$
          $= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{N}\cdot\text{kg}}{6.81\,\text{N}}$
          $= 0.4405\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $0.44\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures like the data $3.0\,\text{N}$ and $1.0\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          Check:

          $T$$= \dfrac{1.0 \cdot 0.4405}{1.4405} \cdot 9.81\,\text{N}$
          $\approx 3.0\,\text{N}$

          With a hanging mass heavier than the limit, $T$ is greater, but always below the weight of the cart $9.81\,\text{N}$, because $T$ approaches $m_1 g$ only for an infinitely large $m_2$.

        3. A cart of mass $1.5\,\text{kg}$ is on a frictionless table and is tied by a light, inextensible string over a light, frictionless pulley to a hanging mass of $0.50\,\text{kg}$. The cart is released from rest.

          How long does it take for the cart to travel $0.90\,\text{m}$? First draw the free-body diagram of each body.

          s
          Answer

          $0.86\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          cart $m_1 = 1.5\,\text{kg}$, hanging mass $m_2 = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, distance $s = 0.90\,\text{m}$, starting from rest, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; friction is neglected, the string and the pulley are light

          Find

          the time $t$ in $\text{s}$

          Principle

          First the equations of motion of the bodies give the acceleration. The acceleration is constant, so for motion from rest $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, which gives the time.

          Free-body diagram

          The cart as a rectangle, positive direction to the right: horizontally only $\overline{T}_1$. The hanging mass as a rectangle, positive direction down: $\overline{G}_2$ down and $\overline{T}_2$ up, and $T_1 = T_2 = T$.

          Formula

          $T = m_1 a$ and $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$, so $a = \dfrac{m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$. From the distance $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, so $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2s}{a}}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $a$$= \dfrac{0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{2.0\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 2.4525\,\text{m/s}^2$

          and

          $t$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 0.90\,\text{m}}{2.4525\,\text{m/s}^2}}$
          $= 0.857\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $0.86\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures like the data $0.90\,\text{m}$ and $0.50\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          Check: the final speed is $at \approx 2.1\,\text{m/s}$ and the average speed $1.05\,\text{m/s}$, so $1.05\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.857\,\text{s} \approx 0.90\,\text{m}$. A freely falling mass ($a = g$) would cover the same distance in $0.43\,\text{s}$, so $0.86\,\text{s}$ is sensibly longer.
