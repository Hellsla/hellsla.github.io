## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–7 min, Think about it.** Read the hook aloud. Pairs discuss the Cold War and secret research today, and write two or three sentences.
- **7–12 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, committed, with a short reason. Do not discuss the answers yet.
- **12–40 min, Investigate.** Jigsaw in groups of four (10 min reading one section each, 8 min telling the group, 4 min for the expert notes). Then everyone reads *ZETA* and *Geneva 1958* (5 min) and orders the eight events (2 min).
- **40–50 min, Theory summary.** Read the key idea and the misconception together; back to the predictions (the reflection). The biographies are optional.
- **50–64 min, Apply.** ZETA's temperature (numeric, 4 min), claim–evidence–motive sorting (3 min), the headline error (2 min), the pair debate and the paragraph (5 min speaking; the paragraph can be finished at home).
- **64–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the transfer task (cold fusion), the summary and the French corner in pairs.

**Fast pairs** do the optional biographies and the comparison of Richter and Spitzer, or open the course timeline (unit *Fusion history: an interactive timeline*) and find two more events of the secret years. **If time runs short**, give the expert notes only orally, make the debate paragraph homework, and do the French corner at the start of the next lesson.

## Organisation and preparation
- Groups of four for the jigsaw: assign the four sections in advance so that stronger readers take *War, bombs and secrecy* and *Pinches, stellarators and a Soviet ring*, the two longest and hardest. In groups of three, one student takes two short sections (*Stars* and *Huemul*).
- Pairs for the debate: assign the sides (A helped, B harmed) so that nobody argues only their own opinion; swap if there is time.
- Optional: a map of the world in 1951 (Argentina, Britain, the USA, the USSR) on the board, and the pinch figure on the projector for a two-minute explanation of the Z-pinch and theta pinch before the jigsaw.
- The unit builds on chapter 4 (*Charged particles and magnetic confinement*); recall in one sentence that a magnetic field pushes moving charges sideways.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed with the criteria shown after the student writes. For a grade, use the same criteria: the debate paragraph gives an argument on each side, a concrete example with a date or a name, and a conclusion that follows; the transfer task asks for independent repetition, for what was measured and links the questions to Huemul or ZETA.
- Physics: the student explains the two pinches by the direction of the current and why ZETA's neutron directions decided the case; the ZETA calculation is correct with units.
- Language: key terms used correctly (claim, evidence, to classify, to declassify, instability, plasma) and linking words in the paragraph.

## Common difficulties
- *Controlled* fusion and the hydrogen bomb are confused. Ask: "Is this fusion for a bomb, or slow fusion for electricity?" The secrecy is about the shared physics and neutrons, not about a reactor exploding.
- Students think that neutrons prove fusion. The neutron-direction figure helps: random motion in a hot plasma gives neutrons in all directions, a few accelerated ions give a beam.
- Temperatures in kelvin and energies in electronvolts: the numeric task links them with $E = k_\text{B} T$; $1\ \text{eV}$ is about $11\ 600\ \text{K}$.
- Many names in a short time: the order task and the summary give the backbone; students do not need to learn every name.

## Extension ideas
- Compare Rutherford's "moonshine" of 1933 with fission's rapid success (1938 discovery, 1942 first reactor): why was fission so much quicker than fusion?
- Read about Oleg Lavrentiev, the Soviet soldier on Sakhalin who sent fusion ideas to the Central Committee in 1950 (timeline event *A soldier's fusion letter*).
- Look up Scylla I, the Los Alamos theta pinch of 1958, often called the first controlled thermonuclear fusion experiment: how did its result differ from ZETA's?
