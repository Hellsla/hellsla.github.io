Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting

Why do two groups get different speeds for the same toy car, even though both measure carefully?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • formulate a research question and a testable hypothesis.
  • plan a fair experiment: choose the variables, the measuring range and the repeats.
  • record measurements in a lab record and report a result with its uncertainty.

Prior knowledge

  • A measurement result has an uncertainty, and repeating the measurement gives a meanchapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement
  • A straight line is fitted to the data points, and its slope tells the rate of changechapter Graphs and fitting a straight line

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 5.

1Think about it

Two groups measure the speed of the same toy car with a hand stopwatch. Group A runs the car once over a distance of $0.50\,\text{m}$, gets a time of $1.0\,\text{s}$ and calculates a speed of $0.50\,\text{m/s}$. Group B runs five different distances, three times each. Over $2.00\,\text{m}$ the mean time is $4.73\,\text{s}$, and group B calculates a speed of $0.42\,\text{m/s}$. The car and the batteries are the same.

Why do the results differ, and how should an investigation be designed so that its result can be trusted?

Optional Review: repeats and uncertainty
A student measures a walking time five times and gets $4.7\,\text{s}$, $4.9\,\text{s}$, $4.6\,\text{s}$, $4.8\,\text{s}$ and $4.7\,\text{s}$. What is the most sensible way to report the result?

  • A single extreme value is just one measurement among the others. When a measurement is repeated, the mean is a better estimate than the largest or smallest reading (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

  • The mean gives the result, but not how much it can vary. Without an uncertainty no one else can judge whether the result fits a prediction or another measurement (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

  • Correct. The mean is $23.7\,\text{s} : 5 = 4.74\,\text{s}$ and half of the range is $(4.9\,\text{s} - 4.6\,\text{s}) : 2 = 0.15\,\text{s}$. The first digit of the uncertainty is 1, so it is given with two figures and the mean to the same decimal place. Today you plan an experiment so that this uncertainty is small compared with the measurement.

  • Without the uncertainty the reader does not know how much the result can vary: the readings already vary by a tenth of a second. A result is given together with its uncertainty and with the precision that matches it (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

Prediction: why the results differ

The speed of group A is $0.50\,\text{m/s}$ and that of group B $0.42\,\text{m/s}$. What explains the difference best?

  • Correct. The uncertainty of the time is about $0.2\,\text{s}$, so the relative uncertainty of the time of group A is $0.2 : 1.0 = 20\,\%$ and the speed is $0.50 \pm 0.10\,\text{m/s}$. The result $0.42\,\text{m/s}$ of group B fits within this interval, and the uncertainty of B itself is only about $\pm 0.01\,\text{m/s}$. The lab record supports this.

  • This is a testable hypothesis, and you can check it by measuring several distances. The lab record of group B (section Investigate) does not support it: over the shortest distance the speed is lower, $0.39\,\text{m/s}$, not higher.

  • Random variation does not make a measurement worthless. It is estimated with an uncertainty and reduced by repeats and a longer measuring time. The result of group B is accurate to about $\pm 0.01\,\text{m/s}$, and the result of group A is very imprecise.

  • This is a testable hypothesis, but the lab record of group B (section Investigate) does not support it: the speed of the car stays about the same even over long distances, $0.42\,\text{m/s}$. So the difference is not due to the distance but to how the time was measured.

Show the solution

Given

a point on the line $(t, s) = (5.0\,\text{s}$; $2.1\,\text{m})$, and the line passes through the origin $(0, 0)$

Find

the speed $v$ of the car in $\text{m/s}$

Principle

The car moves uniformly and the clock is started when the car passes the start line, so at the time $t = 0$ the distance is $s = 0$: the distance is directly proportional to time, $s = vt$, and the line of the $(t, s)$ graph passes through the origin. The slope of the line is the speed.

Reading the graph

Read a point from the line whose coordinates are known accurately: when $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$, the line is at the height $s = 2.1\,\text{m}$. The other point is the origin. The slope is calculated between these points.

Formula

$v$$= \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$
$= \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

Conversions

The distance is in metres and the time in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$v$$= \dfrac{2.1\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{2.1\,\text{m}}{5.0\,\text{s}}$
$= 0.42\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$0.42\,\text{m/s}$

Two significant figures, because the distance $2.1\,\text{m}$ read from the graph can be read to two figures.

Reasonableness

The result agrees with the speeds $0.42\,\text{m/s}$ in the table for the longer distances. The last point of the graph gives $2.50\,\text{m} : 5.93\,\text{s} = 0.42\,\text{m/s}$, so the line describes the points well.

A fair experiment?

Group C investigates how the number of batteries affects the speed of a toy car. It first runs a new car with two batteries on the floor and then an old car with four batteries on a carpet. The speed is lower the second time.What is the most important flaw in the plan?

  • Two points is very few, and more values and repeats would be better. Even so, comparing two points would be sensible if the other variables were the same. The essential flaw is that the comparison is not fair.

  • Correct. In a fair experiment you change only the number of batteries and keep the car and the surface the same. Now the slowing down could be due to the carpet, the old car or the batteries, and the measurement does not separate them.

  • The investigator chooses the number of batteries, because that is the thing whose effect is being investigated. What is measured is the speed of the car. The worst flaw is still that other things change as well.

4Theory summary

Key idea

Physics is an experimental science: a claim holds only once it has been tested with measurements that anyone can repeat. An investigation is a systematic way of answering a question by measuring. It begins with a question and ends with a conclusion that the measurement results support. A good investigation is not one where the assumption turns out to be right, but one where the measurement could have shown the assumption to be wrong.

Research question and hypothesis

A research question is a question that a measurement can answer: "How does the distance travelled by a toy car depend on time?" The question "Which car is best?" is no good, because it cannot be measured.

A hypothesis is a reasoned, testable prediction that answers the question. For example: "If the car moves at constant speed, the distance travelled is directly proportional to time, $s = vt$." A hypothesis must lead to something measurable that could turn out to be wrong. The prediction "the car goes somehow" is not a hypothesis, because no measurement can disprove it.

Variables

A variable is a quantity whose value can vary in an investigation. In an experiment you change one variable, measure another and keep everything else the same: - independent variable: the experimenter chooses its values, here the distance $s$ - dependent variable: its value is read from an instrument, here the time $t$ - controlled variables: stay the same throughout the experiment, here the car, the batteries, the surface and the way of starting.

If two variables change at the same time, for example the distance and the batteries, you cannot tell what caused the change in the result. That is why an experiment is fair only when just the independent variable changes.

Planning before measuring

Before measuring, the plan answers these questions: what is measured and with which instrument, which values are chosen for the independent variable and how many, how many repeats are made for each value, and what is kept constant. Try it out first with a trial run. It tells you whether the measurement is possible at all, and it gives a rough estimate of the result.

Choose the measuring range so that the quantity being measured is clearly larger than the uncertainty of the measurement (see the section Relative uncertainty and choosing the measuring range). Choose several values at equal intervals, because one or two points do not show whether the dependence is a straight line.

Lab record

A lab record is a log made during the experiment, not from memory afterwards. It contains the research question, the date, the equipment and its accuracy, the controlled variables, the measurements as a table with units, and observations, for example "the car veered sideways".

Record the readings as they are. Do not quietly delete or correct a measurement that looks odd: mark it and write down what happened. A short example:

$s\ (\text{m})$ $t_1\ (\text{s})$ $t_2\ (\text{s})$ $t_3\ (\text{s})$ note
0.50 1.3 1.1 1.4 hard to time
1.00 2.5 2.3 2.6
1.50 3.7 3.5 3.6

Report

A report describes the investigation so that someone else can repeat it and judge the conclusion. Its parts are: 1. research question and hypothesis 2. method: equipment, variables and procedure 3. results: table, graph and the value of the quantity with its uncertainty 4. discussion: sources of error and what you would do differently next time 5. conclusion: the answer to the research question and whether the results support the hypothesis.

A conclusion is only as strong as the data. If the measurements agree with the prediction within the uncertainty, the hypothesis has gained support, but it has not been proved.

Relative uncertainty and choosing the measuring range

The uncertainty $\Delta t$ of a time measured by hand comes mainly from reaction time, and it is almost the same in a short and a long measurement. The quality of a measurement is therefore described by the relative uncertainty, the uncertainty compared with the measured value. To keep it at most $\varepsilon_\text{max}$, the measured time must be at least

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon &= \frac{\Delta t}{t} \\ t &\ge \frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \end{aligned}$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the relative uncertainty (often given as a percentage), $\Delta t$ the uncertainty of the time (s) and $t$ the measured time (s). With an uncertainty $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$ and a limit of $5\,\%$, the time must be at least $4\,\text{s}$.

The required distance follows from the model of uniform motion $s = vt$. If the measurement is made at several distances, a straight line is fitted to the data points (chapter Graphs and fitting a straight line). When $t$ is on the horizontal axis and $s$ on the vertical axis, the slope of the line is the speed $v$. The shortest valid distance is the one at which the relative uncertainty just stays within the chosen limit $\varepsilon_\text{max}$. On a shorter distance the relative uncertainty exceeds this limit, and the result is too imprecise.

Common misconception

Many people think an investigation has failed if the measurement does not support the hypothesis. This is not so: if a measurement disproves the hypothesis, the investigator knows something they did not know before. A failed investigation is one from which nothing can be concluded, because the variables were not controlled or the measurements are too uncertain.

Another common mistake is to treat one measurement as enough. A single reading does not show how much the result varies, so the spread caused by random error cannot be estimated. A third mistake is to pick the measurements that fit the result and leave out the others: all measurements are recorded, and an outlier is explained or marked.

Summary

  • An investigation begins with a question that can be measured and a testable hypothesis that could turn out to be wrong.
  • In a fair experiment you change one variable, measure another and keep the others constant.
  • The measurement is planned in advance: measuring range, number of points, repeats and controlled variables. A trial run checks the plan.
  • The lab record contains the readings, units, equipment and observations, written during the experiment. No measurement is quietly deleted.
  • The relative uncertainty $\varepsilon = \Delta t : t$ gets smaller as the measured time gets longer; therefore the time must be at least $\Delta t : \varepsilon_\text{max}$.
  • A report gives the question, method, results, discussion and conclusion so that someone else can repeat the investigation.

    Worked example

    Problem

    Students investigate the motion of a toy car. In a trial run the speed of the car is about $0.42\,\text{m/s}$. The time is measured with a hand stopwatch, and the timing uncertainty is $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$. The relative uncertainty of the measurement should be at most $5.0\,\%$. What is the shortest distance the car must travel?

    Step 1/7

    Given and unknown. Speed $v = 0.42\,\text{m/s}$, uncertainty of the time $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$ and largest allowed relative uncertainty $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 5.0\,\% = 0.050$. We are asked for the shortest distance $s_\text{min}$.

    Step 2/7

    Principle. The relative uncertainty gets smaller as the measured time gets longer, because $\Delta t$ stays the same. First calculate the shortest allowed time from the uncertainty limit. The car moves at constant speed, so the model of uniform motion $s = vt$ gives the distance from the time.

    Step 3/7

    Rearranged formula. $$\begin{aligned} t_\text{min} &= \frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \\ s_\text{min} &= v\, t_\text{min} = \frac{v\,\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4/7

    Unit conversions. Not needed: the speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the distance comes out in metres. The percentage has been converted to a decimal number: $5.0\,\% = 0.050$.

    Step 5/7

    Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} t_\text{min} &= \frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \\ &= \frac{0.2\,\text{s}}{0.050} = 4\,\text{s} \\ s_\text{min} &= v\, t_\text{min} \\ &= 0.42\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 4\,\text{s} \\ &= 1.68\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 6/7

    Result. The distance must be at least $1.7\,\text{m}$. The result is rounded to two significant figures like the speed $0.42\,\text{m/s}$; the uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$ is given with one figure, and it gives only an estimate of the shortest time.

    Step 7/7

    Reasonableness. Over $1.7\,\text{m}$ the travel time of the car is about $1.7\,\text{m} : 0.42\,\text{m/s} \approx 4.05\,\text{s}$, so

    $\varepsilon$$= 0.2\,\text{s} : 4.05\,\text{s}$
    $\approx 4.9\,\%$

    which is just within the limit. Over $0.50\,\text{m}$ the time would be only $1.2\,\text{s}$ and the uncertainty about $17\,\%$, which is why the short distance was a poor choice.

    Optional Find the error
    A student plans an experiment in which the speed of the car is $0.60\,\text{m/s}$ and the uncertainty of the time is $0.2\,\text{s}$. The limit of the relative uncertainty is $5\,\%$.They calculate the shortest distance:

    $$\begin{aligned} s_\text{min} &= \frac{v\,\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}} \\ &= \frac{0.60\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.2\,\text{s}}{5} \\ &= 0.024\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Where is the error?

    • Correct. The relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ is a quotient, so $5\,\%$ must be substituted as the number $0.050$. The error could have been noticed with a reasonableness check: you cannot measure a distance of a couple of centimetres with a hand stopwatch when the time is under a second.

    • The formula is correct. The shortest time is $t_\text{min} = \Delta t : \varepsilon_\text{max}$ and the distance is $s = v\,t_\text{min}$. Dividing the uncertainty by the speed would give a combination of seconds and speed that has no unit of distance.

    • No conversion is needed, because the speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the uncertainty in seconds; the seconds cancel and the result is in metres. The error is in how the percentage was handled.

    Plan your own investigation

    Choose a phenomenon that you can investigate at school or at home and in which the quantities can be measured: for example the speed of walking or of a rolling ball, the cooling of water in a cup, or the stretching of a rubber band. Write a plan: research question, hypothesis, independent, dependent and controlled variables, measuring range and repeats, and how you will keep the lab record.

    Model answer

    Research question: how does the time a ball takes to roll depend on the distance on a level floor? The ball is always released from the same point over a short starting ramp, and timing starts when the ball passes a start line marked on the floor, by which point its speed has levelled off. Hypothesis: if the speed of the ball on the floor is approximately constant, the travel time is directly proportional to the distance, $s = vt$. If the time grows faster than the distance, the hypothesis is wrong. The independent variable is the distance from the start line (for example $1.6$–$3.2\,\text{m}$ at five equally spaced values), the dependent variable is the time with a hand stopwatch, and I keep constant the ball, the height of the ramp and the starting point, the surface and the way of starting (the ball is released without a push). In a trial run the speed of the ball is about $0.4\,\text{m/s}$. A real ball slows down slightly while rolling because of rolling resistance, so the experiment also tests whether the speed is constant enough. The uncertainty of timing by hand is about $0.2\,\text{s}$, so over the shortest distance the time must be at least $4\,\text{s}$ for the relative uncertainty to stay at most $5\,\%$. The distance $1.6\,\text{m}$ gives a time of $4\,\text{s}$. I make three repeats for each distance and write every reading with its unit in the lab record, together with observations such as "the ball hit the wall". The trial run shows whether the distance is long enough.

      Optional 4. Explain the conclusion

      The conclusion of a student's report reads: "The hypothesis was proved correct, because the measurement gave the speed $0.42\,\text{m/s}$ as predicted." Explain what is too strong in the claim and how the conclusion should be worded.

      Model answer

      One experiment cannot prove a hypothesis correct. It can give it support if the result agrees with the prediction within the uncertainty, or refute it. A conclusion must contain the result with its uncertainty, for example $v = 0.42 \pm 0.02\,\text{m/s}$, and it must be compared with the prediction: the result fits the prediction, so the measurement supports the hypothesis. It must also say under what conditions the result was obtained, for example with a particular car and surface, and what was left uninvestigated, such as longer distances. This lets someone else judge how far the conclusion can be trusted.

        1. The uncertainty of the time is $0.2\,\text{s}$. How long a time must be measured at least for the relative uncertainty to be at most $2.0\,\%$?
          Answer
          $t$$\ge \dfrac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon_\text{max}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.2\,\text{s}}{0.020}$
          $= 10\,\text{s}$
        2. What goes in a lab record, and why is a measurement that looks unusual not quietly deleted?
          Answer

          The lab record contains the research question, the date, the equipment and its accuracy, the controlled variables, the readings with their units, and observations. A clearly deviating measurement is checked, marked and explained, and it can be repeated if necessary. A result is not deleted just because it differs from the others, because then the result would be chosen to fit the expectation and the investigation would no longer test the hypothesis. Any rejection must be justified and documented.

        Level 1 · basic practice 5 exercises

        One formula or one step of reasoning. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          A ball rolls down a slope, and a student measures its travel time with a hand stopwatch as $4.0\,\text{s}$. The timing uncertainty is $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$.

          What is the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the time as a percentage?

          %
          Answer

          5.0 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          measured time $t = 4.0\,\text{s}$, timing uncertainty $\Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the time as a percentage

          Principle

          The relative uncertainty tells what fraction of the measurement result is uncertainty. You get it by dividing the uncertainty by the measured value.

          Formula

          $\varepsilon = \dfrac{\Delta t}{t}$

          Conversions

          Both $\Delta t$ and $t$ are in seconds, so the units cancel. The quotient is multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage.

          Substitution

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.2\,\text{s}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.050$
          $= 5.0\,\%$

          Answer

          $5\,\%$

          One significant figure like the uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$ (calculator value $5.0\,\%$).

          Reasonableness

          Four seconds is 20 times the uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$, so the uncertainty is about $\tfrac{1}{20} = 5\,\%$ of the measured time.

        2. Core

          A student walks a distance of ten metres five times and measures the times $12.3\,\text{s}$, $12.5\,\text{s}$, $12.2\,\text{s}$, $12.6\,\text{s}$ and $12.4\,\text{s}$. What is the mean of the measurements?

          s
          Answer

          12.4 s

          Show the solution

          Given

          five measurements $12.3\,\text{s}$, $12.5\,\text{s}$, $12.2\,\text{s}$, $12.6\,\text{s}$ and $12.4\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the mean $\bar{t}$ of the measurements in seconds

          Principle

          Random timing errors are sometimes too high and sometimes too low. When the measurement is repeated, the errors partly cancel, so the mean is a better estimate than a single measurement.

          Formula

          $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{t_1 + t_2 + t_3 + t_4 + t_5}{5}$

          Conversions

          All times are already in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          the sum of the times is $62.0\,\text{s}$, so

          $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{62.0\,\text{s}}{5}$
          $= 12.4\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $12.4\,\text{s}$

          Three significant figures like the measurements.

          Reasonableness

          The mean lies between the smallest measurement $12.2\,\text{s}$ and the largest $12.6\,\text{s}$, as a mean must. It is also at the middle measurement $12.4\,\text{s}$, because the deviations are fairly symmetrical.

        3. The marked driving distance of a toy car is $1.50\,\text{m}$, and the car covers it in $3.6\,\text{s}$.

          What is the speed of the car? Assume the speed is constant and answer in m/s.

          m/s
          Answer

          0.42 m/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance $s = 1.50\,\text{m}$, time $t = 3.6\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the speed $v$ of the car in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          The car moves at constant speed, so the distance is directly proportional to time: $s = vt$. The speed is the ratio of distance to time.

          Formula

          $s = vt$, so $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$

          Conversions

          The distance is in metres and the time in seconds, so the result comes out directly in $\text{m/s}$.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \dfrac{1.50\,\text{m}}{3.6\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.4167\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $0.42\,\text{m/s}$

          Two significant figures like the time $3.6\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The result is of the order of an ordinary toy car, less than a third of walking pace ($1.4\,\text{m/s}$). Rough check: $0.4\,\text{m/s} \cdot 3.6\,\text{s} \approx 1.4\,\text{m}$, which is close to 1.5 metres.

        4. The length of a table is measured five times with a ruler: $24.1\,\text{cm}$, $24.4\,\text{cm}$, $24.0\,\text{cm}$, $24.3\,\text{cm}$ and $24.2\,\text{cm}$. The uncertainty is estimated as half of the range of the measurements.

          How large is the uncertainty?

          cm
          Answer

          0.2 cm

          Show the solution

          Given

          five measurements $24.1\,\text{cm}$, $24.4\,\text{cm}$, $24.0\,\text{cm}$, $24.3\,\text{cm}$ and $24.2\,\text{cm}$

          Find

          the uncertainty $\Delta x$ in centimetres

          Principle

          Here the uncertainty is estimated simply as half of the range: it describes the spread of the repeated measurements around the mean. Repeats do not reveal a systematic error.

          Formula

          $\Delta x$$= \dfrac{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}}{2}$

          Conversions

          All lengths are in centimetres, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\Delta x$$= \dfrac{24.4\,\text{cm} - 24.0\,\text{cm}}{2}$
          $= \dfrac{0.4\,\text{cm}}{2}$
          $= 0.2\,\text{cm}$

          Answer

          $0.2\,\text{cm}$

          One significant figure, because the uncertainty is given with one figure.

          Reasonableness

          The uncertainty is two millimetres, which suits a measurement made with a ruler whose smallest scale division is a millimetre. The largest deviation of the measurements from the mean $24.2\,\text{cm}$ is $0.2\,\text{cm}$.

        5. In a row of a lab record the distance is $2.00\,\text{m}$, the mean of three times is $4.73\,\text{s}$ and half of the range is $\Delta t = 0.15\,\text{s}$.

          What is the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the time as a percentage?

          %
          Answer

          3.2 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          on the row of the lab record, distance $s = 2.00\,\text{m}$, mean of the times $\bar{t} = 4.73\,\text{s}$ and half of the range $\Delta t = 0.15\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the time as a percentage

          Principle

          The relative uncertainty tells what fraction of the measured time the uncertainty is. You get it by dividing the uncertainty written in the lab record by the mean time; the distance is not needed.

          Formula

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\bar{t}}$

          Conversions

          Both times are in seconds, so no conversions are needed. The quotient is multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage.

          Substitution

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.15\,\text{s}}{4.73\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.0317$
          $= 3.2\,\%$

          Answer

          $3.2\,\%$

          Two significant figures like the uncertainty $0.15\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          Rough estimate: $0.15 : 4.7$ is about $0.03$, that is, about $3\,\%$. The result agrees with the value $3.2\,\%$ in the table of the lab record and is below $5\,\%$, so the distance $2.00\,\text{m}$ is enough.

        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          The travel time of a toy car is measured five times, and the results are $7.8\,\text{s}$, $8.2\,\text{s}$, $7.9\,\text{s}$, $8.1\,\text{s}$ and $8.0\,\text{s}$. Half of the range is taken as a simple and cautious estimate of the uncertainty.

          What is the relative uncertainty of the result as a percentage?

          %
          Answer

          2.5 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          five times $7.8\,\text{s}$, $8.2\,\text{s}$, $7.9\,\text{s}$, $8.1\,\text{s}$ and $8.0\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the result as a percentage, when the uncertainty is half of the range

          Principle

          First calculate the mean and a simple estimate of the uncertainty, half of the range. The relative uncertainty is the uncertainty divided by the mean, so it tells the quality of the measurement regardless of the unit.

          Formula

          $\bar{t} = \dfrac{\sum t_i}{5}$,

          $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{t_\text{max} - t_\text{min}}{2}$

          and

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{\bar{t}}$

          Conversions

          All times are in seconds, so no conversions are needed. The quotient is multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage.

          Substitution

          $\bar{t}$$= \dfrac{40.0\,\text{s}}{5}$
          $= 8.0\,\text{s}$

          and

          $\Delta t$$= \dfrac{8.2\,\text{s} - 7.8\,\text{s}}{2}$
          $= 0.2\,\text{s}$

          so

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.2\,\text{s}}{8.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.025$
          $= 2.5\,\%$

          Answer

          $2.5\,\%$

          (calculator value). The uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$ is a rough estimate with one figure, so in a report it is enough to round the relative uncertainty to one significant figure, about $3\,\%$. The answer field accepts both.

          Reasonableness

          Eight seconds is 40 times the uncertainty $0.2\,\text{s}$, so the fraction is about $\tfrac{1}{40} = 2.5\,\%$. It is smaller than the $5.0\,\%$ of the $4.0\,\text{s}$ measurement, as it should be for a longer time.

        2. Core

          A car covers a distance of $3.0\,\text{m}$ at a constant speed of $1.5\,\text{m/s}$. The travel time is measured with a hand stopwatch, and the timing uncertainty is $0.20\,\text{s}$.

          What is the relative uncertainty of the measured time as a percentage?

          %
          Answer

          10 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed $v = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$, distance $s = 3.0\,\text{m}$, timing uncertainty $\Delta t = 0.20\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the relative uncertainty $\varepsilon$ of the time as a percentage

          Principle

          The car moves at constant speed, so the travel time follows from the model of uniform motion $s = vt$. After that the relative uncertainty is the uncertainty divided by the time.

          Formula

          $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$ and

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{\Delta t}{t}$
          $= \dfrac{\Delta t\, v}{s}$

          Conversions

          The distance is in metres and the speed in $\text{m/s}$, so the time comes out in seconds. The quotient is multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage.

          Substitution

          $t$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{m}}{1.5\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= 2.0\,\text{s}$

          so

          $\varepsilon$$= \dfrac{0.20\,\text{s}}{2.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.10$
          $= 10\,\%$

          Answer

          $10\,\%$

          Two significant figures like the distance and the speed.

          Reasonableness

          In a two-second measurement, a reaction-time error of $0.20\,\text{s}$ is a tenth of the total time. The result is twice the $5\,\%$ that a long measurement would reach, which is why the short distance is a poor choice.

        3. In a lab record the position of a car is $90\,\text{cm}$ when $2.0\,\text{s}$ has passed, and $342\,\text{cm}$ when $8.0\,\text{s}$ has passed. Assume the motion is uniform.

          What is the speed of the car? Answer in m/s.

          m/s
          Answer

          0.42 m/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          points $(t_1, s_1) = (2.0\,\text{s}, 90\,\text{cm})$ and $(t_2, s_2) = (8.0\,\text{s}, 342\,\text{cm})$

          Find

          the speed $v$ in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          The motion is uniform, so the distance changes steadily with time, and the speed is the ratio of the change in distance to the change in time, that is, the slope between two points.

          Formula

          $v$$= \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$
          $= \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

          Conversions

          Convert the distances to metres: $90\,\text{cm} = 0.90\,\text{m}$ and $342\,\text{cm} = 3.42\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \dfrac{3.42\,\text{m} - 0.90\,\text{m}}{8.0\,\text{s} - 2.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= \dfrac{2.52\,\text{m}}{6.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 0.42\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $0.42\,\text{m/s}$

          Two significant figures, because the time difference $6.0\,\text{s}$ is given with two figures.

          Reasonableness

          Between the points $6.0\,\text{s}$ has passed and the distance has grown by just under three metres, so the speed is about $\tfrac{2.5\,\text{m}}{6\,\text{s}} \approx 0.4\,\text{m/s}$. The line crosses the distance axis at

          $s_0$$= 0.90\,\text{m} - 0.42\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.0\,\text{s}$
          $\approx 0.06\,\text{m}$

          so it passes almost through the origin.

        4. According to the hypothesis the speed of the car is $0.50\,\text{m/s}$, but in the experiment the speed is measured as $0.42\,\text{m/s}$.

          By how many percent does the measured value differ from the value predicted by the hypothesis?

          %
          Answer

          16 %

          Show the solution

          Given

          speed predicted by the hypothesis $v_\text{pred} = 0.50\,\text{m/s}$, measured speed $v_\text{meas} = 0.42\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the deviation of the measured value from the predicted value as a percentage

          Principle

          A hypothesis is tested by comparing the measurement with its prediction. The deviation is expressed relative to the prediction, so that the difference can be compared with the uncertainty of the measurement regardless of the unit.

          Formula

          $\delta$$= \dfrac{\left| v_\text{pred} - v_\text{meas} \right|}{v_\text{pred}}$

          Conversions

          Both speeds are in $\text{m/s}$, so the units cancel. The quotient is multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage.

          Substitution

          $\delta$$= \dfrac{\left| 0.50\,\text{m/s} - 0.42\,\text{m/s} \right|}{0.50\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= \dfrac{0.08}{0.50}$
          $= 0.16$
          $= 16\,\%$

          Answer

          $16\,\%$

          Two significant figures like the speeds.

          Reasonableness

          The measured speed is below the prediction, and the difference $0.08\,\text{m/s}$ is about a sixth of the prediction ($\tfrac{1}{6} \approx 17\,\%$). If the uncertainty of the speed were only a few per cent, the difference would be too large to be chance, and the hypothesis should be rejected or checked.

        1. A student fits two lines to the measurement points (distance as a function of time): the steepest, whose slope is $0.463\,\text{m/s}$, and the shallowest, whose slope is $0.397\,\text{m/s}$. The measurement points fit between these lines. Half of the difference of the slopes of the lines is taken as the uncertainty.

          How large is the uncertainty of the speed?

          m/s
          Answer

          0.033 m/s

          Show the solution

          Given

          slope of the steepest line $v_\text{max} = 0.463\,\text{m/s}$, of the shallowest $v_\text{min} = 0.397\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the uncertainty $\Delta v$ of the speed in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          All lines that fit the data points within their uncertainties are plausible, so the speed is probably between the slopes of the steepest and the shallowest line. The best estimate is their mean, and the uncertainty is half of the difference.

          Formula

          $v$$= \dfrac{v_\text{max} + v_\text{min}}{2}$

          and

          $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{v_\text{max} - v_\text{min}}{2}$

          Conversions

          The slopes are already in the same unit, $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{0.463\,\text{m/s} - 0.397\,\text{m/s}}{2}$
          $= \dfrac{0.066\,\text{m/s}}{2}$
          $= 0.033\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $0.033\,\text{m/s}$

          (calculated value; the difference $0.066\,\text{m/s}$ is a two-figure number). As a stated uncertainty it is rounded to one significant figure, so the speed is $v = 0.43\,\text{m/s} \pm 0.03\,\text{m/s}$. The answer field accepts both.

          Reasonableness

          The uncertainty is about $\tfrac{0.033}{0.430} \approx 8\,\%$ of the speed, which suits motion measured with a hand stopwatch. If the hypothesis predicted $0.50\,\text{m/s}$, it would be more than two uncertainties above the measured value: the interval is $0.397$–$0.463\,\text{m/s}$, and the prediction does not fit in it.

        2. Hot water cools in a cup. The temperature is $80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $t = 0\,\text{min}$, $68.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $5.0\,\text{min}$, $52.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $15.0\,\text{min}$ and $47.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $20.0\,\text{min}$. The student's hypothesis is that the temperature falls steadily.

          How much greater is the rate of temperature fall during the first five minutes than during the last five minutes? Answer in K/min.

          K/min
          Answer

          1.4 K/min

          Show the solution

          Given

          temperatures $80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at time $0\,\text{min}$, $68.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $5.0\,\text{min}$, $52.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $15.0\,\text{min}$ and $47.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at $20.0\,\text{min}$

          Find

          the difference between the rates of fall during the first and the last five minutes in $\text{K/min}$

          Principle

          The hypothesis "the temperature falls steadily" predicts the same rate of fall everywhere. So calculate the rate of fall for two periods and compare them. A temperature change is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius.

          Formula

          $r$$= \dfrac{T_\text{start} - T_\text{end}}{\Delta t}$

          and $\Delta r = r_1 - r_2$

          Conversions

          A fall in temperature in degrees Celsius equals the fall in kelvins, so a change of $1\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is $1\,\text{K}$ and no conversion is needed.

          Substitution

          $r_1$$= \dfrac{80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 68.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
          $= \dfrac{12.0\,\text{K}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
          $= 2.4\,\text{K/min}$

          and

          $r_2$$= \dfrac{52.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 47.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
          $= \dfrac{5.0\,\text{K}}{5.0\,\text{min}}$
          $= 1.0\,\text{K/min}$

          so

          $\Delta r$$= 2.4\,\text{K/min} - 1.0\,\text{K/min}$
          $= 1.4\,\text{K/min}$

          Answer

          $1.4\,\text{K/min}$

          Two significant figures, because the temperature differences are $12.0\,\text{K}$ and $5.0\,\text{K}$.

          Reasonableness

          The first rate of fall is more than twice the last one, and this difference cannot come from a reading error of $0.1\,\text{K}$ alone. So the hypothesis of a steady fall does not hold: the temperature falls more slowly the closer the water is to room temperature.

        3. 60 minutes have been set aside for an investigation. Assembling and testing the equipment takes 12 minutes, and the measurement is made at six different distances. Each measurement repeat takes 2.0 minutes, including returning to the start and recording.

          How many repeats can be done for each distance, when the number of repeats is the same at every distance?

          toistoa
          Answer

          4.0 toistoa

          Show the solution

          Given

          total time $60\,\text{min}$, preparation $12\,\text{min}$, number of distances $N = 6$, one repeat $2.0\,\text{min}$

          Find

          the number of repeats $n$ for each distance

          Principle

          The time available for measuring is the total time minus the preparation. It is divided equally among all the repeats: there are $n$ repeats at each of the $N$ distances, and each repeat takes the same time.

          Formula

          $t_\text{tot} - t_\text{prep} = N\, n\, t_\text{rep}$, so

          $n$$= \dfrac{t_\text{tot} - t_\text{prep}}{N\, t_\text{rep}}$

          Conversions

          All times are in minutes, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $n$$= \dfrac{60\,\text{min} - 12\,\text{min}}{6 \cdot 2.0\,\text{min}}$
          $= \dfrac{48\,\text{min}}{12\,\text{min}}$
          $= 4.0$

          Answer

          $4.0$

          Repeats, two significant figures like the duration of a repeat. The number of repeats must be a whole number, and here the result is exactly four.

          Reasonableness

          Four repeats at six distances make 24 measurements, and $24 \cdot 2.0\,\text{min} = 48\,\text{min}$ plus the preparation of $12\,\text{min}$ gives $60\,\text{min}$. No time is wasted and there is no overrun.
