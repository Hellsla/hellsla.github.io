Pressure and hydrostatic pressure

Why is the wall of a dam much thicker at the bottom than at the top, even though the water pressing on it is the same water all the way down?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate pressure from force and area and explain how a force is spread over a surface.
  • calculate the pressure of a liquid at different depths.
  • interpret the constant term and the slope of a graph of pressure against depth.

Prior knowledge

  • Weight is a force, $G = mg$, and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$chapter Force as a measure of interaction and weight G = mg
  • Pressure is a state variable that arises in the micro model from particles colliding with the wallchapter Thermodynamic system, state variables and temperature

1Think about it

You dive towards the bottom of a swimming-pool diving pool. Already at a depth of a couple of metres you feel pressure in your ears, and the lower you go, the more clearly you feel it. At the surface your ears feel nothing, even though the atmosphere presses on them too. The same thing loads the wall of a dam and your eardrum: the water above.

How does pressure change as you go deeper in a liquid, and what does it depend on?

Optional Review: the weight of water
A cubic metre of water (density $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$) has a mass of $1000\,\text{kg}$. What is the weight $G = mg$ of this amount of water?

  • The kilogram is the unit of mass. Weight is a force, and the unit of force is the newton: $G = mg = 9810\,\text{N}$.

  • You mixed up mass and weight. Weight is a force, and you get it by multiplying the mass by the acceleration of free fall: $G = mg = 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 9810\,\text{N}$.

  • You divided the mass by the acceleration of free fall. Weight is mass times $g$: every kilogram weighs 9.81 N at the Earth's surface, so $1000\,\text{kg}$ weighs 9810 N.

  • Correct: $G = mg = 1000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 9810\,\text{N} \approx 9.8\,\text{kN}$. You need this weight of a column of water in this lesson.

In the simulation a pressure gauge is lowered into a liquid.

Simulation

How to use

Adjust the depth and the density and follow the reading of the gauge and the $(h, p)$ graph. The box Show gauge pressure only removes atmospheric pressure from the graph.

Assumptions of the model

Atmospheric pressure at the surface is a fixed 101 kPa, and the liquid is at rest and equally dense everywhere (its compression is ignored). The readings are values from a model, not measurements from a real lake.

How the calculation works

Pressure grows steadily with depth: $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, so every metre adds $\rho g \cdot 1\,\text{m}$ of pressure. The slope of the line, $\rho g$, depends on the density, but at the surface ($h = 0$) the pressure is always the atmospheric pressure $p_0$.

Show the solution

Given

water, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$ constant; measurements at two depths with $\Delta h = 10.0\,\text{m} - 5.0\,\text{m} = 5.0\,\text{m}$ between them: at the depth $5.0\,\text{m}$ the total pressure is $p_1 = 150.1\,\text{kPa}$ and at the depth $10.0\,\text{m}$ the pressure is $p_2 = 199.1\,\text{kPa}$

Find

the increase in pressure per metre of depth, $\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$, in $\text{kPa/m}$

Principle

In a liquid $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, and because only the depth changes ($\rho$ and $p_0$ stay the same), the pressure grows steadily: the increase per metre is always the same number $\rho g$. Atmospheric pressure $p_0$ does not change with depth, so it cancels when we take the difference between the pressures at two depths.

Formula

$p_2 - p_1 = \rho g (h_2 - h_1)$, so

$$\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta h} = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{h_2 - h_1} \;(= \rho g)$$

Conversions

The pressures are already in kilopascals and the depths in metres, so the result comes directly in $\text{kPa/m}$ and no conversions are needed. For comparison,

$\rho g$$= 1000 \cdot 9.81\,\text{Pa/m}$
$= 9.81\,\text{kPa/m}$

Substitution

$\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$$= \dfrac{199.1\,\text{kPa} - 150.1\,\text{kPa}}{10.0\,\text{m} - 5.0\,\text{m}}$
$= \dfrac{49.0\,\text{kPa}}{5.0\,\text{m}}$
$= 9.80\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}}$

Answer

$9.8\,\text{kPa/m}$

(from your readings), two significant figures, because the depth difference $5.0\,\text{m}$ is known to two figures, although the pressure difference $49.0\,\text{kPa}$ has three. The value of the model is $\rho g = 9.81\,\text{kPa/m}$.

Reasonableness

The increase is about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, which fits the fact that $10\,\text{m}$ of water adds about one atmosphere ($101\,\text{kPa}$). Pressure grows in the same proportion as depth only for the gauge pressure: $5\,\text{m}$ gives $49\,\text{kPa}$ and $10\,\text{m}$ gives $98\,\text{kPa}$, which is double. The total pressure does not double ($150 \to 199\,\text{kPa}$), because $101\,\text{kPa}$ is included in both.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    When a force is spread over a surface, its effect depends on how large an area it is spread over. The sharp tip of a drawing pin sinks into a wall with the same force that leaves no mark when applied to the blunt head, and skis carry you on deep snow although boots sink in. The quantity that describes this is pressure: force divided by the area over which the force is spread. The unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), and 1 Pa = 1 N/m². The pascal is a small unit, so in everyday life we use the kilopascal: 1 kPa = 1000 Pa.

    Where does pressure come from?

    According to the micro model, the pressure of a gas comes from particles colliding with the walls of the container. The pressure of a liquid comes from the weight of the liquid itself: above every layer of water there is more water pressing down on it. The deeper you go, the more water there is above you and the greater the pressure. In a liquid at rest, a horizontal surface of area $A$ below the surface carries the weight of the column of water above it. The height of the column is the depth $h$ and its volume is $V = Ah$. Density $\rho$ is mass per volume ($m = \rho V$), so the mass of the column is $\rho A h$ and its weight is $G = mg = \rho A h g$. Pressure is weight divided by area, so the area cancels and what is left is $\rho g h$. The pressure produced by the weight of a liquid is called hydrostatic pressure. It does not depend on the shape of the container or on the total amount of water, only on the depth and the density of the liquid.

    Pressure is not a force but the ratio of force to area, and in a liquid at rest the pressure acts equally in every direction. If the pressure sideways were smaller than downwards, the water would flow sideways and would not stay at rest. If you make holes at several heights in the side of a bottle, the water squirts out hardest from the lowest hole, because the pressure grows with depth. The force caused by pressure is always perpendicular to the surface: on the vertical wall of a dam the force points horizontally, and on a diver's body it acts from all sides. That is why here we can treat the force as just a magnitude; the direction comes from the surface.

    Atmospheric pressure and gauge pressure

    Atmospheric pressure, the pressure of the atmosphere, already acts on the surface of the water. At sea level it is about 101 kPa. It is the weight of the column of air per unit area. Atmospheric pressure also acts on the surface of the water and is transmitted through the water to depth. Under water the total pressure is therefore the sum of atmospheric pressure and hydrostatic pressure. At a depth of ten metres the hydrostatic pressure is already about the same as one atmosphere.

    This answers the opening question. Atmospheric pressure acts on both sides of the dam wall, so the wall is loaded only by the gauge pressure of the water, that is, the part of the pressure that is greater than atmospheric pressure. The gauge pressure is zero at the top and grows steadily with depth: at a depth of 30 metres it is about 290 kPa, so every square metre of the wall feels a force of about 290 kN. The lower part of the wall must therefore withstand a much greater force than the upper part, and the wall is built thick at the bottom.

    Pressure and hydrostatic pressure

    Pressure is the force perpendicular to a surface divided by the area:

    $$p = \frac{F}{A}$$

    where $p$ is the pressure (Pa), $F$ the force perpendicular to the surface (N) and $A$ the area (m²). One pascal is a force of one newton on an area of one square metre: 1 Pa = 1 N/m².

    In a liquid at depth $h$ the pressure is

    $$p = p_0 + \rho g h$$

    where $p_0$ is the pressure at the surface of the liquid, usually atmospheric pressure (Pa), $\rho$ the density of the liquid (kg/m³), $g$ = $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $h$ the depth below the surface (m). The term $\rho g h$ is the hydrostatic pressure, that is, the gauge pressure $\Delta p = p - p_0$, which tells how much greater the pressure is than the pressure at the surface. The graph $(h, p)$ is a straight line whose slope is $\rho g$ and whose constant term is $p_0$, as in the linear model $y = kx + b$ of FY1. For water $\rho$ = $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, so the pressure grows by 9.81 kPa for every metre.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the pressure at the bottom depends on how much water the container holds. In a narrow tube and a wide pool, where the water reaches the same height, the pressure at the bottom is the same, even though the pool holds a thousand times as much water. Pressure depends only on the depth, the density and the pressure at the surface. Another mistake is to confuse pressure and force: the sharp tip of a drawing pin does not produce a greater force than the head, but spreads the same force over a smaller area, so the pressure is greater. A third mistake is to forget atmospheric pressure: $\rho g h$ alone gives only the gauge pressure, and the total pressure under water is always greater than that.

    Summary

    • Pressure is force per unit area: $p = F/A$. The unit is the pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m², and 1 kPa = 1000 Pa.
    • In a liquid the pressure grows with depth: $p = p_0 + \rho g h$. The term $\rho g h$ is the hydrostatic pressure, and it depends only on the depth and the density, not on the shape of the container.
    • The force caused by pressure is perpendicular to the surface and acts in every direction in a liquid.
    • Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101 kPa. In water the pressure grows by about one atmosphere for every ten metres.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A diver is in a lake at a depth of $12.0\,\text{m}$, next to the window of an underwater observation room. Atmospheric pressure at the surface is 101 kPa and the density of water is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$. What is the pressure around the diver, and how large is the force on a window of area 150 cm² when the room contains air at normal pressure?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Depth $h = 12.0\,\text{m}$, atmospheric pressure $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$, the density of water from a table or from the problem $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and window area $A = 150\,\text{cm}^2$. We want the total pressure $p$ around the diver and the force $F$ on the window.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The water is at rest and its density is constant, so the pressure grows steadily with depth: the total pressure is the atmospheric pressure at the surface plus the hydrostatic pressure of the water $\rho g h$. Only the gauge pressure $\rho g h$ acts on the window, because the atmospheric pressure in the room pushes the window from inside just as much as the atmospheric pressure at the surface pushes from outside. The force is pressure times area, because pressure is force per unit area.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. Both unknowns are already alone on the left-hand side:

      $$\begin{aligned} p &= p_0 + \rho g h \\ F &= \rho g h \cdot A \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Atmospheric pressure in pascals is $101\,\text{kPa} = 101\,000\,\text{Pa}$. The area of the window in square metres is

      $150\,\text{cm}^2$$= 150 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$
      $= 0.015\,\text{m}^2$

      because $1\,\text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$. The other quantities are already in SI units.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. We calculate the hydrostatic pressure, the total pressure and the force one after another:

      $$\begin{aligned} &\rho g h \\ &= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \\ &\quad \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \\ &\quad \cdot 12.0\,\text{m} \\ &= 117\,720\,\text{Pa} \\[6pt] p &= p_0 + \rho g h \\ &= 101\,000\,\text{Pa} \\ &\quad + 117\,720\,\text{Pa} \\ &= 218\,720\,\text{Pa} \\[6pt] F &= \rho g h \cdot A \\ &= 117\,720\,\text{Pa} \\ &\quad \cdot 0.015\,\text{m}^2 \\ &= 1765.8\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The total pressure is $p \approx 219\,\text{kPa}$ and the force on the window is $F \approx 1.8\,\text{kN}$. The pressure is given with three significant figures, because the depth $12.0\,\text{m}$ and the atmospheric pressure $101\,\text{kPa}$ are given with three figures. The force is given with two, because the area $150\,\text{cm}^2$ is given with two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The total pressure is about 2.2 times atmospheric pressure, which fits the fact that 10 metres of water add about one atmosphere and 12 metres add a little more. Estimate:

      $10\,\text{kPa/m} \cdot 12\,\text{m} + 101\,\text{kPa}$
      $\approx 220\,\text{kPa}$

      The force equals the weight of a mass of about $180\,\text{kg}$, although the window is small. The pressure does not crush the diver's body, because in water the same pressure acts on the body from all sides and the body fluids transmit it to the inside as well. Only the pressure in the body's air spaces, such as the ears, has to be equalised.

      • Multiplying by the area would give a force, $F = pA$, not a pressure. The pressure $\rho g h$ does not depend on the area. Check where the depth $h$ is measured from.

      The $(h, p)$ graph of an unknown liquid is a straight line through the points ($0\,\text{m}$, 101 kPa) and ($8.0\,\text{m}$, 171 kPa).

      What is the density of the liquid? Give the answer in kg/m³.

      kg/m³
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What does the slope of the graph tell you when $p = p_0 + \rho g h$?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The slope is $\rho g = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$, so $\rho = \dfrac{\Delta p}{g\,\Delta h}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h}$$= \dfrac{171\,\text{kPa} - 101\,\text{kPa}}{8.0\,\text{m}}$

      Show the solution

      Given

      two points of the line $(h, p)$: $(0\,\text{m},\ 101\,\text{kPa})$ and $(8.0\,\text{m},\ 171\,\text{kPa})$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the density of the liquid $\rho$ in $\text{kg/m}^3$

      Principle

      In a liquid $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, so the $(h, p)$ graph is a straight line whose constant term is the pressure at the surface $p_0$ and whose slope is $\rho g$. The density therefore comes from the slope, when the density of the liquid is constant at all depths. The constant term does not affect the density.

      Reading the graph

      From the point $h = 0$ we read $p_0 = 101\,\text{kPa}$ (atmospheric pressure at the surface) and from the point $h = 8.0\,\text{m}$ the pressure $171\,\text{kPa}$. The slope is the change in pressure divided by the change in depth:

      $\Delta p$$= 171\,\text{kPa} - 101\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 70\,\text{kPa}$

      and $\Delta h = 8.0\,\text{m}$.

      Formula

      slope $\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta h} = \rho g$, so

      $$\rho = \frac{\Delta p}{g\,\Delta h}$$

      Conversions

      $\Delta p$$= 70\,\text{kPa}$
      $= 70\,000\,\text{Pa}$

      so that the result comes in $\text{kg/m}^3$. At the same time the slope is

      $\dfrac{70\,000\,\text{Pa}}{8.0\,\text{m}}$$= 8750\,\text{Pa/m}$

      Substitution

      $\rho$$= \dfrac{70\,000\,\text{Pa}}{9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 8.0\,\text{m}}$
      $= \dfrac{70\,000\,\text{Pa}}{78.48\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
      $= 891.9\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}$

      Answer

      $890\,\text{kg/m}^3$

      Two significant figures, because the pressure difference $\Delta p = 70\,\text{kPa}$ and the depth difference $\Delta h = 8.0\,\text{m}$ are given to two figures.

      Reasonableness

      The slope for water would be $9.81\,\text{kPa/m}$, so $8.0\,\text{m}$ would give $78.5\,\text{kPa}$. The measured increase of $70\,\text{kPa}$ is slightly smaller, so the liquid is lighter than water, for example oil. Checking backwards, $890 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 8.0\,\text{Pa} \approx 70\,000\,\text{Pa}$.

      Optional 4. Explain and transfer

      A diver is at a depth of $3.0\,\text{m}$ and feels pressure in an ear. When the diver equalises the pressure by letting air from the throat into the middle ear, the feeling goes away. Use pressure to explain why a force acts on the eardrum at first and why it disappears when the pressure is equalised. Use the concepts pressure, gauge pressure and force.

      Model answer

      At the surface there is atmospheric pressure on both sides of the eardrum, so the forces cancel. At a depth of three metres the outside pressure is greater than atmospheric pressure by the gauge pressure $\rho g h = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m} \approx 29\,\text{kPa}$, but the air in the middle ear is still at atmospheric pressure. The pressure difference pushes the membrane inwards, perpendicular to its surface, with a force $F = \Delta p \cdot A$; if the area of the membrane is about $0.6\,\text{cm}^2$ (an approximation), the force is about 1.8 N. When the diver lets more air into the middle ear, its pressure rises to the same size as the outside pressure. Then the pressure on the two sides of the membrane is the same, the forces cancel and the feeling goes away.

        1. A drawing pin is pushed into a wall with a finger. Why does the sharp tip sink into the wall, although the same force acts on the tip and on the head?

        1. Core

          A skier's weight is 720 N. It is spread evenly over the bases of the skis, whose total area is 0.30 m².

          How large is the pressure that the skis exert on the snow?

          Pa
          Answer

          2400 Pa

          Show the solution

          Given

          the skier's weight, that is, the force on the snow, $F = 720\,\text{N}$, total area of the skis $A = 0.30\,\text{m}^2$

          Find

          the pressure $p$ on the snow in pascals

          Principle

          Pressure is the ratio of the force acting perpendicular to a surface to the area of that surface. The weight is spread evenly over the bases of the skis, so the whole force $720\,\text{N}$ is spread over the whole area $0.30\,\text{m}^2$.

          Formula

          $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed, because the force is in newtons and the area in square metres: $1\,\text{N}/\text{m}^2 = 1\,\text{Pa}$.

          Substitution

          $p$$= \dfrac{720\,\text{N}}{0.30\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= 2400\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2}$
          $= 2400\,\text{Pa}$

          Answer

          $2400\,\text{Pa}$

          Two significant figures like the area $0.30\,\text{m}^2$ (the zeros are only place holders).

          Reasonableness

          Estimate: $700\,\text{N} / 0.3\,\text{m}^2 \approx 2300\,\text{Pa}$. If the same person stood in boots whose soles have about a tenth of the area of the skis, the pressure would be ten times greater, about $24\,\text{kPa}$. That is why skis carry you on deep snow although boots sink in. The result is only about $2\,\%$ of atmospheric pressure ($101\,\text{kPa}$).

        2. Core

          The deepest point of Lake Päijänne is at a depth of about $95\,\text{m}$.

          How large is the hydrostatic pressure there? The density of water is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$. Give the answer in kilopascals.

          kPa
          Answer

          930 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          depth $h = 95\,\text{m}$, density of water $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the hydrostatic pressure $\Delta p$ in kilopascals

          Principle

          In a liquid at rest the pressure grows with depth, because deeper down there is more liquid above. Water is practically incompressible, so $\rho$ is constant at all depths and the pressure grows in direct proportion to the depth. The hydrostatic pressure asked for is only the part caused by the water; atmospheric pressure is not included.

          Formula

          $\Delta p = \rho g h$; the unknown $\Delta p$ is already alone on the left-hand side

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so the result comes in pascals. At the end $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.

          Substitution

          $\Delta p$$= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 95\,\text{m}$
          $= 931\,950\,\text{Pa}$
          $= 931.95\,\text{kPa}$

          Answer

          $930\,\text{kPa}$

          Two significant figures like the depth $95\,\text{m}$.

          Reasonableness

          Water adds about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, so $95\,\text{m}$ gives about $950\,\text{kPa}$. The exact value is slightly smaller, because $g < 10\,\text{m/s}^2$. The result is about $930/101 \approx 9.2$ atmospheres.

        3. A pressure gauge has been installed on the bottom of a lake at a depth of $18.0\,\text{m}$. During a low-pressure weather system the atmospheric pressure at the surface of the lake is 98.5 kPa.

          What is the total pressure at the gauge? The density of water is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

          kPa
          Answer

          275 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          depth $h = 18.0\,\text{m}$, atmospheric pressure at the surface $p_0 = 98.5\,\text{kPa}$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the total pressure $p$ at the gauge in kilopascals

          Principle

          The gauge feels both the weight of the water and the weight of the air above the water surface. The surface of the liquid transmits atmospheric pressure to the whole liquid unchanged, and the hydrostatic pressure $\rho g h$ is added on top of it, so the pressures are added.

          Formula

          $p = p_0 + \rho g h$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side

          Conversions

          Atmospheric pressure in pascals: $98.5\,\text{kPa} = 98\,500\,\text{Pa}$. At the end the result is converted back: $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.

          Substitution

          $\rho g h$$= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 18.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 176\,580\,\text{Pa}$
          $p$$= 98\,500\,\text{Pa} + 176\,580\,\text{Pa}$
          $= 275\,080\,\text{Pa}$
          $= 275.08\,\text{kPa}$

          Answer

          $275\,\text{kPa}$

          Three significant figures like the depth $18.0\,\text{m}$ and the atmospheric pressure $98.5\,\text{kPa}$.

          Reasonableness

          Estimate: $18\,\text{m}$ of water is about $1.8$ atmospheres, that is, about $180\,\text{kPa}$, and atmospheric pressure of about $100\,\text{kPa}$ is added to it, giving about $280\,\text{kPa}$ in all. The result is greater than the hydrostatic pressure alone, $177\,\text{kPa}$, and almost three atmospheres.

          300 kPa

          Principle

          Pressure is the ratio of force to area. The stove stands still, so the force on the floor is equal to the weight of the stove, and it is transmitted to the floor only through the small area of the legs.

          The force is in newtons and the area in square metres, so the result comes in pascals. At the end $1\,\text{kPa} = 1000\,\text{Pa}$.

          $300\,\text{kPa}$

          Two significant figures like the area $0.0025\,\text{m}^2$ (the zeros are place holders).

          The area of one leg is $0.0025\,\text{m}^2 / 4 \approx 6\,\text{cm}^2$, for example $2.5\,\text{cm} \cdot 2.5\,\text{cm}$, which is a believable size for a leg.         1. Core

          A person's weight is 600 N. At the start of a step the person presses on a parquet floor for a moment with only the heel of a high-heeled shoe, whose contact area with the floor is 1.5 cm².

          How large is the pressure that the heel exerts on the floor? Give the answer in megapascals (MPa); the prefix mega means a million.

          MPa
          Answer

          4.0 MPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          force $F = 600\,\text{N}$, contact area of the heel $A = 1.5\,\text{cm}^2$

          Find

          the pressure $p$ in megapascals

          Principle

          Pressure is the ratio of force to area. At the start of a step the whole weight $600\,\text{N}$ is transmitted to the floor through the small area of the heel, so the force is spread only over the area $1.5\,\text{cm}^2$.

          Formula

          $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$; the unknown $p$ is already alone on the left-hand side

          Conversions

          $1\,\text{cm}^2$$= (0.01\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$

          so $A = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$. At the end $1\,\text{MPa} = 10^6\,\text{Pa}$.

          Substitution

          $p$$= \dfrac{600\,\text{N}}{1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= 4\,000\,000\,\text{Pa}$
          $= 4.0\,\text{MPa}$

          Answer

          $4.0\,\text{MPa}$

          Two significant figures like the area $1.5\,\text{cm}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          The pressure is about $4.0\,\text{MPa} / 0.101\,\text{MPa} \approx 40$ atmospheres. A bare sole with an area of about $200\,\text{cm}^2$ would give only about $600\,\text{N} / 0.02\,\text{m}^2 = 30\,\text{kPa}$, so the pressure under the heel is more than a hundred times greater.         2. Core

          A house gets its water from a water tower. When the taps are closed, a pressure gauge at the tap in the cellar shows a gauge pressure of 350 kPa.

          How high above the tap is the water surface in the tower? The density of water is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

          m
          Answer

          35.7 m

          Show the solution

          Given

          gauge pressure $\Delta p = 350\,\text{kPa}$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the height $h$ of the water surface above the tap in metres

          Principle

          When the taps are closed, the water is at rest, so the gauge pressure at the tap is the hydrostatic pressure of the column of water, $\Delta p = \rho g h$. The water between the tower and the tap is continuous, so only the difference in height matters, not the length of the pipes.

          Formula

          $\Delta p = \rho g h$, so $h = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\rho g}$

          Conversions

          $350\,\text{kPa} = 350\,000\,\text{Pa}$, so that the units $\text{Pa} = \text{kg}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^2)$ cancel to metres.

          Substitution

          $h$$= \dfrac{350\,000\,\text{Pa}}{1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}}$
          $= 35.678\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $35.7\,\text{m}$

          Three significant figures: the gauge pressure $350\,\text{kPa} = 3.50\cdot10^{2}\,\text{kPa}$ and the density $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$ are given here to three figures.

          Reasonableness

          Water gives about $10\,\text{kPa}$ per metre, so $350\,\text{kPa}$ corresponds to about $35\,\text{m}$. The exact value is slightly greater, because $g < 10\,\text{m/s}^2$. Checking backwards,

          $\rho g h$$= 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 35.7\,\text{Pa}$
          $\approx 350\,\text{kPa}$

          Water towers are about this tall.

        3. The graph shows the pressures of two liquids, A and B, as a function of depth. At the surface the pressure is the same in both.

          At what depth in liquid A is the pressure equal to the pressure in liquid B at a depth of $4.0\,\text{m}$?

          m
          Answer

          6.0 m

          Show the solution

          Given

          from the graph, in both liquids $p_0 = 100\,\text{kPa}$; line A passes through the point $(10\,\text{m}$; $200\,\text{kPa})$ and line B through the point $(10\,\text{m}$; $250\,\text{kPa})$; $h_\text{B} = 4.0\,\text{m}$

          Find

          the depth $h_\text{A}$ at which the pressure in liquid A equals the pressure in liquid B at depth $h_\text{B}$

          Principle

          In a liquid $p = p_0 + \rho g h$, so the $(h, p)$ graph is a straight line whose slope $k = \rho g$ is greater for the denser liquid. The densities do not have to be calculated; the slopes are read from the graph.

          Reading the graph

          The lines start from the same point $(0\,\text{m}$; $100\,\text{kPa})$ and end at crossings of the grid. So

          $k_\text{A}$$= \dfrac{(200 - 100)\,\text{kPa}}{10\,\text{m}}$
          $= 10\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}}$

          and

          $k_\text{B}$$= \dfrac{(250 - 100)\,\text{kPa}}{10\,\text{m}}$
          $= 15\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}}$

          Formula

          $p_0 + k_\text{A} h_\text{A} = p_0 + k_\text{B} h_\text{B}$, so

          $h_\text{A}$$= \dfrac{k_\text{B}}{k_\text{A}}\, h_\text{B}$

          Conversions

          No conversions are needed: the kilopascals cancel in the ratio $k_\text{B}/k_\text{A}$.

          Substitution

          $h_\text{A}$$= \dfrac{15\,\text{kPa/m}}{10\,\text{kPa/m}} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 6.0\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $h_\text{A} = 6.0\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the depth $4.0\,\text{m}$. A value read by eye in the range $5.8$–$6.2\,\text{m}$ is accepted.

          Reasonableness

          Liquid A is less dense, so a greater depth than $4.0\,\text{m}$ is needed to reach the same pressure. Check:

          $100\,\text{kPa} + 10\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m}$
          $= 160\,\text{kPa}$
          $= 100\,\text{kPa} + 15\,\tfrac{\text{kPa}}{\text{m}} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m}$

        4. Inside a pressure cooker there is a gauge pressure of 0.85 bar (1 bar = 100 kPa). The area of the lid is 380 cm².

          How large a force does the gauge pressure exert on the lid? Give the answer in kilonewtons.

          kN
          Answer

          3.2 kN

          Show the solution

          Given

          gauge pressure $\Delta p = 0.85\,\text{bar}$ (according to the problem $1\,\text{bar} = 100\,\text{kPa}$), area of the lid $A = 380\,\text{cm}^2$

          Find

          the force $F$ on the lid in kilonewtons

          Principle

          The gauge pressure pushes the lid from the inside at every point, and pressure is force divided by area. The total force is therefore the gauge pressure multiplied by the area of the lid. The pressure of the outside air is not part of this, because only the force caused by the gauge pressure is asked for.

          Formula

          $\Delta p = \dfrac{F}{A}$, so $F = \Delta p\,A$

          Conversions

          $0.85\,\text{bar}$$= 85\,\text{kPa}$
          $= 85\,000\,\text{Pa}$

          and

          $380\,\text{cm}^2$$= 380 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 0.0380\,\text{m}^2$

          At the end $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.

          $3.2\,\text{kN}$

          Two significant figures like the gauge pressure $0.85\,\text{bar}$.

        1. A hiker's mass with a rucksack is $85\,\text{kg}$. Soft snow supports the hiker if the pressure on it is at most 6.0 kPa. When walking, the hiker is for a moment on only one foot.

          How large must the area of one snowshoe be at least, so that the hiker does not sink?

          m²
          Answer

          0.14 m²

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass with the rucksack $m = 85\,\text{kg}$, greatest permitted pressure $p_\text{max} = 6.0\,\text{kPa}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; the whole weight on one foot

          Find

          the smallest area $A$ of one snowshoe in square metres

          Principle

          The hiker does not sink when the pressure on the snow is at most $p_\text{max}$. For a moment the whole weight is on one foot, so the force on this shoe is the whole weight $G = mg$, and the area is smallest when the pressure is exactly $p_\text{max}$.

          Formula

          $G = mg$ and $p_\text{max} = \dfrac{G}{A}$, so

          $A$$= \dfrac{G}{p_\text{max}}$
          $= \dfrac{mg}{p_\text{max}}$

          Conversions

          $6.0\,\text{kPa} = 6000\,\text{Pa}$. The mass is already in kilograms.

          Substitution

          $G$$= 85\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
          $= 833.85\,\text{N}$
          $A$$= \dfrac{833.85\,\text{N}}{6000\,\text{Pa}}$
          $= 0.13898\,\text{m}^2$

          Answer

          $0.14\,\text{m}^2$

          Two significant figures like the pressure $6.0\,\text{kPa}$ and the mass $85\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          Checking backwards, $833.85\,\text{N} / 0.14\,\text{m}^2 \approx 5960\,\text{Pa}$, which is less than $6000\,\text{Pa}$. The area is, for example, $25\,\text{cm} \cdot 56\,\text{cm}$, the size of an ordinary snowshoe. The area of the sole of an ordinary shoe is about $0.03\,\text{m}^2$, and with it the pressure would be about $28\,\text{kPa}$, so the shoe would sink.

        2. The rainwater barrel at a cottage is full of water, and its tight lid is against the water. The area of the lid is 0.25 m².           By how much does the force with which the water in the barrel presses on the lid increase? Give the answer in kilonewtons.

          kN
          Answer

          9.8 kN

          Show the solution

          Given

          height of the hose above the lid $h = 4.0\,\text{m}$, area of the lid $A = 0.25\,\text{m}^2$, $\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; amount of water in the hose $0.40\,\textsf{l}$

          Find

          the increase $\Delta F$ in the force on the lid in kilonewtons

          Principle

          The water in the barrel and the water in the hose are one liquid at rest, so at the lid the pressure grows by the hydrostatic pressure $\rho g h$ of the column of water in the hose.           Formula

          $\Delta p = \rho g h$ and $\Delta F = \Delta p \, A$, so $\Delta F = \rho g h A$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units. At the end $1\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$.

          $\Delta p$$= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m}$

          $\Delta F$$= 39\,240\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.25\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 9810\,\text{N}$
          $= 9.81\,\text{kN}$

          Answer

          $9.8\,\text{kN}$

          Two significant figures like the height $4.0\,\text{m}$ and the area $0.25\,\text{m}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          The $0.40\,\textsf{l}$ of water in the hose weighs only $0.40\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 3.9\,\text{N}$, but almost $10\,\text{kN}$ acts on the lid. The ratio is $9810 / 3.9 \approx 2500$, which is the same as the ratio of the cross-sectional areas of the lid and the hose ($1\,\text{cm}^2$). That is why a thin hose can break the barrel.

        3. At the bottom of an oil separator well at a petrol station there is a $1.60\,\text{m}$ layer of water and on top of it a $0.40\,\text{m}$ layer of oil with a density of $850\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

          How large is the gauge pressure at the bottom of the well? The density of water is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$. Give the answer in kilopascals.

          Answer

          19 kPa

          Show the solution

          Given

          layer of water $h_2 = 1.60\,\text{m}$, $\rho_2 = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$; on top of it a layer of oil $h_1 = 0.40\,\text{m}$, $\rho_1 = 850\,\text{kg/m}^3$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the gauge pressure $\Delta p$ at the bottom of the well in kilopascals

          Principle

          The liquids are at rest, and the bottom carries the whole column of liquid above it. Because the density changes at the interface, the hydrostatic pressure of each layer is calculated with its own density and height, and the pressures are added. Gauge pressure means that atmospheric pressure is not included.

          Formula

          the pressures of the layers are added, so $\Delta p = \rho_1 g h_1 + \rho_2 g h_2$

          $= 3335.4\,\text{Pa}$
          $\rho_2 g h_2$$= 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 1.60\,\text{m}$
          $= 15\,696\,\text{Pa}$
          $\Delta p$$= 3335.4\,\text{Pa} + 15\,696\,\text{Pa}$
          $= 19\,031.4\,\text{Pa}$

          Answer

          $19.0\,\text{kPa}$

          In a sum the decimals are determined by the least precise term: the $3.3\,\text{kPa}$ of the oil layer (thickness $0.40\,\text{m}$, two figures) is precise to one decimal place, so $3.3 + 15.7 = 19.0\,\text{kPa}$.

          Reasonableness

          If the whole $2.00\,\text{m}$ were water, the gauge pressure would be $1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 2.00 = 19\,620\,\text{Pa}$. Oil is lighter, so the result is smaller: the difference is $(1000 - 850) \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.40\,\text{Pa} = 588.6\,\text{Pa}$ and $19\,620 - 588.6 = 19\,031.4\,\text{Pa}$, as calculated.
