Motion as graphs

Why can you work out the distance between tram stops from the tram's velocity graph, even though the graph does not contain a single metre?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve problems on uniform and uniformly accelerated motion, substituting the units
  • draw and interpret $(t,x)$ and $(t,v)$ graphs from measurement data
  • assess the motions of two bodies and the reliability of measurement results with reasons

Prior knowledge

  • Displacement is the change in position, and its sign gives the directionchapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs
  • In uniform motion $x = x_0 + vt$, and the slope of the $(t,x)$ graph is the velocitychapter Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs
  • Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit of time, and the area of the $(t,v)$ graph is the displacementchapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion
  • Two bodies meet when their positions are equal at the same momentchapter Comparing the motions of two bodies

1Think about it

A tram leaves a stop, accelerates, runs at a steady speed for a while and brakes to the next stop. A passenger recorded the tram's velocity over the whole interval with a phone app. The app shows only the velocity as a function of time: the graph has seconds and metres per second, but not a single metre. In the matriculation exam a graph like this may be the only data of a problem.

How can you work out the distance between the stops from the velocity graph alone?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting level 1: a horizontal line

From $4\,\text{s}$ to $6\,\text{s}$ the $(t,x)$ graph of a cart is a horizontal line at the height $x = 8\,\text{m}$. What does the cart do during this time?

  • Velocity is the change in position per unit of time, not the position divided by the time. The change in position is zero, so the velocity is zero too. Review in the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs.

  • A $(t,x)$ graph is not a picture of the track. A horizontal line means that the position does not change, so the velocity is zero. Review in the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs.

  • Correct. The position stays the same, so $\Delta x = 0$ and the velocity $v = \Delta x : \Delta t = 0$. If needed, review in the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs.

Starting level 2: a lap of the track

A runner runs one lap of a $400\,\text{m}$ track and stops at the starting line after $80\,\text{s}$. Which statement is correct?

  • Correct. The final position is the same as the initial position, so $\Delta x = 0$ and the average velocity $\Delta x : \Delta t = 0$. The distance $400\,\text{m}$ is the length of the route. If needed, review in the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs.

  • $400\,\text{m}$ is the distance travelled. The displacement is the final position minus the initial position, and the runner ends up at the starting point. Review in the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs.

  • The displacement depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the route. They are the same, so the displacement is zero. Review in the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs.

Starting level 3: twice the time

At $t = 0$ a bus is at the position $200\,\text{m}$ and moves uniformly at $15\,\text{m/s}$. At $10\,\text{s}$ it is at the position $350\,\text{m}$. Where is it at $20\,\text{s}$?

  • $300\,\text{m}$ is the distance travelled. The position is found by adding it to the initial position $200\,\text{m}$. Review in the chapter Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs.

  • The position would double only if the bus started from the origin. What doubles is the distance travelled: $150\,\text{m}$ becomes $300\,\text{m}$. Review in the chapter Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs.

  • Correct. $x = x_0 + vt = 200\,\text{m} + 15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 500\,\text{m}$. The position is not proportional to the time when $x_0 \neq 0$. If needed, review in the chapter Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs.

Starting level 4: a negative acceleration

A car moves in the negative direction of the $x$ axis, and its acceleration is $-2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. What happens to the car's speed?

  • The acceleration is not zero, so the velocity changes by $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ every second. An acceleration in the same direction increases the speed. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • Correct. Both point in the negative direction, so the speed grows by $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ every second. If needed, review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • The sign gives the direction. Here the velocity and the acceleration point in the same direction, so the speed increases. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

Starting level 5: the highest point

A ball is thrown straight up, and air resistance is neglected.What is the ball's acceleration at the highest point, where it is at rest for an instant?

  • While rising the ball slows down, so the acceleration is opposite to the velocity, that is, downwards. It stays the same during the whole flight. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • The velocity is zero only for an instant. Just before the highest point the ball moves up and just after it down, so the velocity changes all the time. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • Correct. Gravity acts all the time, so the acceleration is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards at the highest point too. If needed, review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

Starting level 6: the area

What does the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis tell you about the motion of a body?

  • The area tells you how much the position changes. The final position is the initial position plus this displacement. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • The average velocity is found when the area, that is, the displacement, is divided by the time. The area itself is the displacement. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • Correct. Velocity times time is in metres, and the area adds these products up. The area below the time axis is a negative displacement. If needed, review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

  • The acceleration is the slope of the $(t,v)$ graph. The area is velocity times time, so it is in metres: the displacement. Review in the chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion.

A tram between stops

The graph shows the tram's velocity from one stop to the next. Read the moments and velocities you need from the graph, work out the distance between the stops from them and answer below. The model answer also gives the accelerations and the average velocity.

How long is the distance between the stops? Answer in metres.

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

Velocity times time is distance. Which region of the graph is velocity times time?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Divide the region under the graph into three parts: a triangle, a rectangle and a triangle.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

Accelerating: $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 10\,\text{s} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}$; steady part: $30\,\text{s} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}$; braking: $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 20\,\text{s} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s}$.

Show the solution

Given

from the graph: the velocity grows uniformly from zero to $v = 10\,\text{m/s}$ by $t_1 = 10\,\text{s}$, stays there until $t_2 = 40\,\text{s}$ and falls uniformly to zero at $t_3 = 60\,\text{s}$

Find

the distance between the stops $\Delta x$ in metres

Principle

The tram moves in the same direction all the time, so the distance is the same as the displacement. The displacement is the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis, because velocity times time is the distance travelled (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion).

Reading the graph

The corners of the graph are at the grid crossings $(0\,\text{s}$; $0)$, $(10\,\text{s}$; $10\,\text{m/s})$, $(40\,\text{s}$; $10\,\text{m/s})$ and $(60\,\text{s}$; $0)$. The region divides into a triangle, a rectangle and a triangle.

Formula

$\Delta x$$= \tfrac{1}{2}\,v\,t_1 + v\,(t_2 - t_1) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,v\,(t_3 - t_2)$

Conversions

The velocity is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the area comes out directly in metres. No conversions are needed.

Substitution

$\Delta x$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s} \cdot 10\,\text{s} + 10\,\text{m/s} \cdot 30\,\text{s} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 10\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s}$
$= 50\,\text{m} + 300\,\text{m} + 100\,\text{m}$
$= 450\,\text{m}$

Answer

$450\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures ($4.5 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{m}$), because the velocity $10\,\text{m/s}$ and the times read from the graph are known to about two figures.

Reasonableness

The acceleration is $10\,\text{m/s} : 10\,\text{s} = 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ while accelerating and $-10\,\text{m/s} : 20\,\text{s} = -0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$ while braking, usual values for a tram. The average velocity

$450\,\text{m} : 60\,\text{s}$$= 7.5\,\text{m/s}$
$\approx 27\,\text{km/h}$

is smaller than the greatest velocity $10\,\text{m/s}$, as it must be, and a distance of about half a kilometre between stops is usual in a city.

4Theory

Key idea

Motion is always described in a chosen coordinate system: a straight $x$ axis, an origin, a positive direction and a clock. The position $x$ tells you where a body is, the displacement $\Delta x$ the change in position and the velocity $v$ how fast and in which direction the position changes. The acceleration $a$ tells you how fast the velocity changes. The sign of the displacement, the velocity and the acceleration gives the direction. The distance is the length of the route, and it is never negative.

The same motion can be shown as three graphs: $(t,x)$, $(t,v)$ and $(t,a)$. You move between the graphs in two ways. Downwards: the slope of a graph is the value of the next quantity, so the slope of the $(t,x)$ graph is the velocity and the slope of the $(t,v)$ graph the acceleration. Upwards: the area between a graph and the time axis is the change in the previous quantity, so the area of the $(t,v)$ graph is the displacement.

A motion problem in the exam starts with choosing the coordinate system and identifying the type of motion: is the velocity constant or the acceleration constant? Only then do you write the equation.

This answers the opening question. The velocity tells you how many metres the tram travels in a second, so the product of velocity and time is in metres. The area under the $(t,v)$ graph is exactly this product added up over the whole trip, that is, the displacement. The tram in the Investigate task travelled $450\,\text{m}$ from one stop to the next.

Equations and graphs of motion

Uniform motion

The average velocity is the displacement divided by the time it took:

average velocity
$$\overline{v}_\text{k} = \frac{\Delta\overline{x}}{\Delta t}$$

where $\Delta\overline{x}$ is the displacement ($\text{m}$) and $\Delta t$ the time elapsed ($\text{s}$). In linear motion the signed displacement $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ is enough, and the average velocity is $v_\text{k} = \tfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$. When the velocity $v$ is constant and the body is at the position $x_0$ at $t = 0$,

position in uniform linear motion
$$x = x_0 + v\,t$$

This is the straight line of the $(t,x)$ graph: $x_0$ is its intercept on the position axis and $v$ its slope. The position is directly proportional to the time only when $x_0 = 0$.

Uniformly accelerated motion

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit of time:

average acceleration
$$\overline{a}_\text{k} = \frac{\Delta\overline{v}}{\Delta t}$$

where $\Delta\overline{v}$ is the change in velocity ($\text{m/s}$), $\Delta t$ the elapsed time ($\text{s}$) and $\overline{a}_\text{k}$ the average acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). In rectilinear motion the signed acceleration $a = \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is enough, and with a constant acceleration the average acceleration equals the instantaneous one.

When the acceleration is constant and the motion rectilinear, the velocity and the position at time $t$ are

velocity in uniformly accelerated linear motion
$$v = v_0 + a\,t$$
position in uniformly accelerated linear motion
$$x = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$$

where $v_0$ is the initial velocity ($\text{m/s}$) and $a$ the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). The speed increases when $a$ and $v$ point in the same direction and decreases when they point in opposite directions. In free fall $a = g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards when air resistance is small.

Slope and area

The instantaneous velocity on a curved $(t,x)$ graph is the slope $k$ of the tangent. A ratio calculated through two points of the curve is the average velocity. The displacement is the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis, and the part below the axis is counted as negative. In uniformly accelerated motion the region is a trapezium, so the average velocity is the mean of the initial and final velocities:

displacement in uniformly accelerated linear motion
$$\Delta x = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$$

Two bodies and measurement data

The positions of two bodies are written in the same coordinate system, and the bodies meet when

condition for meeting
$$x_\text{A}(t) = x_\text{B}(t)$$

In a $(t,x)$ graph a meeting is the crossing point of the lines. The upper line means a greater position coordinate. When the body behind is slower at first and accelerates, the distance between the bodies is at its greatest when their velocities are equal.

A straight line or a quadratic polynomial $x = A\,t^2 + B\,t + C$ is fitted to measured points. The acceleration is found from the coefficient of the polynomial: $a = 2A$.

Common mistakes

  • The $(t,x)$ graph is read as a picture of the route. A rising line means that the position grows, not an uphill.
  • The velocity is read as $x : t$ when it should be $\Delta x : \Delta t$. The initial position is not velocity.
  • The slope from two adjacent measured points. Read it from the fitted line or the tangent, from two points far apart.
  • A negative acceleration is always read as slowing down. The speed decreases only when the acceleration and the velocity point in opposite directions.
  • At the highest point the acceleration is zero. The velocity is zero for an instant, but the acceleration is still $g$ downwards.
  • The crossing point of $(t,v)$ graphs is read as a meeting. There the velocities are equal; at a meeting it is the positions that are equal.
  • Mixed-up units. Convert the units before you substitute, by writing them out: $72\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{72 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20\,\text{m/s}$.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: average speed

A cyclist rides $10\,\text{km}$ uphill at $20\,\text{km/h}$ and comes back down the same distance at $60\,\text{km/h}$. A student calculated the average speed: “$(20\,\text{km/h} + 60\,\text{km/h}) : 2 = 40\,\text{km/h}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • Correct. The uphill takes $10\,\text{km} : 20\,\text{km/h} = 0.50\,\text{h}$ and the downhill $10\,\text{km} : 60\,\text{km/h} \approx 0.17\,\text{h}$, in total $\tfrac{2}{3}\,\text{h}$. The slower speed is ridden for longer, so it weighs more.

  • The average velocity, the displacement divided by the time, is zero here, but the question is about the average speed, which is calculated from the distance travelled: $20\,\text{km} : \tfrac{2}{3}\,\text{h} = 30\,\text{km/h}$.

  • The mean of the speeds would do only if each speed were ridden for equally long. The uphill takes three times as long as the downhill.

Choose the graph: a cart on a track

A cart is pushed up a sloping track. It slows down, stops for an instant and rolls back down. The acceleration is the same all the time, and up is the positive direction.What does the cart's $(t,v)$ graph look like?

  • The cart is at rest for only an instant. The acceleration does not change at the moment of turning, so the velocity keeps decreasing and becomes negative.

  • This is the graph of the speed, not of the velocity. The sign of the velocity changes at the moment of turning, so the line continues in the same direction below the time axis.

  • When the acceleration is constant, the velocity changes by the same amount every second: the $(t,v)$ graph is a straight line. The curved one is the $(t,x)$ graph.

  • Correct. A constant acceleration gives a straight line with a negative slope. The velocity is zero at the moment of turning and negative after it, because the cart moves down.

Read the tangent

The graph shows the position of a cart accelerating from rest and the tangent drawn at the point of the curve at $4.0\,\text{s}$.What is the cart's velocity at $4.0\,\text{s}$?

  • This is the average velocity over $0$–$4.0\,\text{s}$, because you calculated it from the origin to the point of the curve. The instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent.

  • The height of the curve is the position, not the velocity. The velocity is the steepness of the tangent: the change in position divided by the change in time along the tangent.

  • Correct. The tangent passes through the points $(2.0\,\text{s}$; $0)$ and $(6.0\,\text{s}$; $16\,\text{m})$: $v = 16\,\text{m} : 4.0\,\text{s} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$. For a body accelerating from rest the velocity is twice the average velocity.

Which one is ahead?

Two cyclists ride along the same straight road in the negative direction of the $x$ axis. In their $(t,x)$ graphs, cyclist A's line is above cyclist B's line all the time.Which statement is correct?

  • A crossing point would be a meeting. The gap between the lines gives the distance between the cyclists, and B's smaller position coordinate means here that B is ahead. Review in the chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies.

  • Correct. In the graph the upper line means a greater position coordinate. When riding in the negative direction, the smaller position coordinate is ahead, that is, B. If needed, review in the chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies.

  • The upper line means a greater position coordinate. When moving in the negative direction, the one ahead is the one with the smaller position coordinate. Review in the chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies.

The kick scooter

The graph shows the velocity of a kick scooter. How far does the scooter travel during the time shown in the graph? Answer in metres.

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which region of the graph corresponds to the distance travelled?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The region is a trapezium: you can divide it into a rectangle and a triangle or calculate it with the average velocity.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$\Delta x$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s} + 7.0\,\text{m/s}}{2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
Show the solution

Given

from the graph, the initial velocity $v_0 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, the final velocity $v = 7.0\,\text{m/s}$, the time $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$; the velocity grows uniformly

Find

the distance travelled $\Delta x$ in metres

Principle

The scooter moves in one direction, so the distance is the displacement, and the displacement is the area under the $(t,v)$ graph (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The velocity grows uniformly, so the region is a trapezium.

Reading the graph

The line starts at the point $(0$; $2.0\,\text{m/s})$ and ends at the point $(5.0\,\text{s}$; $7.0\,\text{m/s})$.

Formula

$\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$

Conversions

The velocities are in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$\Delta x$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s} + 7.0\,\text{m/s}}{2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
$= 4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
$= 22.5\,\text{m}$

Answer

$23\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the velocities and the time read from the graph.

Reasonableness

The result lies between the rectangles $2.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 10\,\text{m}$ and $7.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 35\,\text{m}$, as it must. By dividing: the rectangle $10\,\text{m}$ and the triangle $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 12.5\,\text{m}$, in total the same $22.5\,\text{m}$.

A moose on the road

A car is driving on a country road at $72\,\text{km/h}$ when the driver notices a moose on the road. The reaction time is $0.80\,\text{s}$, and after it the car brakes uniformly to a stop with a deceleration of $6.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

How far does the car travel from the moment the driver notices the moose until it stops? Answer in metres.

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

Draw the $(t,v)$ graph: first a horizontal part for the reaction time, then a falling straight line to zero.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The braking time is $t_\text{b} = v_0 : a$, and each distance is the area of its own part.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$v_0$$= 20\,\text{m/s}$
$t_\text{b}$$= 20\,\text{m/s} : 6.5\,\text{m/s}^2$
$\approx 3.08\,\text{s}$
Show the solution

Given

velocity $v_0 = 72\,\text{km/h}$, reaction time $t_\text{r} = 0.80\,\text{s}$, deceleration $a = 6.5\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the stopping distance $s$ in metres

Principle

During the reaction time the car moves uniformly, and after it the velocity falls uniformly to zero (chapters Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs and Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The distance of each stage is a region of the $(t,v)$ graph: a rectangle and a triangle.

Formula

$v = v_0 - a\,t_\text{b} = 0$, so $t_\text{b} = \dfrac{v_0}{a}$, and

$s$$= v_0\,t_\text{r} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,t_\text{b}$
$= v_0\,t_\text{r} + \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$

Conversions

$72\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{72 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 20\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$s$$= 20\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.80\,\text{s} + \dfrac{(20\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 6.5\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= 16\,\text{m} + 30.77\,\text{m}$
$= 46.77\,\text{m}$

Answer

$47\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the reaction time $0.80\,\text{s}$ and the deceleration $6.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Reasonableness

Braking lasts $20\,\text{m/s} : 6.5\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 3.1\,\text{s}$ at an average velocity of $10\,\text{m/s}$, that is, about $31\,\text{m}$. The reaction distance is a third of the whole distance, so attentiveness matters a great deal. A stopping distance of a few tens of metres is usual at country-road speed on a dry road.

The dog and the jogger

A jogger runs along a straight path at a constant velocity of $3.0\,\text{m/s}$. When the jogger is $24\,\text{m}$ ahead of a dog, the dog, which has been standing still, sets off after the jogger and accelerates uniformly at $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

After how many seconds does the dog catch up with the jogger?

s
Hint 1: a guiding question

Choose the origin at the dog's starting point and write the position of each as a function of time.

Hint 2: what is the connection

Dog: $x_\text{D} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. Jogger: $x_\text{J} = 24\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot t$. Catching up: $x_\text{D} = x_\text{J}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$0.75\,t^2 - 3.0\,t - 24 = 0$ (with the units left out); choose the positive root.

Show the solution

Given

the jogger's velocity $v = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$ and head start $x_0 = 24\,\text{m}$; the dog starts from rest with the acceleration $a = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the moment of catching up $t$ in seconds

Principle

When the dog catches up with the jogger, they are at the same position at the same moment: $x_\text{D}(t) = x_\text{J}(t)$ (chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies). The origin is at the dog's starting point and the positive direction is the direction of running. The dog's motion is uniformly accelerated, the jogger's uniform.

Formula

$\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 = x_0 + v\,t$, that is, $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 - v\,t - x_0 = 0$, whose positive root is

$t$$= \dfrac{v + \sqrt{v^2 + 2\,a\,x_0}}{a}$

Conversions

All quantities are in SI units. Both terms under the square root are in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

Under the root

$v^2 + 2\,a\,x_0$$= (3.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 24\,\text{m}$
$= 81\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

and its square root is $9.0\,\text{m/s}$. So

$t$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{m/s} + 9.0\,\text{m/s}}{1.5\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= 8.0\,\text{s}$

Answer

$8.0\,\text{s}$

Two significant figures like the velocity $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the acceleration $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. The other root, $-4.0\,\text{s}$, is before the start, so it is rejected.

Reasonableness

The dog's position is $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (8.0\,\text{s})^2 = 48\,\text{m}$ and the jogger's $24\,\text{m} + 3.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s} = 48\,\text{m}$: the same position. The dog's velocity is then $12\,\text{m/s}$, four times the jogger's, so the dog is faster when it catches up, as it must be.

The pesäpallo pitch

In pesäpallo (Finnish baseball) the pitcher throws the ball vertically upwards at $8.0\,\text{m/s}$. Air resistance is neglected, and up is the positive direction.

What is the ball's velocity $1.0\,\text{s}$ after the pitch? Give the answer with its sign in $\text{m/s}$.

m/s
Hint 1: a guiding question

The acceleration is $g$ downwards during the whole flight. What is its sign when up is positive?

Hint 2: what is the connection

$v = v_0 + a\,t$, where $a = -9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The ball reaches its highest point at $8.0\,\text{m/s} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.82\,\text{s}$. In which direction does it move at $1.0\,\text{s}$?

Show the solution

Given

initial velocity $v_0 = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$ upwards, time $t = 1.0\,\text{s}$, acceleration $a = -g = -9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ (up positive)

Find

the velocity $v$ with its sign at time $t$

Principle

When air resistance is neglected, the ball's acceleration is $g$ downwards during the whole flight, at the highest point too. The motion is uniformly accelerated, and the sign of the velocity gives the direction (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion).

Formula

$v = v_0 + a\,t = v_0 - g\,t$

Conversions

The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$v$$= 8.0\,\text{m/s} - 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.0\,\text{s}$
$= 8.0\,\text{m/s} - 9.81\,\text{m/s}$
$= -1.81\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v \approx -1.8\,\text{m/s}$

That is, the ball moves downwards at a speed of $1.8\,\text{m/s}$. Two significant figures like the initial velocity $8.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the time $1.0\,\text{s}$.

Model answer

a)

Given

length of the lane $\Delta x = 270\,\text{m}$, velocity at the beginning of the lane $v_0 = 54\,\text{km/h}$ and at the end $v = 108\,\text{km/h}$; the acceleration is uniform

Find

the duration of the acceleration $t$ in seconds

Principle

The acceleration is constant, so the $(t,v)$ graph is a straight line and the region under it is a trapezium. The displacement is the area of this region, so the average velocity is the mean of the initial and final velocities (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The acceleration is not needed to calculate the time.

Formula

$\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$, so $t = \dfrac{2\,\Delta x}{v_0 + v}$

Conversions

$54\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{54 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 15\,\text{m/s}$
$108\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{108 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 30\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$t$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 270\,\text{m}}{15\,\text{m/s} + 30\,\text{m/s}}$
$= \dfrac{540\,\text{m}}{45\,\text{m/s}}$
$= 12.0\,\text{s}$

Answer

$t \approx 12\,\text{s}$

Two significant figures like the velocities $54\,\text{km/h}$ and $108\,\text{km/h}$.

Reasonableness

The average velocity is $22.5\,\text{m/s}$, and $22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} = 270\,\text{m}$, the length of the lane. An acceleration of about ten seconds to motorway speed is usual for a car.

b)

Given

$v_0 = 15\,\text{m/s}$, $v = 30\,\text{m/s}$ and from part a $t = 12.0\,\text{s}$

Find

the acceleration $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

Principle

The acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it took; in uniformly accelerated motion it is the same all the time and equal to the slope of the $(t,v)$ graph (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion).

Formula

$a$$= \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$
$= \dfrac{v - v_0}{t}$

Conversions

The velocities were converted to $\text{m/s}$ in part a, and the time is in seconds.

Substitution

$a$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{m/s} - 15\,\text{m/s}}{12.0\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{15\,\text{m/s}}{12.0\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a \approx 1.3\,\text{m/s}^2$

Two significant figures like the velocities ($1.25\,\text{m/s}^2$ is also accepted).

Reasonableness

The velocity grows by $1.25\,\text{m/s} = 4.5\,\text{km/h}$ every second, so in twelve seconds it grows by $54\,\text{km/h}$, as in the data. A check with the equation of position:

$15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (12.0\,\text{s})^2$
$= 180\,\text{m} + 90\,\text{m}$
$= 270\,\text{m}$

c)

Given

origin at the beginning of the acceleration lane, positive direction the direction of driving; car: $x_{0\text{A}} = 0$, $v_0 = 15\,\text{m/s}$, $a = 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2$; lorry: $x_{0\text{L}} = -40\,\text{m}$, $v_\text{L} = 81\,\text{km/h}$ (constant)

Find

whether the lorry draws level with the car, and the smallest distance $d_\text{min}$

Principle

The positions are written in the same coordinate system (chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies). The lorry is faster at first and approaches the car. The distance decreases as long as the lorry is faster and starts to grow when the car is faster, so it is at its smallest when the velocities are equal. If the distance is then positive, the lorry does not draw level.

Formula

$x_\text{A} = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$ and $x_\text{L} = x_{0\text{L}} + v_\text{L}\,t$. Equal velocities: $v_0 + a\,t_1 = v_\text{L}$, so $t_1 = \dfrac{v_\text{L} - v_0}{a}$ and $d_\text{min} = x_\text{A}(t_1) - x_\text{L}(t_1)$

Conversions

$81\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{81 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 22.5\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$t_1$$= \dfrac{22.5\,\text{m/s} - 15\,\text{m/s}}{1.25\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= 6.0\,\text{s}$
$x_\text{A}$$= 15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 6.0\,\text{s} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (6.0\,\text{s})^2$
$= 90\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m}$
$= 112.5\,\text{m}$
$x_\text{L}$$= -40\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 6.0\,\text{s}$
$= 95\,\text{m}$
$d_\text{min}$$= 112.5\,\text{m} - 95\,\text{m}$
$= 17.5\,\text{m}$

Answer

The lorry does not draw level with the car. The distance is at its smallest, about $18\,\text{m}$, at $6.0\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the distance $40\,\text{m}$ and the velocities.

Reasonableness

At the end of the lane ($12.0\,\text{s}$) the lorry is at $-40\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} = 230\,\text{m}$ and the car at $270\,\text{m}$, so the gap is $40\,\text{m}$ again, as it should be: the areas between the velocity graphs before and after $6.0\,\text{s}$ are equal. The meeting equation $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 + (v_0 - v_\text{L})\,t - x_{0\text{L}} = 0$ has no real solutions, because the discriminant $(-7.5)^2 - 4 \cdot 0.625 \cdot 40 < 0$.

d)

Find

why the lorry catches up with a car that accelerates uniformly only to $72\,\text{km/h}$, and why it is worth accelerating to the speed of the traffic

Principle

The displacement is the area of the $(t,v)$ graph, and the distance between two bodies changes by the area between their two graphs (chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies).

Model answer

This car is slower than the lorry for the whole lane, because its greatest velocity $72\,\text{km/h} = 20\,\text{m/s}$ is smaller than the lorry's $22.5\,\text{m/s}$. In the $(t,v)$ graph the lorry's horizontal line is above the car's rising line all the time, so the lorry's displacement grows more than the car's at every moment and the gap of $40\,\text{m}$ shrinks all the time. When the gap has been closed, the lorry is level with the car and goes past. A calculation confirms this (the car accelerates uniformly over the whole length of the lane): the acceleration lasts $2 \cdot 270\,\text{m} : (15\,\text{m/s} + 20\,\text{m/s}) \approx 15.4\,\text{s}$ and the car's average velocity is $17.5\,\text{m/s}$, so the lorry travels on average $5\,\text{m/s}$ faster and makes up $5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 15.4\,\text{s} \approx 77\,\text{m}$, more than the gap of $40\,\text{m}$; and in that time the lorry gets to $-40\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 15.4\,\text{s} \approx 307\,\text{m}$, past the car before the end of the lane ($270\,\text{m}$). The car must then slow down and merge behind the lorry. When the car accelerates to the speed of the traffic, it is faster than the lorry in the final stage, the distance grows again and the car can merge safely in front of the lorry.

  • In part c the meeting condition is solved and the negative discriminant is taken for a calculation error, although it tells you that there is no meeting.

Also accepted

The effect of the uncertainty is estimated by calculating the result with the extreme values of the time $t \pm \Delta t$ (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

$a$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 1.20\,\text{m}}{(1.6\,\text{s})^2}$
$= \dfrac{2.40\,\text{m}}{2.56\,\text{s}^2}$
$= 0.9375\,\text{m/s}^2$

$\dfrac{2.40\,\text{m}}{(1.8\,\text{s})^2}$$= 0.741\,\text{m/s}^2$

b)

Find

whether the group's conclusion is justified, and the main weaknesses of the measurement

Principle

That a model fits can be shown only by comparing the model with many measurement points. In uniformly accelerated motion the $(t,x)$ points lie on a parabola and the $(t,v)$ points on a straight line (chapters Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion and Graphing and fitting a straight line).

Model answer

The conclusion is not justified. The group used the formula $a = \dfrac{2x}{t^2}$, which is derived assuming a uniform acceleration, so a number obtained with the formula cannot prove the assumption: one distance and one time always give some number, whatever the motion is like. The reasoning is circular. A single $(t,x)$ point fits any accelerated motion; several points at different moments are needed. The measurement also has weaknesses: in hand timing the reaction time affects both the start and the end, the cart's starting moment and the point of passing are judged by eye and from the side, the cart may have got some speed as it was released, and the measurement was made only once, so the size of the random error is not known. The number $0.94\,\text{m/s}^2$ is at best a rough estimate of the acceleration, if the motion is uniformly accelerated.

c)

Find

a plan for an experiment that reliably finds out whether the motion is uniformly accelerated and determines the acceleration

Principle

A model is tested by measuring the position at many moments and fitting a curve of the model to the measured points; the acceleration is found from a coefficient of the fit. The variables are kept constant and the measurements are repeated (chapters Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion, Graphing and fitting a straight line and Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting).

Model answer

The cart's motion is filmed with a camera on a stand perpendicular to the track, and the scale is set with a measuring tape in the plane of the track. Video analysis is used to read the cart's position from, for example, every third frame, which gives tens of $(t,x)$ points. Alternatively, light gates are placed at different distances along the track and the times are measured electronically. The cart is released from rest without a push, for example by releasing a holder, and the slope of the track is kept the same in all repeats. The points are presented as a $(t,x)$ graph and a quadratic polynomial $x = A\,t^2 + B\,t + C$ is fitted to them; if the points lie on the curve without a systematic deviation, the motion is uniformly accelerated, and the acceleration is $a = 2A$. Another way is to calculate the velocities from successive points and check whether the $(t,v)$ points lie on a straight line. The experiment is repeated at least three times, and the uncertainty of the acceleration is estimated from the spread of the repeats or from the uncertainty of the coefficient given by the fit.

  • The uncertainty is assessed only in words, without calculating its effect on the result.
  • In part c the only suggestion is “a more accurate stopwatch” or “more repeats” with the same method, so that a single point still does not test the model.

$0.00$ $1.653$
$0.10$ $1.607$
$0.20$ $1.469$
$0.30$ $1.253$

Model answer

a)

Given

the fitted polynomial $y = A\,t^2 + B\,t + C$, where $A = -4.44\,\text{m/s}^2$, $B = -0.007\,\text{m/s}$ and $C = 1.651\,\text{m}$; the ball started from rest, $y$ grows upwards

Find

the measured acceleration of free fall $a$

Principle

The motion of a freely falling ball is uniformly accelerated, so its position is $y = y_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. When the fitted polynomial is compared with this, the coefficients of $t^2$ are the same: $A = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a$ (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The coefficient $B \approx 0$ corresponds to an initial velocity of zero, and $C$ to the initial height.

Formula

$A = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a$, so $a = 2A$

Conversions

The coefficient is already in $\text{m/s}^2$, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$a$$= 2 \cdot (-4.44\,\text{m/s}^2)$
$= -8.88\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

The measured acceleration of free fall is $8.88\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards (minus sign, because $y$ grows upwards), three significant figures like the coefficient $A$.

Reasonableness

A check from the table: when $t = 0.50\,\text{s}$, the ball has fallen $1.653\,\text{m} - 0.538\,\text{m} = 1.115\,\text{m}$, and

$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 8.88\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (0.50\,\text{s})^2$
$= 1.11\,\text{m}$

The result is of the same order of magnitude as $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ but clearly smaller, so something affects the measurement.

b)

Given

$A = (-4.44 \pm 0.05)\,\text{m/s}^2$, from part a $a = 8.88\,\text{m/s}^2$; table value $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the uncertainty of the acceleration $\Delta a$, and whether the result agrees with the table value

Principle

When a quantity is multiplied by a constant, its uncertainty is multiplied by the same constant. A result agrees with a reference value if the difference is the size of the uncertainty or smaller (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

Formula

$a = 2A$, so $\Delta a = 2\,\Delta A$

Conversions

None needed: the units are the same.

Substitution

$\Delta a$$= 2 \cdot 0.05\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 0.10\,\text{m/s}^2$

the difference from the table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 8.88\,\text{m/s}^2 = 0.93\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a = (8.88 \pm 0.10)\,\text{m/s}^2$

The difference from the table value, $0.93\,\text{m/s}^2$, is about nine times the uncertainty, so the result does not agree with the table value.

Reasonableness

The relative uncertainty is $0.10 : 8.88 \approx 1.1\,\%$, which suits the small scatter of the points, a few millimetres. The uncertainty of the fit tells you only about the random scatter, not about a systematic error that shifts all the points in the same direction.

c)

Given

the scale was set in the plane of the metre stick $3.00\,\text{m}$ from the camera, the ball fell $3.30\,\text{m}$ from the camera; from parts a and b $a = (8.88 \pm 0.10)\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the cause and the direction of the systematic error, and the corrected acceleration $a_\text{c}$

Principle

An object further away looks smaller in the picture in the same ratio as its distance from the camera is greater. The program converts the distances the ball moves in the picture to metres with the scale set in the plane of the stick, so it gives too small values for the ball's distances. The acceleration is proportional to the distance, so it is corrected by the same ratio (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

Formula

$a_\text{c}$$= \dfrac{d_\text{ball}}{d_\text{stick}}\,a$
$\Delta a_\text{c}$$= \dfrac{d_\text{ball}}{d_\text{stick}}\,\Delta a$

Conversions

The distances are in the same unit, so their ratio has no unit.

Substitution

$a_\text{c}$$= \dfrac{3.30\,\text{m}}{3.00\,\text{m}} \cdot 8.88\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 1.10 \cdot 8.88\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 9.768\,\text{m/s}^2$
$\Delta a_\text{c}$$= 1.10 \cdot 0.10\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 0.11\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

The corrected acceleration of free fall is $a_\text{c} \approx (9.8 \pm 0.1)\,\text{m/s}^2$, with one decimal instead of two, because the uncertainty is about $0.1\,\text{m/s}^2$. The error made the result about $9\,\%$ smaller.

Reasonableness

The corrected result differs from the table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ by only $0.04\,\text{m/s}^2$, less than the uncertainty, so the scale error explains the whole difference. The direction of the error is right too: too small a scale gives too small an acceleration.

d)

Find

whether calculating the acceleration from the first and the last point only is a good method

Principle

Every measurement point has a small random error. The fit uses all the points at once, so the error of a single point affects it less, and the fit also gives the uncertainty of the coefficient (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line).

Model answer

The suggestion is worse than the fit. Here the two-point calculation gives $a = \dfrac{2 \cdot 1.115\,\text{m}}{(0.50\,\text{s})^2} \approx 8.92\,\text{m/s}^2$, almost the same as the fit, but the result depends entirely on two points: an error of a few millimetres or a wrongly chosen starting moment would change it directly, and the method does not tell you the uncertainty of the result. The formula also assumes that the ball is exactly at rest at the moment $0$. The fit uses all eleven points, allows a small initial velocity in the coefficient $B$, gives the uncertainty $\Delta A$ and shows whether the model fits the points. Neither method, however, corrects the systematic error of the scale: it has to be corrected separately, or the scale has to be set in the plane in which the ball falls.

Common mistakes

  • The acceleration is read directly as $A = 4.44\,\text{m/s}^2$ without the factor $2$.
  • The difference from the table value is explained by air resistance or reaction time, although the scatter of the points is small and the data tell you where the scale was set.
  • The correction is made in the wrong direction, that is, the result is multiplied by the ratio $3.00 : 3.30$.
  • The uncertainty of the fit is taken as the whole uncertainty of the result, and the systematic error is ignored.

Also accepted

  • In part c first correcting the coefficient $A$: $A_\text{c} = 1.10 \cdot (-4.44\,\text{m/s}^2) = -4.88\,\text{m/s}^2$ and then $a_\text{c} = 2A_\text{c}$.

The claim is wrong. At the crossing point of the $(t,v)$ graphs the car and the moped have the same velocity, but they are not at the same position.
