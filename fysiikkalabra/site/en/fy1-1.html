Physics as a science and the scales of the universe

Why can physics study both a proton and the whole universe, even though their sizes are so far apart?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • describe the structure of matter from molecules to quarks and Earth's place in the structures of the universe.
  • write a size in scientific notation and compare sizes using orders of magnitude.
  • recognise the four fundamental interactions and link a phenomenon to each.

Prior knowledge

  • Powers from lower secondary school mathematics, for example $10^{3} = 1000$ and $10^{-2} = 0.01$
  • The units of length: metre, millimetre and kilometre

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 6.

1Think about it

Physics studies the smallest and the largest structures we know with the same principles. The size of a proton is about $1.7\cdot10^{-15}\,\text{m}$ and the diameter of the observable universe about $8.8\cdot10^{26}\,\text{m}$. Neither can be seen with the naked eye or measured with a metre rule, and yet their sizes are known.

What is matter made of, what larger structures is Earth a part of, and how many powers of ten are there between the smallest and the largest size?

4Theory

Key idea

Physics builds models and tests them

Physics is a natural science that looks for regularities in nature and explains them with models. A model is a simplified description of how a phenomenon works. From a model you can work out a prediction, and a measurement can disprove that prediction. So physics does not rely on who says something, but on what experiments and measurements show.

Research proceeds in cycles. An observation raises a question, a model is proposed to answer it, and a prediction is derived from the model. The prediction is tested in an experiment, and the result either supports the model or forces us to change it. A measurement gives its result as a number and a unit: the SI unit of length is the metre, $\text{m}$.

Sizes vary by powers of ten

The objects of physics come in hugely different sizes: a proton is about $1.7\cdot10^{-15}\,\text{m}$ and the observable universe about $8.8\cdot10^{26}\,\text{m}$. A range this wide cannot be written as ordinary numbers. That is why we use scientific notation $a\cdot10^{n}$, where $1 \le a < 10$ and $n$ is an integer. When the exponent $n$ grows by one, the number becomes ten times larger. The Planck length, about $1.6\cdot10^{-35}\,\text{m}$, is a scale below which, according to physicists, current theories no longer work. It has not been measured directly but calculated from constants of nature.

Order of magnitude tells the rough size

The order of magnitude is the power of ten that is closest to the number on the axis of powers of ten. The order of magnitude of $a\cdot10^{n}$ is $10^{n}$ when $a$ is less than about 3, and $10^{n+1}$ when $a$ is more than about 3. A person's height $1.7\,\text{m}$ is of the order $10^{0}\,\text{m}$, but the height of Mount Everest, $8.8\cdot10^{3}\,\text{m}$, is of the order $10^{4}\,\text{m}$.

The sizes of objects are rounded values: people, ants and galaxies are not all the same size. That is why the order of magnitude is often enough for a comparison, and it is worth asking how accurate the numbers are.

How many times larger?

When you want to know how many times larger one thing is than another, divide the larger size by the smaller:

$$k = \frac{d_\text{larger}}{d_\text{smaller}}$$

Here $k$ is the ratio of the sizes, that is, how many times larger the object is. Both sizes must be in the same unit. Then the unit cancels, and $k$ is just a number.

Powers of ten let you estimate the ratio in your head. Each step from one power of ten to the next is a tenfold size. A hydrogen atom is of the order $10^{-10}\,\text{m}$ and a proton $10^{-15}\,\text{m}$. There are five steps between them, so the atom is about $10\cdot10\cdot10\cdot10\cdot10 = 10^{5}$, that is, a hundred thousand times larger than the proton. The number of steps is the difference of the exponents: $-10-(-15) = 5$.

A light-year is a unit of length

Distances between stars are so large that the metre is impractical. A light-year is the distance that light travels in a vacuum in one year, about $9.46\cdot10^{15}\,\text{m}$. So it is a length, not a time. Light travel times and the astronomical units of length are covered in more detail in the chapter Quantities, units and the SI system.

The structure of matter

Matter consists of nested structures

All the matter around us consists of atoms. Atoms can join together to form molecules: a water molecule has one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. At the centre of every atom there is a small atomic nucleus, and electrons move around it. The nucleus consists of protons and neutrons, and each of these consists of three quarks. Quarks and the electron are elementary particles: as far as we know they have no smaller parts, and no size has been measured for them. According to experiments they are smaller than about $10^{-18}\,\text{m}$.

An atom is tens of thousands of times larger than its nucleus

The diameter of an atom is about $10^{-10}\,\text{m}$, but that of the nucleus is only $10^{-15}$–$10^{-14}\,\text{m}$. There are four or five orders of magnitude between them, so the atom is tens of thousands of times larger than its nucleus. If an atom were enlarged to the size of a football stadium, the nucleus at its centre would be the size of a pea. Even so, the nucleus holds over 99.9 percent of the mass of the atom. The size of the atom is set by the region where the electrons move, and the nucleus is small and very dense at its centre. The nucleus of a hydrogen atom is a single proton.

Structures of the universe

Earth's address in the universe

Earth is a planet: it orbits a star, the Sun, and the Moon orbits Earth. A star is a hot ball of gas that produces its own light in nuclear reactions in its core. A planet does not produce light, but it is visible because it reflects the light of its star. The Sun, its eight planets with their moons, and the dwarf planets, asteroids and comets form the Solar System. It is one planetary system among many: thousands of planets orbiting other stars are already known.

The Sun is one of the hundreds of billions of stars in the Milky Way. The Milky Way is a galaxy, that is, a huge collection of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity, and its diameter is about $10^{5}$ light-years. There are an estimated hundreds of billions of galaxies in the observable universe. They form groups of galaxies and larger clusters of galaxies: the Milky Way and the Andromeda Galaxy belong to the Local Group. Groups and clusters form superclusters, and on the largest scale the galaxies lie in web-like filaments with almost empty regions between them.

The edge of the Solar System depends on what is measured

The Solar System has no single sharp edge. Particles flow out from the Sun, the solar wind, which blows a bubble around it, the heliosphere. Its boundary, the heliopause, is about $120\,\text{AU}$ from the Sun, and the Voyager probes have crossed it. However, the Sun's gravity dominates much farther away. From the orbits of long-period comets we conclude that a swarm of comets, the Oort cloud, orbits the Sun far away, about $2000$–$100\,000\,\text{AU}$ from it. So the Voyagers are in interstellar space but still within the reach of the Sun's gravity.

Types of objects, not steps

Not all astronomical objects are steps in the same nested series. Instead, they are found inside galaxies, among the stars. A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust; for example, new stars are forming in the Orion Nebula. A neutron star is the remnant of the explosion of a massive star. Its mass is usually greater than that of the Sun, but its diameter is only about $20$–$25\,\text{km}$, because the matter has been squeezed into almost pure neutrons.

A black hole is a region from which, because of gravity, nothing, not even light, can escape. It has no surface: its size is given as the diameter of its boundary, the event horizon, which grows with the mass. The black hole at the centre of the Milky Way has a mass of about four million solar masses and an event horizon diameter of about $2.5\cdot10^{10}\,\text{m}$. So size does not tell the type of object: a neutron star is smaller than Earth, and this black hole is larger than the Sun.

The observable universe is not the whole universe

The observable universe is the part of the universe from which light has had time to reach us during the age of the universe, about 13.8 billion years. Its diameter is about $8.8\cdot10^{26}\,\text{m}$, or about 93 billion light-years. The number is greater than twice 13.8 billion light-years because space has expanded during the light's journey. The boundary of the observable universe is not the edge of the universe but the limit of our horizon: for an observer in another galaxy, the observable universe is a sphere around that observer. The size of the whole universe is not known, and it may be infinite.

Fundamental interactions

Four fundamental interactions

Structures stay together and change because of interactions. All known interactions come down to four fundamental interactions:

  • Gravity acts between all objects that have mass, and it is always attractive. It keeps the Moon around Earth and the planets in their orbits around their stars, and it gathers clouds of gas into stars and galaxies.
  • The electromagnetic interaction acts between electrically charged particles: opposite charges attract and like charges repel each other. It keeps the electrons around the nucleus in an atom, binds atoms into molecules and causes electric and magnetic phenomena, such as the ability of a rubbed balloon to attract bits of paper.
  • The strong interaction binds quarks into protons and neutrons. Its residual effect holds protons and neutrons together in a nucleus, even though the protons repel each other electrically. It reaches only a distance the size of a nucleus, about $10^{-15}\,\text{m}$.
  • The weak interaction causes some radioactive decays. For example, in beta decay a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton, and an electron and a particle called an antineutrino leave the nucleus. The weak interaction is also involved in the Sun's energy production.

Scale decides which interaction dominates

The same four interactions act everywhere, from inside the nucleus to clusters of galaxies, but the scale decides which of them dominates. The strong and weak interactions reach only the scale of the nucleus. Between particles the electromagnetic interaction is enormously stronger than gravity, but large objects are almost neutral: their positive and negative charges cancel each other's effect. Between particles gravity is the weakest, but it is always attractive and grows with mass. That is why gravity dominates on the scale of planets, stars and galaxies.

Common misconception

A difference in powers of ten is not a difference in sizes. If the orders of magnitude of two objects differ by six, one is not six times larger but $10^{6}$, that is, a million times larger. In addition, a scale picture can show truthfully only one scale at a time: everything cannot be drawn in the same picture in the right proportions.

Neutron stars and black holes are not steps larger than a galaxy, but objects inside galaxies. A black hole also does not suck in everything around it: far from it, its gravity is the same as that of any object of equal mass, and stars can orbit it like planets orbit the Sun.

Summary

  • Physics builds models from which predictions are derived, and an experiment or measurement decides whether the model holds.
  • Scientific notation $a\cdot10^{n}$ writes very small and very large numbers briefly; one step in the exponent means a tenfold size.
  • The order of magnitude is the nearest power of ten on the axis of powers of ten. The ratio of sizes is found by dividing the larger size by the smaller, and each step of a power of ten is a tenfold size.
  • A light-year is the distance light travels in a year, about $9.46\cdot10^{15}\,\text{m}$.
  • Molecules consist of atoms, atoms of a nucleus and electrons, the nucleus of protons and neutrons, and these of quarks. An atom is tens of thousands of times larger than its nucleus.
  • Earth is a planet that orbits a star. The Solar System is one planetary system in the Milky Way, and the Milky Way is one of the hundreds of billions of galaxies of the observable universe.
  • Nebulae, neutron stars and black holes are types of objects inside galaxies, not steps of structure.
  • The four fundamental interactions are gravity and the electromagnetic, strong and weak interactions. On small scales the interactions of the nucleus and the atom dominate, and on large scales gravity.

    Which interaction?

    Which fundamental interaction explains each phenomenon?

    1. A dropped bunch of keys falls to the floor.
    2. A fridge magnet stays stuck to the fridge door.
    3. The two protons and two neutrons of a helium nucleus stay together.
    4. A banana contains a little radioactive potassium-40. Sometimes a neutron in its nucleus changes into a proton, and an electron leaves the nucleus.

    • Correct. Earth and the keys attract each other because of gravity. The force between the magnet and the steel door is magnetic, that is, electromagnetic.     • In the decay of potassium-40 (beta decay) a neutron changes into a proton, that is, the kind of particle changes. A change like this is caused by the weak interaction. The strong interaction binds quarks and the parts of the nucleus together, but it does not change a neutron into a proton.

    • The electromagnetic interaction does not hold the nucleus together but tends to break it apart: protons have charges of the same sign and repel each other, and neutrons have no charge. The nucleus stays together because of the residual effect of the strong interaction.

    • As a whole, the bunch of keys and Earth are each almost electrically neutral, so there is no significant electric force between them. The fall is due to gravity, which acts between all masses and is always attractive.

    • Gravity between the particles of a nucleus is far too weak: the electric repulsion of two protons is about $10^{36}$ times stronger than their gravity. The nucleus is held together by the residual effect of the strong interaction.

        $d$$= 119.7\,\text{AU} + 23.46\,\text{AU}$
        $= 143.16\,\text{AU}$

        Answer

        $143.2\,\text{AU}$

        One decimal like the initial distance $119.7\,\text{AU}$, which is the least precise number in the sum. The student multiplied the speed in $\text{km/s}$ by years instead of seconds, so the result was not a distance, and in addition added kilometres and astronomical units. The model was not wrong.

        Model answer on the measurement

        The measurement $143.05\,\text{AU}$ differs from the prediction by only about $0.1\,\text{AU}$, so the prediction passed the test and the measurement supports the model. One match does not, however, prove the model for all situations: the speed may change slowly, and the model holds only near the measured interval.

        Reasonableness

        In a year Voyager travels

        $15.341\,\text{km/s}\cdot3.156\cdot10^{7}\,\text{s}$
        $= 4.84\cdot10^{8}\,\text{km}$
        $= 3.2\,\text{AU}$

        and in seven and a quarter years about $23\,\text{AU}$. The result agrees.
