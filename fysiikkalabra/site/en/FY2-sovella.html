Challenge tasks

In these tasks nobody tells you which formula to use. Decide yourself which phenomena and models belong to the situation, solve the task on paper and justify your solution, as in the matriculation examination.

Two extended tasks bring together the skills of the module: energy, power and the environmental effects of energy production. In the first one you decide which data from the material and which models you need, and you write a justified recommendation. In the second one you find two publications yourself on the same energy production question and assess whether their figures can be compared. In neither task has the right answer been decided in advance: the points come from the reasoning.

Solve the task yourself first, on paper or on a computer. You may use a calculator, a data booklet and graphing software. Write out your reasoning, formulas, substitutions and answers with units.

Check the concepts before the tasks

These cards of the module are in your review deck. Go through them before you start the tasks.

1A city gives up coal power 18 points

Made-up example data · The city, its electricity consumption, the plants' powers, capacity factors and seasonal shares are invented example values whose order of magnitude matches Finnish power plants. The emission factors are real: the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2014), Working Group III, Annex III, life-cycle emission medians, rounded. The biomass factor is the median of the IPCC's life-cycle comparison of certain biomass supply chains, not a universal factor for all electricity produced from forest wood chips.

The old coal-fired power plant of an example city is being closed. The city council asks you for a recommendation on how the city's electricity should be produced in the future. The city is connected to the national electricity grid, so it can also buy and sell electricity.

The city's electricity demand (example)

  • Annual consumption $E = 380\,\text{GWh}$. Until now the coal plant has produced all of it.
  • The highest power demand $P_\text{peak} = 80\,\text{MW}$ occurs on a calm, frosty evening in January.
  • A year has $8760\,\text{h}$, and the winter months December–February have $90$ days.

The options, one unit at a time (power and capacity factor are example values, emission factor from IPCC AR5, 2014)

The capacity factor $k$ is the plant's average power over a year divided by its rated power $P_\text{n}$. For a controllable plant it is a typical value: the plant can run more or less when needed.

Power plant $P_\text{n}\ (\text{MW})$ $k$ $e\ (\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}})$
Natural gas $50$ $0.60$ $490$
Biomass (wood chips) $40$ $0.70$ $230$
Wind (onshore) $5.0$ $0.32$ $11$
Solar (park) $10$ $0.11$ $48$
Small nuclear $60$ $0.90$ $12$
Hydro (share) $20$ $0.50$ $24$
Existing coal plant $90$ $820$

In the table, $e$ is the life-cycle emission factor in grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilowatt-hour. It includes the construction of the plant and the production and transport of the fuel.

For the biomass option this calculation exercise uses the emission factor $230\,\text{g/kWh}$. The value is based on the IPCC's life-cycle comparison of certain biomass supply chains, in which the carbon dioxide from burning is counted as zero because new growth binds carbon back. This is an accounting assumption, not a claim that burning biomass releases no carbon dioxide. The real life-cycle emission factor of forest wood chips can differ considerably from this: it depends on where the biomass comes from, on changes in carbon stocks and on how quickly the carbon is bound back into vegetation.

Properties of the production methods (example)

  • Natural gas: the power can be adjusted quickly up and down. Gas is brought in by pipeline or ship, so availability and price depend on imports.
  • Biomass: the power can be adjusted, but more slowly than in a gas plant. Wood chips are transported by lorry, about $30$ lorry loads per day at full power, and forest is felled to produce them.
  • Wind power: production depends on the weather. Production can be curtailed but not increased. During a calm, frosty spell the combined output of the wind turbines can be below $5\,\%$ of the rated power for several days. The turbines can be seen and heard, and their area takes up land.
  • Solar power: production depends on the time of day, the season and cloud cover. On a dark winter evening production is zero. The park takes up about $15\,\text{ha}$ of field or forest for every ten megawatts of rated power.
  • Small nuclear power plant: usually runs steadily at almost rated power, and the power can also be reduced. Such plants are not yet in use in Finland; licensing and construction are estimated to take more than ten years. Spent fuel is radioactive waste that must be disposed of permanently.
  • Hydropower: the power can be adjusted quickly, but the energy of a year depends on rainfall and varies from year to year by about $\pm 20\,\%$. The region's undeveloped rapids are protected, so the city can only buy a share of an existing plant.

Model answer

a)

Given

rated power of a wind turbine $P_\text{n} = 5.0\,\text{MW}$, capacity factor $k = 0.32$, length of a year $t = 8760\,\text{h}$ and the city's annual consumption $E = 380\,\text{GWh}$

Find

the number of wind turbines needed $N$

Principle

A wind turbine does not run at rated power all year, because the wind varies. The capacity factor gives the average power over a year: $P_\text{avg} = k \cdot P_\text{n}$. The power varies, but the energy of a year is the average power times the time. Once the annual energy of one turbine is known, the number of turbines is the consumption divided by it. The number is a whole number, and it must be at least the calculated value.

Formula

$E_1 = k \cdot P_\text{n} \cdot t$ and $N = \dfrac{E}{E_1}$

Conversions

When the power is in megawatts and the time in hours, the energy is in megawatt-hours: $1\,\text{GWh} = 1000\,\text{MWh}$.

Substitution

$E_1$$= 0.32 \cdot 5.0\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h}$
$= 14\,016\,\text{MWh}$
$= 14.0\,\text{GWh}$
$N$$= \dfrac{380\,\text{GWh}}{14.0\,\text{GWh}}$
$= 27.1$

Answer

$28$

Wind turbines are needed. The calculated value $27.1$ is rounded up, because $27$ turbines would produce only $27 \cdot 14.0\,\text{GWh} = 378\,\text{GWh}$, which is too little. The number is a whole number, so the rule of significant figures does not limit it.

Reasonableness

The city's average power is

$\dfrac{380\,000\,\text{MWh}}{8760\,\text{h}}$$\approx 43\,\text{MW}$

The combined rated power of the turbines is $28 \cdot 5.0\,\text{MW} = 140\,\text{MW}$, about three times as much, which fits the capacity factor $0.32 \approx \tfrac{1}{3}$.

b)

Given

annual consumption $E = 380\,\text{GWh}$ and life-cycle emission factors: coal $e = 820\,\text{g/kWh}$, natural gas $490\,\text{g/kWh}$ and onshore wind power $11\,\text{g/kWh}$

Find

the annual emission $m$ for each method and the decrease in the emission as a percentage compared with coal

Principle

The emission factor tells how many grams of carbon dioxide equivalent are produced per kilowatt-hour when the whole life cycle is included. When all the kilowatt-hours are produced in the same way, the emission is directly proportional to the energy. The percentage decrease is the difference of the emissions relative to the emission of coal. The energy is the same in all the alternatives, so it cancels, and the ratio can be calculated directly from the emission factors.

Formula

$m = e \cdot E$ and the decrease

$\dfrac{m_\text{coal} - m}{m_\text{coal}}$$= 1 - \dfrac{e}{e_\text{coal}}$

Conversions

$E$$= 380\,\text{GWh}$
$= 380 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh}$

because the factor is in the unit $\text{g/kWh}$. Finally $1\,\text{t} = 10^{6}\,\text{g}$.

Substitution

Coal:

$m$$= 820\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 380 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh}$
$= 3.116 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{g}$
$= 3.116 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}$

Natural gas:

$m$$= 490\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 380 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh}$
$= 1.862 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{g}$
$= 1.862 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}$

Wind power:

$m$$= 11\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 380 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh}$
$= 4.18 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{g}$
$= 4.18 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t}$

Decrease: natural gas

$1 - \dfrac{490\,\text{g/kWh}}{820\,\text{g/kWh}}$$= 0.40$

and wind power

$1 - \dfrac{11\,\text{g/kWh}}{820\,\text{g/kWh}}$$= 0.99$

Answer

With coal $m \approx 3.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}$, with natural gas $1.9 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}$ and with wind power $4.2 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t}$ per year, to two significant figures like the emission factors. Natural gas reduces the emission by about $40\,\%$, wind power by about $99\,\%$.

Reasonableness

Coal produces just under a kilogram per kilowatt-hour, and there are hundreds of millions of kilowatt-hours, so hundreds of thousands of tonnes is the right order of magnitude. The percentages agree with those calculated from the tonnes: with natural gas

$\dfrac{1.862 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}}{3.116 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}}$$\approx 0.60$

of the coal emission is left, so the decrease is $40\,\%$. Even the emission of wind power is not zero, because the manufacture and construction of the turbines cause emissions.

c)

Given

$N = 28$ wind turbines, the annual energy of one $E_1 = 14.0\,\text{GWh}$, the share of winter in the annual energy of wind power $32\,\%$ and in the city's annual consumption $31\,\%$, consumption $E = 380\,\text{GWh}$, length of winter $90\,\text{d}$, peak power $80\,\text{MW}$ and the power during a calm spell below $5\,\%$ of the rated power

Find

the average power $P_\text{w}$ of the wind turbines in December–February and whether it meets the city's needs

Principle

The table gives what share of the annual energy is produced and consumed in winter. The average power in winter is the energy of winter divided by the hours of winter. The average power only tells whether the energy is enough on average: consumption and wind vary even during the winter, so the need must also be compared with the peak power and with the power during a calm spell.

Formula

$P_\text{w}$$= \dfrac{0.32 \cdot N E_1}{t}$

and the city's average power in winter $P_\text{c} = \dfrac{0.31 \cdot E}{t}$

Conversions

$t$$= 90 \cdot 24\,\text{h}$
$= 2160\,\text{h}$

$1\,\text{GWh} = 1000\,\text{MWh}$

Substitution

$N E_1$$= 28 \cdot 14.016\,\text{GWh}$
$= 392.4\,\text{GWh}$
$P_\text{w}$$= \dfrac{0.32 \cdot 392.4\,\text{GWh}}{2160\,\text{h}}$
$= \dfrac{125\,600\,\text{MWh}}{2160\,\text{h}}$
$= 58.1\,\text{MW}$
$P_\text{c}$$= \dfrac{0.31 \cdot 380\,\text{GWh}}{2160\,\text{h}}$
$= \dfrac{117\,800\,\text{MWh}}{2160\,\text{h}}$
$= 54.5\,\text{MW}$

During a calm spell the power is

$P_\text{wind}$$< 0.05 \cdot 28 \cdot 5.0\,\text{MW}$
$= 7\,\text{MW}$

so the power needed from elsewhere is

$P_\text{elsewhere}$$> 80\,\text{MW} - 7\,\text{MW}$
$= 73\,\text{MW}$

Answer

The average power of the wind turbines in winter is $P_\text{w} \approx 58\,\text{MW}$, to two significant figures like the percentages in the table. It is slightly larger than the city's average demand in winter, $55\,\text{MW}$, so the winter energy is enough on average. On a calm, frosty evening, however, the combined output of the wind turbines can fall below $7\,\text{MW}$. If the city's power demand is then $80\,\text{MW}$, more than $73\,\text{MW}$ of electric power is needed from elsewhere.

Reasonableness

Winter is a quarter of the year and accounts for about a third of both production and consumption, so the average powers in winter are larger than the average powers over the year ($45\,\text{MW}$ and $43\,\text{MW}$), as they should be. Both average powers are smaller than the peak power of $80\,\text{MW}$, as an average must be.

d)

Find

how much the solar parks help on the peak evening, and which production methods could produce power on a calm, frosty evening, with their limitations

Principle

The annual energy does not tell whether power is available exactly when it is needed. On a peak evening the power at that moment is what matters. It can be produced by plants whose power can be adjusted or which run steadily regardless of the weather. Controllability and weather dependence are properties that have limits, not absolute categories.

Model answer

The annual energy of one solar park is $0.11 \cdot 10\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h} \approx 9.6\,\text{GWh}$, so $40$ parks produce about $385\,\text{GWh}$ per year. In winter, according to the table, only $4\,\%$ of that comes, about $15\,\text{GWh}$, which corresponds to an average power of $\dfrac{15\,400\,\text{MWh}}{2160\,\text{h}} \approx 7\,\text{MW}$. An evening in January is dark, so the power at the peak is zero. Solar electricity is mostly produced in summer, when consumption is at its lowest.

On a calm, frosty evening power could be produced by
- a natural gas plant ($50\,\text{MW}$): the power can be raised quickly, but gas is an imported fuel and its emission factor is the highest of the new alternatives
- a biomass plant ($40\,\text{MW}$): controllable, but slower to start, and the fuel has to be transported and stored
- a share of hydropower ($20\,\text{MW}$): the power can be adjusted quickly, but the energy of a year depends on rainfall, so the water cannot be used without limit
- a small nuclear plant ($60\,\text{MW}$): runs steadily regardless of the weather and is therefore running also on the peak evening, but the power cannot be raised above the rated power, and the plant is not yet available
- electricity bought from the grid: available when production elsewhere has spare capacity, but the consumption of the whole of Finland is at its highest on the same frosty evenings.

e)

Find

a justified recommendation for the city's electricity production with calculations, a counterargument and assumptions

Principle

A good recommendation covers two different requirements: the annual energy (the calculations of parts a and b) and the winter peak power (parts c and d). The emission is calculated separately for each production method with the formula $m = e \cdot E$ and added together. The choice is a value judgement in which, besides emissions, controllability, fuel, land use, waste and schedule also weigh. That is why there is no single right production method, and the answer is assessed on the basis of the reasoning. Below is one possible recommendation.

Model answer

I recommend a combination: $18$ wind turbines, the city's share of the hydropower plant and one biomass plant that runs mainly when the wind is not enough.

Energy. The wind turbines produce $18 \cdot 14.0\,\text{GWh} \approx 252\,\text{GWh}$ and the hydropower share $0.50 \cdot 20\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h} \approx 88\,\text{GWh}$ per year. The remaining $380\,\text{GWh} - 252\,\text{GWh} - 88\,\text{GWh} \approx 40\,\text{GWh}$ is produced by the biomass plant, whose capacity factor is then only about $\dfrac{40\,100\,\text{MWh}}{40\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h}} \approx 0.11$.

Emission. $m = 11\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 252 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh} + 24\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 88 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh} + 230\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 40 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t} + 2.1 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t} + 9.2 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t} \approx 1.4 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{t}$ per year. That is about $95\,\%$ less than with coal ($3.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}$) and clearly less than with natural gas alone ($1.9 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{t}$). Wind power alone ($4.2 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t}$) would have lower emissions, but it does not cover the peak.

Peak. On a calm, frosty evening the biomass plant ($40\,\text{MW}$), hydropower ($20\,\text{MW}$) and the wind turbines (below $0.05 \cdot 90\,\text{MW} \approx 4.5\,\text{MW}$) give about $65\,\text{MW}$ in total. The remaining $15\,\text{MW}$ or so is bought from the grid, or part of the consumption, such as electric heating, is shifted a few hours later.

Other arguments. The combination does not depend on a single fuel or a single kind of weather: wind power has no fuel, and the power of hydropower and biomass can be adjusted. The wind turbines take up land and change the landscape. The biomass plant needs wood chips, but only about a sixth of what it would need at full capacity factor.

The strongest counterargument is that the biomass plant stands idle for much of the year, so building it is expensive per unit of energy produced, and the carbon dioxide from burning biomass is bound back only over decades, so the real emission can be larger than the assumed $230\,\text{g/kWh}$. My answer is that the plant is needed precisely because of its controllability: without it, more than $50\,\text{MW}$ would be needed from the grid on the peak evening, when the consumption of the whole country is at its highest. The share of biomass in the energy is only about $11\,\%$.

Assumptions. I assumed that the capacity factors are the same every year. Windiness and rainfall vary, so in a bad year the biomass plant runs more and the emission grows. I also assumed that consumption stays at $380\,\text{GWh}$. If heating and transport are electrified, consumption grows and more wind turbines are needed. The price and the reinforcement of the electricity grid were left uncalculated, although they may decide the matter.

Also accepted

Any production method or combination receives full points when the recommendation covers the annual energy and either covers the winter peak or estimates the missing power and how it is handled, the emission has been calculated correctly, the limitations have been discussed, and a counterargument and assumptions have been presented. For example: - A small nuclear plant. It produces $0.90 \cdot 60\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h} \approx 473\,\text{GWh}$ per year, which is more than the city needs, and the emission for the whole consumption would be only $12\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 380 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh} \approx 4.6 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{t}$. The reasoning must discuss that the plant is not yet available and that construction takes more than ten years (what the city does in the meantime), that $20\,\text{MW}$ is missing from the peak, and that spent fuel must be disposed of permanently. - Wind power with a natural gas plant as reserve power. When the gas plant runs only during calm spells, the emission stays small. The reasoning estimates what share of the energy is produced with gas and calculates its emission. - Two biomass plants. They cover the peak of $80\,\text{MW}$ and the annual energy at a capacity factor of $\dfrac{380\,000\,\text{MWh}}{80\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h}} \approx 0.54$. The emission $230\,\text{g/kWh} \cdot 380 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{kWh} \approx 8.7 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{t}$ is about $72\,\%$ lower than with coal. The reasoning discusses the availability of wood chips, transport and the slow binding of carbon, and that the factor $230\,\text{g/kWh}$ is an accounting assumption from which the real factor of forest wood chips can differ. - Wind power and buying electricity from the grid during peaks, when the dependence on the grid on the coldest days is noted and assessed.

Points are given not for the production method chosen but for how the choice is justified.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the energy is calculated with the rated power without the capacity factor: $5.0\,\text{MW} \cdot 8760\,\text{h} = 43.8\,\text{GWh}$, so it seems that only $9$ turbines are needed.
  • In part a $27.1$ is rounded down to $27$, so some electricity is left missing.
  • In part b gigawatt-hours are not converted to kilowatt-hours, or grams are not converted to tonnes.
  • In part c it is concluded from the average power that wind power is always enough in winter, even though the peak power and calm spells are decisive. Energy and power are confused.
  • Wind power, solar power or nuclear power is called emission-free, even though their life-cycle emission factors are also $11\text{–}48\,\text{g/kWh}$.
  • Nuclear power is considered completely non-adjustable or hydropower unlimited: the power of a nuclear plant can be reduced, and the energy of hydropower depends on rainfall.
  • In part e only an opinion is given, without a calculation, or the winter peak is left undiscussed.

2Two sources on the same energy question 14 points

Many figures are published about energy production, and two publications can give very different answers to the same question. A difference does not always mean that one of them is wrong: the publications may calculate a different thing, for a different year or with a different boundary. In this task you find out what causes the difference between two publications.

Choose a question. Choose one question about energy production that is answered with a number. For example:

  • How large are the carbon dioxide emissions of a production method, for example wind power, nuclear power, natural gas or peat, per unit of energy produced?
  • What share of Finland's electricity or of all its energy is produced from renewable energy sources?
  • How large is the capacity factor or the annual production of a production method in Finland?

You can also choose your own question, as long as it is answered with a number and two publications can be found on it.

Find two publications. Find two publications yourself that answer the same question and have different publishers. The publishers can be, for example, a statistics authority, a research institute, an international organisation, a ministry, an energy company or its trade association, an environmental organisation or a news medium. Record a reference for each: author or publisher, title, year of publication, web address and the date on which you read the publication.

Model answer

a)

Find

the question, the references of the two sources and the author, publisher and purpose of each

Principle

From the reference the reader can find the publication themselves. The author and the publisher tell who is responsible for the figure, and the purpose tells why the publication was made. The purpose does not make the figure wrong, but it can affect which figures the publication chooses and how it presents them. Therefore a source is not rejected because of its publisher; instead its purpose is taken into account in the interpretation.

Model answer

Structure. The question in one sentence. For each source the reference, the author or publisher, the type of publication and its purpose, and one or two sentences on how the purpose may show in the figures.

Example. My question is how large the greenhouse gas emission of wind power is per unit of electricity produced.

References:

  • Source 1: Schlömer, S. et al. (2014). Annex III: Technology-specific cost and performance parameters. In Climate Change 2014: Mitigation of Climate Change. Contribution of Working Group III to the Fifth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Cambridge University Press. Accessed (date of reading).
  • Source 2 (invented example): Esimerkkienergia Oy. Tuulivoima – puhdasta sähköä [Wind power – clean electricity]. Web page, updated (year). (web address). Accessed (date of reading).

Source 1 is an annex of an assessment report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the IPCC, which collects the emissions of production methods from many studies. Its authors are researchers, and its purpose is to collect research findings as a basis for decision-making. The report gives a range and a median and does not recommend any production method.

Source 2 is the web page of an energy company on which the company presents its wind farms. Its purpose is to inform customers about the company's production and to market it. Therefore the page tells about the advantages of wind power and does not necessarily tell how the figure has been calculated.

b)

Find

the time of publication of the sources, the period of the data, and the figures with their quantities and units in the same unit

Principle

The time of publication and the period of the data are different things: a new publication can use old data. Production methods and their equipment change, so figures from different years can differ for that reason alone. Figures can be compared only when they are the same quantity and in the same unit. The units of an emission factor are converted into each other by converting both the mass prefix and the energy prefix.

Model answer

Structure. For each source the year of publication and the years of the data, the figure with its quantity and unit and, if needed, the conversion to the same unit.

Example. Source 1 was published in 2014, and it collects studies published before that. Source 2 was updated last year, but it does not say which year the figure is from. Source 1 gives the median of the life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions of onshore wind power as $11\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}}$ in carbon dioxide equivalent. The quantity is thus the combined effect of all greenhouse gases converted into an amount of carbon dioxide. Source 2 says that wind power produces electricity without carbon dioxide emissions, so its figure is $0\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}}$, and it concerns carbon dioxide only. If one source gave the emission in the unit $\tfrac{\text{t}}{\text{GWh}}$, the conversion would be $11\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}} = \tfrac{11 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{t}}{10^{-6}\,\text{GWh}} = 11\,\tfrac{\text{t}}{\text{GWh}}$, so the number stays the same.

c)

Find

the system boundary, assumptions and uncertainty of each source

Principle

The system boundary tells what the figure includes. Electricity is only part of all energy, because energy is also used for heating, transport and industry. Operational emissions are produced when the power plant produces energy. Life-cycle emissions also include the construction of the plant, the supply of fuel, maintenance and decommissioning. Therefore a production method that has no operational emissions can still cause life-cycle emissions. Assumptions such as the plant type, the region and the capacity factor change the figure, and the uncertainty shows as a range.

Model answer

Structure. For each source: electricity or total energy, operational or life-cycle emission (or the corresponding boundary for another question), the most important assumptions and the uncertainty. If the source does not tell something, this is stated.

Example. Both sources concern electricity. Source 1 calculates life-cycle emissions, so the manufacture, construction and decommissioning of the plants are included. Source 2 means operational emissions: a rotating wind turbine burns nothing. Source 1 assumes the capacity factors and plant lifetimes obtained from its studies, and the result depends on them: a longer lifetime spreads the construction emissions over a larger amount of energy. Besides the median, source 1 gives the range of the studies, $7\text{–}56\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}}$ for onshore wind power, so its uncertainty is visible. Source 2 does not give the uncertainty, but the operational emission is zero by definition.

d)

Find

the comparability of the sources and a justified conclusion

Principle

Figures are comparable when they are the same quantity in the same unit, from the same period or at least from a period in which the matter has not had time to change, and with the same system boundary and the same key assumptions. If one of these differs, the difference can explain the difference between the figures. The conclusion distinguishes what the sources say from what can be inferred from them.

Model answer

Structure. Comparability item by item, an explanation of the difference between the figures and a conclusion that answers the question or justifies why the sources are not enough for it.

Example. The figures are in the same unit, and both concern electricity. The quantity is almost the same: source 1 counts all greenhouse gases in carbon dioxide equivalent and source 2 only carbon dioxide. The figures are still not directly comparable, because the system boundary is different: source 1 gives the life-cycle emission and source 2 the operational emission. The difference between $11\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}}$ and $0\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}}$ is explained by this, and neither source is wrong. My conclusion is that wind power has no operational carbon dioxide emissions and that its life-cycle greenhouse gas emission is about $11\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{kWh}}$ in carbon dioxide equivalent according to source 1. When comparing production methods I use life-cycle emissions, because in them all production methods are calculated with the same boundary. The claim that wind power is emission-free holds only for the time of operation.

Another equally good conclusion would be, for example, this: the sources are not comparable, because source 2 states neither its boundary nor its year, and an answer requires a source that gives the life-cycle emission with the same boundary as source 1.

Also accepted

  • Any energy production question that is answered with a number, and any two publications with different publishers. The points come from the assessment, not from the choice of sources.
  • A justified conclusion that the figures are not comparable, for example because one concerns electricity and the other all energy, or because the data are from different decades. In that case part d tells what kind of source would be needed for a comparison.
  • A conclusion that the sources are comparable and support each other, when it has been justified item by item.
  • Different conclusions from the same sources, when they have been justified with the information in the sources and do not claim more than the sources support.

Common mistakes

  • The source is a search engine results page, an AI answer or the front page of a website on which the figure cannot be found. The reference lacks the publisher, the year or the date of reading.
  • The sources have the same publisher, for example two pages of the same company, or a news item that only repeats the figure of the other source.
  • A source is rejected on the basis of its publisher alone, for example because it is a company page, even though its figure may be correct within its own boundary.
  • The time of publication and the period of the data are confused.
  • Figures are compared in different units, or in the conversion only the mass prefix or only the energy prefix is converted.
  • Electricity and total energy, or operational and life-cycle emissions, are compared with each other without noticing the difference in the boundary.
  • The conclusion chooses one of the sources as correct without justification, or it claims more on the basis of the sources than they say.
