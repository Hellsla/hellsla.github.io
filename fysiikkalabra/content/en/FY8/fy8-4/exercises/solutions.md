## fy8-4-L1-01
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 532\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the momentum $p$ of a photon in units of $10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** A photon has no mass, but it has a momentum that depends only on the wavelength. The light has one wavelength, so every photon has the same momentum.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $532\,\text{nm} = 532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. Unit: $\tfrac{\text{J}\,\text{s}}{\text{m}} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{532 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 1.2455 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $1.25 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** According to the theory section, the momentum of a photon of $500\,\text{nm}$ is $1.3 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. The wavelength $532\,\text{nm}$ is a little longer, so the momentum is a little smaller, as it should be. The value is very small because $h$ is very small.

## fy8-4-L2-01
**Given:** wavelength of the incident photon $\lambda = 0.0711\,\text{nm}$, of the scattered one $\lambda' = 0.0735\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ received by the electron in electronvolts
**Principle:** In the Compton effect, the photon collides with an almost free electron. Energy is conserved, so the electron receives the energy that the photon loses: $hf = hf' + E_\text{k}$. The energies of the photons are obtained from the wavelengths.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} + E_\text{k}$, from which $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'}$
**Conversions:** $0.0711\,\text{nm} = 0.0711 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $0.0735\,\text{nm} = 0.0735 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. Finally joules to electronvolts by substituting $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0711 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} - \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0735 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 2.7939 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} - 2.7027 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} = 9.123 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$ and in electronvolts $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{9.123 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV} = 569.5\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $5.7 \cdot 10^2\,\text{eV}$, two significant figures, because the difference of the wavelengths $0.0024\,\text{nm}$ is known only to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of the incident photon is $\tfrac{2.7939 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV} \approx 17.4\,\text{keV}$. The wavelength grows by about $3\,\%$, so the photon loses about $3\,\%$ of its energy, about $0.6\,\text{keV}$. The electron gains energy, so the result is positive.

## fy8-4-L2-03
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 1550\,\text{nm}$, the photon is reflected perpendicularly back; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the momentum $\Delta p$ received by the mirror from one photon in units of $10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Momentum is conserved. In reflection the momentum of the photon reverses, so its change is $p - (-p) = 2p$. The mirror receives a momentum of the same magnitude in the direction of arrival of the photon, because the total momentum is conserved. The wavelength of the reflected photon is the same, because a heavy mirror hardly receives any energy.
**Formula:** $\Delta p = p - (-p)$, where $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so $\Delta p = 2 \cdot \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $1550\,\text{nm} = 1550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $\Delta p = \dfrac{2 \cdot 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{1550 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 8.550 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $8.55 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures as in the wavelength $1550\,\text{nm}$ (the constant $2$ is exact).
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength is about three times that of green light, $532\,\text{nm}$, so the momentum of one photon is about a third of $1.25 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, that is, about $4 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, and the reflection doubles it.

## fy8-4-L3-01
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 589\,\text{nm}$, mass of the atom $m = 3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, initial speed $v_0 = 3.00 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m/s}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the number $N$ of photons needed, in units of $10^4$
**Principle:** Momentum is conserved. When the atom absorbs a photon coming against it, the momentum of the atom decreases by the momentum $\tfrac{h}{\lambda}$ of the photon, so its speed decreases by $\Delta v = \tfrac{h}{\lambda m}$. The atom stops when $N$ photons have reduced its momentum $m v_0$ to zero. The re-emitted photons leave in random directions, so on average their effects cancel.
**Formula:** $m v_0 = N p$, where $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so $N = \dfrac{m v_0}{p}$
**Conversions:** $589\,\text{nm} = 589 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{589 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 1.1250 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ and $m v_0 = 3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m/s} = 1.146 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, so $N = \dfrac{1.146 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{1.1250 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}} = 1.0187 \cdot 10^4$
**Answer:** $1.02 \cdot 10^4$ photons, three significant figures as in the mass, the speed and the wavelength.
**Reasonableness:** One photon changes the speed of the atom by $\tfrac{1.1250 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}} \approx 0.029\,\text{m/s}$, so stopping $300\,\text{m/s}$ takes about $300 : 0.029 \approx 10\,000$ photons. An atom can absorb and emit photons millions of times per second, so the braking takes only about a millisecond.

## fy8-4-L1-06
**Asked:** which explanation fits the fact that photons arriving one at a time build up a stripe pattern.
**Principle:** In the double-slit experiment the wave gives the probability of detection, and when detected, the photon is registered as a whole in one place. The stripe pattern is therefore a distribution of hits and not the trace of a single photon.
**Model answer:** The place where a single photon hits is random, but the wave determines the probability with which it is registered at each place. The most hits accumulate in the bright stripes and the fewest in the dark ones, so thousands of hits build up a stripe pattern. Collisions between photons are not a valid explanation, because the photons arrive one at a time and the pattern still forms. A photon does not split into parts either, nor does it travel like a bullet through only one of the slits, because the bullet model would not produce dark stripes.

## fy8-4-L1-08
**Asked:** what is wrong in the student's reasoning.
**Principle:** The momentum of a photon does not come from mass but from energy: $p = \tfrac{E}{c} = \tfrac{h}{\lambda}$. Momentum is conserved, so in a reflection the momentum lost by the photon is transferred to the mirror.
**Model answer:** The error is the assumption that momentum requires mass. A photon has no mass, but it has momentum that depends on its energy. When a photon reflects from a mirror, its momentum reverses, and the mirror receives the change in momentum. Light therefore does push the mirror. The push is explained neither by heat nor by a mass of the photon, and momentum does not disappear in the reflection.

## fy8-4-L2-05
**Given:** the numbers of hits in seven bands when $N = 50$, $N = 500$ and $N = 5000$; band 4 in the middle (table). The bright bands are 2, 4 and 6 and the dark bands 1, 3, 5 and 7.
**Asked:** a) the share of the hits in band 4 for each value of $N$ as a percentage, b) the ratio of the number of hits in band 4 to that in band 3 when $N = 5000$, c) the smallest $N$ at which the bands are clearly distinguishable
**Principle:** The stripe pattern is a distribution of hits. The share is the number of hits in the band divided by the total number of hits $N$, and the hits of two bands are compared by dividing one by the other. With a small number of hits the random variation is large in proportion, so the pattern becomes sharper only when there are many hits.
**Formula:** Share $= \dfrac{n_4}{N} \cdot 100\,\%$ and ratio $= \dfrac{n_4}{n_3}$, where $n_4$ and $n_3$ are the numbers of hits in bands 4 and 3.
**Conversions:** Not needed: the numbers of hits are counts, and the share is given as a percentage by multiplying by one hundred.
**Substitution:** a) $\dfrac{22}{50} \cdot 100\,\% = 44\,\%$, $\dfrac{248}{500} \cdot 100\,\% = 49.6\,\%$ and $\dfrac{2377}{5000} \cdot 100\,\% = 47.5\,\%$. b) $\dfrac{n_4}{n_3} = \dfrac{2377}{208} = 11.43$. c) When $N = 500$, the bright bands have $88$, $248$ and $87$ hits and the dark ones $22$, $18$, $23$ and $14$. When $N = 50$, the bright side bands have $6$ and $7$ hits and the dark ones $3$, $3$, $5$ and $4$.
**Answer:** b) about $11$ times (calculated $11.43$). The number of hits is random: with $n$ hits the variation is of the order of $\sqrt{n}$, so $208 \pm 14$ is about $\pm 7\,\%$ and the ratio is known only to two significant figures. c) The stripes are clearly distinguishable when $N = 500$: the numbers of hits in the bright bands ($88$, $248$, $87$; variation about $\pm 9$ and $\pm 16$) are many times those in the dark ones ($14$–$23$; variation about $\pm 4$). When $N = 50$, the difference between the side bands $6$ and $7$ ($\pm 2.5$) and the dark ones ($3$–$5$; $\pm 2$) is smaller than the variation, so the stripes are not distinguishable.
**Reasonableness:** The numbers of hits in each column add up to $N$, for example $3 + 6 + 3 + 22 + 5 + 7 + 4 = 50$. The share of the middle band stays at about half of all hits ($44\,\%$, $49.6\,\%$, $47.5\,\%$), but it fluctuates the most with a small $N$. If photons travelled like bullets, about as many hits would land on the dark bands as on the bright side bands, and no dark stripes would form. Now only about $4\,\%$ of the hits land on the dark bands (for example $\tfrac{208}{5000} = 4.2\,\%$).

## fy8-4-L3-05
**Given:** wavelength of the incident photon $\lambda = 0.07110\,\text{nm}$, of the scattered photon $\lambda' = 0.07476\,\text{nm}$ at the angle $\theta = 120\,{}^\circ$ (third row of the table), the electron initially at rest, $m_\text{e} = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the magnitude $p_\text{e}$ of the momentum of the electron in units of $10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ and a comparison of the kinetic energies
**Principle:** Momentum is conserved as a vector quantity: $\overline{p} = \overline{p}' + \overline{p}_e$, so $\overline{p}_e = \overline{p} - \overline{p}'$. The vectors $\overline{p}$, $\overline{p}'$ and $\overline{p}_e$ form a triangle in which the angle between $\overline{p}$ and $\overline{p}'$ is the scattering angle $\theta$. The momentum of a photon is $\tfrac{h}{\lambda}$. Energy is also conserved, so the kinetic energy of the electron is the energy lost by the photon.
**Formula:** $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, $p' = \dfrac{h}{\lambda'}$ and by the law of cosines $p_\text{e} = \sqrt{p^2 + p'^2 - 2pp'\cos\theta}$. The kinetic energy is $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{p_\text{e}^2}{2m_\text{e}}$ or $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'}$.
**Conversions:** $0.07110\,\text{nm} = 0.07110 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $0.07476\,\text{nm} = 0.07476 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. At the angle $\theta = 120\,{}^\circ$, $\cos 120\,{}^\circ = -0.5$.
**Substitution:** a) $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{0.07110 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 9.319 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ and $p' = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{0.07476 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 8.863 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. b) Drawing: two sides of the vector triangle are $p$ and $p'$ with the angle between them $\theta = 120\,{}^\circ$ (the vectors are drawn from the same point), and the third side is $p_\text{e}$; the table rows $60\,{}^\circ$ and $90\,{}^\circ$ are not needed. Law of cosines: $p_\text{e}^2 = (9.319^2 + 8.863^2 + 9.319 \cdot 8.863) \cdot 10^{-48}\,\text{kg}^2\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = 2.480 \cdot 10^{-46}\,\text{kg}^2\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so $p_\text{e} = 1.575 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. c) From the momentum, $E_\text{k} = \dfrac{2.480 \cdot 10^{-46}\,\text{kg}^2\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{2 \cdot 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}} = 1.361 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J} = 850\,\text{eV}$. From conservation of energy, $E_\text{k} = 2.794 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} - 2.657 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} = 1.368 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J} = 854\,\text{eV}$.
**Answer:** $1.57 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, three significant figures, because the change in wavelength $\Delta\lambda = 0.07476\,\text{nm} - 0.07110\,\text{nm} = 0.00366\,\text{nm} = 3.66\,\text{pm}$ is known to three digits. The two values of the kinetic energy, $850\,\text{eV}$ and $854\,\text{eV}$, differ from each other by only about $0.5\,\%$, so they agree: conservation of momentum and conservation of energy give the same result. The small difference is due to the change in wavelength $3.66\,\text{pm}$ being known to only three digits.
**Reasonableness:** The side $p_\text{e}$ of the triangle must be larger than the difference of the photon momenta and smaller than their sum: $0.456 \cdot 10^{-23} < 1.575 \cdot 10^{-23} < 1.818 \cdot 10^{-23}$ (unit $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$). The angle $120\,{}^\circ$ is close to backscattering, so the result is closer to the sum, as it should be. The electron received about $850\,\text{eV}$, that is, about $5\,\%$ of the energy $17.4\,\text{keV}$ of the incident photon ($E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = 2.794 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$), and the wavelength grew by about $5\,\%$, which is of the same order of magnitude.
