**Model answer:** The skier is drawn as a rectangle parallel to the slope.
- The weight $\overline{G}$ starts from the centre and points straight down, not perpendicular to the slope.
- The normal force $\overline{N}$ starts from the lower surface and points perpendicular to the slope, away from it. It is equal to the component of the weight perpendicular to the slope, so it is slightly smaller than the weight.
- The kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ starts from the upper corner of the lower surface and points up the slope, because the skier slides down the slope.

The acceleration $\overline{a}$ points down the slope, so the sum of the forces also points down the slope: the component of the weight along the slope is larger than the friction. The acceleration arrow is drawn next to the object in its own colour, because it is not a force. The dashed arrows $G_x$ and $G_y$ in the model figure are the components of the weight along the slope and perpendicular to it, not new forces: together they replace the weight and help with the equations of motion in part c. They do not need to be drawn in your own free-body diagram. In the model figure the slope is drawn steeper and the friction larger than in the problem so that the arrows can be told apart: on a $4.0^\circ$ slope the normal force is almost equal to the weight and the friction is very short.

### c)
**Given:** the angle of inclination of the slope $\alpha = 4.0^\circ$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the graph of part a; mass $m = 72\,\text{kg}$
**Find:** the coefficients of kinetic friction $\mu_\text{A}$ and $\mu_\text{B}$
**Principle:** The graphs are straight lines, so the acceleration is constant and it is the slope of the line. By Newton's second law the acceleration is caused by the sum of the forces, so the friction force is deduced from the acceleration and the coefficient of friction from that. On a slope it is best to choose the axes along the slope, because the motion is along the slope.
**Free-body diagram:** The diagram of part b: the $x$-axis down the slope and the $y$-axis perpendicular to the slope, away from it. The components of the weight are $G_x = mg\sin\alpha$ down the slope and $G_y = mg\cos\alpha$ towards the slope. The normal force is in the direction of the $y$-axis and the friction opposite to the $x$-axis.
**Reading the graph:** The line of wax A passes through the points $(0\,\text{s};\ 2.10\,\text{m/s})$ and $(8\,\text{s};\ 5.22\,\text{m/s})$, so $a_\text{A} = \dfrac{3.12\,\text{m/s}}{8\,\text{s}} = 0.39\,\text{m/s}^2$. The line of wax B passes through the points $(0\,\text{s};\ 1.90\,\text{m/s})$ and $(8\,\text{s};\ 3.86\,\text{m/s})$, so $a_\text{B} = \dfrac{1.96\,\text{m/s}}{8\,\text{s}} = 0.245\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Formula:** $y$: $N - mg\cos\alpha = 0$, so $N = mg\cos\alpha$. $x$: $mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = ma$, that is, $mg\sin\alpha - \mu mg\cos\alpha = ma$. The mass cancels, and $\mu = \dfrac{g\sin\alpha - a}{g\cos\alpha}$.
**Conversions:** Not needed, the accelerations are in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$.
**Substitution:** $g\sin\alpha = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot \sin 4.0^\circ = 0.684\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g\cos\alpha = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot \cos 4.0^\circ = 9.786\,\text{m/s}^2$. $\mu_\text{A} = \dfrac{(0.684 - 0.39)\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.786\,\text{m/s}^2} = 0.030$ and $\mu_\text{B} = \dfrac{(0.684 - 0.245)\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.786\,\text{m/s}^2} = 0.045$
**Answer:** $\mu_\text{A} \approx 0.030$ and $\mu_\text{B} \approx 0.045$. The coefficient of friction has no unit. Two significant figures, because the slopes are read from the graph to about two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The coefficient of friction between a waxed ski and snow is a few hundredths, much smaller than, for example, between a shoe and a floor. Both accelerations are smaller than $g\sin\alpha = 0.684\,\text{m/s}^2$, which the skier would get without friction. The mass $72\,\text{kg}$ was not needed, because the weight, the normal force and the friction are all proportional to the mass.

### d)
**Find:** how reliable the conclusion "wax A glides better" is
**Principle:** The conclusion is reliable if the difference between the waxes is clearly larger than the uncertainty of the results and if the sources of error cannot turn the difference the other way round.
**Model answer:** *Uncertainty.* The points are close to the lines, so the slope can be read to an accuracy of about $\pm 0.02\,\text{m/s}^2$. In the coefficient of friction this means $0.02\,\text{m/s}^2 : 9.786\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.002$. An uncertainty in the angle of the slope, for example $\pm 0.1^\circ$, changes both coefficients by about $0.002$ in the same direction, so it does not change the difference. The difference between the waxes, $0.045 - 0.030 = 0.015$, is many times larger.

*Air resistance.* Air resistance points up the slope like friction, so it reduces the acceleration. When it is left out, all of the slowing effect is counted as friction, and both coefficients of friction come out larger than the true ones. Air resistance grows with the speed, and in run A the skier moves faster, so the coefficient of wax A is overestimated more. The true advantage of wax A is therefore at least as large as the calculated one.

*Conclusion.* With this data wax A glides better. However, each wax has been tried only once, and the snow or the wind may change between the runs. A more certain result is obtained by repeating the runs alternately with each wax.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part c the slope can also be obtained from a straight-line fit by a calculator or spreadsheet ($0.390$ and $0.245\,\text{m/s}^2$) or from two other points of the line far from each other. Slopes of $0.37\text{–}0.41\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $0.23\text{–}0.26\,\text{m/s}^2$ are accepted.
- In part b the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ may be drawn up the slope when it is named, but the problem does not require it.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The coefficient of friction is calculated as $\mu = a : g$ as on a horizontal surface, which forgets the inclination of the slope. Then wax A would seem to glide worse, because its acceleration is larger.
- The normal force is taken to be $N = mg$, although on a slope $N = mg\cos\alpha$. At a small angle the error is small, but the justification is wrong.
- A forward force is drawn in the free-body diagram because the skier moves down. The motion along the slope is caused by the component of the weight.
- The components of the weight are drawn as forces of their own in addition to the weight, so the weight is counted twice.
- The slope is read from two adjacent measurement points, so the random error of a single point has a large effect on the result.

## fy4-s2

### a)
**Given:** the height difference from the starting platform to the beginning of the brake $h = 15.0\,\text{m}$, the rider starts from rest, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the speed $v$ at the beginning of the brake with the instructor's model
**Principle:** The instructor's figure is obtained when friction and air resistance are not taken into account. Then only the weight and the force of the cable through the trolley act on the rider. The force of the cable is perpendicular to the cable, that is, to the motion, so it does no work. Only the weight does work, so the mechanical energy is conserved: potential energy changes into kinetic energy. The shape and length of the cable then do not affect the final speed, only the height difference.
**Formula:** $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$
**Conversions:** Not needed in the calculation. For comparison $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 15.0\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{294.3}\,\text{m/s} = 17.2\,\text{m/s}$ and $17.2 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} = 61.8\,\text{km/h}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 17.2\,\text{m/s} \approx 62\,\text{km/h}$, three significant figures like the height $15.0\,\text{m}$. The instructor's claim is thus based on a model in which mechanical energy is conserved.
**Reasonableness:** The same speed would be reached if the rider fell freely $15\,\text{m}$. It is the greatest possible speed, because in reality part of the energy changes into heat.

### b)
**Given:** $m = 62\,\text{kg}$, $h = 15.0\,\text{m}$, the length of the cable from the starting platform to the beginning of the brake $s = 180\,\text{m}$ and the measured speed $v = 11.2\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the force $F_\text{res}$ opposing the motion on average
**Principle:** The measured $11.2\,\text{m/s} \approx 40\,\text{km/h}$ is much smaller than the $17.2\,\text{m/s}$ of part a, so mechanical energy is not conserved. The friction of the trolley wheels and air resistance oppose the motion for the whole distance and do negative work. By the work–energy principle $E_{\text{k},1} + E_{\text{p},1} + W_\text{other} = E_{\text{k},2} + E_{\text{p},2}$. The zero level is chosen to be the beginning of the brake, where the speed was measured. When the combined effect of the resisting forces is described by an average force $F_\text{res}$ pointing against the direction of motion, its work is $W_\text{other} = -F_\text{res}\,s$.
**Formula:** $mgh - F_\text{res}\,s = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $F_\text{res} = \dfrac{mgh - \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2}{s}$
**Conversions:** Not needed, all quantities are in base units.
**Substitution:** $E_\text{p} = 62\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 15.0\,\text{m} = 9123\,\text{J}$ and $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 62\,\text{kg} \cdot (11.2\,\text{m/s})^2 = 3889\,\text{J}$. $F_\text{res} = \dfrac{9123\,\text{J} - 3889\,\text{J}}{180\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{5234\,\text{J}}{180\,\text{m}} = 29.1\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $F_\text{res} \approx 29\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $62\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The force is about $5\,\%$ of the rider's weight $608\,\text{N}$, which fits the friction of the wheels and air resistance. However, the distance is long, so more than half of the energy changes into heat: $5234\,\text{J} : 9123\,\text{J} \approx 0.57$.

### c)
**Given:** $m = 95\,\text{kg}$, $h = 15.0\,\text{m}$, $s = 180\,\text{m}$ and the work of the resisting force from part b, $F_\text{res}\,s = 5234\,\text{J}$ ($F_\text{res} \approx 29\,\text{N}$)
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the heavier rider at the beginning of the brake with the instructor's assumption
**Principle:** The same energy principle as in part b, but now the unknown is the speed. According to the assumption the work of the resisting force is the same as for the light rider, but the potential energy is larger in proportion to the mass.
**Formula:** $mgh - F_\text{res}\,s = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\,(mgh - F_\text{res}\,s)}{m}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed.
**Substitution:** $mgh = 95\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 15.0\,\text{m} = 13\,979\,\text{J}$ and from part b $F_\text{res}\,s = 5234\,\text{J}$, so $E_\text{k} = 13\,979\,\text{J} - 5234\,\text{J} = 8745\,\text{J}$. $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 8745\,\text{J}}{95\,\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{184.1}\,\text{m/s} = 13.6\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 14\,\text{m/s}$ (more precisely $13.6\,\text{m/s} \approx 49\,\text{km/h}$). Two significant figures, because the mass $95\,\text{kg}$ and the resisting force $29\,\text{N}$ are known to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The speed is between the $11.2\,\text{m/s}$ of the light rider and the $17.2\,\text{m/s}$ of the frictionless model, as it should be: the same resisting work takes a smaller share of the energy of the heavy rider.

### d)
**Find:** the estimate of each instructor's model and according to which estimate the brake is designed
**Principle:** The models differ in how the resisting force depends on the mass of the rider. From the energy equation we can see what each assumption means for the speed.
**Model answer:** *The other instructor's model.* If the whole resisting force were friction proportional to the normal force and thus to the mass, $F_\text{res} = k\,mg$, the energy equation would be $mgh - k\,mg\,s = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. The mass cancels, and every rider would get the same speed, $11.2\,\text{m/s}$.

*The resisting forces separated.* The friction of the trolley wheels grows with the mass, because the normal force between the cable and the wheels grows with the rider's weight. Air resistance, on the other hand, depends on the speed and on the size and posture of the rider, not on the mass. For a heavy rider air resistance is therefore a smaller part of the weight, and they arrive faster than a light rider.

*The estimates.* The estimate $14\,\text{m/s}$ of part c is too large, because in it friction also stays the same, although in reality it grows with the mass. The other instructor's estimate $11.2\,\text{m/s}$ is too small, because air resistance does not grow with the mass. The true speed is between these.

*Design.* The brake is designed for the larger estimate, $14\,\text{m/s}$: if the true speed is smaller, the brake is certainly enough, but a brake designed for too small a speed could fail. The best information is obtained by measuring the speeds of heavy riders with the radar as well.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In parts b and c the rounded resisting force $F_\text{res} = 29\,\text{N}$ may be used: then $F_\text{res}\,s = 29\,\text{N} \cdot 180\,\text{m} = 5220\,\text{J}$, and the speed of part c is again $13.6\,\text{m/s} \approx 14\,\text{m/s}$. In part c, $13.6\,\text{m/s}$ and $14\,\text{m/s}$ are accepted.
- In part d the brake may be designed for the upper limit of the frictionless model, $17.2\,\text{m/s}$, or for the estimate of part c with a safety margin for heavier riders. Either is a correct and safe conclusion when justified, and earns the point for the conclusion.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part b the whole difference between the potential energy and the kinetic energy is divided by the height $15.0\,\text{m}$ and not by the distance travelled by the resisting force, $180\,\text{m}$.
- In part a the acceleration and distance along the cable are calculated using the angle, although conservation of energy needs only the height difference.
- In part c the mass is assumed to cancel as in part a, which gives the same $11.2\,\text{m/s}$ without justifying why the resisting force would grow with the mass.
- In part d it is only stated that "the heavy one goes faster" or "the mass does not matter", without comparing either claim with the resisting forces.

### a)
**Given:** $m_\text{A} = 1250\,\text{kg}$, $m_\text{B} = 1700\,\text{kg}$, sliding distance $s = 4.2\,\text{m}$, coefficient of friction $\mu = 0.75$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the cars' common speed $v'$ right after the collision
**Principle:** After the collision the cars move as one object with the mass $m = m_\text{A} + m_\text{B}$. The road is horizontal, so there is no acceleration in the vertical direction and the normal force is equal to the weight, $N = mg$. The wheels are locked, so the kinetic friction is $F_\mu = \mu N = \mu mg$, and it is the only horizontal force. Friction does negative work until the kinetic energy has been used up (the work–energy principle). Newton's second law gives the same result: the deceleration is $\mu g$.
**Formula:** $\tfrac{1}{2}mv'^2 - \mu mg\,s = 0$, so $v' = \sqrt{2\mu g s}$
**Conversions:** Not needed. The masses cancel, so they are not needed in this part.
**Substitution:** $v' = \sqrt{2 \cdot 0.75 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 4.2\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{61.8}\,\text{m/s} = 7.86\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v' \approx 7.9\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the distance $4.2\,\text{m}$ and the coefficient of friction $0.75$.
**Reasonableness:** The deceleration $\mu g = 0.75 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 7.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ is typical for braking on dry asphalt. The speed $7.9\,\text{m/s} \approx 28\,\text{km/h}$ is smaller than the speed of car B before the collision, as it should be, because B had to accelerate car A as well.

### b)
**Given:** $m_\text{A} = 1250\,\text{kg}$ at rest, $m_\text{B} = 1700\,\text{kg}$ and $v' = 7.86\,\text{m/s}$ from part a
**Find:** the speed $v_\text{B}$ of car B just before the collision, and whether the account is true

Therefore momentum is almost conserved. The positive direction is chosen to be the direction of motion of car B.

### c)
**Given:** the mass of the driver $m = 68\,\text{kg}$, the change in velocity $\Delta v = v' - 0 = 7.86\,\text{m/s}$ and the duration of the collision $\Delta t = 0.12\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the average force $F_\text{avg}$ of the seat back on the driver and its comparison with the weight
**Principle:** The driver is at first at rest and after the collision moves with the car at the speed $v'$. In the horizontal direction the driver's momentum is changed by the force of the seat back, so by the impulse principle its impulse is equal to the change in the driver's momentum. The force is not constant, so the average force is calculated.
**Formula:** $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = m\,\Delta v$, so $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t}$, and the comparison $\dfrac{F_\text{avg}}{mg}$
**Conversions:** Not needed.
**Substitution:** $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{68\,\text{kg} \cdot 7.86\,\text{m/s}}{0.12\,\text{s}} = 4455\,\text{N}$ and $\dfrac{F_\text{avg}}{mg} = \dfrac{4455\,\text{N}}{68\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{4455\,\text{N}}{667\,\text{N}} = 6.7$
**Answer:** $F_\text{avg} \approx 4.5\,\text{kN}$, that is, about $6.7$ times the driver's weight. Two significant figures like the duration of the collision $0.12\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The driver's average acceleration is $7.86\,\text{m/s} : 0.12\,\text{s} \approx 66\,\text{m/s}^2$, almost $7g$. It is large but typical for a rear-end collision, and the force is spread over the whole area of the seat back, so the body can withstand it. The peak force is larger than the average.

### d)
**Find:** why a head restraint and a flexible seat back reduce injuries
**Principle:** The velocity of an object changes only when a force acts on it (Newton's first law). The impulse needed for the change in velocity is $m\,\Delta v$, and the average force is $F_\text{avg} = m\,\Delta v : \Delta t$.
**Model answer:** In a rear-end collision the seat back pushes the driver's torso forward. The head is not attached to the seat back, so it tends to keep its old velocity, that is, to stay in place. The torso moves forward from under the head, and the neck bends backwards and stretches until the muscles and ligaments of the neck pull the head along. This sudden bending causes a neck injury, so-called whiplash.

When the head restraint is close to the back of the head, it pushes the head forward almost at the same time as the seat back pushes the torso. The head gets the impulse it needs directly from the head restraint, so the neck does not bend and does not have to transmit a large force to the head. A head restraint that is far away does not help, because the neck has time to bend before the head hits the restraint.

The change in the driver's velocity $\Delta v$ is the same whether the seat back is rigid or flexible. A flexible seat back lengthens the time $\Delta t$ in which the velocity changes, so the average force $F_\text{avg} = m\,\Delta v : \Delta t$ is smaller. For example, twice the time halves the average force.

### e)
**Find:** two assumptions of part b and the direction of their effect on the estimate of the speed of car B
**Principle:** For each assumption we think about whether the true speed would be larger or smaller than the calculated one.
**Model answer:** Any two, justified:
- *Momentum is conserved in the collision.* Friction already acts during the collision and reduces the cars' common momentum. Friction is at most $\mu (m_\text{A} + m_\text{B})\,g \approx 21.7\,\text{kN}$, so during $0.12\,\text{s}$ its impulse is at most about $2600\,\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, about $11\,\%$ of the momentum of car B. The true $v_\text{B}$ may thus be as much as about $15\,\text{m/s}$, so the calculated value is a lower limit.
- *The coefficient of friction is $0.75$.* If the true coefficient of friction was smaller, the same sliding distance corresponds to a smaller speed, and $v_\text{B}$ is smaller than the calculated one. With a larger coefficient it is larger.
- *The wheels were locked for the whole distance.* If some wheel turned at times, the friction was smaller, and the true $v_\text{B}$ is smaller than the calculated one.

The conclusion that the limit was exceeded is still strong: the effect of friction during the collision increases the estimate, and the coefficient of friction would have to be clearly smaller than $0.75$ for the speed to be under $40\,\text{km/h}$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part b conservation of kinetic energy is used, although the cars stay stuck together and kinetic energy is used in crumpling. Then $v_\text{B}$ comes out too small.
- In part a friction is assumed to depend only on the mass of car B, although both cars slide together.
- The speed calculated from the sliding distance is interpreted directly as the speed of car B before the collision.
- In part c the speed is converted incorrectly from $\text{km/h}$, or the force is compared with the mass and not with the weight.
- In part d it is said that "the head flies backwards". The head does not get a force directed backwards: the torso moves forward from under it.
