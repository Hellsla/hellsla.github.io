## Lesson plan (75 min)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** The cottage battery. Students estimate with a partner how large a current the battery supplies and how long the battery lasts. The answer is not given yet: it is at the end of the Key idea part of the theory summary (currents $0.50\,\text{A}$ and $4.0\,\text{A}$, $4.5\,\text{A}$ in total; the battery lasts about $22\,\text{h}$, $200\,\text{h}$ for the lights alone).
- **5–15 min, Predict (starting-level test).** Six questions individually, without notes. Ask students to write the numbers of the questions they got wrong in their notebooks.
- **15–25 min, Investigate.** The unidentified wire in pairs: why the resistance is not proportional to the length, where the resistivity is obtained from and what the material is. Discuss together: the slope gives $\tfrac{\rho}{A}$, the constant term $0.30\,\Omega$ is the resistance of the measuring leads and connections, and the wire is constantan. If time runs out, the fit is made on a ready-made graph or the interpretation of the constant term is left as homework.
- **25–35 min, Theory summary.** Go through the concept map one branch at a time: current and resistance, connections, power and energy, the source. For each formula ask when it holds (Ohm's law at constant temperature, the forms of Joule's law only for a resistor, $E$ and $R_\text{s}$ constant). Return to Think about it with the cottage circuit.
- **35–55 min, Apply.** Four multiple-choice questions in pairs (about $6\,\text{min}$), then four calculations individually. Fast students continue without hints; slower ones use the hints one at a time and in the lesson do only the calculations *A car battery on a frosty morning* and *A mixed circuit on a circuit board*, the others at home. If time runs out, the calculations *The heating wire of a toaster* and *The kettle on the electricity bill* are homework for everyone.
- **55–65 min, Check your understanding and exit ticket.** Individually: the concept and calculation tasks and the exit ticket. The explanation task (two lamps in series) is homework, and it is self-assessed with the criteria.
- **65–75 min, Problems.** Ten minutes is enough for the start of one long problem in exam conditions, without the model answer: recommended *The rear-window heater* a–b (a calculation problem) or *Torch batteries under load* a (a graph). The rest of the problem and the other five problems are homework, two or three at a time before the next lesson.

## Using the starting-level test
The Predict questions are a diagnostic test, not a grade. Each question corresponds to one chapter:

- Question 1: current is not used up in a series connection (chapters *Electric circuit, current and voltage* and *Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit*).
- Question 2: a voltmeter in series (chapter *Measurements in an electric circuit*).
- Question 3: the resistance of a filament lamp depends on the temperature of the filament (chapter *Resistance and Ohm's law*).
- Question 4: a resistor added in parallel reduces the total resistance (chapter *Resistors in series and in parallel*).
- Question 5: power in a parallel connection (chapter *Electric power and the heating of a resistor*).
- Question 6: terminal voltage and internal resistance (chapter *Electromotive force and internal resistance*).

If more than a third of the group answers question 1 or 4 wrongly (typically “current is used up” or “a battery always gives the same current”), draw the cottage circuit on the board and show that the currents of the branches add up and that each branch gets its own current at the same voltage. The Apply multiple-choice question *Which connection heats more* and the long problem *Three claims about electrical safety* return to the same idea. Mistakes in question 5 are revisited in the Apply multiple-choice question *Find the mistake: the power of a series resistor* and in the explanation task.

## Pair work
- Investigate: one student calculates the slope from the end points, the other fits a line to all the points; the results are compared and the constant term is interpreted together.
- Apply multiple-choice questions: each student first chooses alone and only then justifies the choice to the partner. The pair answers only when they agree.
- In the long problem *Three claims about electrical safety* (written), pairs can write the answers together and assess them with another pair using the marking scheme.

## Fast and slow students
- **Fast students** do the Apply calculations without hints and move straight on to the long problem *Torch batteries under load* (a graph and a fit, $20$ points), which has the most steps. Extra question: at what resistance of the bulb would the power transferred to the bulb be largest? (Try the values $0.4\,\Omega$, $0.73\,\Omega$ and $2.0\,\Omega$; the largest power is obtained when the resistance of the bulb equals the internal resistance.)
- **Slow students** do the calculations *A car battery on a frosty morning* and *A mixed circuit on a circuit board* of the Apply part with the hints and leave the other two calculations for home. The *Review if needed* links of the theory summary lead straight to the original chapters, which have basic-level exercises.

## Assessment
The long problems are self-assessed with the marking scheme. The points follow the division of the matriculation exam: the graph and fitting problem *Torch batteries under load* is of the Part III type ($20$ points), the other five of the Part II type ($15$ points each), $95$ points in total. If the teacher assesses them, the same criteria work: identifying the connection and choosing the model with reasons (for example the same voltage across parallel branches, or $E$ and $R_\text{s}$ assumed constant), the symbolic solution, unit conversions by substitution, precision and reasonableness; in the written part, justifying the claim with the concepts and numbers of physics. All the data are invented examples, not real measurements.
