Resistors in series and in parallel

Why do all the bulbs of an old string of fairy lights go out when one bulb breaks, while at home the floor lamp stays on even when the desk lamp is switched off?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how current and voltage are shared in series and parallel connections.
  • calculate the total resistance of resistors connected in series and in parallel.
  • solve the currents and voltages of a mixed connection step by step.

Prior knowledge

  • The voltage, current and resistance of a resistor are linked by Ohm's law $U = RI$chapter Resistance and Ohm's law
  • An ammeter is connected in series and a voltmeter in parallel with the component being measuredchapter Measurements in an electric circuit
  • The agreed direction of the current is from the positive terminal of the battery to the negative terminalchapter Circuit, electric current and voltage

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 3.

1Think about it

On a December evening an old string of fairy lights hangs in a window. All its bulbs went out at the same moment when one small bulb came loose from its socket. The floor lamp and the desk lamp plugged into the same extension cord are still on, though, and either of them can be switched off independently of the other.

How does the way the lamps are connected decide which lamps stop carrying current when one lamp goes out?

Optional Recall: Ohm's law
A resistor whose resistance is $100\,\Omega$ is connected to a $6.0\,\text{V}$ battery. How large is the current through the resistor?

  • The current is not the product of voltage and resistance: solving Ohm's law $U = RI$ gives $I = U : R$. The unit of the product $6.0\,\text{V} \cdot 100\,\Omega$ is not even the ampere. The correct current is $60\,\text{mA}$.

  • The voltage is divided by the resistance: $6.0\,\text{V} : 100\,\Omega = 0.060\,\text{A}$. To go from amperes to milliamperes you multiply by a thousand, so the current is $60\,\text{mA}$, not $600\,\text{mA}$.

  • Here the resistance has been divided by the voltage. Solving Ohm's law $U = RI$ for the current gives $I = U : R$, so a larger resistance gives a smaller current: $6.0\,\text{V} : 100\,\Omega = 60\,\text{mA}$.

  • Correct. By Ohm's law $I = U : R = 6.0\,\text{V} : 100\,\Omega = 0.060\,\text{A} = 60\,\text{mA}$ (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law). Today we find out what happens when there are two resistors.

How the calculation works

In a series connection $R = R_1 + R_2$ and the same current $I = U : R$ passes through the whole circuit. The voltages of the resistors are $U_1 = R_1 I$ and $U_2 = R_2 I$.

In a parallel connection the battery voltage $U$ is across both resistors, so the branch currents are $I_1 = U : R_1$ and $I_2 = U : R_2$. The current leaving the battery is $I = I_1 + I_2$ and the total resistance is $R = U : I$, which is the same as

$$R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

    4Theory

    Key idea

    An electric circuit often has more than one resistor or lamp. Two resistors can be connected to a battery in two ways: one after the other in the same loop, that is, in series, or in separate branches between the same two points, that is, in parallel. The way they are connected decides how the current and the voltage are shared between the resistors and how large a current leaves the battery. In this chapter the battery is ideal, that is, the voltage between its terminals does not depend on the current, and the resistance of the wires is ignored.

    Series connection

    In a series connection the resistors are one after the other, and the charges have only one route. So the same current $I$ passes through every resistor. Current is not used up in a resistor: charge does not vanish or pile up anywhere in the circuit. The voltage across a resistor tells how much of the electrical energy of each charge is transformed into internal energy of the resistor, which is transferred to the surroundings as heat. The battery voltage is shared between the resistors, $U = U_1 + U_2$. By Ohm's law $U_1 = R_1 I$ and $U_2 = R_2 I$, so the larger voltage is across the larger resistance.

    If a component connected in series breaks, for example the filament of a lamp burns through, the only route is opened and the current stops in the whole circuit. This is why all the bulbs of an old-style string of fairy lights go out when one of them breaks.

    Parallel connection

    In a parallel connection the resistors are in separate branches between the same two branching points. The ends of both branches are joined by wires to the terminals of the battery, so the same voltage $U$ is across both resistors. At a branching point the current divides, and at the second branching point the branch currents join again: the current leaving the battery is the sum of the branch currents, $I = I_1 + I_2$. By Ohm's law the branch current is $I_1 = U : R_1$, so a larger current passes through the branch with the smaller resistance.

    A new parallel branch does not change the currents of the other branches, because their voltage stays the same. It adds routes to the circuit, so the current leaving the battery increases. The lamps and sockets in homes are connected in parallel: each appliance gets the same mains voltage, and switching one appliance off breaks only its own branch.

    Total resistance

    A connection of resistors can be replaced with a single resistor from which the battery gives the same current. The resistance of this resistor is the total resistance $R$ of the connection: $U = RI$ holds for it, where $I$ is the current leaving the battery. In a series connection the charges pass through every resistor, so the total resistance is greater than any of the resistors. In a parallel connection each branch is a new route, so the total resistance is smaller than the smallest of the branch resistances.

    Total resistance of resistors

    In a series connection the voltages add up, $U = U_1 + U_2$, and the same current $I$ passes through every resistor. When the voltages are written using Ohm's law, we get $RI = R_1 I + R_2 I$, and the current $I$ cancels. So the total resistance of resistors connected in series is

    $$R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots$$

    where $R$ is the total resistance ($\Omega$) and $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$, … are the resistances of the resistors ($\Omega$).

    In a parallel connection the currents add up, $I = I_1 + I_2$, and the same voltage $U$ is across every resistor. When the currents are written using Ohm's law, we get $\tfrac{U}{R} = \tfrac{U}{R_1} + \tfrac{U}{R_2}$, and the voltage $U$ cancels. For the total resistance of resistors connected in parallel

    $$\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \dots$$

    where $R$ is the total resistance and $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$, … are the resistances of the branches. The equation first gives the number $\tfrac{1}{R}$, whose unit is $\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$. The total resistance is its reciprocal, so remember to invert at the end of the calculation. For two parallel resistors the equation can be solved in advance:

    $$R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

    When two equal resistors are connected in parallel, the total resistance is half the resistance of one resistor.

    In the graph the resistor $R_1 = 100\,\Omega$ stays the same and $R_2$ changes. The total resistance of the series connection grows by as much as $R_2$ and is always over $100\,\Omega$. The total resistance of the parallel connection always stays smaller than either resistor; when $R_2 = 100\,\Omega$, it is $50\,\Omega$.

    Mixed connection

    Many circuits have both resistors in series and resistors in parallel. The solution goes step by step, from the inside out:

    1. Replace the parallel resistors $R_2$ and $R_3$ with a single resistor $R_{23}$ using the formula for a parallel connection.
    2. The resistor $R_1$ is in series with it, so the total resistance is $R = R_1 + R_{23}$.
    3. The current leaving the battery is $I = U : R$. It passes through both $R_1$ and the parallel part.
    4. The voltages of the series parts are obtained from Ohm's law: $U_1 = R_1 I$ and $U_{23} = R_{23} I$. They add up to the battery voltage.
    5. The voltage $U_{23}$ is the same across both parallel resistors, so the branch currents are $I_2 = U_{23} : R_2$ and $I_3 = U_{23} : R_3$. They add up to the current $I$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that current is used up in a resistor, so that the first resistor in a series connection would get the most current and the later ones less. But when $10\,\Omega$ and $20\,\Omega$ resistors are connected in series to a $6.0\,\text{V}$ battery, the same current of $0.20\,\text{A}$ passes through both. No charge is lost in a resistor; instead, the electrical energy of the charges is transformed into internal energy, and in a lamp also into light.

    Another common mistake is to think that every added resistor increases the total resistance. This is true only in a series connection. A resistor added in parallel opens a new route, so the total resistance decreases and the current leaving the battery increases.

    Summary

    • In a series connection the same current passes through every resistor, the voltages add up, $U = U_1 + U_2$, and the total resistance is $R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$
    • In a parallel connection the same voltage is across every branch, the currents add up, $I = I_1 + I_2$, and $\tfrac{1}{R} = \tfrac{1}{R_1} + \tfrac{1}{R_2} + \dots$. The total resistance is smaller than the smallest of the branch resistances.
    • When a component connected in series breaks, the current stops in the whole circuit; when a parallel branch breaks, it stops only in that branch.
    • The total resistance of a mixed connection is calculated step by step: first the parallel parts, then those connected in series.

      5Examples

      Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

      Example 1: Two resistors in parallel

      Two resistors, $R_1 = 120\,\Omega$ and $R_2 = 60\,\Omega$, are connected in parallel to a $9.0\,\text{V}$ battery. How large a current leaves the battery? The battery is considered ideal, and the resistance of the wires is ignored.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $R_1 = 120\,\Omega$, $R_2 = 60\,\Omega$ and $U = 9.0\,\text{V}$. We want the current $I$ leaving the battery.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The resistors are in parallel, so the battery voltage is across both. The connection can be replaced with the total resistance $R$, and Ohm's law then gives the current leaving the battery, $I = U : R$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. We solve $R$ from the equation of the parallel connection, and with it the current $I$:

      $$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{R} &= \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \\[4pt] R &= \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \\[4pt] I &= \frac{U}{R} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the resistances are in ohms and the voltage in volts, and $\tfrac{\text{V}}{\Omega} = \text{A}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} R &= \frac{120\,\Omega \cdot 60\,\Omega}{120\,\Omega + 60\,\Omega} \\ &= 40\,\Omega \\[6pt] I &= \frac{9.0\,\text{V}}{40\,\Omega} \\ &= 0.225\,\text{A} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The current leaving the battery is $0.23\,\text{A}$, or about $230\,\text{mA}$. The result is given to two significant figures like the voltage $9.0\,\text{V}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The branch currents give the same result: $9.0\,\text{V} : 120\,\Omega = 0.075\,\text{A}$ and $9.0\,\text{V} : 60\,\Omega = 0.15\,\text{A}$, together $0.225\,\text{A}$. The total resistance $40\,\Omega$ is smaller than the smaller of the resistors, $60\,\Omega$, as it should be in a parallel connection.

      Example 2: Find the error

      A student calculates the total resistance of resistors $R_1 = 20\,\Omega$ and $R_2 = 30\,\Omega$ connected in parallel.She writes

      $$\begin{aligned} R &= \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \\ &= \frac{1}{20\,\Omega} + \frac{1}{30\,\Omega} \\ &\approx 0.083\,\Omega \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • Correct. The sum $\tfrac{1}{20\,\Omega} + \tfrac{1}{30\,\Omega} \approx 0.083\,\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$ is the number $\tfrac{1}{R}$, and its unit is $\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$, not the ohm. Taking the reciprocal gives $R = 12\,\Omega$, which is smaller than $20\,\Omega$, as it should be in a parallel connection.

      • No unit conversion is needed, because both resistances are in the same unit. The error is that the calculated sum is $\tfrac{1}{R}$, not $R$: its unit is $\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$, and the total resistance is obtained only as the reciprocal, $12\,\Omega$.

      • The total resistance is smaller than the smallest resistor, but not this small: $0.083\,\Omega$ would be almost a short circuit. The calculated sum is $\tfrac{1}{R}$, whose unit is $\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$. Taking the reciprocal gives $R = 12\,\Omega$.

      • Resistances are added together in a series connection. In a parallel connection each branch is a new route, so the total resistance is smaller than the smallest resistor, not greater than the largest. The student's error is that she did not invert the sum $\tfrac{1}{R}$.

        From the smallest to the largest total resistance the order is C ($110\,\Omega$), B ($132\,\Omega$), E ($150\,\Omega$), D ($265\,\Omega$) and A ($550\,\Omega$). The resistance of circuit B is smaller than that of a single $150\,\Omega$ resistor, although both of its resistors are larger than $150\,\Omega$. If one forgets to invert the number $\tfrac{1}{R}$ in a parallel connection, the number of circuit B, $0.0076\,\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$, and that of circuit C, $0.0091\,\tfrac{1}{\Omega}$, look the smallest, and B and C change places.

        In the circuit in the figure the battery is ideal and the resistance of the wires is ignored.

        How large a current passes through resistor $R_3$? Give the answer in milliamperes.

        mA
        Answer

        $50\,\text{mA}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        from the figure, battery voltage $U = 12\,\text{V}$, resistor $R_1 = 40\,\Omega$ in series with the parallel resistors $R_2 = 60\,\Omega$ and $R_3 = 120\,\Omega$

        Find

        the current $I_3$ of resistor $R_3$ in milliamperes

        Principle

        The parallel resistors $R_2$ and $R_3$ are replaced with one resistor $R_{23}$, which is in series with $R_1$. The current leaving the battery passes through $R_1$ and through the whole parallel part. The voltage of the parallel part is the same across both branches, so the current of $R_3$ is obtained from it by Ohm's law.

        Reading the figure

        From the figure we read the values $12\,\text{V}$, $R_1 = 40\,\Omega$, $R_2 = 60\,\Omega$ and $R_3 = 120\,\Omega$. The resistors $R_2$ and $R_3$ are between the same two branching points, that is, in parallel, and the whole current leaving the battery passes through $R_1$ and then through the parallel part, so $R_1$ and the parallel part are one after the other, that is, in series.

        Formula

        $R_{23} = \dfrac{R_2 R_3}{R_2 + R_3}$, $I = \dfrac{U}{R_1 + R_{23}}$, $U_{23} = R_{23} I$, so $I_3 = \dfrac{U_{23}}{R_3}$

        Conversions

        Not needed during the calculation. At the end $1\,\text{A} = 1000\,\text{mA}$.

        Substitution

        $R_{23}$$= \dfrac{60\,\Omega \cdot 120\,\Omega}{180\,\Omega}$
        $= 40\,\Omega$
        $I$$= \dfrac{12\,\text{V}}{40\,\Omega + 40\,\Omega}$
        $= 0.15\,\text{A}$
        $U_{23}$$= 40\,\Omega \cdot 0.15\,\text{A}$
        $= 6.0\,\text{V}$
        $I_3$$= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{120\,\Omega}$
        $= 0.050\,\text{A}$
        $= 50\,\text{mA}$

        Answer

        $50\,\text{mA}$

        Two significant figures like the voltage $12\,\text{V}$.

        Reasonableness

        The current of $R_2$ is $6.0\,\text{V} : 60\,\Omega = 0.10\,\text{A}$, and the branch currents together are $0.10\,\text{A} + 0.050\,\text{A} = 0.15\,\text{A}$, the same as the current leaving the battery. The branch with the larger resistance carries the smaller current, as it should.

      2. Exercise 4-18

        An electric heater has two identical $46\,\Omega$ heating resistors, and it is connected to a mains voltage of $230\,\text{V}$. At low power the resistors are connected in series, at high power in parallel.

        How many times as large is the current the heater draws in the parallel connection compared with the series connection? Assume that the resistances of the resistors do not change.

        times
        Answer

        $4$

        Show the solution

        Given

        two heating resistors, each $R_1 = 46\,\Omega$, mains voltage $U = 230\,\text{V}$; the resistance does not change

        Find

        how many times as large the current $I_\text{parallel}$ is compared with the current $I_\text{series}$

        Principle

        In both connections the same mains voltage is across the heater, so by Ohm's law the current of the heater depends only on the total resistance. The total resistance of equal resistors in series is twice the resistance of one, and in parallel half of it.

        Formula

        $R_\text{series} = 2R_1$ and $R_\text{parallel} = \dfrac{R_1}{2}$, so

        $\dfrac{I_\text{parallel}}{I_\text{series}}$$= \dfrac{U : R_\text{parallel}}{U : R_\text{series}}$
        $= \dfrac{R_\text{series}}{R_\text{parallel}}$
        $= \dfrac{2R_1}{R_1 : 2}$
        $= 4$

        Conversions

        Not needed: the voltage and the resistance cancel out of the ratio.

        Substitution

        With numbers

        $I_\text{series}$$= \dfrac{230\,\text{V}}{92\,\Omega}$
        $= 2.5\,\text{A}$
        $I_\text{parallel}$$= \dfrac{230\,\text{V}}{23\,\Omega}$
        $= 10\,\text{A}$

        so

        $\dfrac{I_\text{parallel}}{I_\text{series}}$$= \dfrac{10\,\text{A}}{2.5\,\text{A}}$
        $= 4.0$

        Answer

        $4$

        Times. The ratio is exact, because it does not depend on the values of the resistance or the voltage when the resistors are equal.

        Reasonableness

        In the parallel connection the full $230\,\text{V}$ is across each resistor, in the series connection only half. So the current of each resistor is doubled, and there are two branches: altogether four times the current. A larger current means a higher heating power, as the settings of the heater promise.

      3. Exercise 4-19

        Two resistors give a total resistance of $250\,\Omega$ when connected in series and $60\,\Omega$ when connected in parallel. How large is the resistance of the larger of the resistors?

        Ω
        Answer

        $150\,\Omega$

        Show the solution

        Given

        total resistance of the series connection $R_\text{s} = 250\,\Omega$, of the parallel connection $R_\text{r} = 60\,\Omega$

        Find

        the resistance of the larger resistor

        Principle

        In a series connection the resistances add up, and the total resistance of a parallel connection is the product of the resistances divided by their sum. The two equations give the sum and the product of the resistances, and these determine the resistors.

        Formula

        $R_1 + R_2 = R_\text{s}$ and $R_1 R_2 = R_\text{r} R_\text{s}$ (from the formula of the parallel connection). Substituting $R_2 = R_\text{s} - R_1$ gives $R_1^2 - R_\text{s} R_1 + R_\text{r} R_\text{s} = 0$, so

        $R_1$$= \dfrac{R_\text{s} \pm \sqrt{R_\text{s}^2 - 4 R_\text{r} R_\text{s}}}{2}$

        Conversions

        Not needed, both resistances are in ohms.

        Substitution

        $R_\text{r} R_\text{s}$$= 60\,\Omega \cdot 250\,\Omega$
        $= 15\,000\,\Omega^2$
        $R_\text{s}^2 - 4 R_\text{r} R_\text{s}$$= 62\,500\,\Omega^2 - 60\,000\,\Omega^2$
        $= 2500\,\Omega^2$

        whose square root is $50\,\Omega$. So

        $R_1$$= \dfrac{250\,\Omega \pm 50\,\Omega}{2}$

        so $R_1 = 150\,\Omega$ or $R_1 = 100\,\Omega$

        Answer

        $150\,\Omega$

        ; the other resistor is $100\,\Omega$. Two significant figures like the value $60\,\Omega$; the data are taken as exact, because the difference $62\,500 - 60\,000$ under the root is small and the result is sensitive to the last digit of the data.

        Reasonableness

        Check:

        $150\,\Omega + 100\,\Omega$$= 250\,\Omega$
        $\dfrac{150\,\Omega \cdot 100\,\Omega}{250\,\Omega}$$= 60\,\Omega$

        The result of the parallel connection is smaller than the smaller resistor, $100\,\Omega$, as it should be.

      Given

      In a series connection the same current passes through both resistors, and it is obtained from the total resistance by Ohm's law. The voltage across a resistor is its resistance times this current.

      Optional 4. Explanation task

      Explain why the total resistance of two resistors connected in parallel is always smaller than the resistance of either resistor. Use voltage and current in your explanation.

      Model answer

      In a parallel connection the same battery voltage $U$ is across both resistors. Each branch therefore gets the same current it would get alone, for example $I_1 = U : R_1$. The current leaving the battery is the sum of the branch currents, $I = I_1 + I_2$, so it is larger than $I_1$ or $I_2$. The total resistance is $R = U : I$. When the same voltage gives a larger current than either resistor alone, the total resistance is smaller than $R_1$ and smaller than $R_2$. Every new branch is an extra route for the charges.
