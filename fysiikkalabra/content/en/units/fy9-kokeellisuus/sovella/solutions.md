## fy9-kokeellisuus-s1

### a)
**Find:** an experiment plan for comparing the grip of two shoes: the quantities measured and the instruments, the measuring method, the things kept constant and the number of measurements
**Principle:** Grip means static friction, which keeps the shoe from starting to slide. The largest value of static friction is $\mu_0 N$, so it depends both on the surfaces and on how hard the shoe presses on the floor. The shoes have different weights, so the coefficient of static friction $\mu_0$, which describes only the surfaces, is suitable for the comparison. In a fair test only the shoe changes.
**Model answer:** *Measured quantity.* I compare the coefficients of static friction of the shoes against the floor board. The coefficient does not depend on the weight of the shoe, so shoes of different weights can be compared.

*Measurement (tilting method).* I place the shoe on the board and put the same mass inside it, for example $1\,\text{kg}$, so that the sole presses against the board as in a game. I raise one end of the board slowly and steadily until the shoe starts to slide, and read the angle $\alpha_0$ of the board with the inclinometer at exactly that moment. If the angle becomes so large that the measurement fails, I use the pulling method: the board horizontal, the shoe is pulled horizontally with the spring balance, and I read the largest reading just before it starts to move. In that case the mass of the shoe and the mass inside it is weighed on the kitchen scale.

*Kept constant:* the same board and the same spot on it, a clean and dry surface (I wipe off dust before every measurement), the same mass inside the shoe, the same position of the shoe and equally slow tilting or pulling. The room temperature and humidity stay the same when the measurements are made on the same day.

*Repetitions.* I measure each shoe five times in turn, so that any change in the surface affects both equally. I record every reading in a table.

### b)
**Find:** how the coefficient of static friction is calculated from the measurements and how the difference between the shoes is shown
**Principle:** At the limiting angle, static friction is at its largest and equal to the component of the weight along the slope: $\mu_0\, mg\cos\alpha_0 = mg\sin\alpha_0$. In the pulling method on a horizontal board $F = \mu_0 mg$. The variation of the repetitions tells how large a difference can arise from chance alone.
**Model answer:** In the tilting method the mass cancels, and each measurement gives $\mu_0 = \tan\alpha_0$. In the pulling method $\mu_0 = \tfrac{F}{mg}$, where $F$ is the largest reading of the spring balance and $m$ the mass of the shoe and the mass inside it. For each shoe I calculate the mean of the five results and the error estimate as half the range, for example $\mu_0 = 0.62 \pm 0.03$.

The difference is probably real if the intervals $\bar{\mu}_0 \pm \Delta\mu_0$ of the shoes do not overlap. If the intervals overlap, the difference is not larger than the variation of the measurements and cannot be shown with these measurements. That still does not prove that the shoes grip equally well.

### c)
**Find:** two significant sources of error and ways to reduce their effect
**Principle:** A source of error is best named so that it shows what happens and in which direction the result changes. The effect of a random error is reduced by repetitions, a systematic error by changing the measuring method.
**Model answer:** *Tilting too fast or a jerky pull.* If the board is raised quickly, the angle has time to grow before I read it, and the result is too large. A jerk in the pulling method raises the reading of the spring balance for a moment. Remedy: I tilt or pull slowly and steadily and repeat the measurement; the measurement can also be filmed with a phone, so that the moment of the reading can be seen from the video.

*Changes in the surface.* Dust, sand or moisture on the board or the sole changes the friction, and wear of the sole during the measurements can change it gradually. Remedy: I wipe the board and the soles before every measurement and measure the shoes in turn, so that a change affects both in the same way.

The zero of the spring balance is also checked before the measurement, with the balance in its measuring position.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- Only the pulling force is chosen as the measure of grip, although the shoes have different weights. A heavier shoe needs a larger force even if its sole does not grip better.
- The plan does not say what is kept constant, or the shoes are measured on different boards or on different days.
- Each shoe is measured once, so the variation cannot be seen and the difference cannot be shown.
- “Human error” or “measurement error” is written as a source of error without saying what the error is and how it is reduced.
- The result is compared with kinetic friction: the shoe is pulled at a steady speed, although grip means static friction just before sliding.

### Hyväksytään myös
- Instead of the coefficient of friction, the shoes can be compared if masses are added to both so that the total mass is the same, and the readings of the spring balance are compared. The mass must then be the same and weighed.

## fy9-kokeellisuus-s2

### a)
**Find:** an experiment plan for determining the specific heat capacity of rapeseed oil: the quantities measured, the measuring method and the things kept constant
**Principle:** The resistor converts electrical energy into internal energy at the power $P$, and this energy is transferred as heat to the oil, so in the time $t$ the energy $Pt$ is transferred to the oil when the losses are small. The heat $Q = cm\,\Delta T$ holds when the oil does not change phase. When the mass of the oil, the power and the change in temperature as a function of time are measured, $c$ can be solved.
**Model answer:** *Measurements.* I weigh the empty vacuum flask and then the flask with the oil, so that the mass of the oil is obtained from the difference, for example $m = 200.0\,\text{g}$. I record the power of the resistor, $50\,\text{W}$. I place the resistor and the thermometer probe in the oil and close the lid. I record the initial temperature, switch on the resistor and start the watch at the same moment. I read the temperature every $30\,\text{s}$ and stir all the time, so that the temperature is the same everywhere. I record the times and temperatures in a table.

*Kept constant:* the same flask and lid, the same way of stirring, an initial temperature close to room temperature and a temperature rise of at most about $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so that the losses stay small. The resistor is completely inside the liquid.

*Comparison.* I make the same measurement with the same mass of water. Then the teacher's claim can be tested directly, and the known specific heat capacity of water shows how much the container and the losses affect the result.

### b)
**Find:** how the specific heat capacity is obtained from the $(t, T)$ graph
**Principle:** When the losses are small, $P\,\Delta t = cm\,\Delta T$, so the temperature rises steadily and the $(t, T)$ graph is a straight line whose slope is $k = \tfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta t} = \tfrac{P}{cm}$.
**Model answer:** I draw the $(t, T)$ graph: time on the horizontal axis and temperature on the vertical axis. At the beginning the points lie on a line, and I fit a straight line to them. I read the slope from two points of the line far apart. Then $c = \tfrac{P}{mk}$. For example, a slope of $0.125\,\text{K/s}$ would give $c = \tfrac{50\,\text{W}}{0.200\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.125\,\text{K/s}} = 2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$.

I use the initial part of the graph, because when the oil becomes warmer than its surroundings, more and more heat is transferred to the surroundings and the graph curves and becomes less steep. Using the slope is better than a single initial and final reading, because all the points affect the result.

### c)
**Given:** power $P = 50\,\text{W}$, mass $m = 200\,\text{g}$, temperature change $\Delta T = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 20\,\text{K}$, specific heat capacity $c = 2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$; no heat is lost
**Find:** the heating time $t$ in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** When no heat is lost, the energy $Pt$ given by the resistor goes entirely into heating the oil. The oil does not boil or change phase, so the heat is $Q = cm\,\Delta T$.
**Formula:** $Pt = cm\,\Delta T$, from which $t = \dfrac{cm\,\Delta T}{P}$
**Conversions:** $m = 200\,\text{g} = 200 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.200\,\text{kg}$ and $c = 2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} = 2.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} = 2000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$. A temperature change is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{2000\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \cdot 0.200\,\text{kg} \cdot 20\,\text{K}}{50\,\text{W}} = \dfrac{8000\,\text{J}}{50\,\text{J/s}} = 160\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $t \approx 160\,\text{s}$, or about $2.7\,\text{min}$. Two significant figures as in the specific heat capacity $2.0\,\text{kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and the power $50\,\text{W}$.
**Reasonableness:** The same mass of water would need about twice the time, because the specific heat capacity of water, $4190\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, is about twice as large. A measurement of a few minutes at $30\,\text{s}$ intervals gives six points, which is enough for fitting a straight line.

### d)
**Find:** whether the specific heat capacity comes out too large or too small because of heat losses, and how the effect is reduced
**Principle:** The calculation assumes that all the energy $P\,\Delta t$ heats the oil. The losses are a systematic error, which always takes the result in the same direction.
**Model answer:** Part of the energy from the resistor heats the vacuum flask and the surrounding air, so the temperature of the oil rises more slowly than without losses. The slope $k$ is therefore too small, and $c = \tfrac{P}{mk}$ comes out **too large**. The error is systematic, so repeating the measurement does not remove it.

The effect is reduced by keeping the lid closed and the container insulated, by measuring over a short temperature range and by using only the initial part of the graph, as in part b. An alternative is to start a few degrees below room temperature and fit the line to the whole measurement: then the oil gains roughly as much energy from the surroundings at the beginning as it loses at the end. In addition, the same measurement can be made with water: since the specific heat capacity of water is known, one sees how much the container and the losses change the result, and the result for the oil can be corrected.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The temperature is measured only at the beginning and at the end, so no graph can be drawn and it cannot be seen where the losses start to grow.
- Oil and water are compared with different masses or in different containers, so the comparison is not fair.
- In part c the mass is left in grams or the kilojoules are not converted to joules, so the time comes out a thousand times too large or too small.
- In part d it is stated that the losses “distort the result” without giving the direction. The losses reduce the slope, so $c$ comes out too large.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b, $c$ can also be calculated from two points of the line on the graph directly with the formula $c = \tfrac{P\,\Delta t}{m\,\Delta T}$, which is the same thing as $c = \tfrac{P}{mk}$.
- In part a, instead of the measurement with water, the heat capacity of the container can be measured separately, or it can be argued that the effect of the vacuum flask is small.

## fy9-kokeellisuus-s3

### a)
**Find:** the shortcomings of the report and why each weakens the result or the conclusion
**Principle:** A report is good if someone else can repeat the experiment and assess how reliable the result is. A result includes its uncertainty, and a conclusion is only as strong as the data.
**Model answer:** *No uncertainty and too many digits.* The result $9.6955\,\text{m/s}^2$ claims a precision of five digits, although the times vary by $0.07\,\text{s}$ and the result is uncertain already in the first decimal place. Without an uncertainty the result cannot be compared with the hypothesis.

*Timing by hand.* The reaction time, about $0.1\,\text{s}$, is about $13\,\%$ of the fall time $0.8\,\text{s}$, and in the formula for $g$ the time is squared. The person dropping and the person timing are different people, so the watch does not start exactly at the moment of release. Three repetitions from one height are not enough to show the spread and do not reveal a systematic error.

*Air resistance.* The explanation “the difference is due to air resistance” is unjustified. The difference from the table value is about $0.1\,\text{m/s}^2$, which is much smaller than the timing uncertainty (part b). The measurement therefore cannot show the effect of air resistance, and the timing error could just as well change the result in either direction.

*Proving.* A measurement cannot prove a hypothesis. It can only support it: the table value is within the uncertainty interval.

*Description of the method.* The report does not say from where to where the height was measured (from the bottom of the ball to the floor) or to what precision, so the experiment cannot be repeated exactly. The research question is also loose: it does not state the method or what the experiment is meant to test.

### b)
**Given:** drop height $h = 3.0\,\text{m}$, fall times $0.82\,\text{s}$, $0.75\,\text{s}$ and $0.79\,\text{s}$; the ball starts from rest
**Find:** the free-fall acceleration $g$ and its uncertainty $\Delta g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The ball starts from rest, and over a short drop the air resistance is small, so the motion is uniformly accelerated: $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$. The best estimate is obtained from the mean of the times. In the extreme-value method $g$ is calculated with the shortest and the longest time; the shortest time gives the largest $g$.
**Formula:** $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, from which $g = \dfrac{2h}{t^2}$, and $\Delta g = \dfrac{g_\text{max} - g_\text{min}}{2}$, where $g_\text{max} = \dfrac{2h}{t_\text{min}^2}$ and $g_\text{min} = \dfrac{2h}{t_\text{max}^2}$
**Conversions:** None needed, the height is in metres and the times in seconds.
**Substitution:** $\bar{t} = \dfrac{0.82\,\text{s} + 0.75\,\text{s} + 0.79\,\text{s}}{3} = 0.7867\,\text{s}$ and $g = \dfrac{2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{(0.7867\,\text{s})^2} = 9.695\,\text{m/s}^2$. $g_\text{max} = \dfrac{2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{(0.75\,\text{s})^2} = 10.67\,\text{m/s}^2$, $g_\text{min} = \dfrac{2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m}}{(0.82\,\text{s})^2} = 8.923\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $\Delta g = \dfrac{10.67\,\text{m/s}^2 - 8.923\,\text{m/s}^2}{2} = 0.872\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $g = (9.7 \pm 0.9)\,\text{m/s}^2$. The error estimate is rounded to one significant figure and the result to the same decimal place.
**Reasonableness:** The table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is within the interval $8.8\text{–}10.6\,\text{m/s}^2$. The relative uncertainty is about $9\,\%$, which fits the fact that the variation of the time, $\pm 0.035\,\text{s}$, is about $4.5\,\%$ of the time and squaring doubles it.

### c)
**Find:** an improved method and how the result is calculated
**Principle:** The largest source of error is timing by hand. It disappears when the time is measured by a device. Several heights and a graph make the result independent of any single measurement.
**Model answer:** I film the drop with a phone in front of a vertical tape measure, with the camera at the height of the middle of the fall, so that perspective does not distort the readings. From the video I read the position of the ball frame by frame; at a frame rate of $60\,\text{frames/s}$ the interval between frames is about $0.017\,\text{s}$, which is much less than the reaction time. Alternatively I use two light gates.

I drop the ball from, for example, five heights $0.5\text{–}3.0\,\text{m}$, three times from each. I draw the $(t^2, h)$ graph: according to the model $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ it is a straight line through the origin whose slope is $\tfrac{g}{2}$, so $g$ is twice the slope. From the video one can also calculate the velocity and draw a $(t, v)$ graph, whose slope is $g$. Instead of a tennis ball it is better to use a small heavy ball, for which the air resistance is smaller.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- Only “measurement errors” or “human errors” are listed as shortcomings without saying which error is meant and how it affects the result.
- Air resistance is accepted as the explanation, although the difference from the table value is much smaller than the uncertainty of the measurement.
- In part b, $g_\text{max}$ is calculated with the longest time. The time is in the denominator, so the shortest time gives the largest acceleration.
- In part b, $g$ is calculated with the formula $g = \tfrac{h}{t^2}$ or $g = \tfrac{v}{t}$, forgetting that the ball starts from rest and the average speed is half the final speed.
- In part c only “more repetitions” is suggested. Repetitions damp the random error, but the systematic part of timing by hand remains.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b, the uncertainty can also be estimated from half the range of the time: $\Delta t = 0.035\,\text{s}$ is $4.5\,\%$ of the time, and the relative uncertainty of $g$ is about twice that, $9\,\%$, or about $0.9\,\text{m/s}^2$.
- As shortcomings in part a, it is also acceptable to mention, for example, that the hypothesis has not been justified, and that a tennis ball is light and large, so air resistance is relatively larger for it than for a heavy ball.

### b)
**Given:** the $(l, T^2)$ graph of part a and the line fitted to it
**Find:** the free-fall acceleration $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** According to the model $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\, l$, the slope of the line is $\tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$. A constant error in measuring the length shifts the line but does not change the slope, so $g$ is obtained from the slope. The starting angle $5^\circ$ is so small that the small-angle model holds.
**Reading the graph:** Two points of the line far apart are read: $(0.200\,\text{m};\ 0.91\,\text{s}^2)$ and $(1.000\,\text{m};\ 4.12\,\text{s}^2)$.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{\Delta (T^2)}{\Delta l}$ and $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$, from which $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the length is in metres and $T^2$ in $\text{s}^2$.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{4.12\,\text{s}^2 - 0.91\,\text{s}^2}{1.000\,\text{m} - 0.200\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{3.21\,\text{s}^2}{0.800\,\text{m}} = 4.013\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$ and $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{4.013\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}} = 9.839\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. Two significant figures, because the slope is read from the drawn line to a precision of about $\pm 0.1\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$ (part d).
**Reasonableness:** The result is close to the table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. Check: the period of a one-metre pendulum would be $2\pi\sqrt{1.0\,\text{m} : 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 2.0\,\text{s}$, and the measured $T$ at $1.000\,\text{m}$ is $2.03\,\text{s}$, which fits, because the true length is slightly larger than the measured one.

### c)
**Given:** the line of part a, the slope $k = 4.013\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$ of part b; the length was measured to the top of the ball
**Find:** the cause of the constant term and the diameter $d$ of the ball in centimetres
**Principle:** The length of a pendulum is from the fixing point to the centre of the bob. Each measured length $l$ lacks the radius $r$ of the ball, so the true length is $l + r$ and the model is $T^2 = \tfrac{4\pi^2}{g}(l + r) = kl + kr$. The constant term is therefore $b = kr$, and it is the same systematic error at every point.
**Reading the graph:** The dashed extension of the line cuts the vertical axis at $b \approx 0.10\,\text{s}^2$.
**Formula:** $b = kr$, from which $r = \dfrac{b}{k}$ and $d = 2r = \dfrac{2b}{k}$
**Conversions:** $r$ comes out in metres, and $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $r = \dfrac{0.10\,\text{s}^2}{4.013\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}} = 0.0249\,\text{m} = 0.0249 \cdot 100\,\text{cm} = 2.49\,\text{cm}$ and $d = 2 \cdot 2.49\,\text{cm} = 4.98\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $d \approx 5\,\text{cm}$, one or two significant figures, because the constant term is read from the graph only to about $\pm 0.02\,\text{s}^2$, which changes the diameter by about $\pm 1\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** A steel ball a few centimetres across is an ordinary pendulum bob. The extension of the line cuts the horizontal axis at $l = -r \approx -2.5\,\text{cm}$: there the true length would be zero.

### d)
**Find:** how the systematic error in the length affected the result of part b and a row-by-row calculation, and whether the result is consistent with the value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** A constant error that is the same at every point shifts the line but does not change its slope. In a row-by-row calculation the same error goes straight into every result.
**Model answer:** *Slope.* Since every length lacks the same $2.5\,\text{cm}$, the whole line shifts, but its steepness does not change. The $g$ of part b is therefore not distorted, and the error shows as the constant term.

*Row-by-row calculation.* If $g = \tfrac{4\pi^2 l}{T^2}$ were calculated from each row with the measured length, every result would be too small, because $l$ is too small. The error would be largest with a short string, where $2.5\,\text{cm}$ is the largest part of the length: $l = 0.250\,\text{m}$ gives $8.9\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $l = 1.000\,\text{m}$ gives $9.6\,\text{m/s}^2$. The mean would not correct the error.

*Consistency.* When the line is turned within the uncertainties of the points, the slope can change by about $\pm 0.1\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$. The values $3.91$ and $4.11\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$ give $g = 10.1$ and $9.6\,\text{m/s}^2$, so $g = (9.8 \pm 0.3)\,\text{m/s}^2$. The table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is within the interval, so the measurement is consistent with it.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The graph drawn is $(l, T)$, whose points lie on a curve, and a straight line is fitted to it.
- $T^2$ is calculated from the time of ten swings without first dividing by ten.
- The line is forced through the origin, which changes the slope and loses the information in the constant term.
- $g$ itself is taken as the slope, or $g = \tfrac{k}{4\pi^2}$.
- In part c the constant term is taken as a measurement error that cannot be explained, or air resistance is guessed as its cause, although air resistance would not shift the line by a constant amount.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b, the slope can also be obtained from a calculator or spreadsheet line fit ($k = 4.02\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$, $g = 9.82\,\text{m/s}^2$) or from other points of the line far apart. Slopes $3.9\text{–}4.1\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$, that is, $g = 9.6\text{–}10.1\,\text{m/s}^2$, are accepted.
- In part c, a constant term of $0.08\text{–}0.12\,\text{s}^2$ and a diameter of $4\text{–}6\,\text{cm}$ are accepted, as is the radius $r$ from the point where the line cuts the horizontal axis.

## fy9-kokeellisuus-s5

### a)
**Given:** mass of the stone $m = 187.4\,\text{g}$, water level readings $V_1 = 120\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ and $V_2 = 190\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$
**Find:** the density $\rho$ of the stone in $\text{kg/m}^3$
**Principle:** A submerged stone displaces its own volume of water, so the volume of the stone is the rise of the water level. Density is mass divided by volume.
**Formula:** $V = V_2 - V_1$ and $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{m}{V_2 - V_1}$
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l} = 1\,\text{cm}^3$ and $1\,\text{g/cm}^3 = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
**Substitution:** $V = 190\,\text{cm}^3 - 120\,\text{cm}^3 = 70\,\text{cm}^3$ and $\rho = \dfrac{187.4\,\text{g}}{70\,\text{cm}^3} = 2.677\,\text{g/cm}^3 = 2.677 \cdot 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3 = 2677\,\text{kg/m}^3$
**Answer:** $\rho \approx 2680\,\text{kg/m}^3$. Three significant figures, because the volume is the difference of two three-digit readings; part b shows that even the third digit is uncertain.
**Reasonableness:** The density is about $2.7$ times that of water, which is typical of ordinary rock types: the stone sinks, and a palm-sized stone weighs hundreds of grams.

### b)
**Given:** $m = (187.4 \pm 0.1)\,\text{g}$; readings $120\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ and $190\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$, each with an uncertainty of half the scale division $2\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$, that is, $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$
**Find:** the uncertainty $\Delta\rho$ of the density in $\text{kg/m}^3$
**Principle:** The volume is the difference of two readings, and each reading can be wrong by at most the estimated uncertainty, so the uncertainty of the difference is the sum of the uncertainties. In the extreme-value method the largest density is obtained with the largest mass and the smallest volume, the smallest the other way round.
**Formula:** $\Delta V = \Delta V_1 + \Delta V_2$, $\rho_\text{max} = \dfrac{m + \Delta m}{V - \Delta V}$, $\rho_\text{min} = \dfrac{m - \Delta m}{V + \Delta V}$ and $\Delta\rho = \dfrac{\rho_\text{max} - \rho_\text{min}}{2}$
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l} = 1\,\text{cm}^3$ and $1\,\text{g/cm}^3 = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta V = 1\,\text{cm}^3 + 1\,\text{cm}^3 = 2\,\text{cm}^3$, $\rho_\text{max} = \dfrac{187.5\,\text{g}}{68\,\text{cm}^3} = 2.757\,\text{g/cm}^3$, $\rho_\text{min} = \dfrac{187.3\,\text{g}}{72\,\text{cm}^3} = 2.601\,\text{g/cm}^3$ and $\Delta\rho = \dfrac{2.757\,\text{g/cm}^3 - 2.601\,\text{g/cm}^3}{2} = 0.078\,\text{g/cm}^3 = 78\,\text{kg/m}^3$
**Answer:** $\Delta\rho \approx 80\,\text{kg/m}^3$, one significant figure, so $\rho = (2680 \pm 80)\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
**Reasonableness:** The relative uncertainty $80 : 2680 \approx 3\,\%$ is almost the same as the relative uncertainty of the volume, $2 : 70 \approx 2.9\,\%$, because the uncertainty of the mass is very small.

### c)
**Find:** whether the stone can be identified as granite or basalt by its density
**Principle:** The result is compared with the ranges in the table, taking the uncertainty into account. If the uncertainty interval falls only in the range of one rock type, the result supports it.
**Model answer:** The density of the stone is probably between $2600\text{–}2760\,\text{kg/m}^3$. The interval lies entirely in the typical range of granite, $2600\text{–}2800\,\text{kg/m}^3$, and the lower limit of basalt, $2800\,\text{kg/m}^3$, is outside the interval. The result therefore supports granite, and basalt is unlikely.

Density alone does not prove the rock type, however: the table values are typical, not absolute limits, and many other rock types also have a density of $2.6\text{–}2.8\,\text{g/cm}^3$. Certainty comes only when the density is compared with, for example, the colour, grains and hardness of the stone.

### d)
**Find:** which measurement limits the precision more and how the result is made more precise
**Principle:** The relative uncertainties tell which measurement affects the result most. It pays to improve the precision of the measurement whose relative uncertainty is largest.
**Model answer:** The relative uncertainty of the mass is $0.1\,\text{g} : 187.4\,\text{g} \approx 0.05\,\%$ and that of the volume $2\,\text{cm}^3 : 70\,\text{cm}^3 \approx 3\,\%$. The volume therefore limits the result about 50 times more than the mass, and a more precise balance would not help.

The uncertainty of the volume is reduced with a narrower measuring cylinder whose scale division is, for example, $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ or smaller, if the stone fits in it. Another way is an overflow can: the stone is lowered into a full can, the overflowing water is collected and weighed, and $V = \tfrac{m_\text{water}}{\rho_\text{water}}$. The uncertainty of the weighing is only $0.1\,\text{g}$, but the water left in the spout and on the walls of the can brings a larger uncertainty, about $0.5\,\text{cm}^3$, so the method is more precise than the measuring cylinder only when done carefully. Air bubbles, which would otherwise increase the volume, are removed from the surface of the stone.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The final reading $190\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ is taken as the volume of the stone instead of the rise of the water level.
- Only the $1\,\text{m}\textsf{l}$ of one reading is taken as the uncertainty of the volume, although the volume is the difference of two readings.
- In the extreme values the largest mass is combined with the largest volume, so the range of the density comes out too small.
- Units are converted wrongly: $1\,\text{g/cm}^3$ is $1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, not $1\,\text{kg/m}^3$ or $100\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
- In part c the rock type is “proved” from the density, or the uncertainty interval is left out of the comparison.

### Hyväksytään myös
- As the answer to part a, $2.7 \cdot 10^3\,\text{kg/m}^3$ or $2.68\,\text{g/cm}^3$, when the unit is correct.
- In part b, the uncertainty can also be calculated from the relative uncertainties: $\tfrac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \approx 0.05\,\% + 2.9\,\% \approx 2.9\,\%$, giving $\Delta\rho \approx 80\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

**Principle:** When the deceleration $a$ is constant, the motion is uniformly changing, and up to the stop $0 = v^2 - 2as$ holds, as in calculating a braking distance.

The distant point $(60\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2;\ 7.2\,\text{m})$ is read from the line, so the slope is $k = \tfrac{7.2\,\text{m}}{60\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 0.12\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$. Since $s = \tfrac{1}{2a}\, v^2$, the slope is $k = \tfrac{1}{2a}$ and the deceleration is

$a = \tfrac{1}{2k} = \tfrac{1}{2 \cdot 0.12\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}} = 4.17\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$,

two significant figures as in the braking distances and the slope.

### c)
**Given:** deceleration $a = 4.17\,\text{m/s}^2$ (part b), a horizontal road, the wheels do not lock, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the smallest coefficient of static friction $\mu_0$ between the tyres and the asphalt
**Principle:** When air resistance is left out, the bicycle and the cyclist are slowed horizontally only by the friction force of the road on the tyres. The tyres do not slip on the asphalt, so this is static friction, which can be at most $\mu_0 N$. The road is horizontal, so vertically $N = mg$. By Newton's second law the friction force must be $ma$.
**Formula:** $ma = F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$, from which the mass cancels and $\mu_0 \ge \dfrac{a}{g}$
**Conversions:** None needed, the accelerations are in $\text{m/s}^2$.
**Substitution:** $\mu_0 \ge \dfrac{4.17\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = 0.425$
**Answer:** $\mu_0 \ge 0.42$, two significant figures as in the deceleration $4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. A coefficient of friction has no unit.
**Reasonableness:** The coefficient of static friction between dry asphalt and rubber is typically clearly larger, about $0.7\text{–}0.9$, so the braking is possible without the wheels locking. A deceleration of $4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ is a little under half the free-fall acceleration, which fits the braking of a bicycle.

### d)
**Given:** the app's speed is $5\,\%$ higher than the true speed in every run: $v_\text{app} = 1.05\, v$; the deceleration of part b is $4.17\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** by how many per cent and in which direction the deceleration of part b differs from the true value, and whether the conclusion changes
**Principle:** The error is systematic: every speed has been multiplied by the same number. In the graph every $v^2$ has therefore been multiplied by $1.05^2$, and the slope of the line $k = \tfrac{s}{v^2}$ is too small in the same proportion. The deceleration is $a = \tfrac{1}{2k}$, so it is too large in the same proportion.
**Formula:** $v_\text{app}^2 = 1.05^2\, v^2$, so $k_\text{app} = \dfrac{k}{1.05^2}$ and $a_\text{app} = 1.05^2\, a$; the deviation is $\dfrac{a_\text{app} - a}{a} = 1.05^2 - 1$
**Conversions:** The percentage as a decimal number: $5\,\% = 0.05$.
**Substitution:** $1.05^2 - 1 = 1.1025 - 1 = 0.1025 = 10.25\,\%$ and the true deceleration $a = \dfrac{4.17\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.1025} = 3.78\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** The deceleration of part b is about $10\,\%$ too large; the true deceleration is about $3.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The result is given as an approximate value, because the deceleration $4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ is known to two figures. The conclusion about constant deceleration does not change: every $v^2$ changes in the same proportion, so the points still lie on a straight line through the origin; only its slope changes.
**Reasonableness:** The speed is squared in the formula, so a small relative error doubles: $2 \cdot 5\,\% = 10\,\%$. The smallest coefficient of friction of part c also decreases in the same proportion, to about $0.39$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The graph drawn is $(v, s)$, and a straight line is fitted to the curved set of points.
- The speeds are squared in $\text{km/h}$, so the deceleration comes out in the wrong unit and with the wrong size; the conversion is $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
- The slope itself or $\tfrac{1}{k}$ is taken as the deceleration; the two of the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is forgotten.
- In part c kinetic friction is used, although the wheels do not slip, or an exact value is given as the answer instead of a lower limit.
- In part d the deviation is given as $5\,\%$, forgetting that the speed is squared in the formula.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b, the slope can be determined from a calculator fit through the origin ($k \approx 0.121\,\text{s}^2/\text{m}$, $a \approx 4.15\,\text{m/s}^2$) or from another distant point of the line. Decelerations of $4.0\text{–}4.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ are accepted, as are coefficients of $0.41\text{–}0.45$ in part c.
- In part d the answer $10.25\,\%$ or “about $10\,\%$”.
