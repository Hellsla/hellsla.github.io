Direct-current circuits and electrical energy

Why does the cottage battery last more than a week for the LED lights alone, but only a day together with the cool box?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve the currents, voltages and powers of direct-current circuits by choosing a suitable model
  • draw and interpret graphs of measurement data of a battery and a wire
  • assess claims about electrical energy, batteries and electrical measurements with reasons

Prior knowledge

1Think about it

The electricity of a summer cottage comes from a battery whose voltage is $12\,\text{V}$ and capacity $100\,\text{Ah}$. A solar panel fully charges the battery during the day. In the evening LED lights with a total power of $6.0\,\text{W}$ and a cool box with a power of $48\,\text{W}$ are connected to the battery. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the formula is not.

How large a current is drawn from the battery, and for how many hours does a full battery last when both devices are on?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Starting level 1: the current between the lamps

Two identical lamps are connected in series to a battery. An ammeter between the battery and the first lamp shows $0.20\,\text{A}$.What does a second ammeter between the lamps show?

  • The second lamp adds to the resistance of the circuit, so the current is smaller than in a circuit with one lamp, not larger. In series the same current passes through both lamps: $0.20\,\text{A}$. Review the chapter Resistors in series and in parallel.

  • Correct. Charge does not disappear in a lamp, so both meters show $0.20\,\text{A}$. What decreases in the lamps is the potential, not the current. If needed, review the chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit.

  • A lamp does not use current: it transforms electrical energy into light and internal energy. The same charge that enters the lamp leaves it, so in an unbranched circuit the current is $0.20\,\text{A}$ at every point. Review the chapter Electric circuit, current and voltage.

Starting level 2: a voltmeter in series

A student sets a multimeter to a voltage range and by mistake connects it in series with a lamp to a $4.5\,\text{V}$ battery. What happens?

  • Correct. The resistance of a voltmeter is very large, so the current is almost zero. There is no voltage across the lamp, and the meter shows almost $4.5\,\text{V}$. If needed, review the chapter Measurements in an electric circuit.

  • The resistance of a voltmeter is very large, so in series it reduces the current almost to zero. The lamp does not light, and almost the whole voltage of the battery is across the meter. Review the chapter Measurements in an electric circuit.

  • A large current arises if an ammeter is wrongly connected in parallel, because the resistance of an ammeter is very small. The large resistance of a voltmeter stops the current. Review the chapter Measurements in an electric circuit.

Starting level 3: a filament lamp at half the voltage

A current of $0.50\,\text{A}$ passes through a filament lamp when the voltage across it is $6.0\,\text{V}$.How large a current passes through the lamp at a voltage of $3.0\,\text{V}$?

  • Ohm's law holds for a metal at constant temperature. With a smaller current the filament of the lamp is cooler, so its resistance is less than $12\,\Omega$, and the current stays above $0.25\,\text{A}$. Review the chapter Resistance and Ohm's law.

  • The resistance of a lamp increases when the filament heats up, that is, with a larger current. At a smaller voltage the filament is cooler and its resistance smaller, so the current is more than $0.25\,\text{A}$. Review the chapter Resistance and Ohm's law.

  • Correct. At $6.0\,\text{V}$ the resistance of the lamp is $\tfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{0.50\,\text{A}} = 12\,\Omega$. At half the voltage the filament is cooler and its resistance smaller, so the current is more than half. If needed, review the chapter Resistance and Ohm's law.

Starting level 4: a third resistor in parallel

Two resistors are connected in parallel to a battery. A third resistor is connected in parallel with them.How does the current supplied by the battery change when the terminal voltage stays almost the same?

  • Correct. The voltage across every branch is the same, so the currents of the old branches do not change, and the current of the new branch is added to them. The total resistance decreases. If needed, review the chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit.

  • A resistor added in series would increase the resistance, but one added in parallel opens a new path. The total resistance decreases, and the current increases. Review the chapter Resistors in series and in parallel.

  • A battery maintains an almost constant voltage, and the current is set by the resistance of the circuit. The new branch takes its own current, and the currents of the branches add up. Review the chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance.

Starting level 5: which resistor heats up more

Resistors of $10\,\Omega$ and $20\,\Omega$ are connected in parallel to a $9.0\,\text{V}$ voltage source. In which resistor is more electrical energy transformed into internal energy?

  • The same voltage does not mean the same power: a larger current passes through the smaller resistance, so its power $P = UI$ is larger. Review the chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor.

  • Correct. $P = \tfrac{U^2}{R}$, so the power of the $10\,\Omega$ resistor is $\tfrac{(9.0\,\text{V})^2}{10\,\Omega} = 8.1\,\text{W}$ and that of the $20\,\Omega$ resistor $4.05\,\text{W}$. If needed, review the chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor.

  • That is true in a series connection, where the current is the same. In parallel the voltage across both is the same, so the power $\tfrac{U^2}{R}$ is larger for the smaller resistance: $8.1\,\text{W}$ and $4.05\,\text{W}$. Review the chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor.

Starting level 6: the headlights dim

The headlights of a car are on while the engine is being started. For a moment the starter motor draws a current of over $100\,\text{A}$ from the battery, and the headlights dim.Why?

  • Correct. $U = E - R_\text{s} I$: when the current is large, $R_\text{s} I$ grows and the terminal voltage falls, so the power of the lights $\tfrac{U^2}{R}$ decreases. If needed, review the chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance.

  • A battery has no fixed amount of current in store. The lights are in parallel with the motor, and their current is set by the voltage across them. The voltage falls because there is a voltage drop across the internal resistance of the battery. Review the chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance.

  • The emf stays almost the same, and the lights brighten again as soon as the motor stops. The momentary dimming is caused by the voltage drop $R_\text{s} I$ across the internal resistance. Review the chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance.

Show the solution

Given

diameter of the wire $d = 0.30\,\text{mm}$; lengths $0.20$–$1.00\,\text{m}$ and resistances $1.73$–$7.36\,\Omega$ in the table

Find

the resistivity $\rho$ of the wire in the unit $\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

Principle

The resistance of a wire of uniform thickness is directly proportional to its length, $R = \rho\,\tfrac{l}{A}$ (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law). The leads of the meter and the connections add the same resistance to every reading, so the data fit a straight line $R = \tfrac{\rho}{A}\,l + R_0$. The resistivity is obtained from the slope of the line, which the constant term $R_0$ does not affect.

Formula

$\dfrac{\Delta R}{\Delta l}$$= \dfrac{\rho}{A}$

so

$\rho$$= A \cdot \dfrac{\Delta R}{\Delta l}$

where

$A$$= \pi r^2$
$= \pi \left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2$

Conversions

$0.15\,\text{mm} = 0.15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$ and finally $1\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} = 10^{6}\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

Substitution

The slope of the line fitted to the points is $7.07\,\Omega/\text{m}$, and the constant term is $0.30\,\Omega$.

$A$$= \pi \cdot (0.15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2$
$= 7.069 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{m}^2$

so

$\rho$$= 7.069 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{m}^2 \cdot 7.07\,\tfrac{\Omega}{\text{m}}$
$= 4.998 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$
$= 0.4998\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

Answer

$\rho \approx 0.50\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the diameter $0.30\,\text{mm}$. The wire is constantan. When the length approaches zero, the resistance approaches $0.30\,\Omega$: this is the resistance of the measuring leads and of the crocodile-clip connections, which is included in every reading.

Reasonableness

From the end points

$\tfrac{7.36\,\Omega - 1.73\,\Omega}{1.00\,\text{m} - 0.20\,\text{m}}$
$= 7.04\,\Omega/\text{m}$

which gives the same result $0.50\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$. Iron ($0.10\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$) and nichrome ($1.1\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$) have clearly different values, so the identification is reliable. If the resistivity were calculated from a single point without correcting for the leads, the result would be about $22\,\%$ too large for a short wire.

4Theory

Key idea

In a direct-current circuit the source maintains a voltage, and the voltage drives a current through the circuit. The size of the current is set by the resistance of the whole circuit, so the same battery gives different currents to different loads. Current is not used up in a component: the component transforms the electrical energy carried by the charges into light, motion or internal energy. Power tells how fast energy is transformed.

In a circuit problem in the exam it pays to work out the connection first. The same current passes through components connected in series, and the voltage across components connected in parallel is the same. After that Kirchhoff's laws and Ohm's law give the currents and voltages, and the power and energy follow from them.

This answers the opening question. The devices of the cottage are connected to the battery in parallel, so the voltage across each is $12\,\text{V}$. The LED lights take a current of $\tfrac{6.0\,\text{W}}{12\,\text{V}} = 0.50\,\text{A}$ and the cool box $\tfrac{48\,\text{W}}{12\,\text{V}} = 4.0\,\text{A}$, and the battery supplies their sum $4.5\,\text{A}$. The battery capacity of $100\,\text{Ah}$ lasts $200\,\text{h}$, that is, more than eight days, for the lights alone, but only about $22\,\text{h}$ for both.

Direct-current circuits and electrical energy

Current, voltage and resistance

$$\begin{aligned} I &= \frac{Q}{t} \\ R &= \frac{U}{I} \\ R &= \rho\,\frac{l}{A} \end{aligned}$$

where $I$ is the current ($\text{A}$), $Q$ the charge that has passed through a cross-section ($\text{C}$), $t$ the time ($\text{s}$), $U$ the voltage across the component ($\text{V}$), $R$ the resistance ($\Omega$), $\rho$ the resistivity ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$), $l$ the length of the wire ($\text{m}$) and $A$ the cross-sectional area ($\text{m}^2$). By Ohm's law the resistance of a metal conductor is constant when the temperature stays the same. The resistance of a filament lamp increases as the filament heats up. An ammeter is connected in series and a voltmeter in parallel.

Connections and Kirchhoff's laws

$$\begin{aligned} R &= R_1 + R_2 + \dots \\ \frac{1}{R} &= \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots \\ \sum I_\text{in} &= \sum I_\text{out} \\ \sum \Delta V &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

The first is the total resistance $R$ of resistors connected in series and the second of resistors connected in parallel. The current law holds at a junction, because charge is conserved. The voltage law holds when a loop is gone round once: the potential $V$ rises in the source from the negative terminal to the positive terminal and falls by $RI$ across a resistor in the direction of the current. That is why the voltage across parallel branches is the same.

Power and energy

$$\begin{aligned} P &= UI \\ P &= RI^2 = \frac{U^2}{R} \\ E &= Pt \end{aligned}$$

where $P$ is the power ($\text{W}$) and $E$ the electrical energy transformed in the time $t$ ($\text{J}$ or $\text{kWh}$). The form $P = UI$ holds for every component, the forms of Joule's law only for a resistor. In series the larger resistance gets the larger power, in parallel the smaller. Units of energy are converted by substitution: $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$. When the same problem also has an emf $E$, the letter $W$ is used for energy.

Battery and accumulator

$$\begin{aligned} U &= E - R_\text{s} I \\ I &= \frac{E}{R + R_\text{s}} \\ Q &= It \end{aligned}$$

where $E$ is the emf ($\text{V}$), $R_\text{s}$ the internal resistance ($\Omega$), $U$ the terminal voltage ($\text{V}$), $R$ the resistance of the external circuit ($\Omega$) and $Q$ the capacity of the battery ($\text{Ah}$ or $\text{C}$). The model assumes that $E$ and $R_\text{s}$ stay constant. The energy of a battery is $W = UQ$, where $U$ is the rated voltage, and during charging the terminal voltage is $U = E + R_\text{s} I$.

The $(I, U)$ graph of a battery is a falling straight line. It crosses the vertical axis at the emf $E$ and the horizontal axis at the short-circuit current $I_\text{k} = \tfrac{E}{R_\text{s}}$, and its slope is $-R_\text{s}$.

Common mistakes

  • Current is used up. The same current enters a component and leaves it; what changes in the component is energy, not current.
  • The source voltage for a series resistor. Only part of the source voltage is across a resistor connected in series. First calculate the current, then $U_1 = R_1 I$.
  • The reciprocal is forgotten. A calculation for a parallel connection first gives $\tfrac{1}{R}$, which is inverted at the end. The total resistance is smaller than the smallest resistance of a branch.
  • A filament lamp treated by Ohm's law. The resistance of a hot filament is larger, so the resistance of the lamp is calculated as the ratio $U : I$ separately at each point.
  • The terminal voltage is always the rated voltage. When current is drawn from a battery, the terminal voltage is smaller than the emf by $R_\text{s} I$.
  • The kilowatt-hour as power. $\text{kWh}$ is a unit of energy: kilowatts are multiplied by hours, and minutes are first converted into hours.
  • Milliampere-hours as energy. $\text{mAh}$ is a unit of charge, so batteries are compared by their energy $W = UQ$.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: the power of a series resistor

Resistors $R_1 = 15\,\Omega$ and $R_2 = 30\,\Omega$ are connected in series to a $9.0\,\text{V}$ voltage source. A student calculated the power of the resistor $R_2$: “$P_2 = \tfrac{U^2}{R_2} = \tfrac{(9.0\,\text{V})^2}{30\,\Omega} = 2.7\,\text{W}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • In series the source voltage is shared between the resistors, $U = U_1 + U_2$. The voltage across the resistor $R_2$ is $6.0\,\text{V}$ and across the resistor $R_1$ $3.0\,\text{V}$. Review the chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit.

  • Both forms work when the quantities of this particular resistor are substituted in them. The mistake is using the source voltage: $U_2 = 6.0\,\text{V}$ gives the same $1.2\,\text{W}$ as $R_2 I^2$. Review the chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor.

  • Correct. The current is $I = \tfrac{9.0\,\text{V}}{45\,\Omega} = 0.20\,\text{A}$, so $U_2 = R_2 I = 6.0\,\text{V}$ and $P_2 = \tfrac{(6.0\,\text{V})^2}{30\,\Omega} = 1.2\,\text{W}$.

Read the graph: the characteristic curve of a filament lamp

The graph shows the voltage of a filament lamp as a function of the current. At a voltage of $3.0\,\text{V}$ the current is $0.25\,\text{A}$. Read the other values you need from the graph.Which statement is correct?

  • Correct. $\tfrac{3.0\,\text{V}}{0.25\,\text{A}} = 12\,\Omega$ and $\tfrac{1.0\,\text{V}}{0.15\,\text{A}} \approx 6.7\,\Omega$. A larger current heats the filament, and the resistance of a hot filament is larger.

  • At each point the resistance is the ratio $U : I$, that is, the slope of the line drawn from the origin to the point, not the slope of the tangent. At a voltage of $3.0\,\text{V}$ it is $12\,\Omega$. Review the chapter Resistance and Ohm's law.

  • Every characteristic curve starts at the origin, because without a voltage no current flows. A curve that obeys Ohm's law is a straight line, but this one bends. Review the chapter Resistance and Ohm's law.

A justified claim: which holds more energy

In a shop the battery of an electric scooter ($36\,\text{V}$, $7.8\,\text{Ah}$) is compared with a power bank ($3.7\,\text{V}$, $26\,800\,\text{mAh}$). The salesperson says that the power bank holds more energy, because it has over three times as many milliampere-hours.Which assessment of the claim is correct?

  • Correct. $W = UQ$: $36\,\text{V} \cdot 7.8\,\text{Ah} \approx 281\,\text{Wh}$ and $3.7\,\text{V} \cdot 26.8\,\text{Ah} \approx 99\,\text{Wh}$. The scooter battery holds almost three times as much energy.

  • The internal resistance affects the terminal voltage under load, but the rated voltage and the capacity are enough to estimate the stored energy: $W = UQ$. Review the chapter Batteries and the charging circuit.

  • The milliampere-hour is a unit of charge, not of energy. The energy is $W = UQ$, so the voltage matters as much as the capacity. Review the chapter Batteries and the charging circuit.

Which connection heats more

Two identical $6.0\,\Omega$ heating resistors are connected to the $12\,\text{V}$ battery of a boat either in series or in parallel. The terminal voltage of the battery stays at almost $12\,\text{V}$.Which statement about the total power of the connections is correct?

  • The voltages across the same resistors are different: in series $6.0\,\text{V}$ across each, in parallel $12\,\text{V}$. The power is proportional to the square of the voltage. Review the chapter Resistors in series and in parallel.

  • Correct. In parallel the voltage across each is $12\,\text{V}$, so $P = 2 \cdot \tfrac{(12\,\text{V})^2}{6.0\,\Omega} = 48\,\text{W}$. In series $P = \tfrac{(12\,\text{V})^2}{12\,\Omega} = 12\,\text{W}$.

  • A larger total resistance reduces the current. In series $R = 12\,\Omega$ and $P = \tfrac{U^2}{R} = 12\,\text{W}$, in parallel $R = 3.0\,\Omega$ and $P = 48\,\text{W}$. Review the chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor.

A car battery on a frosty morning

The terminal voltage of a car battery is $12.6\,\text{V}$ when no current is drawn from it. During starting the starter motor draws $150\,\text{A}$ from the battery, and the terminal voltage of the battery falls to $10.2\,\text{V}$.

What is the internal resistance of the battery?

mΩ
Hint 1: a guiding question

What does the terminal voltage tell you when no current is drawn from the battery?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Without a current the terminal voltage is the emf $E$. Under load $U = E - R_\text{s} I$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$R_\text{s} = \tfrac{E - U}{I}$. Finally convert the ohms into milliohms.

Show the solution

Given

terminal voltage without a current $12.6\,\text{V}$, terminal voltage during starting $U = 10.2\,\text{V}$, current $I = 150\,\text{A}$

Find

the internal resistance $R_\text{s}$ of the battery in the unit $\text{m}\Omega$

Principle

The battery is described by a model in which an ideal source is in series with an internal resistance (chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance). Without a current there is no voltage drop across the internal resistance, so the terminal voltage is the emf $E = 12.6\,\text{V}$. When a current is drawn from the battery, the potential falls by $R_\text{s} I$ across the internal resistance, and the terminal voltage decreases.

Formula

$U = E - R_\text{s} I$, so $R_\text{s} = \dfrac{E - U}{I}$

Conversions

Finally $1\,\Omega = 1000\,\text{m}\Omega$.

Substitution

$R_\text{s}$$= \dfrac{12.6\,\text{V} - 10.2\,\text{V}}{150\,\text{A}}$
$= \dfrac{2.4\,\text{V}}{150\,\text{A}}$
$= 0.016\,\Omega$
$= 16\,\text{m}\Omega$

Answer

$R_\text{s} \approx 16\,\text{m}\Omega$

Two significant figures, because the difference of the voltages $2.4\,\text{V}$ is known to two figures.

Reasonableness

The resistance of the starter motor is $\tfrac{10.2\,\text{V}}{150\,\text{A}} = 68\,\text{m}\Omega$, about four times as large, so most of the voltage is across the motor, as it should be. The short-circuit current would be $\tfrac{12.6\,\text{V}}{0.016\,\Omega} \approx 790\,\text{A}$, a usual order of magnitude for a car battery. A cold battery supplies less current, because its internal resistance increases.

A mixed circuit on a circuit board

A circuit board has a $24\,\text{V}$ voltage source. From its positive terminal the current first passes through the resistor $R_1 = 12\,\Omega$ and then divides into two parallel branches containing the resistors $R_2 = 30\,\Omega$ and $R_3 = 20\,\Omega$. The branches join and return to the negative terminal of the source.

How large a current passes through the resistor $R_2$?

Hint 1: a guiding question

First replace the parallel resistors with one resistor. What is the resistance of the whole circuit?

Hint 2: what is the connection
$R_{23}$$= \tfrac{R_2 R_3}{R_2 + R_3}$
$= 12\,\Omega$

$R = R_1 + R_{23} = 24\,\Omega$, so the current of the source is $1.0\,\text{A}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The voltage across the parallel part is $U_{23} = R_{23} I$, and $I_2 = \tfrac{U_{23}}{R_2}$.

Show the solution

Given

source voltage $U = 24\,\text{V}$, $R_1 = 12\,\Omega$ in series with the parallel resistors $R_2 = 30\,\Omega$ and $R_3 = 20\,\Omega$

Find

the current $I_2$ through the resistor $R_2$

Principle

A mixed circuit is solved from the inside out (chapter Resistors in series and in parallel): the parallel resistors are replaced with one resistor, and $R_1$, which is in series with it, is added. The current of the source passes through $R_1$ and through the parallel part. By the voltage law the source voltage is shared between the resistor $R_1$ and the parallel part, and the voltage across the parallel branches is the same (chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit).

Formula

$R_{23} = \dfrac{R_2 R_3}{R_2 + R_3}$, $I = \dfrac{U}{R_1 + R_{23}}$, $U_{23} = R_{23} I$ and $I_2 = \dfrac{U_{23}}{R_2}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$R_{23}$$= \dfrac{30\,\Omega \cdot 20\,\Omega}{30\,\Omega + 20\,\Omega}$
$= 12\,\Omega$
$I$$= \dfrac{24\,\text{V}}{12\,\Omega + 12\,\Omega}$
$= 1.0\,\text{A}$
$U_{23}$$= 12\,\Omega \cdot 1.0\,\text{A}$
$= 12\,\text{V}$
$I_2$$= \dfrac{12\,\text{V}}{30\,\Omega}$
$= 0.40\,\text{A}$

Answer

$I_2 = 0.40\,\text{A}$

Two significant figures like the resistances.

Reasonableness

The current of the resistor $R_3$ is $\tfrac{12\,\text{V}}{20\,\Omega} = 0.60\,\text{A}$, and $0.40\,\text{A} + 0.60\,\text{A} = 1.0\,\text{A}$ is the current of the source, as the current law requires. The larger resistance gets the smaller branch current, and the voltages $12\,\text{V} + 12\,\text{V}$ add up to the $24\,\text{V}$ of the source.

The heating wire of a toaster

The power of a toaster is $850\,\text{W}$ at a voltage of $230\,\text{V}$. Its heating wire is nichrome, whose resistivity is $1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$, and the diameter of the wire is $0.40\,\text{mm}$. Assume that the resistivity does not change when the wire heats up.

How long is the wire?

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

What is the resistance of the wire when the power and the voltage are known?

Hint 2: what is the connection

$P = \tfrac{U^2}{R}$, so

$R$$= \tfrac{U^2}{P}$
$\approx 62.2\,\Omega$

Show the solution

Given

power $P = 850\,\text{W}$, voltage $U = 230\,\text{V}$, resistivity $\rho = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$, diameter $d = 0.40\,\text{mm}$

Find

the length $l$ of the wire

Principle

The wire of the toaster is a resistor in which electrical energy is transformed into internal energy at the power $P = \tfrac{U^2}{R}$ (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor), so the ratings give the resistance of the wire. The resistance of a wire of uniform thickness depends on its length, cross-sectional area and material (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law).

Formula

$R = \dfrac{U^2}{P}$ and $R = \rho\,\dfrac{l}{A}$, so

$l$$= \dfrac{RA}{\rho}$
$= \dfrac{U^2 \pi r^2}{P \rho}$

Conversions

$r$$= \tfrac{d}{2}$
$= 0.20\,\text{mm}$
$= 0.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$R$$= \dfrac{(230\,\text{V})^2}{850\,\text{W}}$
$= 62.24\,\Omega$
$A$$= \pi \cdot (0.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2$
$= 1.257 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$
$l$$= \dfrac{62.24\,\Omega \cdot 1.257 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2}{1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}}$
$= 7.11\,\text{m}$

Answer

$l \approx 7.1\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the resistivity and the diameter.

Reasonableness

A seven-metre wire fits in a toaster when it is wound in zigzags on both sides of the bread slots. Exercise 5.1-4 Three claims about electrical safety 15 points

Made-up example data · The claims and the figures are invented.

At a residents' evening of a housing company, electrical safety was discussed, and three claims were made during the evening.

  1. “An extension cord heats up because part of the current is used up in the cord. That is why a thin extension cord is dangerous only if it is long.”
  2. “When more devices are connected to the same socket circuit, the current of each device decreases, because the current of the circuit is shared between more devices.”
  3. “A bird can sit safely on a bare high-voltage line, but if at the same time it touches an earthed pylon with its wing, it gets an electric shock.”

The voltage of the sockets at home is $230\,\text{V}$, and the current and power of heating devices can be estimated with the same laws as in a direct-current circuit. The resistivity of copper is $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$ and that of aluminium $2.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.

  1. a) Assess claim 1. As an example, estimate the power that is transformed into internal energy in a $15\,\text{m}$ extension cord whose two conductors each have a cross-sectional area of $0.75\,\text{mm}^2$, when a current of $10\,\text{A}$ passes through the cord. 5 p
  2. b) Assess claim 2. Use as an example a $2000\,\text{W}$ kettle and an $850\,\text{W}$ toaster on the same circuit, whose fuse is $10\,\text{A}$. 5 p
  3. c) Assess claim 3. Estimate the voltage between the bird's feet when the feet are $4.0\,\text{cm}$ apart on an aluminium conductor whose cross-sectional area is $400\,\text{mm}^2$ and which carries $600\,\text{A}$. 5 p

Model answer

a)

Find

whether claim 1 is true and how much power is transformed into internal energy in the $15\,\text{m}$ extension cord

Principle

Current is not used up: the same charge passes along the cord to the device and returns along the other conductor (chapter Electric circuit, current and voltage). The conductors have the resistance $R = \rho\,\tfrac{l}{A}$ (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law), and in a resistance electrical energy is transformed into internal energy at the power $P = RI^2$ (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor).

Model answer

The claim is wrong. No current is used up in the cord: the current going to the device and the current returning from it are equal. The cord heats up because in its resistance electrical energy is transformed into internal energy at the power $P = RI^2$. The current passes through two conductors, so the distance is $2 \cdot 15\,\text{m} = 30\,\text{m}$, and $R = 1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot \tfrac{30\,\text{m}}{0.75 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2} = 0.68\,\Omega$. With a current of $10\,\text{A}$ the power is $0.68\,\Omega \cdot (10\,\text{A})^2 = 68\,\text{W}$, like a small heater inside the cord. The resistance is proportional to the length and inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area, so a thin cord heats up equally much per unit length whether it is short or long. The power grows as the square of the current, so the decisive factor is a large current: a thin cord is dangerous with a large current even when it is short, and especially when it is wound on a reel, so that the heat cannot escape. A thicker cord of $1.5\,\text{mm}^2$ would halve the power to $34\,\text{W}$.

b)

Find

whether claim 2 is true when a $2000\,\text{W}$ kettle and an $850\,\text{W}$ toaster are on the same $10\,\text{A}$ circuit

Principle

The sockets of a home are connected in parallel, so the mains voltage is across every device (chapter Resistors in series and in parallel). The current of a heating device is $I = \tfrac{P}{U}$ (chapter Electrical energy at home). The wiring of the circuit carries the sum of the currents of the branches (chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit).

Model answer

The claim is wrong. The sockets are in parallel, so the voltage across every device is $230\,\text{V}$, and a device takes its own current $I = \tfrac{P}{U}$ regardless of the other devices. The kettle takes $\tfrac{2000\,\text{W}}{230\,\text{V}} = 8.7\,\text{A}$ and the toaster $\tfrac{850\,\text{W}}{230\,\text{V}} = 3.7\,\text{A}$ whether or not the other one is on. By the current law the wiring of the circuit carries their sum $12.4\,\text{A}$, which exceeds the $10\,\text{A}$ of the fuse. The fuse cuts off the current, because too large a current would heat the wiring of the circuit (power $RI^2$). So a new device does not reduce the current of the others but increases the total current of the circuit, and that is exactly why a circuit must not be overloaded.

c)

Find

whether claim 3 is true, and how large the voltage between the bird's feet is

Principle

The current through the body is driven by the potential difference, that is, the voltage, between the two points where the body is in contact (chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit). In a conductor the potential falls by $RI$ in the direction of the current, and the resistance of a short piece of conductor is $R = \rho\,\tfrac{l}{A}$ (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law).

Model answer

The claim is true. The piece of line between the bird's feet is $4.0\,\text{cm}$ long, so its resistance is $2.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot \tfrac{0.040\,\text{m}}{400 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2} = 2.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega$, and the voltage between the feet is $2.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega \cdot 600\,\text{A} \approx 1.6\,\text{mV}$. Such a small voltage drives hardly any current through the bird's body, although the whole bird is, together with the line, at a high potential relative to the earth. The pylon is at the potential of the earth. If the bird touches it at the same time, almost the whole voltage between the line and the earth, tens or hundreds of kilovolts, is across its body, and a dangerous current passes through the body. So what is dangerous is not a high potential but a large potential difference between two points of the body.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the length of the cord is taken as $15\,\text{m}$, although the current passes through two conductors, or square millimetres are not converted into square metres.
  • In part a the power is calculated with the mains voltage, $\tfrac{(230\,\text{V})^2}{0.68\,\Omega}$, although only $RI \approx 6.8\,\text{V}$ is across the cord.
  • In part b the devices are assumed to be in series, in which case a new device would reduce the current.
  • In part c the argument is that the bird's feet insulate it, or that the voltage of the line is small.

$\Delta x$$= p \cdot x + n \cdot r$
$R_\text{min}$$= \dfrac{U - \Delta U}{I + \Delta I}$
$R_\text{max}$$= \dfrac{U + \Delta U}{I - \Delta I}$

c)

Find

how the measurement is improved so that the validity of Ohm's law can be established reliably

Principle

A dependence is established most reliably from a graph in which a straight line has been fitted to many points (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line). The error limit of a meter is smallest when the smallest range on which the reading fits is used (chapter Measurements in an electric circuit). Ohm's law concerns a constant temperature, so the temperature must be kept constant (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law).

Model answer

At least eight points are measured over a wide voltage range, for example $0.5$–$10\,\text{V}$, and an $(I, U)$ graph is drawn. If the resistor obeys Ohm's law, the points lie on a straight line through the origin whose slope is $R$; a deviation from the line shows better in a graph than in three ratios. Each reading is taken on the smallest range on which it fits: for example $0.510\,\text{V}$ on the $2\,\text{V}$ range gives the error limit $0.005 \cdot 0.510\,\text{V} + 2 \cdot 0.001\,\text{V} \approx 0.005\,\text{V}$, that is, about $0.9\,\%$. The temperature is kept constant: the power of the resistor is kept small, the readings are taken quickly, and the voltage is both increased and decreased, so that any effect of heating would show as differences. The ammeter is kept in series and the voltmeter in parallel with the resistor, and the effect of the meters is assessed: the resistance of the voltmeter is of the order of megohms, that is, much larger than $100\,\Omega$, so a negligibly small current passes through it. The result is given with its error limits.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the error limit is calculated from the percentage alone, so that the digits part $n \cdot r$ is forgotten and the error limit is too small.
  • In part a milliamperes are not converted into amperes, so that the resistance is wrong by a factor of a thousand.
  • In part b the conclusion is accepted because the numbers increase, without comparing the difference with the error limits.
  • In part c the only suggestion is to repeat the measurements in the same way, which does not reduce the error limit of the meter.

Also accepted

  • In part a the error limit as the sum of the relative error limits $\tfrac{\Delta R}{R} = \tfrac{\Delta U}{U} + \tfrac{\Delta I}{I} \approx 9\,\%$.

The manufacturer states that the warm heater gives off $1.6\,\text{kWh}$ of energy to its surroundings per day. Electricity including transmission costs $14\,\text{c/kWh}$. The specific heat capacity of water is $4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, and the mass of one litre of water is $1.0\,\text{kg}$.

  2. b) What is the average power of the heat losses of the heater, and how much do the losses cost in a year? 4 p

  4. d) A shower uses $8.0\,\text{l}$ of water at $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ per minute for $10\,\text{min}$. How much electrical energy does the hot water of the shower correspond to, when the water has been heated from $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$? 3 p

Model answer

a)

Given

volume of the water $300\,\text{l}$, so the mass $m = 300\,\text{kg}$; temperatures $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $70\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; power $P = 3.0\,\text{kW}$; $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$

Find

the heating time $t$

Principle

In the resistor electrical energy is transformed into internal energy (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor), and from the resistor it is transferred as heat to the water. The efficiency of a resistive heater is practically $100\,\%$, and when the heat losses are ignored, all the electrical energy heats the water (chapter Heating and cooling of matter). The power is constant, so the energy is the power times the time.

Formula

$Pt = cm\Delta T$, so $t = \dfrac{cm\Delta T}{P}$

Conversions

$3.0\,\text{kW}$$= 3.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{W}$
$= 3000\,\text{W}$

the change in temperature

$70\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$$= 55\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
$= 55\,\text{K}$

finally $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$

Substitution

$Q$$= 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 300\,\text{kg} \cdot 55\,\text{K}$
$= 6.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{J}$
$t$$= \dfrac{6.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{J}}{3000\,\text{W}}$
$= 23\,045\,\text{s}$
$= 6.40\,\text{h}$

Answer

The heating takes about $t \approx 6.4\,\text{h}$, two significant figures like the power $3.0\,\text{kW}$.

Reasonableness

The energy received by the water is

$\tfrac{6.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{J}}{3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/kWh}}$
$= 19.2\,\text{kWh}$

and $3.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 6.4\,\text{h} = 19.2\,\text{kWh}$. The heating fits into the night hours, and it costs about

$19.2\,\text{kWh} \cdot 0.14\,\tfrac{\text{€}}{\text{kWh}}$
$\approx 2.7\,\text{€}$

Heat losses lengthen the time slightly.

b)

Given

Find

the average power $P$ of the losses and the annual cost $K$

Formula

Conversions

$P$$= \dfrac{1600\,\text{Wh}}{24\,\text{h}}$
$= 66.7\,\text{W}$

in a year

$365 \cdot 1.6\,\text{kWh}$$= 584\,\text{kWh}$
$K$$= 0.14\,\tfrac{\text{€}}{\text{kWh}} \cdot 584\,\text{kWh}$
$= 81.8\,\text{€}$

Answer

The average power of the losses is $P \approx 67\,\text{W}$, and the losses cost about $82\,\text{€}$ a year. The precision is two significant figures like the loss $1.6\,\text{kWh}$ and the price $14\,\text{c/kWh}$.

Reasonableness

c)

Given

power $P = 3.0\,\text{kW}$ at the voltage $U = 230\,\text{V}$, new voltage $U' = 220\,\text{V}$, resistance assumed to be the same

Find

the power $P'$ of the resistor at a voltage of $220\,\text{V}$

Principle

The power marked on a device is the power at the rated voltage, and it gives the resistance of the resistor. When the resistance stays the same, the power of the resistor is proportional to the square of the voltage (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor).

Formula

$R$$= \dfrac{U^2}{P}$
$P'$$= \dfrac{U'^2}{R}$
$= P\left(\dfrac{U'}{U}\right)^2$

Conversions

$3.0\,\text{kW} = 3000\,\text{W}$ and finally $1\,\text{kW} = 1000\,\text{W}$

Substitution

$R$$= \dfrac{(230\,\text{V})^2}{3000\,\text{W}}$
$= 17.63\,\Omega$
$P'$$= \dfrac{(220\,\text{V})^2}{17.63\,\Omega}$
$= 2745\,\text{W}$
$= 2.74\,\text{kW}$

Answer

The power is about $P' \approx 2.7\,\text{kW}$, two significant figures like the power $3.0\,\text{kW}$.

Reasonableness

The voltage decreases by $4.3\,\%$, and the power by about twice as much, $8.5\,\%$, as the square dependence gives. The heating would take about $\tfrac{19.2\,\text{kWh}}{2.74\,\text{kW}} \approx 7.0\,\text{h}$, but the same amount of energy is used.

Find

Principle

Subtracting one equation from the other gives first $R_\text{s}$ and then $E$.

Formula

$E = U_1 + R_\text{s} I_1$
