Magnetism, induction and light

Why does the electricity for the cottages on an island travel in a submarine cable at a voltage of $20\,\text{kV}$, although the cottages use a voltage of $230\,\text{V}$?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve problems on magnetism, induction and light by choosing a suitable model and its conditions
  • draw and interpret graphs of measured data on magnetic fields and light
  • assess claims and measurement reports on power transmission and on light phenomena with justification

Prior knowledge

  • The magnetic force acts on a moving charge perpendicular to both the velocity and the fieldchapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields

1Think about it

The ten cottages of an island village get their electricity from the mainland through a submarine cable $1.0\,\text{km}$ long. The total resistance of the cable's conductors is $0.50\,\Omega$, and at most $40\,\text{kW}$ of power is transmitted along the cable from the mainland. In the cottages the voltage is $230\,\text{V}$, but the transmission voltage in the cable is $20\,\text{kV}$. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a problem: the numbers are given, but the formula is not.

How large a current would flow in the cable at each voltage, and what fraction of the power would turn into heat in the cable?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Diagnostic 1: a proton in a magnetic field

A proton flies in a vacuum chamber perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. There is no electric field.How does the motion of the proton change in the field?

  • The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it neither brakes nor increases the speed. In this situation only an electric field could change the speed. Review this in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields.

  • Correct. The force $F = qvB$ is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work. It turns the direction of motion and acts as the centripetal force, and the radius of the path is $r = \tfrac{mv}{qB}$. Review if needed in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields.

  • The magnetic force does not point in the direction of the field but perpendicular to both the velocity and the field. That is why it cannot accelerate the proton in the direction of the field. Review this in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields.

Diagnostic 2: a magnet inside a coil

A strong neodymium magnet is at rest inside a coil, and the ends of the coil are connected to a voltmeter. What does the meter show?

  • A strong field gives a large flux, but the voltage depends on the rate of change of the flux $\tfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$. The flux of a magnet at rest does not change. Review this in the chapter Electromagnetic induction.

  • Correct. By the law of induction $e = -N\tfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$ a voltage arises only from a change of flux. When the magnet is moved, the reading differs from zero. Review if needed in the chapter Electromagnetic induction.

  • The magnet does not weaken, and a voltage is produced not by the field but by its change. When the flux is constant, the voltage is zero. Review this in the chapter Electromagnetic induction.

Diagnostic 3: a receding magnet

A light copper ring hangs from a thread. The north pole of a bar magnet is next to the ring, and the magnet is pulled quickly away from the ring.How does the ring move?

  • Copper is not ferromagnetic, but it conducts electricity. The changing flux induces a current in the ring, and the field of the magnet exerts a force on the current. Review this in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

  • The induced current does not oppose the field but its change. When the magnet recedes, the flux decreases, and the ring opposes the decrease by attracting the magnet. Review this in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

  • Correct. The flux through the ring decreases, so the field of the induced current points in the middle of the ring in the same direction as the field of the magnet. A south pole forms on the side of the ring facing the magnet, and it attracts the north pole. Review if needed in the chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents.

Diagnostic 4: two current-carrying wires

Currents in the same direction flow in two long parallel wires. What do the wires do to each other?

  • Correct. The field $B_1 = \tfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ of wire $1$ exerts the force $F = I_2 l B_1$ on wire $2$, and with currents in the same direction the force points towards the other wire. Review if needed in the chapter Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker.

  • The field lines are circles around the wire, and at the other wire it is perpendicular to it. That is why the force is as large as possible, $F = I_2 l B_1$. Review this in the chapter Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker.

  • The wires are electrically neutral, so the electric force does not decide this. Each wire is in the magnetic field of the other, and with currents in the same direction the force pulls the wires together. Review this in the chapter Force on a current-carrying wire: the electric motor and the loudspeaker.

Diagnostic 5: light from glass into air

Light inside glass reaches the boundary between glass and air at an angle of incidence of $50^\circ$. The refractive index of the glass is $1.52$, so the critical angle is about $41^\circ$.What happens to the light?

  • This happens at angles of incidence smaller than the critical angle. When the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle, the law of refraction would give $\sin\alpha_2 > 1$, so there is no refracted ray. Review this in the chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre.

  • Correct. $\sin\alpha_\text{r} = \tfrac{1.00}{1.52}$, so $\alpha_\text{r} \approx 41^\circ$. At the angle of incidence $50^\circ$ the law of refraction would give $\sin\alpha_2 = 1.52 \cdot \sin 50^\circ \approx 1.16$, which is impossible, so the light is totally internally reflected. Review if needed in the chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre.

  • Into an optically less dense medium light refracts away from the normal, and at an angle of incidence larger than the critical angle there is no refracted ray at all. Review this in the chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre.

Diagnostic 6: white light in a grating

White light falls perpendicularly on a grating. Which colour deviates most from the direction of the central maximum in the first-order spectrum?

  • Correct. By the grating equation $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, in the same order $\sin\theta$ is proportional to the wavelength, so red deviates most. Review if needed in the chapter Interference and diffraction of light.

  • In a prism violet deviates most, because the refractive index is largest for violet. In a grating the direction is set by the path difference, $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, so the longest wavelength deviates most. Review this in the chapter Interference and diffraction of light.

  • The grating is the same, but the direction of a maximum depends on the wavelength: $\sin\theta = \tfrac{k\lambda}{d}$. That is why white light is split into a spectrum by a grating. Review this in the chapter Interference and diffraction of light.

A magnet in four tubes

Made-up example data · Invented example: the time for the plastic tube is calculated from free fall, and the times for the metal tubes are of the order of magnitude of school experiments.

In a physics lesson the same $12.0\,\text{g}$ neodymium magnet was dropped into four vertical tubes $1.00\,\text{m}$ long. The tubes have the same diameter, and the magnet does not stick to any of them. The fall time was read from a slow-motion phone video. The video also shows that in the aluminium and copper tubes the magnet falls at a steady speed for almost the whole distance.

Tube $t\ (\text{s})$
plastic $0.46$
aluminium $3.4$
copper $6.3$
copper with a lengthwise slit $0.62$

First think about why the metal tubes slow the magnet down although it does not stick to them, and why the slit changes the result. Answer below with the number for the copper tube. The model answer compares all the tubes.

At what rate does the potential energy of the magnet turn into internal energy of the copper tube? Answer in milliwatts.

mW
Hint 1: a guiding question

What force brakes the magnet when it falls at a steady speed? How large must it be?

Hint 2: what is the connection

In steady motion the braking force is equal to the weight $mg$, and the speed is $v = \tfrac{h}{t}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The power is $P = Fv = mg \cdot \tfrac{h}{t}$, and finally $1\,\text{W} = 1000\,\text{mW}$.

Show the solution

Given

mass of the magnet $m = 12.0\,\text{g}$, length of the tube $h = 1.00\,\text{m}$, fall time in the copper tube $t = 6.3\,\text{s}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; steady speed for almost the whole distance

Find

the power $P$ at which potential energy turns into internal energy of the copper tube, in $\text{mW}$

Principle

The falling magnet changes the magnetic flux through the tube, so eddy currents are induced in the metal tube. By Lenz's law their field opposes the change and brakes the magnet, and the currents heat the tube (chapter Lenz's law and eddy currents). The speed is steady, so by Newton's first law the braking force is equal to the weight, and the kinetic energy does not change: the whole decrease in potential energy turns into internal energy. Power is force times speed.

Formula

$v = \dfrac{h}{t}$, $F = mg$ and $P = Fv = \dfrac{mgh}{t}$

Conversions

$12.0\,\text{g}$$= 12.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
$= 0.0120\,\text{kg}$

and finally $1\,\text{W} = 1000\,\text{mW}$

Substitution

$v$$= \dfrac{1.00\,\text{m}}{6.3\,\text{s}}$
$= 0.159\,\text{m/s}$

so

$P$$= 0.0120\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 0.159\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
$= 0.01869\,\text{W}$
$= 18.69\,\text{mW}$

Answer

$P \approx 19\,\text{mW}$

Two significant figures like the fall time $6.3\,\text{s}$.

Reasonableness

The plastic tube does not brake: the free-fall time is

$\sqrt{\tfrac{2h}{g}}$$= \sqrt{\tfrac{2 \cdot 1.00\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}}$
$\approx 0.45\,\text{s}$

the same as the measured $0.46\,\text{s}$. The resistivity of copper is smaller than that of aluminium, so the eddy currents and the braking are stronger. In the aluminium tube the power is

$\tfrac{0.0120\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.00\,\text{m}}{3.4\,\text{s}}$
$\approx 35\,\text{mW}$

larger, because the same energy is converted in a shorter time. The slit cuts the path of the currents circling the tube, so the braking stays small. At the end of the copper tube the kinetic energy is only

$\tfrac12 \cdot 0.0120\,\text{kg} \cdot (0.159\,\text{m/s})^2$
$\approx 0.15\,\text{mJ}$

while $118\,\text{mJ}$ of potential energy was released.

4Theory

Key idea

The phenomena of the module are connected with two fields. A magnetic field $\overline{B}$ exerts a force only on a moving charge and on a current-carrying wire, and the force is perpendicular to both the motion and the field. A changing magnetic flux produces a voltage: the generator, the transformer, the induction hob and the eddy-current brake are based on this. When the electric and magnetic fields change and produce each other, an electromagnetic wave forms, and its visible part is light.

In exam problems it pays to ask first what changes. If the flux does not change, no voltage is produced. When light passes from one medium into another, the frequency stays the same, but the speed and the wavelength change. The colours of light and bright and dark spots are explained either by refraction or by interference, and in a problem you have to recognise which one it is.

This gives the answer to the opening question. A power of $40\,\text{kW}$ is transmitted along the cable from the mainland, and the resistance of the cable is $0.50\,\Omega$. At the voltage $230\,\text{V}$ the current in the cable would be $I = \tfrac{P}{U} \approx 174\,\text{A}$, and $RI^2 \approx 15\,\text{kW}$, or $38\,\%$ of the power, would turn into heat. At the voltage $20\,\text{kV}$ the current is only $2.0\,\text{A}$, and the loss is $2.0\,\text{W}$. Transformers step the voltage up on the mainland and down on the island.

Magnetism, induction and light

Magnetic field and forces

$$\begin{aligned} F &= qvB\sin\alpha \\ r &= \frac{mv}{qB} \\ F &= IlB\sin\alpha \\ B &= \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} \\ B &= \mu_0 \frac{NI}{l} \end{aligned}$$

where $q$ is the charge ($\text{C}$), $v$ the speed ($\text{m/s}$), $B$ the magnetic flux density ($\text{T}$), $\alpha$ the angle between the velocity or the wire and the field, $m$ the mass ($\text{kg}$), $r$ the radius of the circular path or the distance from the wire ($\text{m}$), $I$ the current ($\text{A}$), $l$ the length of the wire or the coil ($\text{m}$), $N$ the number of turns and $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$. The formula for the radius holds when the velocity is perpendicular to a uniform field and small compared with the speed of light. The magnetic force does not change the speed. The formula for the wire holds for a long straight wire, and the formula for the coil at the middle of a long coil.

Induction and alternating current

$$\begin{aligned} e &= -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} \\ e_0 &= NBA \cdot 2\pi f \\ U &= \frac{U_0}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac{U_1}{U_2} &= \frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{I_2}{I_1} \\ P_\text{h} &= RI^2 \end{aligned}$$

where $e$ is the induced voltage ($\text{V}$), $\Phi = BA$ the magnetic flux ($\text{Wb}$) when the field is perpendicular to the surface $A$, $e_0$ the peak value of the generator voltage, $f$ the rotation frequency ($\text{Hz}$), $U$ and $U_0$ the rms value and the peak value, the indices $1$ and $2$ the primary and secondary side of the transformer, and $P_\text{h}$ the power loss of the transmission line when $I = \tfrac{P}{U}$. The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change of flux. The voltage of a rod moving in a field is $e = vBl$. The transformer equation applies to an ideal transformer and an alternating voltage.

Light

$$\begin{aligned} c &= f\lambda \\ \lambda_\text{max} T &= b \\ n_1\sin\alpha_1 &= n_2\sin\alpha_2 \\ \sin\alpha_\text{r} &= \frac{n_2}{n_1} \\ d\sin\theta &= k\lambda \\ I &= I_0\cos^2\theta \end{aligned}$$

where $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $f$ is the frequency, $\lambda$ the wavelength, $T$ the absolute temperature ($\text{K}$), $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$, $n = \tfrac{c}{v}$ the refractive index, $\alpha$ the angles from the normal, $\alpha_\text{r}$ the critical angle when $n_1 > n_2$, $d$ the grating constant, $\theta$ the direction of a maximum of the grating, $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ and $I_0$ the intensity of the polarised light before the analyser. In Malus's law $\theta$ is the angle between the direction of polarisation and the transmission axis of the analyser. In a thin film the path difference is $2nd$ when the light arrives almost perpendicularly. When only one of the reflections inverts the wave, $2nd = \left(k + \tfrac12\right)\lambda$ gives constructive interference; when both invert it, $2nd = k\lambda$.

Common mistakes

  • Force in the direction of the field. The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity and the field, and on a negative charge it points in the opposite direction.
  • Large field, large voltage. The induced voltage depends on the rate of change of the flux, not on the size of the flux.
  • Lenz opposes the field. The induced current opposes the change of flux: a ring attracts a receding magnet.
  • Loss from the formula $\tfrac{U^2}{R}$. The transmission voltage is not across the line. First calculate $I = \tfrac{P}{U}$, then $RI^2$.
  • Peak value and rms value mixed up. The $230\,\text{V}$ of a socket is the rms value.
  • Angle from the surface. The angles of incidence and refraction are measured from the normal.
  • Small angle in a grating. First calculate the angle from the tangent $\tan\theta = \tfrac{x}{L}$, and only then the sine.
  • Wien in degrees Celsius. The temperature is in kelvins.

Find the mistake: the loss of a power line

A power plant transmits $50\,\text{MW}$ of power along a power line at a voltage of $110\,\text{kV}$. The total resistance of the conductors is $4.0\,\Omega$.A student calculated the power loss: “$P_\text{h} = \tfrac{U^2}{R} = \tfrac{(110\,000\,\text{V})^2}{4.0\,\Omega} = 3.0\,\text{GW}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • Correct. $I = \tfrac{50 \cdot 10^6\,\text{W}}{110 \cdot 10^3\,\text{V}} \approx 455\,\text{A}$ and $P_\text{h} = RI^2 = 4.0\,\Omega \cdot (455\,\text{A})^2 \approx 830\,\text{kW}$, about $1.7\,\%$ of the transmitted power.

  • The resistance $4.0\,\Omega$ is already the resistance of the whole line. In the expression $\tfrac{U^2}{R}$ the voltage should be the voltage across the resistance, and the voltage drop of the line is only $RI$. Review this in the chapter The transformer and the transmission of electrical energy.

  • The transmission voltage and current are given as rms values, with which the power is $P = UI$. The mistake is that $110\,\text{kV}$ is not across the resistance of the line. Review this in the chapter The transformer and the transmission of electrical energy.

Read the graph: the voltage of a generator

The graph shows the voltage of a small generator as a function of time. Which statement is correct?

  • The rms value is correct, but the frequency is not. The voltage is zero every $20\,\text{ms}$, but there are two zeros in each period, once rising and once falling. The period is $40\,\text{ms}$, so $f = \tfrac{1}{T} = 25\,\text{Hz}$. Review this in the chapter The generator and alternating current.

  • The frequency is correct, but $12\,\text{V}$ is the peak value. The rms value of a sinusoidal voltage is $U = \tfrac{U_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 8.5\,\text{V}$. Review this in the chapter The generator and alternating current.

  • Correct. The cycle repeats every $40\,\text{ms}$, so $f = \tfrac{1}{0.040\,\text{s}} = 25\,\text{Hz}$, and $U = \tfrac{12\,\text{V}}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 8.5\,\text{V}$.

Which particle: a curving path

In the picture a particle enters from the left into the magnetic field of a vacuum chamber, which points into the paper, and its path curves downwards. The particle is a proton, an electron or a neutron.Which statement is correct?

  • The field bends positive and negative charges in opposite directions, and an uncharged neutron would go straight. A path curving downwards shows that the charge is negative. Review this in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields.

  • The magnetic force is perpendicular to the field, not along it. On a proton the force would point upwards here, so a particle curving downwards is negative. Review this in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields.

  • Correct. For a positive charge moving to the right in a field pointing into the paper, the right-hand rule gives an upward force. The path curves downwards, so the charge is negative. Review if needed in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields.

Justified claim: polarised sunglasses

An angler notices that polarised sunglasses remove the glare reflected from the surface of a lake almost completely but dim the forest on the shore only a little.Which explanation is correct?

  • The glare is reflected sunlight, not just blue light. Polarised glasses do not filter colour but the direction of oscillation. Review this in the chapter Polarisation of light.

  • Then the forest would also be dimmed in the same proportion. A polariser selects the direction of oscillation: it lets one direction through and stops the direction perpendicular to it. Review this in the chapter Polarisation of light.

  • Correct. Light reflected from the surface of water is partly polarised parallel to the surface, and completely at the Brewster angle. The transmission axis of the glasses is vertical, so the glare is stopped, but about half of the unpolarised light from the forest gets through. Review if needed in the chapter Polarisation of light.

The wings of an aeroplane

A passenger plane flies horizontally over southern Finland at a speed of $230\,\text{m/s}$. The distance between the wing tips is $34\,\text{m}$. The magnitude of the Earth's magnetic field there is $52\,\mu\text{T}$, and its vertical component is $50\,\mu\text{T}$.

How large an induced voltage arises between the wing tips?

V
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which component of the field is perpendicular to both the wing and the velocity when the plane flies horizontally?

Hint 2: what is the connection

A horizontal wing moves horizontally, so only the vertical component $B_\text{v}$ of the field affects the voltage.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The voltage of a moving conductor is $e = vB_\text{v}l$.

Show the solution

Given

speed $v = 230\,\text{m/s}$ horizontally, wing length from tip to tip $l = 34\,\text{m}$, vertical component of the field $B_\text{v} = 50\,\mu\text{T}$ (whole field $52\,\mu\text{T}$)

Find

the induced voltage $e$ between the wing tips in $\text{V}$

Principle

The conduction electrons of the wing move with the plane in the field, and the magnetic force moves them along the wing until a voltage forms between the tips (chapter Electromagnetic induction). A force along the wing comes only from the component of the field that is perpendicular to both the velocity and the wing. When the wing and the velocity are horizontal, this is the vertical component (chapter Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field).

Formula

$e = vB_\text{v}l$

Conversions

$50\,\mu\text{T} = 50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$

Substitution

$e$$= 230\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot 34\,\text{m}$
$= 0.391\,\text{V}$

Answer

$e \approx 0.39\,\text{V}$

Two significant figures like the vertical component $50\,\mu\text{T}$ and the length $34\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

The units match: $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{T} \cdot \text{m} = \text{V}$. The voltage is of the order of a small battery, because the Earth's field is weak. It cannot be measured inside the plane, because the same voltage is induced in the leads of the meter.

The temperature of Sirius

The peak of the radiation spectrum of Sirius, the brightest star in the sky, is at the wavelength $292\,\text{nm}$. Assume that the star radiates like a black body.

What is the surface temperature of Sirius?

K
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which law connects the peak wavelength of a black-body spectrum and the temperature?

Hint 2: what is the connection

Wien's displacement law $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, where $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$T = \tfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$, with the wavelength in metres: $292\,\text{nm} = 292 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.

Show the solution

Given

peak wavelength of the spectrum $\lambda_\text{max} = 292\,\text{nm}$, Wien's constant $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$; the star radiates approximately like a black body

Find

the surface temperature $T$ in $\text{K}$

Principle

The hotter a black body is, the shorter the wavelength of the peak of its spectrum, and the peak wavelength is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature (chapter Electromagnetic radiation and the black-body spectrum). The surface of a star radiates approximately like a black body.

Formula

$\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = \dfrac{b}{\lambda_\text{max}}$

Conversions

$292\,\text{nm}$$= 292 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
$= 2.92 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$T$$= \dfrac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{2.92 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}}$
$= 9925\,\text{K}$

Answer

$T \approx 9.92 \cdot 10^3\,\text{K}$

Three significant figures like the wavelength $292\,\text{nm}$.

Reasonableness

The surface temperature of the Sun is about $5800\,\text{K}$, and its peak is at about $500\,\text{nm}$. Sirius is almost twice as hot, so its peak is at almost half the wavelength, in the ultraviolet. That is why Sirius looks bluish white.

The diver's window

A diver is at a depth of $3.0\,\text{m}$ in a calm lake and looks straight up. She sees the sky only through a circular area directly above her. Outside it the water surface looks like a mirror reflecting the bottom of the lake. The refractive index of water is $1.33$.

What is the radius of the circular area on the water surface?

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

From which directions can light from the air reach the diver's eye at all?

Hint 2: what is the connection

At the edge of the area light comes from the air almost parallel to the surface and refracts into the water at the critical angle: $\sin\alpha_\text{r} = \tfrac{1.00}{1.33}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The radius is $r = h\tan\alpha_\text{r}$, where $h$ is the depth.

Show the solution

Given

depth $h = 3.0\,\text{m}$, refractive index of water $n_1 = 1.33$, refractive index of air $n_2 = 1.00$

Find

the radius $r$ of the circular area on the water surface in $\text{m}$

Principle

Light entering water from air refracts towards the normal. The largest angle of refraction occurs when the light arrives parallel to the surface, and it is equal to the critical angle between water and air (chapters Reflection and refraction of light and Total internal reflection and optical fibre). That is why light from the sky reaches the eye only from a cone whose half-angle is $\alpha_\text{r}$. When the diver looks at the surface further away, light coming from under the water is totally internally reflected, and the surface looks like a mirror.

Formula

$\sin\alpha_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$

$\tan\alpha_\text{r} = \dfrac{r}{h}$, so $r = h\tan\alpha_\text{r}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$\sin\alpha_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{1.00}{1.33}$
$= 0.752$

so

$\alpha_\text{r}$$= 48.75^\circ$
$r$$= 3.0\,\text{m} \cdot \tan 48.75^\circ$
$= 3.0\,\text{m} \cdot 1.140$
$= 3.42\,\text{m}$

Answer

$r \approx 3.4\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the depth $3.0\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

The critical angle is a little over $45^\circ$, so the radius must be a little larger than the depth, as $3.4\,\text{m} > 3.0\,\text{m}$. Deeper down the area is larger but looks the same size, because the angle stays the same.

An oil film on a puddle

On the water surface of a puddle there is a thin oil film whose thickness at one point is $300\,\text{nm}$. The refractive index of the oil is $1.45$ and that of water $1.33$. White light falls on the film almost perpendicularly.

Which wavelength of visible light ($400$–$700\,\text{nm}$) is reflected most strongly from this point of the film?

nm
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which of the two reflections of the film inverts the wave? Compare the refractive indices at each surface.

Hint 2: what is the connection

The reflection from air to oil ($1.00 \to 1.45$) inverts the wave, the one from oil to water ($1.45 \to 1.33$) does not. With one inversion the condition for constructive interference is $2nd = \left(k + \tfrac12\right)\lambda$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$\lambda = \tfrac{2nd}{k + \frac12}$. Try the values $k = 0, 1, 2$ and choose the visible wavelength.

Show the solution

Given

film thickness $d = 300\,\text{nm}$, refractive index of oil $n = 1.45$, refractive index of water $1.33$, light arrives almost perpendicularly; visible light $400$–$700\,\text{nm}$

Find

the wavelength $\lambda$ of visible light that is reflected most strongly

Principle

The waves reflected from the upper and lower surfaces of the film interfere, and their path difference is $2nd$. Formula

$2nd = \left(k + \tfrac12\right)\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{2nd}{k + \frac12}$, $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$

Conversions

$= 870\,\text{nm}$

3. Explain

A light aluminium ring is slipped around the vertical iron core of an electromagnet. When the direct current of the electromagnet is switched on, the ring jumps up and falls back. When the current is already on, the ring rests in place. If the ring has a narrow slit, it does not jump at all. Explain the observations.

Model answer

When the current is switched on, the field of the electromagnet and the magnetic flux through the ring increase quickly. By the law of induction a voltage is induced in the ring, and because the ring is a closed conductor, an induced current flows in it. By Lenz's law the induced current's own field opposes the increase of the flux: on the underside of the ring a pole of the same kind forms as at the top end of the electromagnet, and they repel each other. For a moment the repulsive force is larger than the weight of the ring, and the ring jumps. When the current is steady, the flux no longer changes, so there is no induced current, and no magnetic force acts on the ring. In a slit ring a voltage is induced, but the current cannot circulate, so no force arises. The energy of the jump comes from the power supply of the electromagnet.

    Find

    Principle

    The secondary voltage of a transformer is produced by induction: the primary current produces a magnetic flux in the iron core, and only a change of the flux induces a voltage in the secondary coil (chapters Electromagnetic induction and The transformer and the transmission of electrical energy).

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    Find

    Principle

    The light stays in the core if it is totally internally reflected at the boundary between core and cladding. Total internal reflection is possible because the light goes from a medium with a larger refractive index towards a medium with a smaller refractive index, and it happens when the angle of incidence is larger than the critical angle (chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre). The angle of incidence is measured from the normal of the boundary, and the normal is perpendicular to the axis of the fibre, so the angle between the ray and the axis is $90^\circ - \alpha$.

    Formula

    $n_1 \sin\alpha_\text{r} = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$, so

    $\sin\alpha_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$

    $= 8.19^\circ$

    Reasonableness

    The refractive indices are almost equal, so the critical angle is close to $90^\circ$: only rays almost parallel to the axis stay in the core. Between glass and air the critical angle would be about $43^\circ$.

    d)

    Find

    why a gently curving fibre guides the light but a sharp bend lets it out

    Principle

    The light stays in the core when the angle of incidence at the boundary between core and cladding is larger than the critical angle, in this fibre at least $81.8^\circ$ (chapter Total internal reflection and optical fibre).

    Model answer

    In a gentle curve the direction of the boundary changes only a little along the path of the ray, so the ray still hits the boundary almost parallel to it, and the angle of incidence stays larger than the critical angle. The light is totally internally reflected and follows the fibre. In a sharp bend the boundary on the outer side of the curve turns in front of the ray, so the ray hits it more steeply: the angle of incidence falls below $81.8^\circ$, the light refracts into the cladding and leaks out. Because the critical angle is close to $90^\circ$, even a fairly small change in the angle of incidence is enough. That is why a minimum allowed bending radius is given for a fibre cable.

    Principle

    b)

    Find

    Principle

    The waves reflected from the two surfaces of a thin film interfere. At perpendicular incidence their path difference is $2nd$, and a wave is inverted when it reflects off a medium with a larger refractive index (chapter Thin-film interference).

    Model answer

    A bubble is a thin film of soapy water with air on both sides. Part of the light is reflected from the outer surface of the film and part from the inner surface, and these waves interfere. Their path difference $2nd$ depends on the thickness $d$ of the film: at some wavelengths the waves reinforce each other and at others they weaken each other. What remains of white light is a mixture of wavelengths that is seen as a colour, and when the thickness of the film varies, the colour changes in stripes. Only the reflection at the outer surface inverts the wave, because there the light reflects from air off soapy water. Just before bursting the top of the bubble is very thin, $2nd \approx 0$, so the reflected waves are almost in opposite phase and cancel each other at all wavelengths, and the spot looks dark. For the bubble the claim is wrong: the colours arise from interference, not from refraction, and the colour depends on the thickness of the film.

The result is the wavelength of red light, so the stated wavelength of the laser, $532\,\text{nm}$, is wrong. We did not find the third-order maximum on the wall, so the grating is faulty.”

    1. a) Calculate the wavelength of the laser from each maximum. 5 p
    2. b) Assess the group's conclusions. 5 p
    3. c) Plan how the measurement can be improved. 5 p

    Skills from the chapters: Interference and diffraction of light, Measuring and the reliability of a measurement, Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    the grating has $N = 600$ lines per millimetre, $L = 80.0\,\text{cm}$, $x_1 = 27.0\,\text{cm}$, $x_2 = 66.4\,\text{cm}$

    Find

    the wavelength $\lambda$ of the laser from each maximum

    Principle

    The maxima of a grating lie in the directions for which $d\sin\theta = k\lambda$, and the grating constant is the line spacing (chapter Interference and diffraction of light). The angle is obtained from the right triangle at the screen, $\tan\theta = \tfrac{x}{L}$. The sine and the tangent are almost equal only at small angles, so the angle is calculated first from the tangent and only then its sine.

    Formula

    $d = \dfrac{1\,\text{mm}}{N}$, $\theta_k = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{x_k}{L}$ and $\lambda = \dfrac{d\sin\theta_k}{k}$

    Conversions

    $d$$= \dfrac{1\,\text{mm}}{600}$
    $= \dfrac{1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}}{600}$
    $= 1.667 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$

    in the ratio of the distances the units cancel, and finally $1\,\text{m} = 10^9\,\text{nm}$.

    Substitution

    $\tan\theta_1$$= \dfrac{27.0\,\text{cm}}{80.0\,\text{cm}}$
    $= 0.3375$

    so

    $\theta_1$$= 18.65^\circ$
    $\lambda$$= 1.667 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot \sin 18.65^\circ$
    $= 1.667 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot 0.3198$
    $= 533.0\,\text{nm}$
    $\tan\theta_2$$= \dfrac{66.4\,\text{cm}}{80.0\,\text{cm}}$
    $= 0.830$

    so

    $\theta_2$$= 39.69^\circ$
    $\lambda$$= \dfrac{1.667 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m} \cdot 0.6386}{2}$
    $= 532.2\,\text{nm}$

    Answer

    From the first order $\lambda \approx 533\,\text{nm}$ and from the second $\lambda \approx 532\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures like the distances.

    Reasonableness

    Both orders give the same result, and it is the wavelength of green light, as the colour of the laser shows. The angle $\theta_2 \approx 40^\circ$ is so large that $\sin\theta_2 = 0.64$ and $\tan\theta_2 = 0.83$ differ clearly.

    b)

    Find

    whether the group's conclusions are justified

    Principle

    The small-angle assumption $\sin\theta \approx \tfrac{x}{L}$ holds only at small angles. The result is compared with the manufacturer's value using the uncertainty, and the number of maxima is limited by the condition $k\lambda < d$ (chapters Interference and diffraction of light and Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

    Model answer

    Neither conclusion is justified. The mistake is in the method of calculation, not in the laser. The small-angle assumption gives from the first order $\tfrac{1.667\,\mu\text{m} \cdot 0.270\,\text{m}}{0.800\,\text{m}} \approx 562\,\text{nm}$, about $6\,\%$ too much, and from the second $692\,\text{nm}$, about $30\,\%$ too much, because the difference between the tangent and the sine grows as the angle grows. Calculated correctly, the result is $532$–$533\,\text{nm}$. If the distances are known to about $0.3\,\text{cm}$, the extreme values for the second order give about $530$–$535\,\text{nm}$, so the result agrees with the manufacturer's value $532\,\text{nm}$. In addition, the laser looked green, which already rules out red light. The third order exists, because $3\lambda = 1.60\,\mu\text{m} < d = 1.667\,\mu\text{m}$, but $\sin\theta_3 = 0.958$, $\theta_3 \approx 73^\circ$ and the maximum is $x_3 = 0.800\,\text{m} \cdot \tan 73.3^\circ \approx 2.7\,\text{m}$ from the central maximum. It is probably beyond the edge of the wall or so faint that it was not noticed. So the grating is not faulty.

    c)

    Find

    how the measurement can be improved

    Principle

    The relative uncertainty decreases when the measured distance is large compared with the uncertainty of reading it. Systematic errors, such as a crooked set-up, are removed by the measuring method, and the result is given with its uncertainty (chapters Measuring and the reliability of a measurement and Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting).

    Model answer

    Measure the distance between the maxima $+k$ and $-k$ on both sides of the central maximum and halve it. Measuring the gap between two light spots is more precise than estimating the centre of the central maximum, and if the distances to the two sides are equal, the beam hits the grating perpendicularly. Use the second order, whose $x$ is large, or increase the distance $L$, so that the reading uncertainty of about $0.3\,\text{cm}$ is a small part of the measured distance. Estimate $\Delta x$ and $\Delta L$, calculate the wavelength with their extreme values and give the result in the form $\lambda \pm \Delta\lambda$, for example $(532 \pm 3)\,\text{nm}$, so that it can be compared with the manufacturer's value. Repeat the measurement, preferably with readings by different people, and use the tangent in the calculation instead of the small-angle assumption. Safety: do not look into the laser beam, do not point it at anyone's eyes, and do not leave reflecting objects in the path of the beam.

    Common mistakes

    • In part a $\sin\theta = \tfrac{x}{L}$ is used, or $\sin\theta = \tfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + L^2}}$ while forgetting to divide by the order.
    • In part a the grating constant is taken as $600\,\text{mm}$ or $\tfrac{1}{600}\,\text{m}$.
    • In part b the conclusion is accepted because the calculated number “looks red”, or the uncertainty is not compared with the difference.
    • In part c only repetitions are suggested; they do not remove the systematic error of the calculation method.

    Exercise 5.3-6 The hospital cyclotron 15 points

    Made-up example data · The cyclotron and the numbers are an invented example. The fields and proton energies of cyclotrons producing tracers are of this order of magnitude.

    A hospital cyclotron accelerates protons, which are used to produce short-lived radioactive tracers for imaging. The cyclotron has two hollow D-shaped electrodes in a uniform magnetic field that is perpendicular to the path of the protons. Inside the electrodes the proton goes round half a circle at a time, and in the narrow gap between them an alternating voltage accelerates it every time it crosses the gap.

    • magnetic flux density $1.20\,\text{T}$
    • mass of the proton $1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$ and charge $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
    • kinetic energy of the proton when it leaves the cyclotron $8.0\,\text{MeV}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
    • peak value of the gap voltage $50\,\text{kV}$; the proton always crosses the gap when the voltage is at its peak
    • the speed of the proton stays below a fifth of the speed of light, so classical mechanics is enough
    1. a) Show that the period of revolution of the proton does not depend on its speed. At what frequency must the gap voltage alternate? 4 p
    2. b) What is the speed of the proton when it comes out of the cyclotron? What is then the radius of its path? 4 p
    3. c) How many revolutions does the proton make in the cyclotron? How long does the acceleration last? 4 p
    4. d) What is the acceleration of the proton just before it comes out of the cyclotron? In which direction does it point? 3 p

    Skills from the chapters: Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields, Moving in a circle: period and speed, Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle, Potential energy and potential in an electric field

    Model answer

    a)

    Given

    magnetic flux density $B = 1.20\,\text{T}$, mass of the proton $m = 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$ and charge $q = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$; velocity perpendicular to the field

    Find

    a proof that the period of revolution does not depend on the speed, and the frequency $f$ of the voltage

    Principle

    The magnetic force $qvB$ is perpendicular to the velocity, so inside an electrode the proton is in uniform circular motion, and the force is its centripetal force (chapters Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields and Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle). In one revolution the proton travels the circumference of the circle, $2\pi r$ (chapter Moving in a circle: period and speed). The direction of the voltage must change every half revolution, when the proton crosses the gap, so the frequency of the voltage is the same as the frequency of revolution.

    Formula

    $qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, so $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}$, and

    $T$$= \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$
    $= \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$

    the speed cancels, and $f = \dfrac{1}{T} = \dfrac{qB}{2\pi m}$

    Conversions

    The quantities are in base units; finally $1\,\text{MHz} = 10^6\,\text{Hz}$.

    Substitution

    $T$$= \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1.20\,\text{T}}$
    $= 5.468 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{s}$

    so

    $f$$= \dfrac{1}{5.468 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{s}}$
    $= 1.829 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{Hz}$
    $= 18.29\,\text{MHz}$

    Answer

    The period of revolution $T = \tfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ depends neither on the speed nor on the radius: a faster proton goes round a larger circle in the same time. The voltage must alternate at the frequency $f \approx 18.3\,\text{MHz}$, three significant figures like the magnetic flux density $1.20\,\text{T}$.

    Reasonableness

    Units:

    $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{C} \cdot \text{T}}$$= \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{C} \cdot \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{A} \cdot \text{m}}}$
    $= \tfrac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}}$
    $= \text{s}$

    The frequency is in the radio-frequency range, as for the accelerating voltages of cyclotrons.

    b)

    Given

    kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = 8.0\,\text{MeV}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, $m$, $q$ and $B$ as in part a

    Find

    the speed $v$ of the proton and the radius $r$ of its path when it comes out

    Principle

    In classical mechanics the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12 mv^2$. The radius of the path is obtained from the equation of the centripetal force as in part a (chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields). The condition is met if the speed stays clearly smaller than the speed of light.

    Formula

    $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_\text{k}}{m}}$ and $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}$

    Conversions

    $8.0\,\text{MeV}$$= 8.0 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
    $= 1.282 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{J}$

    Substitution

    $v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.282 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{J}}{1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}}$
    $= 3.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s}$
    $r$$= \dfrac{1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1.20\,\text{T}}$
    $= 0.3406\,\text{m}$

    Answer

    The speed of the proton is $v \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^7\,\text{m/s}$ and the radius of its path $r \approx 0.34\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the energy $8.0\,\text{MeV}$.

    Reasonableness

    $\tfrac{v}{c}$$= \tfrac{3.914 \cdot 10^7\,\text{m/s}}{3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}$
    $\approx 0.13$

    under a fifth, so by the condition of the problem the classical model is sufficient. An electrode diameter of about $70\,\text{cm}$ suits a hospital device.

    c)

    Given

    gap voltage $U = 50\,\text{kV}$ per crossing, two crossings per revolution, $E_\text{k} = 8.0\,\text{MeV}$, $T = 5.468 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{s}$ (part a)

    Find

    the number of revolutions $n$ and the duration $t$ of the acceleration

    Principle

    The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work and does not change the speed. Only the electric field of the gap does work: at each crossing the kinetic energy of the proton increases by $qU$ (chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field). The proton crosses the gap twice per revolution, and the period of revolution stays the same throughout the acceleration (part a).

    Formula

    $n = \dfrac{E_\text{k}}{2qU}$ and $t = nT$

    Conversions

    The charge of the proton is the elementary charge, so

    $qU$$= 50\,\text{keV}$
    $= 0.050\,\text{MeV}$

    finally $1\,\text{s} = 10^6\,\mu\text{s}$.

    Substitution

    $n$$= \dfrac{8.0\,\text{MeV}}{2 \cdot 0.050\,\text{MeV}}$
    $= 80$
    $t$$= 80 \cdot 5.468 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{s}$
    $= 4.37 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{s}$
    $= 4.37\,\mu\text{s}$

    Answer

    The proton makes about $80$ revolutions, and the acceleration lasts about $4.4\,\mu\text{s}$, two significant figures like the energy $8.0\,\text{MeV}$.

    Reasonableness

    Without the magnetic field the same energy would require a voltage of $8.0\,\text{MV}$; the cyclotron reaches it with a voltage of $50\,\text{kV}$ because the same gap is used $160$ times. The distance travelled is under $80 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 0.34\,\text{m} \approx 170\,\text{m}$, so the short time is plausible.

    d)

    Given

    $v = 3.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s}$ and $r = 0.3406\,\text{m}$ (part b)

    Find

    the acceleration $a$ and its direction when the proton comes out

    Principle

    Inside an electrode the proton is in uniform circular motion, so its acceleration is the centripetal acceleration, which points to the centre of the path (chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle). Newton's second law for the magnetic force gives the same.

    Formula

    $a_\text{n} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$, and as a check $a = \dfrac{qvB}{m}$

    Conversions

    The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

    Substitution

    $a_\text{n}$$= \dfrac{(3.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s})^2}{0.3406\,\text{m}}$
    $= 4.498 \cdot 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$

    Answer

    The acceleration is $a \approx 4.5 \cdot 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$ and points to the centre of the path, two significant figures like the results of part b.

    Reasonableness

    $\tfrac{qvB}{m}$$= \tfrac{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 3.914 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.20\,\text{T}}{1.673 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}$
    $\approx 4.5 \cdot 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$

    the same result. The acceleration is enormous because the mass of the proton is very small and the force about $7.5 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{N}$.

    • In part c the answer $n = 80$ without decimals, because the number of revolutions is a whole number.
