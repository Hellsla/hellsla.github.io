## fy1-6-L1-01
**Given:** the ball starts from rest, $t = 3.0\,\text{s}$, acceleration of free fall $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the speed of the ball $v$ in $\text{m/s}$
**Principle:** Air resistance is ignored, so the ball is in free fall. Then the speed increases by the same amount every second, and the speed of an object that starts from rest is directly proportional to time.
**Formula:** $v = g\,t$. The formula is already solved for the speed we want: $v = g\,t$
**Conversions:** The time is in seconds and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes directly in $\text{m/s}$. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.0\,\text{s} = 29.43\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $29\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the time $3.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The speed increases by about $10\,\text{m/s}$ every second, so in three seconds it is about $3 \cdot 10\,\text{m/s} = 30\,\text{m/s}$, which matches the result. In addition, $29\,\text{m/s} \approx 106\,\text{km/h}$ is a motorway speed, which is a reasonable result for a three-second free fall.

## fy1-6-L1-02

**Formula:** $v = g\,t$. The formula is already solved for the speed we want: $v = g\,t$
**Conversions:** The time is given in seconds, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.80\,\text{s} = 7.848\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $7.8\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the time $0.80\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** In less than a second the speed has time to increase by less than $9.81\,\text{m/s}$, and $0.80 \cdot 9.81 \approx 8$ gives the same order of magnitude. A speed of $7.8\,\text{m/s} \approx 28\,\text{km/h}$ is reasonable after a fall of less than a second: it is faster than jogging but not yet the speed of cars.

## fy1-6-L1-04
**Given:** the line of the $(t, v)$ graph passes through the points $(t_1;\, v_1) = (0\,\text{s};\, 0\,\text{m/s})$ and $(t_2;\, v_2) = (5.0\,\text{s};\, 49\,\text{m/s})$
**Find:** the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The speed of a freely falling object increases by the same amount every second, so the $(t, v)$ graph is a straight line. The slope of the line is the change in speed divided by the change in time, and it is the acceleration of free fall.
**Reading the graph:** The two given points are on the line. The rise $\Delta v = v_2 - v_1$ is read in the vertical direction and the run $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$ in the horizontal direction. The points are far apart, so the reading error stays small.
**Formula:** $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** The speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in seconds, so the result comes in $\text{m/s}^2$. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $g = \dfrac{49\,\text{m/s} - 0\,\text{m/s}}{5.0\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{49\,\text{m/s}}{5.0\,\text{s}} = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures as in the numbers $49\,\text{m/s}$ and $5.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The result is close to the value for Earth, $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the graph belongs to an object falling on Earth. The slope is positive, because the speed increases with time.

## fy1-6-L2-02
**Given:** graphs of bodies dropped from rest on two planets without an atmosphere, A and B; the time for the body on planet A $t_\text{A} = 5.0\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the time $t_\text{B}$ after which the body on planet B has the same speed that the body on planet A has after the time $t_\text{A}$
**Principle:** In free fall from rest the speed increases by the same amount every second, so the speed is $v = g\,t$ and the $(t, v)$ graph is a straight line from the origin whose slope is the $g$ of the planet. The same speed is found by first calculating the speed of A and then the time that B needs to reach it.
**Reading the graph:** The line of planet A passes through the grid crossing $(5.0\,\text{s};\, 20\,\text{m/s})$, so the body on A has $v_\text{A} = 20\,\text{m/s}$ after $5.0\,\text{s}$. The line of planet B passes through the crossing $(5.0\,\text{s};\, 40\,\text{m/s})$. The readings are exact from the grid, so an answer that differs by at most $3\,\%$ is accepted.
**Formula:** $g_\text{B} = \dfrac{v}{t}$ and $t_\text{B} = \dfrac{v_\text{A}}{g_\text{B}}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in seconds.
**Substitution:** $g_\text{B} = \dfrac{40\,\text{m/s}}{5.0\,\text{s}} = 8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $t_\text{B} = \dfrac{20\,\text{m/s}}{8.0\,\text{m/s}^2} = 2.5\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $2.5\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the read values and in the time $5.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration of planet B, $8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, is twice that of A, $\dfrac{20\,\text{m/s}}{5.0\,\text{s}} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, so B reaches the same speed in half the time: $\tfrac{5.0\,\text{s}}{2} = 2.5\,\text{s}$. In the graph the line of B passes the $20\,\text{m/s}$ level between $2$ and $3\,\text{s}$, as it should.

## fy1-6-L3-01
**Given:** two balls in a vacuum, masses $m_1 = 0.50\,\text{kg}$ and $m_2 = 4.0\,\text{kg}$, both start from rest, $t = 1.5\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the ratio of the speeds $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}$ (no unit)
**Principle:** In a vacuum there is no air resistance, so both balls are in free fall. The acceleration of free fall $g$ is the same for all objects regardless of mass, so the speed of each is $g\,t$.
**Formula:** $v_1 = g\,t$ and $v_2 = g\,t$, so $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1} = \dfrac{g\,t}{g\,t}$
**Conversions:** The times are in seconds, and the masses are not needed in the calculation. No conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $v_1 = v_2 = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{s} = 14.715\,\text{m/s}$, so $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1} = \dfrac{14.715\,\text{m/s}}{14.715\,\text{m/s}} = 1.0$
**Answer:** $1.0$, two significant figures as in the time $1.5\,\text{s}$. The ratio is exactly one, because the mass cancels out.
**Reasonableness:** If the heavier ball fell faster, mass would have to appear in the formula, but the formula $v = g\,t$ has no mass. This matches the hammer and feather experiment on the Moon, where the objects hit the ground at the same time.

## fy1-6-L2-07
**Given:** length of the object $L = 5.0\,\text{cm}$; gate 1: $\Delta t_\text{light,1} = 25.0\,\text{ms}$ and the moment of the middle of the light blocking $t_1 = 0.320\,\text{s}$; gate 2: $\Delta t_\text{light,2} = 11.1\,\text{ms}$ and the moment of the middle of the light blocking $t_2 = 0.575\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The speed of the object at a gate is the length of the object divided by the time for which the light has been blocked: $v = \dfrac{L}{\Delta t_\text{light}}$. This is the average speed during the blocking, and in uniformly accelerated motion it equals the speed at the middle of the blocking, so the clock records the moment when the blocking is halfway. The speed increases steadily, so the acceleration is the change in speed divided by the change in time: $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. The moment of the drop is not needed, because only the time difference $t_2 - t_1$ and the difference of the speeds affect the result. If the clock reading at the moment of the drop is wrong, it shifts both times by the same amount and cancels in the difference.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{L}{\Delta t_\text{light}}$ and $g = \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** $L = 5.0\,\text{cm} = 0.050\,\text{m}$, because $1\,\text{cm} = 0.01\,\text{m}$. $\Delta t_\text{light,1} = 25.0\,\text{ms} = 0.0250\,\text{s}$ and $\Delta t_\text{light,2} = 11.1\,\text{ms} = 0.0111\,\text{s}$, because $1\,\text{ms} = 0.001\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $v_1 = \dfrac{0.050\,\text{m}}{0.0250\,\text{s}} = 2.00\,\text{m/s}$ and $v_2 = \dfrac{0.050\,\text{m}}{0.0111\,\text{s}} = 4.50\,\text{m/s}$. Then $g = \dfrac{4.50\,\text{m/s} - 2.00\,\text{m/s}}{0.575\,\text{s} - 0.320\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{2.50\,\text{m/s}}{0.255\,\text{s}} = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures, because the length of the object $L = 5.0\,\text{cm}$ is given with two figures and limits the result. The $g$ obtained with two gates does not require knowing the moment of the drop: the speeds are obtained from the length of the object and the time for which the light is blocked, and the acceleration from the time difference $t_2 - t_1$.
**Reasonableness:** The result is close to the known value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The speed $2.00\,\text{m/s}$ requires a fall of only about $0.20\,\text{s}$, although the clock shows $0.320\,\text{s}$: the clock was started before the drop, which does not matter for the time difference. The speed $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ corresponds to a falling time of $\dfrac{2.0}{9.8} \approx 0.20\,\text{s}$ and the speed $4.5\,\text{m/s}$ to a time of $0.46\,\text{s}$, and their difference is $0.25\,\text{s}$, like the time difference of the clock.

## fy1-6-L3-04
**Given:** the speeds of the ball and the filter paper are in the table at the moments $0.05\,\text{s}$–$0.60\,\text{s}$, the speed of the paper at the moment $0.40\,\text{s}$ is $1.49\,\text{m/s}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the point at which the model $v = g\,t$ stops being valid for the filter paper, and the shortfall of the speed of the filter paper from the model at the moment $t = 0.40\,\text{s}$ as a percentage

The filter paper is light and wide, so air slows it: its graph first lies on the same line but then bends downward from it and levels off to an almost constant speed of $1.5\,\text{m/s}$, because air resistance grows as the speed grows and finally almost cancels the increase in speed.
**Reading the graph:** At the moment $0.05\,\text{s}$ the point of the paper is on the line of the ball, but at the moment $0.10\,\text{s}$ the speed of the paper $0.93\,\text{m/s}$ is already $\dfrac{0.98 - 0.93}{0.98} \approx 5\,\%$ below the value of the model $0.98\,\text{m/s}$. **Formula:** $v_\text{model} = g\,t$ and shortfall $= \dfrac{v_\text{model} - v_\text{paper}}{v_\text{model}} \cdot 100\,\%$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in $\text{s}$.

## fy1-6-L3-05
**Given:** clock readings and speeds: $(0.65\,\text{s};\ 4.9\,\text{m/s})$, $(1.65\,\text{s};\ 14.7\,\text{m/s})$ and $(2.65\,\text{s};\ 24.5\,\text{m/s})$; the object started from rest, the clock had been running already before the drop
**Find:** why the quotients $\dfrac{v}{t}$ differ, the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$ and the interval $t_0$ between starting the clock and the drop in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** The formula $v = g\,t$ is valid when $t$ is the time from the start of the fall. The clock reading is $t = t_0 + t_\text{fall}$, so the quotient $\dfrac{v}{t}$ is too small, and the error is proportionally largest at a short reading. The change in speed $\Delta v$ and the time difference $\Delta t$ do not depend on the zero point of the clock, because $t_0$ cancels in the difference. Therefore $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is the correct method.
**Formula:** $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$ and $t_0 = t - t_\text{fall} = t - \dfrac{v}{g}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in $\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** The quotients: $\dfrac{4.9}{0.65} = 7.5$, $\dfrac{14.7}{1.65} = 8.9$ and $\dfrac{24.5}{2.65} = 9.2$ (unit $\text{m/s}^2$); they approach the value $9.8$ as the clock reading grows. The slope: $g = \dfrac{24.5\,\text{m/s} - 4.9\,\text{m/s}}{2.65\,\text{s} - 0.65\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{19.6\,\text{m/s}}{2.00\,\text{s}} = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the pairs $(1)$ and $(2)$ and also $(2)$ and $(3)$ give the same value. The real falling time in the first row is $\dfrac{4.9\,\text{m/s}}{9.8\,\text{m/s}^2} = 0.50\,\text{s}$, so $t_0 = 0.65\,\text{s} - 0.50\,\text{s} = 0.15\,\text{s}$.
**Answer:** $t_0 = 0.15\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the clock readings. The acceleration of free fall is $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The quotients $\dfrac{v}{t}$ are too small, because the clock reading is always $0.15\,\text{s}$ too large, and the error affects the short reading most. The slope works, because the change in speed and the time difference do not depend on the zero point of the clock.
**Reasonableness:** The clock error is the same in every row: the real falling times are $0.50\,\text{s}$, $1.50\,\text{s}$ and $2.50\,\text{s}$, and $9.8 \cdot 2.50 = 24.5\,\text{m/s}$ matches the third row. The result $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is close to the known value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

## fy1-6-L2-08
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the lighter ball at the moment $2.0\,\text{s}$ and the reason why it is what it is.
**Principle:** There is no air resistance in a vacuum, so both balls are in free fall and start from rest. Then the speed is $v = g\,t$, and the acceleration of free fall $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the same for all objects regardless of mass.
**Model answer:** The speed of the lighter ball is the same as that of the heavier one, $19.6\,\text{m/s}$, because $v = g\,t = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 2.0\,\text{s} = 19.62\,\text{m/s}$ does not contain the mass. The reason is that $g$ is the same for all objects without air resistance. Increasing the mass increases both the pull towards the Earth and the difficulty of setting the object in motion, and these cancel each other. The result $19.6\,\text{m/s}$ is a suitable rounding to two significant figures for the time $2.0\,\text{s}$.
