Circular orbits under gravity

Why does the International Space Station ISS not fall to the Earth, and why do the astronauts float inside it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why a satellite stays in a circular orbit and why an astronaut is weightless.
  • calculate the orbital speed and orbital period of a satellite from the equation $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
  • estimate when the model of uniform circular motion describes an orbit well.

Prior knowledge

  • In uniform circular motion a body has a centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre, and the speed is $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration
  • The gravitational force between two bodies is $F = \gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2}$, and the acceleration of free fall at distance $r$ is $g = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2}$chapter Newton's law of gravitation

Optional Review: net force in circular motion
A body goes round a horizontal circular path at a constant speed. In which direction does the net force on it point?

  • A force along the tangent would change the speed. In uniform circular motion the speed stays the same and only the direction of the velocity changes, so the net force is perpendicular to the velocity and points towards the centre (chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration).

  • Correct. In uniform circular motion the direction of the velocity changes all the time, so the body has a centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre, and the net force $\Sigma F = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$ points in the same direction (chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration).

  • The speed does not change, but the direction of the velocity does, so the body accelerates. Without a net force the body would move in a straight line (chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration).

  • The body does not tend to move outwards but straight ahead, along the tangent of the path. To make it turn, a force towards the centre must act on it (chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration).

Simulation

How to use

Choose the launch altitude and the mass of the satellite and adjust the launch speed $v$. Launch sends the satellite from the highest point of its orbit horizontally to the right. The black line is the orbit of the satellite and the dashed line is a circle whose radius is the distance at the moment of launch. The graph on the right (below, on a phone) shows the altitude $h$ of the satellite above the surface as a function of how many degrees the satellite has gone round the Earth; the dashed line is the launch altitude. When the orbit is a circle, the graph is a horizontal line. After the first revolution the readings show the lowest and highest altitude of the orbit and the orbital period $T$. When you change any value, the satellite returns to its starting point. Speed changes only the pace of the animation: a revolution takes a few seconds on the screen, and the time $t$ is simulated time.

Assumptions of the model

Only the gravitational force of the Earth $F = \gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2}$ acts on the satellite, towards the centre of the Earth, where $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and the radius of the Earth is $R = 6371\,\text{km}$. Air resistance, the gravity of the Moon and the Sun and the motion of the Earth are not in the model, so, for example, an orbit that descends to an altitude of $155\,\text{km}$ would not last in reality. The Earth stays in place, because the satellite is much lighter. At the distance of the Moon the satellite is a test body: the real Moon's own mass would move the Earth too. All readings are values of the model.

How the calculation works

The orbit is not drawn ready-made; the motion is calculated in small time steps. At each step the acceleration $\tfrac{F}{m} = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2}$ towards the centre of the Earth is calculated, and the position and velocity are updated with it (the velocity Verlet method, step $\tfrac{1}{1500}$ of the orbital period of the local circular orbit). The orbital period $T$ is the time in which the satellite goes once round the Earth. The length of the red force arrow is the same for all masses at the moment of launch and decreases like $\tfrac{1}{r^2}$; the magnitude of the force is shown in the readings. The length of the blue velocity arrow is proportional to the speed.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    A body that goes round another body under gravity is called a satellite. The Moon is a natural satellite of the Earth; the ISS and weather satellites are built by people. The path along which a satellite goes round is called an orbit. In space practically only one force acts on a satellite: the gravitational force of the Earth, which points towards the centre of the Earth. Because it is the only force, it is also the net force. In the chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration we found that in uniform circular motion the net force points to the centre of the path and gives the body its centripetal acceleration. In a circular orbit the gravitational force does exactly this: it is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does not change the speed but turns the direction of the velocity all the time.

    So a satellite falls towards the Earth all the time, but it never hits it. Without gravity it would continue straight ahead along the tangent of the orbit. Gravity bends the path, and when the speed is just right, the path curves at the same rate as the surface of the Earth curves away beneath the satellite. Then the distance from the centre of the Earth stays the same and the orbit is a circle.

    If the speed is lower, gravity turns the path more sharply than a circle would require: the satellite descends lower and then returns to the starting altitude, or hits the Earth if the speed is much too low. If the speed is higher, the path curves more gently and the satellite rises farther away before returning. In these cases the orbit is not a circle but an ellipse, and the model of uniform circular motion does not describe it.

    Orbital speed in a circular orbit

    When a satellite of mass $m$ goes round a central body in a circular orbit, the gravitational force is the only force and therefore the net force. According to Newton's second law it must be equal to the force required by the circular motion, $m a_\text{n} = \tfrac{m v^2}{r}$:

    $$\gamma \frac{M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$

    where $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$ is the gravitational constant (many English books write it as G), $M$ is the mass of the central body ($\text{kg}$), $m$ is the mass of the satellite ($\text{kg}$), $r$ is the orbital radius ($\text{m}$) and $v$ is the orbital speed of the satellite ($\text{m/s}$). The orbital radius is measured from the centre of the central body: for a satellite of the Earth $r = R + h$, where $R = 6371\,\text{km}$ is the radius of the Earth and $h$ is the altitude of the satellite above the surface.

    The mass $m$ of the satellite is on both sides of the equation and cancels. When the equation is multiplied by $r$ and divided by the mass $m$, we get $v^2 = \tfrac{\gamma M}{r}$, so the orbital speed in a circular orbit is

    $$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{r}}$$

    The orbital speed depends only on the mass $M$ of the central body and on the orbital radius $r$, not on the mass of the satellite. A heavy space station and a small CubeSat orbit at the same altitude at the same speed. The farther away a satellite orbits, the lower its orbital speed.

    In one revolution the satellite travels the circumference $2\pi r$ of the circle, so the orbital period follows from the relation $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ of the chapter Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration:

    $$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}$$

    where $T$ is the orbital period ($\text{s}$). The orbit of a faraway satellite is longer and its speed lower, so the orbital period grows quickly as the distance grows: the ISS goes round the Earth in about an hour and a half, the Moon in about $27\,\text{d}$. When the radius and the orbital period of a satellite are measured, the same equation can also be solved for the mass $M$ of the central body. This is how, for example, the masses of the Earth, the Sun and Mars have been determined.

    Geostationary orbit

    A geostationary satellite orbits above the equator in the same direction as the Earth rotates, and its orbital period is the same as the rotation period of the Earth, $23\,\text{h}\;56\,\text{min}$. It therefore seems to stay in place in the sky, and a satellite dish can be aimed at it once. Because the orbital period determines the orbital radius, all geostationary satellites orbit at the same altitude, about $35\,800\,\text{km}$.

    Weightlessness

    On a space station the astronauts float, and a scale would show zero. The reason is not the absence of gravity. At the altitude of the ISS, $420\,\text{km}$, the acceleration due to the gravitational force is $\tfrac{\gamma M}{r^2} \approx 8.6\,\text{m/s}^2$, about $88\,\%$ of the value at the surface of the Earth. On an astronaut of mass $70\,\text{kg}$ there acts a weight of about $605\,\text{N}$, that is, the gravitational force $F$ of the Earth, whereas at the surface it would be $687\,\text{N}$.

    The station and the astronaut are in free fall: only gravity acts on each, and each gets the same acceleration towards the Earth. Because they fall in the same way, the floor does not press on the astronaut and a scale does not have to support them, so the support force is zero. Weightlessness (apparent weightlessness) is a state in which only gravity acts on a body and it falls freely: the support force is zero, although the weight acts all the time.

    The same state can be experienced for a moment on the Earth too: during a jump, in a freely falling lift, or in an aeroplane whose engines are adjusted so that it flies along the path of a projectile. Weightlessness is not explained by any "centrifugal force" cancelling gravity. If some force cancelled gravity, the net force would be zero, and the station would continue straight ahead and would not orbit the Earth.

    When the circular model holds

    The model of uniform circular motion is a simplification. It describes the motion of a satellite well when

    • the central body is much more massive than the satellite, so it can be considered to stay in place,
    • the gravity of other bodies and air resistance are so small that only the gravity of the central body acts,
    • the speed of the satellite is exactly the circular-orbit speed of the radius of its orbit, so that the distance from the centre stays constant.

    Many real orbits are nearly circles. The distance of the Earth from the Sun varies by only about $3\,\%$ of the mean distance, so the circular model describes the orbit of the Earth well. The distance of the Moon from the Earth varies by about $11\,\%$, so the model gives a good approximation for the Moon. The orbit of Halley's comet, in contrast, is very elongated, and the circular model does not suit it at all.

    Small deviations from the model show up in precise measurements. The orbital speed of the Moon calculated from measured values, $1.023\,\text{km/s}$, is about $0.5\,\%$ greater than the $1.018\,\text{km/s}$ given by the model, because the mass of the Moon is about $\tfrac{1}{81}$ of the mass of the Earth: the Earth and the Moon go round their common centre of mass, and the Earth does not stay in place. At the altitude of the ISS thin air brakes the station and its orbit slowly descends, so the station is raised with engines regularly.

    Common misconception

    "There is no gravity in space, which is why astronauts float." This is wrong. At the altitude of the ISS gravity is almost as strong as at the surface of the Earth, and it is exactly what keeps the station in its orbit. Without gravity the station would fly straight away. The astronauts float because they and the station fall freely with the same acceleration.

    Another error is to put the altitude $h$ into the formula $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$ instead of the distance from the centre of the Earth, $r = R + h$. For the ISS this would give a speed of about $31\,\text{km/s}$, four times the real one. A third error is to think that a heavy satellite has to orbit faster: a greater mass experiences a greater gravitational force, but it also needs a greater force to turn, so the mass cancels.

    Summary

    • Only the gravitational force of the central body acts on a satellite. It is the net force, which in a circular orbit gives the centripetal acceleration: $\gamma \tfrac{Mm}{r^2} = \tfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
    • The orbital speed in a circular orbit is $v = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$ and the orbital period $T = \tfrac{2\pi r}{v}$. The mass of the satellite has no effect, and a satellite orbiting farther away is slower and has a longer orbital period.
    • The orbital radius is measured from the centre of the central body: $r = R + h$.
    • Weightlessness is free fall: gravity acts, but the support force is zero.
    • The circular model holds when the central body is much more massive than the satellite, other forces are small and the orbit is nearly a circle.

        3. At the surface of the Moon the acceleration of free fall is $1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the radius of the Moon is $1737\,\text{km}$.

          What speed is needed for a probe to orbit the Moon in a circular orbit just above its surface? The Moon has no atmosphere. Give the answer in kilometres per second.

          km/s
          Answer

          $1.68\,\text{km/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          acceleration of free fall at the surface of the Moon $g = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$ and radius $R = 1737\,\text{km}$; no atmosphere

          Find

          the speed $v$ of the circular orbit just above the surface of the Moon

          Principle

          Only the gravitational force of the Moon acts on the probe, and it is the net force of the circular motion. At the surface the acceleration due to the gravitational force is $\tfrac{\gamma M}{R^2} = g$ (chapter Newton's law of gravitation), so the mass of the Moon is not needed: the centripetal acceleration must be $g$.

          Formula

          $m g = \dfrac{m v^2}{R}$, from which $v = \sqrt{g R}$

          Conversions

          $R$$= 1737\,\text{km}$
          $= 1.737 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          finally $1\,\text{km/s} = 1000\,\text{m/s}$.

          Substitution

          $v$$= \sqrt{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.737 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}$
          $= 1677\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 1.677\,\text{km/s}$

          Answer

          $1.68\,\text{km/s}$

          Three significant figures as in the acceleration of free fall $1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          The result is slightly greater than the $1.63\,\text{km/s}$ of the level 1 probe, which orbits $110\,\text{km}$ higher: a body orbiting lower is faster. Unit:

          $\sqrt{\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot \text{m}}$$= \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$

        4. An astronaut of mass $75\,\text{kg}$ works on a space station at an altitude of $410\,\text{km}$. The mass of the Earth is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius $6371\,\text{km}$.

          How large is the gravitational force acting on the astronaut, although the astronaut is weightless?

          N
          Answer

          $650\,\text{N}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the astronaut $m = 75\,\text{kg}$, altitude $h = 410\,\text{km}$, mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6371\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

          Find

          the gravitational force $F$ acting on the astronaut

          Principle

          Weightlessness does not mean that gravity is absent: the gravitational force of the Earth acts on the astronaut, and it is calculated from the law of gravitation at the distance $r = R + h$ from the centre of the Earth. The floating is due to free fall, not to the absence of a force.

          Formula

          $F = \gamma \dfrac{M m}{r^2}$, where $r = R + h$

          Conversions

          $r$$= 6371\,\text{km} + 410\,\text{km}$
          $= 6781\,\text{km}$
          $= 6.781 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $\gamma M$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$
          $= 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$

          so

          $F$$= \dfrac{3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2 \cdot 75\,\text{kg}}{(6.781 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m})^2}$
          $= 649.9\,\text{N}$

          Answer

          $650\,\text{N}$

          That is, $6.5 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{N}$, two significant figures as in the mass $75\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          At the surface of the Earth the same astronaut weighs $75\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 736\,\text{N}$. On the station the force is about $88\,\%$ of this, because the distance from the centre is only about $6\,\%$ greater: $\left(\tfrac{6371}{6781}\right)^2 \approx 0.88$.

        1. Mars rotates once about its axis in $24\,\text{h}\;37\,\text{min}$. A communications satellite orbiting Mars is to stay all the time above the same point of the surface, like a geostationary satellite above the Earth. The mass of Mars is $6.42 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius $3390\,\text{km}$.

          At what altitude above the surface of Mars must the satellite orbit? Give the answer in kilometres.

          km
          Answer

          $1.70 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{km}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          rotation period of Mars $T = 24\,\text{h}\;37\,\text{min}$, mass $M = 6.42 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 3390\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

          Find

          the altitude of the satellite above the surface of Mars $h$ in kilometres

          Principle

          For the satellite to stay above the same point of the surface, its orbital period must be the rotation period of Mars, and it must orbit above the equator in the same direction as Mars rotates. The gravitational force is the net force of the circular motion of the satellite, and the speed is $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{\gamma M}{r}$$= v^2$
          $= \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}$

          from which

          $r$$= \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}}$

          $h = r - R$

          Conversions

          $T$$= 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} + 37 \cdot 60\,\text{s}$
          $= 88\,620\,\text{s}$
          $R$$= 3390\,\text{km}$
          $= 3.390 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $\gamma M T^2$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 6.42 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{kg} \cdot (88\,620\,\text{s})^2$
          $= 3.3650 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{m}^3$

          so

          $r$$= \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{3.3650 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{m}^3}{4\pi^2}}$
          $= \sqrt[3]{8.524 \cdot 10^{21}\,\text{m}^3}$
          $= 2.0427 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m}$

          so

          $h$$= 2.0427 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m} - 3.390 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$
          $= 1.7037 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 17\,037\,\text{km}$

          Answer

          about $1.70 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{km}$, that is, $17\,000\,\text{km}$, three significant figures as in the mass of Mars $6.42 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{kg}$.

          Reasonableness

          The geostationary orbit of the Earth is at an altitude of about $35\,800\,\text{km}$. The rotation period of Mars is almost the same as that of the Earth, but its mass is only about a tenth, so the same orbital period is reached at a smaller radius: $r \propto \sqrt[3]{M}$ gives $42\,164\,\text{km} \cdot \sqrt[3]{0.107} \approx 20\,000\,\text{km}$, which agrees with the orbital radius obtained, $r = 20\,400\,\text{km}$.

        2. Thin air brakes a space station, and its nearly circular orbit descends over weeks from an altitude of $400\,\text{km}$ to an altitude of $398\,\text{km}$. The mass of the Earth is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius $6371\,\text{km}$.

          Does the orbital speed of the station increase or decrease, and by how much? Give the change in the orbital speed in metres per second (an increase as positive). Do not round intermediate results before the subtraction.

          m/s
          Answer

          $1.13\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          altitude before $h_1 = 400\,\text{km}$ and after $h_2 = 398\,\text{km}$, mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6371\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

          Find

          the change in the orbital speed $\Delta v = v_2 - v_1$ in metres per second

          Principle

          The orbit stays nearly a circle all the time, so at both altitudes the orbital speed is the circular-orbit speed determined by gravity. Braking lowers the orbit, and the circular-orbit speed is greater lower down, so the station ends up moving faster.

          Formula

          $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$, so

          $\Delta v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma M}{R + h_2}} - \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma M}{R + h_1}}$

          Conversions

          $r_1$$= 6771\,\text{km}$
          $= 6.771 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$
          $r_2$$= 6769\,\text{km}$
          $= 6.769 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          and $\gamma M = 3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$. The two speeds are almost equal and their difference is only about one metre per second, so intermediate results are not rounded before the subtraction: the speeds are calculated to two decimal places and only then subtracted.

          Substitution

          $v_1$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{6.771 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}}$
          $= 7671.03\,\text{m/s}$
          $v_2$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{3.98438 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{6.769 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}}$
          $= 7672.17\,\text{m/s}$

          so

          $\Delta v$$= 7672.17\,\text{m/s} - 7671.03\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 1.13\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          The orbital speed increases by about $1.13\,\text{m/s}$. Three significant figures, because the difference of $2\,\text{km}$ between the altitudes is exact and the change has been calculated without rounding intermediate results.

          Reasonableness

          The radius decreases by only $2\,\text{km}$, about $0.03\,\%$, so the change in speed is small: $1.13\,\text{m/s}$ is about $0.015\,\%$ of the speed $7671\,\text{m/s}$, which fits such a small change in radius. The sign is positive, although the air brakes the station: the orbit descends, and lower down gravity requires a greater speed.

        3. The Earth rotates once about its axis in a day.

          How short would the rotation period of the Earth have to be for a person standing at the equator to be weightless? The mass of the Earth is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius $6371\,\text{km}$, and the shape of the Earth is assumed to be a sphere. Give the answer in minutes.

          min
          Answer

          $84.4\,\text{min}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6371\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$; the Earth is assumed to be a sphere

          Find

          the rotation period $T$ at which a person standing at the equator would be weightless, in minutes

          Principle

          As the Earth rotates, the person goes round a circle of radius $R$. The person is weightless when the ground does not press on them, that is, the support force is zero: then the gravitational force alone is exactly enough to be the net force of the circular motion, as for a satellite in a circular orbit.

          Formula

          $\gamma \dfrac{Mm}{R^2}$$= \dfrac{mv^2}{R}$

          $v = \dfrac{2\pi R}{T}$, so

          $\dfrac{\gamma M}{R}$$= \dfrac{4\pi^2 R^2}{T^2}$

          $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{R^3}{\gamma M}}$

          Conversions

          $R$$= 6371\,\text{km}$
          $= 6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          finally $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$.

          Substitution

          $\gamma M$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$
          $= 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$

          so

          $T$$= 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m})^3}{3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}}$
          $= 5062\,\text{s}$
          $= 5062 : 60\,\text{min}$
          $= 84.36\,\text{min}$

          Answer

          about $84.4\,\text{min}$, three significant figures as in the mass of the Earth.

          Reasonableness

          This is the orbital period of a satellite orbiting at the surface level, and the ISS orbits only a little higher, with a period of $92.8\,\text{min}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude and slightly shorter, as it should be. The real rotation period, about $24\,\text{h}$, is about $17$ times longer, so we are far from weightlessness.
