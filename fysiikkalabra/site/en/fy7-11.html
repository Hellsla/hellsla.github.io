Dispersion: the prism and the rainbow

Why is the red of a rainbow always on the outer edge and the violet on the inner edge, and why can you see a rainbow only when the sun is behind you?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why a prism splits white light and which colour is refracted the most.
  • calculate the angles of refraction of different colours and the angles of deviation of a prism from refractive indices.
  • explain how a rainbow forms and why red is on its outer edge.

Prior knowledge

  • Light is refracted at a boundary according to the law of refraction $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$, and $n = c : v$chapter Reflection and refraction of light
  • The wavelengths of visible light are about $400$–$700\,\text{nm}$, and $c = f\lambda$chapter Electromagnetic radiation and the black-body spectrum

1Think about it

After a summer shower there is a rainbow in the sky. Its outer edge is red and its inner edge violet, and sometimes a second, fainter arc appears outside it. The same colours appear when the sun shines through a glass prism or the crystal of a chandelier. Raindrops and glass are colourless, and sunlight is white.

Where do the colours of a rainbow come from, and why are they always in the same order?

Optional Recap: refraction
Light arrives from air at a slant on the surface of a glass sheet. What happens to the direction of the light at the boundary?

  • Transparency means that light passes through, but its direction still changes: light slows down in glass and bends towards the normal. Only light arriving perpendicular to the surface continues straight (chapter Reflection and refraction of light).

  • According to the law of refraction $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$, in a material with a greater refractive index the angle to the normal is smaller, so light bends towards the normal (chapter Reflection and refraction of light).

  • Correct. The refractive index of glass is greater than that of air, so $\sin\alpha_1 = n \sin\alpha_2$ gives an angle of refraction smaller than the angle of incidence (chapter Reflection and refraction of light). Today you will find that the refractive index is slightly different for different colours.

Assumptions of the model

The model treats light as rays: a narrow beam hits the middle of the left side of the prism, and the direction of each colour is calculated from the law of refraction $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$ at both surfaces. The prism is in air, and the refractive index of air is $1$. The refractive index comes from a curve fitted to the tabulated values of the material: the values for red and violet in crown glass and water are those in the table of the chapter. The part of the light reflected at the surfaces is not drawn. If a colour cannot leave through the second surface, its ray is drawn reflected back into the glass. The readings are values calculated by the model, not measurements.

How the calculation works

At the first surface $\sin\alpha_2 = \sin\alpha_1 : n$. Inside the glass $\alpha_3 = A - \alpha_2$, and at the second surface $\sin\alpha_4 = n \sin\alpha_3$. The angle of deviation is

$$\delta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_4 - A$$

and the difference between the colours is $\Delta\delta = \delta_\text{v} - \delta_\text{r}$. If $n \sin\alpha_3 > 1$, the colour cannot leave the prism.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    The refractive index depends on wavelength

    In the chapter Reflection and refraction of light the refractive index $n$ told how many times slower light travels in a material than in vacuum. Precise measurements show that the refractive index also depends on the wavelength of the light. In water and glass the refractive index is greater for violet light than for red, so violet light travels more slowly in them. In crown glass red light travels at about $198\,000\,\text{km/s}$ and violet at about $196\,000\,\text{km/s}$. The dependence of the refractive index on wavelength is called dispersion. The wavelengths in the table are vacuum wavelengths. At a boundary the frequency of light does not change, and colour is determined by frequency, so the colour stays the same even though the speed and wavelength change.

    colour $\lambda\ (\text{nm})$ water crown glass
    red $656$ $1.3315$ $1.5143$
    yellow $589$ $1.3334$ $1.5167$
    blue $486$ $1.3375$ $1.5224$
    violet $405$ $1.3431$ $1.5302$

    Refractive indices of water at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$: M. Daimon and A. Masumura, Applied Optics 46, 2007. Refractive indices of N-BK7 crown glass: SCHOTT AG, optical glass catalogue, 2017.

    Why violet is slowed down more

    The electric field of light makes the electrons of the material oscillate at the frequency of the light. The oscillating electrons emit light themselves, which combines with the incoming light, and the combined wave travels more slowly than light in vacuum. The response of the electrons depends on frequency. In water and glass it strengthens as the frequency increases towards the ultraviolet, which the material absorbs strongly. So high-frequency violet light is slowed down more than low-frequency red, and its refractive index is greater.

    A prism splits white light

    White light, such as sunlight, is a mixture of different wavelengths, that is, colours. When it enters glass at a slant, each colour is refracted according to the law of refraction but by a different amount: violet has the greatest refractive index, so it bends the most towards the normal. On leaving the second surface of a flat window pane of uniform thickness, the colours emerge again parallel to the original ray, so the colours are not noticed. The surfaces of a prism are slanted relative to each other, so at the second surface the colours are refracted still further apart. A fan of colours, a spectrum, comes out of the prism: red is deviated the least from its original direction and violet the most.

    A prism does not colour light. In the 1660s Isaac Newton showed that when a single colour is separated from the spectrum and sent through a second prism, it is refracted but not split into new colours. A second prism turned the other way, on the other hand, gathers the spectrum back into white light. The colours were therefore already present in white light, and the prism only separates them from each other.

    A rainbow forms in water droplets

    A small raindrop is almost spherical. Sunlight is refracted into the droplet, reflected from the back surface of the droplet and refracted out of the lower part of the droplet. The light therefore returns towards the sun, but turned around. In both refractions violet is refracted more than red, so the colours separate. The direction in which the light leaves also depends on where the ray hits the droplet. However, the light is concentrated in one direction, because over a wide range of points of incidence the rays leave in almost the same direction: red at about $42^\circ$ and violet at about $40.6^\circ$ from the direction the sunlight came from. In this direction the droplet shines most brightly.

    Why red is on the outer edge

    When the sun is behind you, the sun's rays pass your head towards the antisolar point, which is below the horizon at the shadow of your own head. Red light reaches the eye from the droplets seen at $42^\circ$ from the direction of the antisolar point, and violet from those seen at $40.6^\circ$. Droplets at the same angle form a circle whose centre is the antisolar point. The circle of red is larger, so red is on the outer edge of the arc. The different colours leave a droplet at their strongest in slightly different directions. That is why light of a particular colour reaches the eye especially strongly from each droplet, and the strongest directions of the droplet's other colours pass by the eye. That is why each viewer sees their own rainbow.

    A rainbow is therefore seen only when the sun shines from behind you and there are droplets ahead. On level ground only the part of the arc above the horizon is visible. If the sun is at a height $h$ above the horizon, the antisolar point is the same distance below the horizon, and the top of the red arc is at a height $42^\circ - h$. When the sun is higher than $42^\circ$, the whole arc is below the horizon and cannot be seen from the ground. That is why rainbows are seen in midsummer in the morning and evening, when the sun is low.

    The secondary bow

    Outside the primary bow a fainter secondary bow is sometimes seen. In it the light is reflected twice inside the droplet, and the order of the colours is reversed: red is on the inner edge at about $50^\circ$ and violet on the outer edge at about $54^\circ$ from the direction of the antisolar point. At each reflection some of the light escapes from the droplet, so the secondary bow is fainter. The sky between the arcs is darker, because the droplets of the primary bow light only the inside of the arc and those of the secondary bow only the outside.

    Law of refraction for different wavelengths

    When light arrives from air in a material, each wavelength is refracted according to its own refractive index:

    $$\sin \alpha_1 = n(\lambda) \sin \alpha_2$$

    where $\alpha_1$ is the angle of incidence and $\alpha_2$ the angle of refraction, both measured from the normal, and $n(\lambda)$ is the refractive index of the material at wavelength $\lambda$. The refractive index of air is $1.0003$, so it is rounded to one; the general form is $n_1 \sin\alpha_1 = n_2 \sin\alpha_2$ (chapter Reflection and refraction of light). The speed of light in a material is $v = c : n(\lambda)$, where $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$. In the graph the refractive index of crown glass decreases as the wavelength increases, most steeply at the violet end.

    The angle of deviation of a prism

    In a prism the ray is refracted twice, and the angles depend on each other:

    $$\begin{aligned} \alpha_2 + \alpha_3 &= A \\[6pt] \delta &= \alpha_1 + \alpha_4 - A \end{aligned}$$

    where $\alpha_1$ is the angle of incidence and $\alpha_2$ the angle of refraction at the first surface, $\alpha_3$ the angle of incidence from inside the glass at the second surface and $\alpha_4$ the angle of refraction on leaving, all measured from the normals. $A$ is the apex angle of the prism and $\delta$ the angle of deviation, the amount by which the ray is turned from its original direction. The first equation follows from the sum of the angles of a triangle, because the normals of the surfaces meet at an angle $180^\circ - A$. The second follows from the ray turning by $\alpha_1 - \alpha_2$ at the first surface and by $\alpha_4 - \alpha_3$ at the second. For violet $\alpha_4$ is greater than for red, so its angle of deviation is greater. The equations hold when the ray can leave through the second surface of the prism.

    The angle of a rainbow

    In the droplet the ray turns by $\alpha - \beta$ at the point of entry, by $180^\circ - 2\beta$ at the reflection and again by $\alpha - \beta$ at the point of exit. The total turning is therefore $180^\circ + 2\alpha - 4\beta$. The angle between the light leaving the droplet and the direction of the sun is $180^\circ$ minus the total turning, that is,

    $$\theta = 4\beta - 2\alpha$$

    where $\alpha$ is the angle of incidence at the surface of the droplet, $\beta$ the angle of refraction, $\sin\alpha = n \sin\beta$, and $\theta$ is the angle at which the light is seen from the direction of the antisolar point. The graph shows that $\theta$ depends on the angle of incidence but does not exceed $42.3^\circ$ when $n = 1.3315$. Near the peak the curve is almost horizontal, so over a wide range of angles of incidence the rays leave in almost the same direction, and a bright arc is seen in that direction. The rest of the light leaves at smaller angles, so the sky inside the arc is brighter. For violet ($n = 1.3431$) the greatest angle is $40.6^\circ$.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that a prism colours light or that the colours are created in the glass of the prism. The colours are already present in white light, and the prism separates them because the refractive index depends on wavelength. Another common mistake is to conclude that red is refracted the most because it has the longest wavelength. In water and glass it is the other way round: violet has the greatest refractive index, so violet is refracted and deviated the most. A third mistake is to think of a rainbow as an object at a particular place. A rainbow is a direction: it is always seen at $42^\circ$ from the antisolar point, so it moves with the viewer and its end cannot be reached.

    Summary

    • The refractive index depends on wavelength. This is called dispersion. In water and glass violet has the greatest refractive index and red the smallest, so violet is refracted the most.
    • In a prism white light is refracted twice at slanted surfaces, and the colours separate into a spectrum. The angle of deviation is $\delta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_4 - A$, and violet is deviated the most.
    • In a rainbow light is refracted into a droplet, reflected from its back surface and refracted out. Red is seen at about $42^\circ$ and violet at about $40.6^\circ$ from the antisolar point, so red is on the outer edge.
    • A rainbow is seen when the sun is behind you and lower than $42^\circ$. In the secondary bow the light is reflected twice, and the colours are in the opposite order.

      Example 1: White light entering glass

      A narrow beam of white light arrives from air at the surface of a crown-glass plate at an angle of incidence of $60.0^\circ$. What are the angles of refraction of red ($656\,\text{nm}$) and violet ($405\,\text{nm}$) light in the glass, and how large an angle arises between them? Use the refractive indices in the table.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Angle of incidence $\alpha_1 = 60.0^\circ$; from the table the refractive index of red light is $n_\text{r} = 1.5143$ and of violet $n_\text{v} = 1.5302$. We are asked for the angles of refraction $\alpha_\text{r}$ and $\alpha_\text{v}$ and their difference $\Delta\alpha = \alpha_\text{r} - \alpha_\text{v}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Each colour obeys the law of refraction with its own refractive index. The light arrives from air, whose refractive index is rounded to $1$, so $\sin \alpha_1 = n \sin \alpha_2$. The angle of incidence is the same for both colours, because they travel in the same beam of white light.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} \sin \alpha_1 &= n \sin \alpha_2 \\[6pt] \sin \alpha_2 &= \frac{\sin \alpha_1}{n} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: the refractive index has no unit, and the angles are in degrees.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} \sin \alpha_\text{r} &= \frac{\sin \alpha_1}{n_\text{r}} \\ &= \frac{\sin 60.0^\circ}{1.5143} \\ &= 0.57190 \\ \alpha_\text{r} &= 34.88^\circ \\[6pt] \sin \alpha_\text{v} &= \frac{\sin \alpha_1}{n_\text{v}} \\ &= \frac{\sin 60.0^\circ}{1.5302} \\ &= 0.56596 \\ \alpha_\text{v} &= 34.47^\circ \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The angle of refraction of red light is $34.9^\circ$ and of violet $34.5^\circ$, to three significant figures like the angle of incidence $60.0^\circ$. The angle between the rays is calculated from the unrounded angles:

      $\Delta\alpha$$= 34.88^\circ - 34.47^\circ$
      $= 0.41^\circ$

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Both angles of refraction are smaller than the angle of incidence, because light entering an optically denser material bends towards the normal. The angle of refraction of violet is smaller because its refractive index is greater. The difference is less than half a degree, so in glass of uniform thickness the colours separate only a little; the slanted second surface of a prism increases the difference.

      Optional Example 2: Find the error
      Violet light arrives from air on crown glass at an angle of incidence of $60.0^\circ$. The refractive index of the glass for violet light is $1.5302$.A student calculates the angle of refraction

      $$\begin{aligned} \alpha_2 &= \frac{\alpha_1}{n} \\ &= \frac{60.0^\circ}{1.5302} \\ &= 39.2^\circ \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • Each colour is refracted according to its own refractive index, so the value $1.5302$ is the right one for violet. The error is in the method: the law of refraction concerns sines, $\sin\alpha_2 = \sin\alpha_1 : n$, which gives $\alpha_2 = 34.5^\circ$.

      • The angle of refraction cannot exceed $90^\circ$, and when light enters glass it bends towards the normal, so the angle must decrease. The error is that the law of refraction concerns sines: $\sin\alpha_2 = \sin\alpha_1 : n$, which gives $\alpha_2 = 34.5^\circ$.

      • Correct. $\sin\alpha_2 = \sin 60.0^\circ : 1.5302 = 0.5660$, so $\alpha_2 = 34.5^\circ$. Dividing the angle by the refractive index gives a wrong result, because the sine is not proportional to the angle at large angles.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation: colours on the bottom of a swimming pool

      Sunlight hits the surface of a still swimming pool at an angle of incidence of $60.0^\circ$. The pool is $2.00\,\text{m}$ deep. The refractive index of water is $1.3315$ for red light and $1.3431$ for violet.

      How far apart do the red and violet light hit the bottom of the pool when the light reaches the surface at the same point? Give your answer in millimetres.

      mm
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      First calculate the angle of refraction of each colour in water. Which one is refracted more?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Once the angle of refraction $\beta$ is known, the ray travels a horizontal distance $x = h \tan\beta$ at depth $h$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $\beta_\text{r} = 40.57^\circ$ and $\beta_\text{v} = 40.15^\circ$, so $\Delta x = 2.00\,\text{m} \cdot (\tan 40.57^\circ - \tan 40.15^\circ)$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      angle of incidence $\alpha = 60.0^\circ$, depth of the pool $h = 2.00\,\text{m}$, refractive index of red $n_\text{r} = 1.3315$, of violet $n_\text{v} = 1.3431$

      Find

      distance $\Delta x$ between the points where red and violet hit, in millimetres

      Principle

      Each colour is refracted at the surface according to the law of refraction $\sin\alpha = n \sin\beta$ with its own refractive index, because the refractive index depends on wavelength. In water light travels in a straight line, so the ray, the normal of the surface and the bottom form a right-angled triangle: the horizontal distance to the bottom is $x = h \tan\beta$.

      Formula

      $\sin\beta = \dfrac{\sin\alpha}{n}$ and $x = h \tan\beta$, so $\Delta x = h \left(\tan\beta_\text{r} - \tan\beta_\text{v}\right)$

      Conversions

      Finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.

      Substitution

      $\sin\beta_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{\sin 60.0^\circ}{1.3315}$
      $= 0.65041$

      so $\beta_\text{r} = 40.573^\circ$;

      $\sin\beta_\text{v}$$= \dfrac{\sin 60.0^\circ}{1.3431}$
      $= 0.64480$

      so $\beta_\text{v} = 40.150^\circ$.

      $\Delta x$$= 2.00\,\text{m} \cdot (0.85628 - 0.84358)$
      $= 0.02540\,\text{m}$
      $= 25\,\text{mm}$

      Answer

      about $25\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures, because the result is the difference of two almost equal numbers, in which significant figures are lost.

      Reasonableness

      Violet is refracted more, so it hits closer to the point where the light enters the surface, and the difference is positive. Each colour travels horizontally about $2.00\,\text{m} \cdot 0.85 \approx 1.7\,\text{m}$, and the difference is less than $2\,\%$ of this, which fits the small difference in the refractive indices. That is why narrow coloured fringes appear on the bottom.

      Answer on your own first and only then open the answer.

      1. White light passes at a slant through a flat window pane of uniform thickness. Why does the window not visibly split the light into colours, even though glass has dispersion?
        Answer

        Inside the glass the colours travel in slightly different directions, but the surfaces of a pane of uniform thickness are parallel. At the second surface each colour is refracted back to a direction parallel to the original ray, so the colours continue in the same direction, only slightly shifted sideways. The slanted surfaces of a prism, by contrast, increase the difference in direction.

      2. The sun shines from a height of $50^\circ$, and it is raining ahead. Can an observer standing on level ground see a rainbow?
        Answer

        No. The antisolar point is $50^\circ$ below the horizon, so the top of the red arc would be at a height of $42^\circ - 50^\circ = -8^\circ$, that is, below the horizon. A rainbow is seen from the ground only when the sun is lower than $42^\circ$.

      2. Concept task: Why a prism splits light

      White light passes through a glass prism and splits into a spectrum. What explains the separation of the colours?

      • In a prism the ray is refracted twice, on entering and on leaving; reflection does not separate colours, because the law of reflection is the same for all colours. The colours separate in refraction, because the refractive index depends on wavelength.

      • In air all colours travel at practically the same speed. The difference arises in glass, where violet slows down more; that is why its refractive index is greater and it is refracted the most.

      • Correct. The refractive index depends on wavelength (dispersion): violet has the greatest refractive index, so it is refracted the most. The slanted surfaces of the prism increase the angle between the colours.

      • Glass does not create new colours: at a boundary the frequency of light, and so its colour, stays the same. The colours are already present in white light, and the prism separates them because the refractive index is different for different colours.

      3. Calculation task: Calculate: red and violet in water

      The refractive index of water is $1.3315$ for red light and $1.3431$ for violet.

      By how much is the speed of red light in water greater than that of violet? Give your answer in kilometres per second.

      km/s
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      The speed of light in a material is obtained from the refractive index. With which formula?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $v = c : n$, where $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$. Calculate the speed of each colour and subtract.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Keep six figures in the intermediate results: $v_\text{r} = 225\,310\,\text{km/s}$ and $v_\text{v} = 223\,364\,\text{km/s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      refractive index of red $n_\text{r} = 1.3315$, of violet $n_\text{v} = 1.3431$, speed of light in vacuum $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$

      Find

      difference of the speeds $\Delta v = v_\text{r} - v_\text{v}$ in kilometres per second

      Principle

      The refractive index tells how many times slower light travels in a material than in vacuum, $n = c : v$. The refractive index depends on wavelength, so red and violet have different speeds in water.

      Formula

      $n = \dfrac{c}{v}$, so $v = \dfrac{c}{n}$, and

      $\Delta v$$= \dfrac{c}{n_\text{r}} - \dfrac{c}{n_\text{v}}$

      Conversions

      $c$$= 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$
      $= 3.00 \cdot 10^5\,\text{km/s}$

      because $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.

      Substitution

      $v_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^5\,\text{km/s}}{1.3315}$
      $= 225\,310\,\text{km/s}$
      $v_\text{v}$$= \dfrac{3.00 \cdot 10^5\,\text{km/s}}{1.3431}$
      $= 223\,364\,\text{km/s}$

      so

      $\Delta v$$= 225\,310\,\text{km/s} - 223\,364\,\text{km/s}$
      $= 1946\,\text{km/s}$

      Answer

      $1.95 \cdot 10^3\,\text{km/s}$

      Three significant figures like the speed of light $3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$.

      Reasonableness

      The difference is about $1\,\%$ of the speed, since the refractive indices also differ by about one per cent: $1.3431 : 1.3315 \approx 1.009$. Red is faster because its refractive index is smaller.

      4. Explanation task: The order of the colours in a rainbow

      Explain how a rainbow forms in water droplets and why red is on the outer edge of the arc and violet on the inner edge. Also explain why a rainbow is seen only when the sun is behind you.

      Model answer

      Sunlight is refracted into the droplet, reflected from the back surface of the droplet and refracted out, so the light returns towards the sun, turned around. The refractive index of water is greater for violet than for red, so the colours separate in the refractions: the droplets send out red light most strongly at about $42^\circ$ and violet at about $40.6^\circ$ from the direction the sunlight came from. Red light reaches the viewer's eye from the droplets seen at $42^\circ$ from the antisolar point, that is, from the direction of the shadow of your own head, and violet from the droplets seen at $40.6^\circ$. The circle of red is larger, so red is on the outer edge. Because light returns from the droplet towards the sun and the centre of the arc is the antisolar point, the arc is seen only with your back to the sun.
