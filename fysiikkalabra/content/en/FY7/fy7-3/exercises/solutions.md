## fy7-3-L1-01
**Given:** long straight wire, current $I = 12\,\text{A}$, distance $r = 3.0\,\text{cm}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ in microtesla
**Principle:** The wire is long and straight, and the distance is much smaller than the length of the wire, so the formula for a long straight wire holds. The field is directly proportional to the current and inversely proportional to the distance.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $r = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 12\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.030\,\text{m}} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T} = 80\,\mu\text{T}$
**Answer:** $80\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the current $12\,\text{A}$ and the distance $3.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** In the example of the chapter, $10\,\text{A}$ gives $200\,\mu\text{T}$ at $1\,\text{cm}$; three times the distance and a slightly greater current give about $200\,\mu\text{T} \cdot 1.2 : 3 = 80\,\mu\text{T}$. The field is of the same order of magnitude as the Earth's field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$, so it clearly turns a compass nearby.

## fy7-3-L1-02
**Given:** coil, $N = 500$, length $l = 25\,\text{cm}$, current $I = 1.2\,\text{A}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil in millitesla
**Principle:** The coil is long compared with its width, and the field is wanted at the centre, far from the ends. There the field is almost uniform and depends on the turn density and the current, so the formula for a long coil holds.
**Formula:** $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$
**Conversions:** $l = 25 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.25\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{500 \cdot 1.2\,\text{A}}{0.25\,\text{m}} = 3.016 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} = 3.016\,\text{mT}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the current $1.2\,\text{A}$ and the length $25\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The turn density is $500 : 0.25\,\text{m} = 2000$ turns per metre. A thousand turns per metre and $1\,\text{A}$ give about $1.26\,\text{mT}$, so $2000$ turns per metre and $1.2\,\text{A}$ give about $1.26\,\text{mT} \cdot 2 \cdot 1.2 \approx 3\,\text{mT}$.

## fy7-3-L1-03
**Given:** wire of a power line, current $I = 800\,\text{A}$, distance $r = 16\,\text{m}$; the fields of the other wires are ignored; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at ground level in microtesla
**Principle:** A power line is hundreds of metres long and straight, so at a distance of $16\,\text{m}$ it can be treated as a long straight wire. The problem leaves out the effect of the other wires, so we calculate the field of one wire.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the current is in amperes and the distance in metres. Finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 800\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 16\,\text{m}} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T} = 10\,\mu\text{T}$
**Answer:** $10\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the distance $16\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** A large current but a large distance: $2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 800 : 16\,\text{T} = 1 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$. The field is about one fifth of the Earth's magnetic field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$.

## fy7-3-L1-04
**Given:** wire, current $I = 2.5\,\text{A}$, distance $r = 5.0\,\text{mm}$; the effect of the other wire is ignored; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ in millitesla
**Principle:** The cord is much longer than the distance of $5\,\text{mm}$, so the wire can be treated as a long straight wire. The problem asks only for the field of one of the wires.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m} = 0.0050\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 2.5\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.0050\,\text{m}} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T} = 0.10\,\text{mT}$
**Answer:** $0.10\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the current $2.5\,\text{A}$ and the distance $5.0\,\text{mm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The field is about twice the Earth's field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$, because the distance is very small. In a real cord the current in the other wire, in the opposite direction, weakens the field at most points and especially farther from the cord, so the field of the cord is then even smaller.

## fy7-3-L1-05
**Given:** coil, $N = 300$, length $l = 15\,\text{cm}$, current $I = 0.40\,\text{A}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil in millitesla
**Principle:** The coil is long compared with its width, and the field is wanted at the centre, so the formula for a long coil holds: the field depends on the turn density $N : l$ and the current.
**Formula:** $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$
**Conversions:** $l = 15 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.15\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{300 \cdot 0.40\,\text{A}}{0.15\,\text{m}} = 1.005 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} = 1.005\,\text{mT}$
**Answer:** $1.0\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the current $0.40\,\text{A}$ and the length $15\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The turn density is $300 : 0.15\,\text{m} = 2000$ turns per metre, and the current is just under half an ampere: $1.26\,\text{mT} \cdot 2 \cdot 0.4 \approx 1\,\text{mT}$. The field is about $20$ times the Earth's field.

## fy7-3-L2-01
**Given:** target field $B = 20\,\text{mT}$, turns $N = 1200$, length $l = 0.30\,\text{m}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** current $I$ of the coil in amperes
**Principle:** The coil is long, so inside it $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$. The field is directly proportional to the current, so the required current is found by solving $I$ from the formula.
**Formula:** $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$, from which $I = \dfrac{B l}{N \mu_0}$
**Conversions:** $B = 20\,\text{mT} = 20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$; the length is already in metres.
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} \cdot 0.30\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 1200} = 3.979\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the field $20\,\text{mT}$ and the length $0.30\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The turn density is $1200 : 0.30\,\text{m} = 4000$ turns per metre, so one ampere gives about $4 \cdot 1.26\,\text{mT} \approx 5\,\text{mT}$. Four amperes give about $20\,\text{mT}$, as wanted.

## fy7-3-L2-02
**Given:** long straight wire, current $I = 6.0\,\text{A}$, magnitude of the field $B = 14\,\mu\text{T}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** distance $r$ in centimetres
**Principle:** The field of a long straight wire weakens in inverse proportion to the distance, so the field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$ at exactly one distance. It is solved from the formula for a straight wire.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, from which $r = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi B}$
**Conversions:** $B = 14\,\mu\text{T} = 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$; finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $r = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}} = 0.0857\,\text{m} = 8.57\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $8.6\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the current $6.0\,\text{A}$ and the field $14\,\mu\text{T}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check by substituting back: $2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{A} : 0.0857\,\text{m} = 1.4 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$. Closer to the wire its field is stronger than the Earth's field, farther away it is weaker; that is why the compass must be within a few centimetres for Ørsted's experiment to show clearly.

## fy7-3-L2-03
**Given:** length of wire $L = 25\,\text{m}$, diameter of the tube $d = 4.0\,\text{cm}$, length of the coil $l = 0.20\,\text{m}$, current $I = 2.0\,\text{A}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil in millitesla
**Principle:** First we calculate the number of turns: one turn uses a length of wire equal to the circumference $\pi d$ of the tube. The coil is five times as long as it is wide, so the formula for a long coil can be used at its centre.
**Formula:** $N = \dfrac{L}{\pi d}$ and $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$, so $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 L I}{\pi d\, l}$
**Conversions:** $d = 4.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.040\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $N = \dfrac{25\,\text{m}}{\pi \cdot 0.040\,\text{m}} = 198.9$ and $B = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{198.9 \cdot 2.0\,\text{A}}{0.20\,\text{m}} = 2.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$
**Answer:** $2.5\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the length of wire $25\,\text{m}$ and the diameter $4.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** There are about $200$ turns and the turn density is about $1000$ turns per metre, so $2\,\text{A}$ gives about $2 \cdot 1.26\,\text{mT} \approx 2.5\,\text{mT}$. In a short coil the field is in reality slightly smaller than the formula gives.

## fy7-3-L2-04
**Given:** two long parallel wires, distance $d = 10\,\text{cm}$, currents $I = 10\,\text{A}$ in opposite directions; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ midway between the wires in microtesla
**Principle:** The vectors of the fields are added. By the right-hand rule, the fields of currents in opposite directions point the same way between the wires: when the thumb points up in one wire and down in the other, the fingers curl in the same direction between the wires. Midway, each wire is $5.0\,\text{cm}$ away, so the fields are equal and strengthen each other.
**Formula:** $B = 2 \cdot \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, where $r = \dfrac{d}{2}$, so $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.050\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 10\,\text{A}}{\pi \cdot 0.050\,\text{m}} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T} = 80\,\mu\text{T}$
**Answer:** $80\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the currents $10\,\text{A}$ and the distance $10\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** One $10\,\text{A}$ wire gives $40\,\mu\text{T}$ at a distance of $5\,\text{cm}$, and two fields in the same direction give twice that. If the currents were in the same direction, the fields would cancel each other midway.

## fy7-3-L3-01
**Given:** wire at $r = 5.0\,\text{cm}$ above the compass, deflection of the needle $\alpha = 30^\circ$, horizontal component of the Earth's field $B_\text{h} = 14\,\mu\text{T}$; the field of the wire $B_\text{w}$ is perpendicular to $B_\text{h}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** current $I$ in the wire in amperes
**Principle:** The compass needle turns to point along the total field. The total field is the vector sum of two perpendicular fields, so for the deflection of the needle $\tan \alpha = B_\text{w} : B_\text{h}$. The wire is long and straight, so the current is found from its field with the formula for a straight wire.
**Formula:** $\tan \alpha = \dfrac{B_\text{w}}{B_\text{h}}$ and $B_\text{w} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, from which $I = \dfrac{2\pi r B_\text{h} \tan \alpha}{\mu_0}$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.050\,\text{m}$ and $B_\text{h} = 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B_\text{w} = 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot \tan 30^\circ = 8.083 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$ and $I = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 0.050\,\text{m} \cdot 8.083 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}} = 2.02\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the distance $5.0\,\text{cm}$ and the field $14\,\mu\text{T}$.
**Reasonableness:** The angle is less than $45^\circ$, so the field of the wire must be weaker than the Earth's field; $8\,\mu\text{T} < 14\,\mu\text{T}$ satisfies this. A current of two amperes is ordinary for a school power supply, and it was with an experiment like this that Ørsted noticed the phenomenon.

## fy7-3-L3-02
**Given:** coil of an MRI scanner, $B = 1.5\,\text{T}$, length $l = 1.6\,\text{m}$, diameter of a turn $d = 1.0\,\text{m}$, current $I = 400\,\text{A}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** total length of wire $L$ in kilometres
**Principle:** The problem assumes the coil is long, so the number of turns is solved from $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$. Each turn uses a length of wire equal to the circumference $\pi d$, so the length of the wire is the number of turns times the circumference.
**Formula:** $N = \dfrac{B l}{\mu_0 I}$ and $L = N \pi d$, so $L = \dfrac{\pi d B l}{\mu_0 I}$
**Conversions:** None needed before substitution: all quantities are in SI units. Finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $N = \dfrac{1.5\,\text{T} \cdot 1.6\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 400\,\text{A}} = 4775$ and $L = 4775 \cdot \pi \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} = 15\,000\,\text{m} = 15\,\text{km}$
**Answer:** about $15\,\text{km}$, two significant figures like the field $1.5\,\text{T}$ and the diameter $1.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The field is about $30\,000$ times the Earth's field, so both a large current and thousands of turns are needed: the turn density is about $3000$ turns per metre. An ordinary copper wire would overheat at such a current, which is why the wire is a superconductor. The estimate is rough, because a real coil is not long compared with its width. When the length is only $1.6$ times the diameter, the field at the centre is about $85\,\%$ of the value given by the formula for a long coil, so a real coil needs more wire than $15\,\text{km}$.

## fy7-3-L3-03
**Given:** two long parallel wires, distance $d = 10\,\text{cm}$, currents in the same direction $I_1 = 4.0\,\text{A}$ and $I_2 = 6.0\,\text{A}$
**Find:** distance $x$ of the point where the field is zero from the wire carrying current $I_1$, in centimetres
**Principle:** By the right-hand rule, the fields of currents in the same direction point in opposite directions between the wires, so at some point between them they cancel. There the magnitudes of the fields are equal. The wire with the weaker current must be nearer, so that its field is equally strong.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi x} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_2}{2\pi (d - x)}$, from which $I_1 (d - x) = I_2 x$ and $x = \dfrac{I_1 d}{I_1 + I_2}$
**Conversions:** None needed: $\mu_0$ and $2\pi$ cancel, and when $d$ is substituted in centimetres, $x$ comes out in centimetres.
**Substitution:** $x = \dfrac{4.0\,\text{A} \cdot 10\,\text{cm}}{4.0\,\text{A} + 6.0\,\text{A}} = 4.0\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{cm}$ from the $4.0\,\text{A}$ wire, two significant figures like the currents.
**Reasonableness:** The point is nearer the wire with the weaker current, as it should be. Check: $4.0\,\text{A} : 4.0\,\text{cm} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{A}}{\text{cm}}$ and $6.0\,\text{A} : 6.0\,\text{cm} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{A}}{\text{cm}}$, so the fields are equal.
