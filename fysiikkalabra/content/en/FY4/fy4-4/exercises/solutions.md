## fy4-4-L1-01
**Given:** $m = 2.5\,\text{kg}$, net force $\Sigma F = 6.0\,\text{N}$ horizontally
**Find:** the acceleration $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** By Newton's second law the net force determines the acceleration: $\Sigma F = ma$. The net force is given directly, so the forces do not need to be added.
**Formula:** $\Sigma F = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{\Sigma F}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, and $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} = \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{6.0\,\text{N}}{2.5\,\text{kg}} = 2.4\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $2.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the direction of the net force, two significant figures like the data $6.0\,\text{N}$ and $2.5\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The net force has a little more than twice as many newtons as the mass has kilograms, so the acceleration is a little over $2\,\text{m/s}^2$. The speed of the toy car increases by $2.4\,\text{m/s}$ every second, which fits a brisk push.

## fy4-4-L1-02
**Given:** total mass $m = 80\,\text{kg}$, acceleration $a = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the net force $\Sigma F$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** Newton's second law: the acceleration is produced by the net force, and $\Sigma F = ma$. The cyclist and the bicycle are treated as one object because they move together.
**Formula:** $\Sigma F = ma$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, and $\text{kg}\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = \text{N}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\Sigma F = 80\,\text{kg}\cdot 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2 = 120\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $120\,\text{N}$ or $1.2\cdot 10^{2}\,\text{N}$ in the direction of motion, two significant figures like the mass $80\,\text{kg}$ and the acceleration $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** The net force is about one sixth of the weight of the cyclist and the bicycle, $80\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 785\,\text{N}$, so the acceleration is about one sixth of the free-fall acceleration, $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. The net force is what is left when the resistive forces are subtracted from the force of the road on the tyre.

## fy4-4-L1-03
**Given:** net force $\Sigma F = 45\,\text{N}$, acceleration $a = 3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the mass $m$ in $\text{kg}$
**Principle:** By Newton's second law $\Sigma F = ma$. When the net force and the acceleration are known, the mass is obtained by solving the law for the mass: the mass tells how much force is needed for a given acceleration.
**Formula:** $\Sigma F = ma$, so $m = \dfrac{\Sigma F}{a}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, and $\dfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2}{\text{m/s}^2} = \text{kg}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $m = \dfrac{45\,\text{N}}{3.0\,\text{m/s}^2} = 15\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $15\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures like the data $45\,\text{N}$ and $3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** Check by multiplying: $15\,\text{kg}\cdot 3.0\,\text{m/s}^2 = 45\,\text{N}$, which is the given net force. The unit comes out as kilograms, as it must for a mass.

## fy4-4-L1-04
**Given:** $m = 12\,\text{kg}$, pulling force $F = 38\,\text{N}$ horizontally, friction force $F_\mu = 20\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the acceleration $a$ of the box in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** By Newton's second law the acceleration is determined by the net force, not by the pulling force alone. In the vertical direction the box does not accelerate, so the vertical forces cancel and the net force is horizontal.
**Free-body diagram:** The box as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of the pull. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the floor points up from the bottom surface, equally long. The pulling force $\overline{F}$ points forward and the friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the floor points backward from the back corner of the bottom surface, shorter than the pulling force.
**Formula:** In the horizontal direction $F - F_\mu = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{F - F_\mu}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{38\,\text{N} - 20\,\text{N}}{12\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{18\,\text{N}}{12\,\text{kg}} = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the direction of the pull, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** Without friction the acceleration would be $38\,\text{N} : 12\,\text{kg} \approx 3.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. Friction takes more than half of the pulling force, so the acceleration is less than half of that, as it should be.

## fy4-4-L1-05
**Given:** $m = 8.0\,\text{kg}$, the bucket rises at constant velocity; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the tension $T$ in the rope in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** In uniform motion the acceleration is zero, so by Newton's second law the net force is zero even though the bucket is moving.
**Free-body diagram:** The bucket as a rectangle, the positive direction up. The tension $\overline{T}$ of the rope points up from the top surface and the weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre, equally long. The velocity is not drawn as a force.
**Formula:** $T - mg = 0$, so $T = mg$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $T = 8.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 78.48\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $78\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $8.0\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of one kilogram is about $10\,\text{N}$, so the weight of an eight-kilogram bucket is about $80\,\text{N}$. The rope supports the whole weight and no more, because moving upward at constant velocity does not require a net force.

## fy4-4-L2-01
**Given:** $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 0$, final velocity $v = 90\,\text{km/h}$, time $\Delta t = 10\,\text{s}$, uniform acceleration
**Find:** the net force $\Sigma F$ in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The velocity of the car increases steadily, so its acceleration is constant and is obtained from the change in velocity. By Newton's second law the net force is the mass times the acceleration.
**Formula:** $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$ and $\Sigma F = ma$, so $\Sigma F = \dfrac{m(v - v_0)}{\Delta t}$
**Conversions:** $90\,\text{km/h} = 90 : 3.6\,\text{m/s} = 25\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $\Sigma F = \dfrac{1200\,\text{kg}\cdot(25\,\text{m/s} - 0)}{10\,\text{s}} = 1200\,\text{kg}\cdot 2.5\,\text{m/s}^2 = 3000\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $3000\,\text{N}$ or $3.0\,\text{kN}$ in the direction of motion, two significant figures like the time $10\,\text{s}$ and the velocity $90\,\text{km/h}$.
**Reasonableness:** An acceleration of $2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ is a brisk acceleration for an ordinary passenger car. The net force is about a quarter of the weight of the car, $1200\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 12\,\text{kN}$, just as the acceleration is about a quarter of the free-fall acceleration.

## fy4-4-L2-02
**Given:** $m = 900\,\text{kg}$, downward acceleration $a = 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the tension $T$ in the supporting rope in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The elevator accelerates downward, so by Newton's second law the net force points down and equals $ma$. The weight is therefore greater than the force of the rope.
**Free-body diagram:** The elevator car with its passengers as a rectangle, the positive direction down, the direction of the acceleration. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the tension $\overline{T}$ of the rope points up from the top surface, shorter than the weight. The acceleration is marked separately, not as a force.
**Formula:** $mg - T = ma$, so $T = m(g - a)$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $T = 900\,\text{kg}\cdot(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2) = 900\,\text{kg}\cdot 8.31\,\text{m/s}^2 = 7479\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $7500\,\text{N}$ or $7.5\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the acceleration $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the elevator is $900\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 8.8\,\text{kN}$. The force of the rope is $ma = 1.35\,\text{kN}$ smaller than this, so that the net force points down. When an elevator starts to go down, a passenger feels lighter for a moment.

## fy4-4-L2-03
**Given:** $m = 120\,\text{kg}$, downward velocity $v_0 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, final velocity $v = 0$, time $\Delta t = 2.0\,\text{s}$, uniform slowing down; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the tension $T$ in the rope in $\text{N}$
**Principle:** The load moves down but slows down, so its acceleration points up. By Newton's second law the net force points in the direction of the acceleration, that is up, so the force of the rope is greater than the weight.
**Free-body diagram:** The load as a rectangle, the positive direction up. The tension $\overline{T}$ of the rope points up from the top surface, longer than the weight, and the weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre. The downward velocity and the upward acceleration are marked separately next to the diagram.
**Formula:** The magnitude of the acceleration is $a = \dfrac{|v - v_0|}{\Delta t}$ upward, and $T - mg = ma$, so $T = m(g + a)$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s}}{2.0\,\text{s}} = 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $T = 120\,\text{kg}\cdot(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2) = 120\,\text{kg}\cdot 10.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1297.2\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $1300\,\text{N}$ or $1.3\,\text{kN}$, two significant figures like the velocity $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the time $2.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of the load is $120\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 1.18\,\text{kN}$. The force of the rope is $ma = 120\,\text{N}$ greater than this, so that the net force points up and stops the downward-moving load. If the rope held up only the weight, the load would keep going down at constant velocity.

## fy4-4-L2-04
**Given:** $m = 30\,\text{kg}$, forces $F_\text{a} = 60\,\text{N}$ and $F_\text{b} = 45\,\text{N}$ horizontally in opposite directions, no friction
**Find:** the acceleration $a$ of the cart in $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The acceleration is determined by the net force. Forces in opposite directions are subtracted from each other, and in the vertical direction the weight and the normal force from the ground cancel.
**Free-body diagram:** The cart as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of the greater pull. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ points up from the bottom surface, equally long. The force of the rope $\overline{F}_\text{a}$ points forward from one side and $\overline{F}_\text{b}$ points backward from the other side, shorter.
**Formula:** $F_\text{a} - F_\text{b} = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{F_\text{a} - F_\text{b}}{m}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{60\,\text{N} - 45\,\text{N}}{30\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{15\,\text{N}}{30\,\text{kg}} = 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the direction of the greater pull, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** The cart accelerates in the direction of the stronger puller, as in a tug of war. If both pulled with $60\,\text{N}$, the net force and the acceleration would be zero. The net force $15\,\text{N}$ is much smaller than either pull, so the acceleration is small.

## fy4-4-L3-01
**Given:** mass of the student $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$, reading of the scale $54.0\,\text{kg}$, so the normal force of the scale is $N = 54.0\,\text{kg}\cdot g$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the acceleration $a$ of the elevator (and the student) in $\text{m/s}^2$ and its direction
**Principle:** The scale does not measure mass but the normal force with which it pushes on the student. The student accelerates with the elevator, so by Newton's second law the difference between the normal force and the weight is $ma$.
**Free-body diagram:** The student as a rectangle, the positive direction up. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the scale points up from the bottom surface. The reading is smaller than the mass, so $\overline{N}$ is shorter than $\overline{G}$ and the net force points down.
**Formula:** $N - mg = ma$, so $a = \dfrac{N - mg}{m}$
**Conversions:** The reading of the scale is converted to a force: $N = 54.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 529.74\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{529.74\,\text{N} - 60.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{60.0\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{529.74\,\text{N} - 588.6\,\text{N}}{60.0\,\text{kg}} = -0.981\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $0.98\,\text{m/s}^2$ downward, two significant figures, because the difference of the readings, $6.0\,\text{kg}$, is known to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The scale shows ten percent too little, so the acceleration is a tenth of the free-fall acceleration, $0.1\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The minus sign means an acceleration downward: the elevator starts moving down. The same reading would also be shown if an elevator moving upward were slowing down.

## fy4-4-L3-02
**Given:** mass of the empty truck $m_1 = 10\,\text{t}$, of the loaded truck $m_2 = 25\,\text{t}$, change in velocity $\Delta v = 36\,\text{km/h}$, time of the empty truck $\Delta t_1 = 8.0\,\text{s}$, the same net force
**Find:** the acceleration time $\Delta t_2$ of the loaded truck in $\text{s}$
**Principle:** The net force is obtained from the acceleration of the empty truck by Newton's second law. The same net force gives a greater mass a smaller acceleration, and with a smaller acceleration the same change in velocity takes longer.
**Formula:** $\Sigma F = m_1 \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t_1}$ and $\Sigma F = m_2 \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t_2}$, so $\Delta t_2 = \dfrac{m_2 \Delta v}{\Sigma F}$
**Conversions:** $36\,\text{km/h} = 36 : 3.6\,\text{m/s} = 10\,\text{m/s}$, $10\,\text{t} = 10\,000\,\text{kg}$ and $25\,\text{t} = 25\,000\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $\Sigma F = 10\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot\dfrac{10\,\text{m/s}}{8.0\,\text{s}} = 12\,500\,\text{N}$ and $\Delta t_2 = \dfrac{25\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot 10\,\text{m/s}}{12\,500\,\text{N}} = 20\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $20\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the time $8.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The mass grows $2.5$ times, so the acceleration becomes $2.5$ times smaller and the time becomes $2.5$ times longer: $2.5\cdot 8.0\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{s}$. This explains why a loaded truck is left behind at the traffic lights.

## fy4-4-L3-03
**Given:** $m = 30\,\text{kg}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, final velocity $v = 0$, friction force $F_\mu = 18\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the time $\Delta t$ in $\text{s}$ after which the sled stops
**Principle:** The sled is neither pulled nor pushed, so the only horizontal force is the friction, which points against the motion. The friction is constant, so by Newton's second law the deceleration is constant, and the stopping time follows from the change in velocity.
**Free-body diagram:** The sled and the child as a rectangle, the positive direction the direction of motion. The weight $\overline{G}$ points down from the centre and the normal force $\overline{N}$ from the snow points up from the bottom surface, equally long. The friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the snow points backward from the back corner of the bottom surface. There is no force pushing forward.
**Formula:** $-F_\mu = ma$ and $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta t = \dfrac{m(v_0 - v)}{F_\mu}$
**Conversions:** The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\Delta t = \dfrac{30\,\text{kg}\cdot(4.0\,\text{m/s} - 0)}{18\,\text{N}} = \dfrac{120\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}{18\,\text{N}} = 6.67\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $6.7\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the data $4.0\,\text{m/s}$, $18\,\text{N}$ and $30\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The deceleration is $18\,\text{N} : 30\,\text{kg} = 0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the speed decreases by $0.6\,\text{m/s}$ every second, and $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ takes about seven seconds. The sled keeps moving forward the whole time even though the net force points backward: it just slows down.
