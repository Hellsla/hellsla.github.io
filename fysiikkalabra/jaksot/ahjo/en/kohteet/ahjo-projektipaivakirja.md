## Quick start

You make your first entry in five minutes. A good journal grows from small entries written during the lesson.

1. Open the group's shared document or a squared notebook.
2. Write the date and one sentence on what you did today.
3. Take a photo of your work and write a caption for it: what the photo shows and why you took it.
4. Write one sentence on the next step.

### Try

Make a three-line entry: the date, what you did and the next step.

### Modify

Use your own way, such as a sketch, a short video or a comic, as long as the date and the next step are there.

### Challenge yourself

Record one decision and the reason for it, and one measurement as a table with units. The assessment criteria are below in the Learn more part.

## Challenge

Keep a journal of your project so that another student could continue the project from the journal alone. A good journal is not a tidy final report. During the work it records what you decided, why you decided it, what you tried and what followed.

The same journal is used in the Ahjo 2 own project, in competition robotics and in upper secondary diplomas. That is why the assessment criteria are the same in all of them.

## Learning goals

After this work you can

- record the course of the work so that decisions and the reasons for them can be seen
- present measurements and tests so that they can be repeated
- use a failed attempt as information for the next version
- mark your sources and the parts made by others
- assess your own and your group's work with the same criteria as the teacher.

### How the journal is assessed

The assessment has seven criteria, each with four levels: beginning, developing, good and excellent. What is assessed is the process, not only the end result. A project that did not work can be assessed as excellent if the journal shows why it did not work and what was learned from it.

The criteria fit all kinds of projects: devices, crafts, design, music, repair, building from recycled materials and art. A measure of success can be a number, but also a qualitative observation, for example a user's feedback, a before-and-after comparison or a photo of the same spot. The top level is reached when the decisions, experiments, use of feedback and development show in the journal and are justified.

1. Defining the problem
    - Beginning: the topic is named, but it is not said whose problem is being solved.
    - Developing: the problem and the user are described in your own words.
    - Good: the problem is based on an interview with a user or an observation, and the goal has an agreed measure of success. The measure can be a number or a qualitative observation.
    - Excellent: in addition, the problem is refined during the project, and the change is justified.
2. Reasons for design decisions
    - Beginning: the chosen solution is given without alternatives.
    - Developing: there are several alternatives, but the choice is not justified.
    - Good: the alternatives are compared on agreed criteria, for example with a decision matrix, sketches or small experiments.
    - Excellent: in addition, sustainability, accessibility, costs or expression are considered, and the comparison clearly affects the choice.
3. Technical implementation
    - Beginning: there is a photo of the prototype or work, but no description of how it was made or how it works.
    - Developing: the structure, circuit, code or way of making is partly described.
    - Good: what this project needs is included: for example a circuit diagram, a dimensioned drawing, a 3D model, commented code, a pattern or the making steps as photos. The parts and materials are listed.
    - Excellent: another student could make the same prototype or work from the journal.
4. Testing and development
    - Beginning: the journal tells whether the prototype worked.
    - Developing: a test was done, but it is not compared with the measure of success.
    - Good: tests are done with the measure of success, and the results are recorded. Measurements are in a table with units and repeated, where there are any. A qualitative test, for example a user trial or a listening, is described so that it can be repeated.
    - Excellent: the test results or feedback lead to a justified change, and the effect of the change is tested again in the same way. A change that did not improve things also counts when the reason is considered.
5. Documentation
    - Beginning: entries are rare and written afterwards.
    - Developing: there are entries from most lessons, but photos or decisions lack explanations.
    - Good: every lesson has a dated entry with the goal, the work done, the decisions and the next step. Photos have captions, and sources are marked.
    - Excellent: in addition, the files are in editable form and versioned, and a final summary tells the course of the project on one page.
6. Collaboration and personal responsibility
    - Beginning: the division of work in the group is not described.
    - Developing: the roles are named.
    - Good: each member's share is visible in the entries and in their own parts of the hand-ins, and feedback from users or other groups is recorded.
    - Excellent: the group has changed its way of working on the basis of feedback, and the change is recorded.
7. Your own learning
    - Beginning: at the end it says that the project was fun or difficult.
    - Developing: the journal tells what new things were learned.
    - Good: it tells what was learned, what was hardest and how the difficulty was solved.
    - Excellent: in addition, it considers what you would do differently next time and connects what you learned to other subjects.

## Starting level and prior skills

No prior skills are needed. It helps if you have written lab reports in physics.

## Safety and induction

- Record in the journal which devices you have had an induction for and when. Ahjo's induction practices are still being prepared, see the [safety section](ahjo-turvallisuus.html).
- Record dangerous situations and near misses as well. The whole group learns from them.
- Do not publish photos of other people without their permission. From user tests you record observations, not the testers' names or other personal data.

## Steps

1. Set up the journal from the template below in the first lesson. Write the project's name, the group members and their roles at the top.
2. Write down the problem and the user. Who needs a solution, and how do you know? Also record the measure of success: what will you measure at the end of the project to know whether you succeeded?
3. Make an entry at the end of every lesson. Set aside five minutes for it. Record the date, the lesson's goal, the work done, the decisions made and the reasons for them, the problems and the next step.
4. Take photos during the work. Write a caption for every photo that tells what it shows and why it was taken.
5. Record measurements in a table with units. Say what you measured with and how many times you repeated the measurement.
6. When something fails, record what happened, what the likely cause was and what you will change. Do not delete failed attempts from the journal.
7. Mark your sources as soon as you use them: instructions, pieces of code, 3D models and images. Mark their licence too if it is known.
8. Read through the journal together once a week. Check that every lesson has an entry and that everyone's share is visible.
9. In Ahjo 2's hand-ins every group member writes a short part of their own, a few sentences: what I did myself, which problem I helped to solve, what I learned and what I would do differently next time. No other separate report is needed.
10. At the end of the project, write a one-page summary: the problem, the solution, the most important test results and what you learned. Also make a video of at most one minute in which the prototype works.

## Diagrams and example code

The journal template. Copy it into your group's document and repeat the lesson entry in every lesson.

```text
PROJECT NAME
Group and roles:
Problem: whose problem, what is needed, how do we know
Measure of success: what is measured and what result is enough

--- Lesson N, date ---
Goal for this lesson:
Work done (who did what):
Decisions and reasons:
Measurements (table, units, repetitions):
Problems and failures (what, why, what we change):
Photos (a caption for each):
Sources and licences:
Next step:
```

An example of an entry in which an attempt failed:

```text
--- Lesson 6, 14 Feb ---
Goal: the watering pump starts when the soil is dry.
Done: Aino connected the pump to the MOSFET module, Eetu wrote the code.
Decision: the pump starts when the reading rises above 520, because
dry soil gave readings of 540-560 and freshly watered soil 300-340
(3 repetitions, see table 2). So dry soil gives the larger reading.
Problem: the pump switched on and off every second. Cause: the reading
wavered on both sides of the threshold. Change: start when the reading
rises above 520, stop only when it falls below 400 (hysteresis).
Next step: test the change overnight and record the readings.
```

## Test and measure

- Swap journals with another group. Can the other group answer these questions from your journal: What problem is solved? Why did you choose this solution? How did you test it? What failed and why? What would you do next?
- Assess your own journal with the seven criteria before you hand it in. Record which level you are at for each criterion, and one thing that would take you to the next level.

## If something does not work

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Entries are missing | They get lost in the rush at the end of the lesson | Set a timer to ring five minutes before the end of the lesson. |
| Photos cannot be understood afterwards | The caption is missing | Write the caption right after taking the photo. |
| One person writes everything | The scribe is always the same | Rotate the scribe's role every lesson. |
| Nobody remembers the reasons for decisions | They are written only at the end | Record a decision and its reason in the same lesson it is made. |
| Measurements cannot be compared | The measure or the method changed midway | Record the measuring method once and use the same one for the whole project. |

## Extension

1. Version control. Save code and models as versions, for example with `v1`, `v2` and so on in the file names, or learn to use a version control system.
2. A summary to publish. Make a portfolio page about the project with the summary, photos and video. Check the permissions for publishing the photos.
3. A guide for others. Write a guide to your project with the same structure as Ahjo's guides.

## For the teacher: preparation

- This is a draft. The assessment criteria are a proposal and have not yet been adopted as a basis for grades. The teacher decides on the assessment and its weighting.
- Decide which school service is used for the journal and how it is shared with the teacher.
- In the first lesson, show an example of a good entry, including a failed attempt.
- Besides Ahjo 2, the criteria suit competition robotics and upper secondary diplomas. In particular, defining the problem, testing and documentation are the same in all of them.
- The ideas of the guide come from Fab Academy's documentation and assessment practices, from the assessment of engineering notebooks in robotics competitions, and from the design notebook of TeachEngineering's electric vehicle project.
