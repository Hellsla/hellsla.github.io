Resistance and Ohm's law

Why is the heating wire of a toaster made of thin special metal, while the conductors of an extension cord are thick copper?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • determine the resistance of a component from its voltage and current.
  • interpret a characteristic curve and explain when Ohm's law holds.
  • calculate the resistance of a conductor from its resistivity, length and cross-sectional area.

Prior knowledge

  • Current is measured with an ammeter connected in series with the component, and voltage with a voltmeter connected in parallel with itchapter Measurements in an electric circuit
  • The direction of an electric current is agreed to be from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, and in a metal the current is the motion of free electronschapter Electric circuit, current and voltage

When the toaster is on, the same electric current flows in the copper conductors of the extension cord and in the heating wire of the toaster. The extension cord stays cool in the hand, but the thin wire of the toaster glows orange. The conductors of the extension cord are thick copper, the wire of the toaster is thin nichrome.

Optional Review: connecting the meters
You want to measure the voltage across a lamp and the current through the lamp at the same time. How are the meters connected?

  • Correct. The same current must flow through the ammeter as through the lamp, so it is in the same branch of the circuit as the lamp. A voltmeter measures the voltage between two points, so it is connected across the terminals of the lamp (chapter Measurements in an electric circuit).

  • The meters have swapped places. The resistance of an ammeter is very small, so in parallel almost all the current would flow through the meter and bypass the lamp. An ammeter goes in series, a voltmeter in parallel.

  • An ammeter connected in parallel would bypass the lamp, because its resistance is very small. To measure a current, the current must flow through the meter, so the ammeter is connected in series.

  • The resistance of a voltmeter is very large, so in series it almost cuts off the current and the lamp goes out. A voltmeter is connected in parallel with the component whose voltage is measured (chapter Measurements in an electric circuit).

How the calculation works

The meters show the voltage $U$ and the current $I = U : R$. The resistance of the resistor is $R = 10\,\Omega$. The resistance of the wire is $R = \rho l : A$, where the resistivity of constantan is $\rho = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$ and $A$ is converted to square metres, $1\,\text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$. The resistance of the lamp depends on the voltage:

$$R = R_\text{c} + (R_\text{r} - R_\text{c}) \left(\frac{U}{U_\text{r}}\right)^{0.6}$$

where the cold resistance is $R_\text{c} = 1.0\,\Omega$, the rated resistance $R_\text{r} = 12.0\,\Omega$ and the rated voltage $U_\text{r} = 3.0\,\text{V}$. The formula is a model that imitates the behaviour of a real lamp, not a law of physics. The faint curve is calculated from the same formulas at voltages 0–$3.0\,\text{V}$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Resistance tells how strongly a component opposes current

    When a voltage is connected across the terminals of a component, an electric current flows through it. The same voltage produces a different current in different components: in a short thick copper wire the current is large, in a long thin heating wire it is small. The resistance $R$ of a component tells how strongly it opposes electric current. It is defined as the ratio of voltage to current, $R = U : I$. The greater the resistance, the smaller the current a given voltage produces. The unit of resistance is the ohm, $1\,\Omega = 1\,\text{V/A}$: a component has a resistance of one ohm when a voltage of one volt makes a current of one ampere flow through it.

    A component made to have a certain resistance is called a resistor. Its circuit symbol is a rectangle. Resistance is determined by measuring the voltage across the component and the current through it at the same time. The ammeter is connected in series with the component so that the same current flows through both, and the voltmeter in parallel with the component, across its terminals. When the measurement is repeated at several voltages, we can see whether the ratio $U : I$ stays the same.

    Where resistance comes from

    In a metal, the positive ions form a regular lattice, and the free electrons move among them. When there is a voltage between the ends of a conductor, the electrons drift along the conductor, but they keep colliding with the vibrating ions. The collisions slow the electrons down: this is the cause of resistance. In the collisions electrical energy is changed into internal energy of the conductor, so the conductor warms up. In a long conductor an electron collides more often on its way, so the resistance is greater. In a thick conductor more electrons travel side by side, so the resistance is smaller. When a metal gets hot, the ions vibrate more strongly and there are more collisions. This is why the resistance of a metal conductor increases as the temperature rises.

    Ohm's law and resistivity

    The resistance is the ratio of the voltage across a component to the current through it:

    $$R = \frac{U}{I}$$

    where $R$ is the resistance ($\Omega$), $U$ the voltage across the component ($\text{V}$) and $I$ the current through it ($\text{A}$).

    Ohm's law: the current through a metal conductor is directly proportional to its voltage when the temperature of the conductor stays the same. Then the resistance is constant, and

    $$U = R I$$

    When the voltage doubles, the current doubles too. Ohm's law is not a general law of nature but a property of many conducting materials: it holds for metals and resistors at a constant temperature, but not, for example, for a filament lamp, whose wire heats up, nor for a diode.

    Characteristic curve

    The characteristic curve of a component is a graph that shows how the voltage across the component and the current through it depend on each other. When the current $I$ is on the horizontal axis and the voltage $U$ on the vertical axis, the characteristic curve of a resistor that obeys Ohm's law is a straight line through the origin, and the slope of the line is the resistance $R$. The characteristic curve of a filament lamp is curved. The tungsten wire of the lamp gets hotter the greater the current through it, and the resistance of a hot wire is greater. At every point of the curve, however, the resistance is the ratio $U : I$, that is, the slope of the straight line drawn from the origin to the point, not the steepness of the curve at that point.

    Resistivity

    The resistance of a uniform conductor of one material is directly proportional to the length $l$ of the conductor and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area $A$:

    $$R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$$

    where $R$ is the resistance of the conductor ($\Omega$), $l$ its length ($\text{m}$), $A$ its cross-sectional area ($\text{m}^2$) and $\rho$ the resistivity of the material ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$). A wire twice as long is like two wires one after the other, so the resistance doubles. A wire with twice the cross-sectional area is like two wires side by side, so the current can take two routes and the resistance is halved. The cross-sectional area is often given in square millimetres, and $1\,\text{mm}^2 = (10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2 = 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$.

    Resistivity is a property of a material: it tells how poorly the material conducts electricity. The resistivity of metals increases as the temperature rises. The resistivity of constantan changes very little with temperature, which is why it is used to make precise resistors. Heating wires are made of nichrome because of its high resistivity. The values in the table are in $\Omega\cdot\text{m}$ at room temperature ($20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$).

    material resistivity $\rho$
    silver $1.6 \cdot 10^{-8}$
    copper $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$
    aluminium $2.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$
    iron $1.0 \cdot 10^{-7}$
    constantan $5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}$
    nichrome $1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}$

    The resistivity of copper is small, so thick copper conductors let current flow with almost no voltage drop. The wire of a toaster is nichrome, whose resistivity is more than 60 times that of copper, and the wire is also thin. So its resistance is large, and it is the wire that gets hot.

    Common misconception

    Many conclude from the formula $R = U : I$ that the resistance increases when the voltage increases. The resistance of a resistor, however, does not depend on the voltage: when the voltage doubles, the current doubles too, and the ratio stays the same. Resistance depends on the material, length, thickness and temperature of the conductor. The resistance of a filament lamp increases with the voltage only because a larger current heats the wire.

    Another misconception is that current is used up in a resistor. After the resistor the current is as large as before it, because charge does not disappear on the way. In a resistor electrical energy is changed into internal energy, not current.

    Summary

    • Resistance $R = U : I$ tells how strongly a component opposes electric current. The unit is the ohm, $1\,\Omega = 1\,\text{V/A}$.
    • Resistance is measured with an ammeter in series with the component and a voltmeter in parallel with it.
    • Ohm's law: the current through a metal conductor is directly proportional to the voltage when the temperature stays the same, $U = RI$.
    • The characteristic curve shows how the voltage across a component and the current through it depend on each other. The curve of a resistor is a straight line through the origin, that of a filament lamp is curved, because the resistance of a hot wire is greater.
    • The resistance of a conductor is $R = \rho l : A$: it grows with length, falls with thickness and depends on the resistivity $\rho$ of the material.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A resistor with a resistance of $12\,\Omega$ is to be made from constantan wire with a cross-sectional area of $0.20\,\text{mm}^2$. How long a wire is needed? The resistivity of constantan is $5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $R = 12\,\Omega$, $A = 0.20\,\text{mm}^2$ and $\rho = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$. We want the length $l$ of the wire.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The wire is uniform and of one material, so its resistance is proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. The resistivity of constantan stays almost the same even if the wire warms up in use, so the table value will do.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. We solve for the length by multiplying the equation on both sides by $A$ and dividing by $\rho$:

      $$\begin{aligned} R &= \rho \frac{l}{A} \\ l &= \frac{R A}{\rho} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The cross-sectional area in square metres:

      $0.20\,\text{mm}^2$$= 0.20 \cdot (10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2$
      $= 0.20 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} l &= \frac{R A}{\rho} \\ &= \frac{12\,\Omega \cdot 0.20 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}} \\ &= 4.8\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The wire needed is $4.8\,\text{m}$ long. The result is given to two significant figures, because the resistance, the cross-sectional area and the resistivity are given to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. One metre of this wire has a resistance of

      $5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} : (0.20 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2)$
      $= 2.5\,\Omega$

      so $12\,\Omega$ needs just under five metres: $12\,\Omega : 2.5\,\Omega = 4.8$. The units work out: $\Omega \cdot \text{m}^2 : (\Omega\cdot\text{m}) = \text{m}$. At a voltage of $6.0\,\text{V}$ the finished resistor would pass a current of $6.0\,\text{V} : 12\,\Omega = 0.50\,\text{A}$.

      A student wants to determine the resistance of a filament lamp at a voltage of $3.0\,\text{V}$, when the current is $0.25\,\text{A}$.       • The slope of the tangent is not the resistance of the lamp whichever way round it is calculated. Also, the unit of the slope of an $(I, U)$ graph is $\text{V/A} = \Omega$, so the voltage is divided by the current. The resistance is $U : I = 12\,\Omega$.

      • Correct: $3.0\,\text{V} : 0.25\,\text{A} = 12\,\Omega$. Resistance is defined as the ratio of voltage to current at the same operating point. The tangent tells how much the voltage changes when the current changes a little, and on a curved line that is something else. Only on a straight characteristic curve do the tangent and the line drawn from the origin coincide.

      • The heat of the wire is already included in the measured current $0.25\,\text{A}$: it is precisely because the wire is hot that the current is this small. The resistance at this point is $3.0\,\text{V} : 0.25\,\text{A} = 12\,\Omega$.

      6Check your understanding

      1. Concept

      The voltage across a resistor is doubled, and the temperature of the resistor stays the same. What happens to the resistance of the resistor?

      • The formula $R = U : I$ does not mean that resistance depends on voltage. When the voltage doubles, the current doubles too, and the ratio stays the same. Resistance depends on the material, the dimensions and the temperature.

      • A larger voltage produces a larger current, but it does not change the properties of the resistor. The ratio $U : I$ stays the same when the temperature does not change.

      • If the resistance stays the same, a doubled voltage produces a doubled current, because $I = U : R$. So the current does not stay the same.

      • Correct. At a constant temperature a resistor obeys Ohm's law: the current is directly proportional to the voltage, so the ratio $U : I$ does not change. For example, in a $10\,\Omega$ resistor $1.5\,\text{V}$ gives $0.15\,\text{A}$ and $3.0\,\text{V}$ gives $0.30\,\text{A}$.

      2. Characteristic curve from measurement points

      The graph shows the measurement points of a resistor, when the voltage was changed and the current measured. Fit a line through the origin to the points and determine the resistance of the resistor from the slope of the line. Answer in ohms.

      Ω
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Why does the line pass through the origin? What quantity is the slope of an $(I, U)$ graph?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Read the slope from two points of the fitted line, not from two neighbouring measurement points. The origin is one point.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The fitted line passes through the voltage $2.2\,\text{V}$ at the current $0.100\,\text{A}$: $R = 2.2\,\text{V} : 0.100\,\text{A}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      five measurement points on an $(I, U)$ graph; current $0.020$–$0.100\,\text{A}$, voltage $0.47$–$2.21\,\text{V}$

      Find

      the resistance $R$ of the resistor in ohms

      Principle

      The resistor obeys Ohm's law, so $U = RI$: the characteristic curve is a straight line whose slope is $R$. The line passes through the origin, because with no voltage no current flows. In real data sets the measurement points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty, so the line is fitted to all the points.

      Reading the graph

      The line drawn through the origin passes among the points, some of the points lie above it and some below. We read a point of the line far from the origin: at the current $0.100\,\text{A}$ the voltage of the line is $2.2\,\text{V}$. The other point is the origin $(0$; $0)$.

      Formula

      $R = \dfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta I}$

      Conversions

      Not needed: the voltage is in volts and the current in amperes.

      Substitution

      $R$$= \dfrac{2.2\,\text{V} - 0\,\text{V}}{0.100\,\text{A} - 0\,\text{A}}$
      $= 22\,\Omega$

      Answer

      $22\,\Omega$

      Two significant figures, because the voltage is read from the fitted line to about $0.05\,\text{V}$.

      Reasonableness

      Single points give almost the same value: $0.86\,\text{V} : 0.040\,\text{A} \approx 22\,\Omega$ and $1.74\,\text{V} : 0.080\,\text{A} \approx 22\,\Omega$. The value is of the order of magnitude of an ordinary resistor.

      Optional 3. Explain and transfer

      The resistance of a conductor is $R = \rho l : A$. In a long extension cord there is more conductor, so the resistance of a conductor of the same thickness would be greater: a conductor twice as long, twice the resistance. The resistance of a conductor must be small so that the conductor does not heat up dangerously and so that the voltage is left for the appliance and not for the cord. When the cross-sectional area is increased in the same proportion as the length, the resistance stays unchanged. Conductors are made of copper, because its resistivity $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$ is one of the smallest among metals.

        Finally two quick questions. Answer first yourself and open the answer only then.

        1. The voltage across a component is $9.0\,\text{V}$, and a current of $0.060\,\text{A}$ flows through it. What is the resistance of the component?
          Answer
          $R$$= U : I$
          $= 9.0\,\text{V} : 0.060\,\text{A}$
          $= 150\,\Omega$
        2. The length of a copper wire is halved and its cross-sectional area is doubled. How does the resistance of the wire change?
          Answer

          The resistance falls to a quarter, because $R = \rho l : A$: half the length halves the resistance and double the cross-sectional area halves it again.

        1. Exercise 3-1Core

          A kettle is connected to a voltage of $230\,\text{V}$, and a current of $8.7\,\text{A}$ flows through its heating element. What is the resistance of the heating element?

          Ω
          Answer

          $26\,\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the voltage across the heating element $U = 230\,\text{V}$ and the current $I = 8.7\,\text{A}$

          Find

          the resistance $R$ of the heating element in ohms

          Principle

          Resistance is the ratio of the voltage across a component to the current through it. The given values refer to the same resistor at the same moment, while the kettle is on, so the ratio gives the resistance of the element at its operating temperature.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the voltage is in volts and the current in amperes, and $1\,\text{V/A} = 1\,\Omega$.

          Substitution

          $R$$= \dfrac{230\,\text{V}}{8.7\,\text{A}}$
          $= 26.44\,\Omega$

          Answer

          $26\,\Omega$

          Two significant figures like the current $8.7\,\text{A}$.

          Reasonableness

          Estimate: $230\,\text{V} : 10\,\text{A} = 23\,\Omega$; the current is a little under $10\,\text{A}$, so the resistance is a little over $23\,\Omega$. Check with Ohm's law: $26.44\,\Omega \cdot 8.7\,\text{A} = 230\,\text{V}$.

        2. Exercise 3-2Core

          The resistance of a resistor in an electronic circuit is $220\,\Omega$, and a current of $15\,\text{mA}$ flows through it.

          How large is the voltage across the resistor? Answer in volts.

          V
          Answer

          $3.3\,\text{V}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the resistance of the resistor $R = 220\,\Omega$ and the current $I = 15\,\text{mA}$

          Find

          the voltage $U$ across the resistor in volts

          Principle

          The resistor obeys Ohm's law, so the voltage is the product of the resistance and the current. The same equation follows from the definition of resistance $R = U : I$ by multiplying both sides by the current.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$, so $U = R I$

          Conversions

          $I$$= 15\,\text{mA}$
          $= 15 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{A}$
          $= 0.015\,\text{A}$

          Substitution

          $U$$= 220\,\Omega \cdot 0.015\,\text{A}$
          $= 3.3\,\text{V}$

          Answer

          $3.3\,\text{V}$

          Two significant figures like the current $15\,\text{mA}$.

          Reasonableness

          An ohm times an ampere is a volt, so the unit is correct. Estimate: $200\,\Omega \cdot 0.015\,\text{A} = 3\,\text{V}$, and the exact result is a little larger, as it should be. In electronics, voltages of a few volts like this are common.

        3. Exercise 3-3

          The total resistance of the heating wires of a car's rear window is $1.2\,\Omega$, and they are connected to the car's voltage of $12\,\text{V}$.

          How large a current flows through the wires?

          A
          Answer

          $10\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the resistance of the heating wires $R = 1.2\,\Omega$ and the voltage $U = 12\,\text{V}$

          Find

          the current $I$ through the wires in amperes

          Principle

          The current in a heating wire follows from the definition of resistance when the voltage and the resistance are known. The given resistance applies to the wires in use.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$, so $I = \dfrac{U}{R}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the voltage is in volts and the resistance in ohms, and $1\,\text{V}/\Omega = 1\,\text{A}$.

          Substitution

          $I$$= \dfrac{12\,\text{V}}{1.2\,\Omega}$
          $= 10\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $10\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage and the resistance.

          Reasonableness

          The resistance is small, so the current is large. Check: $1.2\,\Omega \cdot 10\,\text{A} = 12\,\text{V}$. The rear window heater is indeed one of the biggest electricity users in a car, and it has a fuse of its own.

        4. Exercise 3-4

          A conductor of a copper cable has a length of $25\,\text{m}$ and a cross-sectional area of $2.5\,\text{mm}^2$. The resistivity of copper is $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.

          What is the resistance of the conductor? Answer in ohms.

          Ω
          Answer

          $0.17\,\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          copper conductor, $l = 25\,\text{m}$, $A = 2.5\,\text{mm}^2$, $\rho = 1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

          Find

          the resistance $R$ of the conductor in ohms

          Principle

          The conductor is uniform copper, so its resistance is directly proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. The constant of proportionality is the resistivity of copper.

          Formula

          $R = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}$

          Conversions

          $A$$= 2.5\,\text{mm}^2$
          $= 2.5 \cdot (10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 2.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$

          Substitution

          $R$$= \dfrac{1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot 25\,\text{m}}{2.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= 0.17\,\Omega$

          Answer

          $0.17\,\Omega$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          Units: $\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot \text{m} : \text{m}^2 = \Omega$. The resistance of an electric cable should indeed be small, under an ohm, so that the cable does not heat up. One metre of this conductor has a resistance of $0.0068\,\Omega$, and $25 \cdot 0.0068\,\Omega = 0.17\,\Omega$.

        5. Exercise 3-5

          There is a voltage of $5.0\,\text{V}$ between the electrodes of a moisture sensor pushed into the soil of a flowerpot, and a current of $2.0\,\text{mA}$ flows through the soil.

          What is the resistance of the soil between the electrodes? Answer in kilohms.

          kΩ
          Answer

          $2.5\,\text{k}\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the voltage between the electrodes $U = 5.0\,\text{V}$ and the current $I = 2.0\,\text{mA}$

          Find

          the resistance $R$ of the soil in kilohms

          Principle

          Between the electrodes the soil is a component of the circuit, and its resistance is the ratio of the voltage to the current at the same moment.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$

          Conversions

          $I$$= 2.0\,\text{mA}$
          $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{A}$

          Finally $1\,\text{k}\Omega = 1000\,\Omega$.

          Substitution

          $R$$= \dfrac{5.0\,\text{V}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{A}}$
          $= 2500\,\Omega$
          $= 2.5\,\text{k}\Omega$

          Answer

          $2.5\,\text{k}\Omega$

          Two significant figures like the voltage and the current.

          Reasonableness

          The current is only milliamperes, so the resistance is large, thousands of ohms. Moist soil conducts electricity better than dry soil, so as the soil dries the resistance increases and the current decreases: this is how the sensor works.

        1. Exercise 3-6Core

          The voltage across a resistor is $6.0\,\text{V}$ and a current of $0.25\,\text{A}$ flows through it. The voltage is raised to $9.0\,\text{V}$, and the temperature of the resistor stays the same.

          How large a current now flows through the resistor?

          A
          Answer

          $0.38\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          at first $U_1 = 6.0\,\text{V}$ and $I_1 = 0.25\,\text{A}$; at the end $U_2 = 9.0\,\text{V}$; the temperature stays the same

          Find

          the current $I_2$ at the voltage $9.0\,\text{V}$

          Principle

          The temperature of the resistor stays the same, so it obeys Ohm's law and its resistance is the same at both voltages. The resistance is calculated from the first measurement and used for the second.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U_1}{I_1}$ and $I_2 = \dfrac{U_2}{R}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the voltages are in volts and the current in amperes.

          Substitution

          $R$$= \dfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{0.25\,\text{A}}$
          $= 24\,\Omega$
          $I_2$$= \dfrac{9.0\,\text{V}}{24\,\Omega}$
          $= 0.375\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $0.38\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the voltages and the current $0.25\,\text{A}$.

          Reasonableness

          The voltage becomes 1.5 times as large ($9.0\,\text{V} : 6.0\,\text{V} = 1.5$), so by Ohm's law the current also becomes 1.5 times as large: $1.5 \cdot 0.25\,\text{A} = 0.375\,\text{A}$. The result is the same.

        2. Exercise 3-7Core

          The graph shows the characteristic curve of a filament lamp.

          What is the resistance of the lamp when the voltage across it is $1.0\,\text{V}$? Read the value you need from the graph.

          Ω
          Answer

          $6.7\,\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the characteristic curve of a filament lamp as an $(I, U)$ graph; the voltage $U = 1.0\,\text{V}$

          Find

          the resistance $R$ of the lamp at the voltage $1.0\,\text{V}$

          Principle

          Resistance is the ratio of voltage to current at the same point of the curve. The lamp does not obey Ohm's law, so the resistance must be read exactly at this voltage, not from the steepness of the curve nor from another point.

          Reading the graph

          On the vertical axis we find $U = 1.0\,\text{V}$ and go horizontally to the curve. The point on the curve is at $I = 0.15\,\text{A}$ on the horizontal axis, halfway between the grid lines $0.10\,\text{A}$ and $0.20\,\text{A}$.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the voltage is in volts and the current in amperes.

          Substitution

          $R$$= \dfrac{1.0\,\text{V}}{0.15\,\text{A}}$
          $= 6.67\,\Omega$

          Answer

          $6.7\,\Omega$

          Two significant figures, because the current is read from the graph to about $0.005\,\text{A}$.

          Reasonableness

          The resistance of the lamp increases as the wire gets hotter. At the voltage $3.0\,\text{V}$ the curve gives $0.25\,\text{A}$ and a resistance of $12\,\Omega$, so at a smaller voltage the value $6.7\,\Omega$ is sensibly smaller.

        3. Exercise 3-8

          A copper conductor $50\,\text{m}$ long is to be run to an outbuilding. The resistance of the conductor may be at most $0.50\,\Omega$. The resistivity of copper is $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.

          What is the smallest cross-sectional area the conductor must have? Answer in square millimetres.

          mm²
          Answer

          $1.7\,\text{mm}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          copper conductor, $l = 50\,\text{m}$, $\rho = 1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$; resistance at most $R = 0.50\,\Omega$

          Find

          the smallest cross-sectional area $A$ in square millimetres

          Principle

          The resistance of a uniform conductor is inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area, so the largest allowed resistance gives the smallest allowed cross-sectional area.

          Formula

          $R = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}$, so $A = \dfrac{\rho l}{R}$

          Conversions

          The result comes out in square metres, and

          $1\,\text{m}^2$$= (10^3\,\text{mm})^2$
          $= 10^6\,\text{mm}^2$

          Substitution

          $A$$= \dfrac{1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot 50\,\text{m}}{0.50\,\Omega}$
          $= 1.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 1.7 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 10^6\,\text{mm}^2$
          $= 1.7\,\text{mm}^2$

          Answer

          $1.7\,\text{mm}^2$

          Two significant figures like the data.

          Reasonableness

          Units: $\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot \text{m} : \Omega = \text{m}^2$. Check:

          $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot 50\,\text{m} : (1.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2)$
          $= 0.50\,\Omega$

          Conductors in homes are typically $1.5$–$2.5\,\text{mm}^2$, so the result is of the usual size.

        4. Exercise 3-9

          A constantan wire has a diameter of $0.40\,\text{mm}$ and a length of $2.0\,\text{m}$. The resistivity of constantan is $5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$. What is the resistance of the wire?

          Ω
          Answer

          $8.0\,\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          constantan wire, diameter $d = 0.40\,\text{mm}$, length $l = 2.0\,\text{m}$, $\rho = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

          Find

          the resistance $R$ of the wire in ohms

          Principle

          The wire is uniform, so $R = \rho l : A$. The cross-section is a circle, so the cross-sectional area is calculated from the radius, which is half the diameter.

          Formula

          $A$$= \pi r^2$
          $= \pi \left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2$

          $R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A}$

          Conversions

          $d$$= 0.40\,\text{mm}$
          $= 0.40 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$

          so $r = 0.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          $A$$= \pi \cdot (0.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 1.257 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$
          $R$$= \dfrac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot 2.0\,\text{m}}{1.257 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2}$
          $= 7.96\,\Omega$

          Answer

          $8.0\,\Omega$

          Two significant figures like the diameter, the length and the resistivity.

          Reasonableness

          The cross-sectional area is about $0.13\,\text{mm}^2$, a little more than that of the thinnest wire in the simulation ($0.10\,\text{mm}^2$). In the simulation $1.00\,\text{m}$ of wire with a cross-sectional area of $0.10\,\text{mm}^2$ has $5.0\,\Omega$, so $2.0\,\text{m}$ of slightly thicker wire has a little under $10\,\Omega$.

        1. Exercise 3-10

          The resistance of a copper wire is $0.80\,\Omega$. The wire is drawn on a drawing bench to twice its original length. The amount of copper, that is, the volume of the wire, does not change, and the wire stays uniform.

          What is the resistance of the stretched wire? Explain why the resistance changes by more than the length.

          Ω
          Answer

          $3.2\,\Omega$

          Show the solution

          Given

          original resistance $R_1 = 0.80\,\Omega$; the length doubles, $l_2 = 2 l_1$; the volume is conserved

          Find

          the resistance $R_2$ of the stretched wire

          Principle

          The wire is of the same material and uniform, so $R = \rho l : A$. The volume $V = A l$ stays the same, so when the length doubles, the cross-sectional area is halved. Both changes increase the resistance: the length doubles it, and the half-size cross-sectional area doubles it again. So the resistance changes more than the length.

          Formula

          $R_1 = \rho \dfrac{l_1}{A_1}$ and $R_2 = \rho \dfrac{l_2}{A_2}$, where $l_2 = 2 l_1$ and $A_2 = \dfrac{A_1}{2}$, so

          $R_2$$= \rho \dfrac{2 l_1}{A_1 / 2}$
          $= 4 R_1$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the resistances are in ohms, and the comparison is made with ratios.

          Substitution

          $R_2$$= 4 \cdot 0.80\,\Omega$
          $= 3.2\,\Omega$

          Answer

          $3.2\,\Omega$

          Two significant figures like the original resistance.

          Reasonableness

          The volume is conserved: $2 l_1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} A_1 = l_1 A_1$. If the cross-sectional area stayed the same, the result would be only $1.6\,\Omega$; the thinning brings the second doubling. The resistivity does not change, because the material is the same.

        2. Exercise 3-11

          A student measured the current through a nichrome wire at different lengths, when the voltage across the wire was $2.00\,\text{V}$ all the time and the cross-sectional area $0.20\,\text{mm}^2$. At the lengths $0.40\,\text{m}$, $0.80\,\text{m}$, $1.20\,\text{m}$ and $1.60\,\text{m}$ the currents were $0.91\,\text{A}$, $0.46\,\text{A}$, $0.30\,\text{A}$ and $0.23\,\text{A}$. Determine the resistivity of nichrome from the measurements. Answer in the unit $\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$ ($1\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m} = 10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$).

          µΩ·m
          Answer

          $1.1\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          nichrome wire, $A = 0.20\,\text{mm}^2$, $U = 2.00\,\text{V}$; measurements of $l$ and $I$: $0.40\,\text{m}$ and $0.91\,\text{A}$, $0.80\,\text{m}$ and $0.46\,\text{A}$, $1.20\,\text{m}$ and $0.30\,\text{A}$, $1.60\,\text{m}$ and $0.23\,\text{A}$

          Find

          the resistivity $\rho$ of nichrome in $\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

          Principle

          The resistance for each length is $R = U : I$. The resistance of a uniform wire is $R = \rho l : A$, so $R$ is directly proportional to the length and the ratio $R : l$ is the constant $\rho : A$. In real data sets the points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty, so all the measurements are used.

          Formula

          $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$ and $R = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}$, so $\rho = \dfrac{R}{l} \cdot A$

          Conversions

          $A$$= 0.20\,\text{mm}^2$
          $= 0.20 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$

          Finally $10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} = 1\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.

          Substitution

          The resistances are $2.00\,\text{V} : 0.91\,\text{A} = 2.20\,\Omega$, $4.35\,\Omega$, $6.67\,\Omega$ and $8.70\,\Omega$, and the ratios $R : l$ are $5.49$, $5.43$, $5.56$ and $5.43\,\Omega/\text{m}$. The slope of the line fitted through the origin is $5.47\,\Omega/\text{m}$, so

          $\rho$$= 5.47\,\tfrac{\Omega}{\text{m}} \cdot 0.20 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 1.09 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

          Answer

          $1.1\,\mu\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the cross-sectional area and the currents $0.30\,\text{A}$ and $0.23\,\text{A}$.

          Reasonableness

          The result is the same as the table value for nichrome, $1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$. The resistance becomes four times as large when the length becomes four times as large ($2.20\,\Omega$ and $8.70\,\Omega$), so the proportionality holds.

        3. Exercise 3-12

          The resistance of the filament lamp of a torch is measured with a multimeter when the lamp is at room temperature: $1.0\,\Omega$. When the lamp is lit at a voltage of $3.0\,\text{V}$, a current of $0.25\,\text{A}$ flows through it.

          How large a current flows through the lamp at the moment of switching on, when the wire is still cold? Explain why a filament lamp most often burns out right when it is switched on.

          A
          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{A}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the resistance of the cold lamp $R_\text{cold} = 1.0\,\Omega$; in use $U = 3.0\,\text{V}$ and $I = 0.25\,\text{A}$

          Find

          the current $I_\text{cold}$ at the moment of switching on at the voltage $3.0\,\text{V}$

          Principle

          At the moment of switching on, the wire is still at room temperature, so its resistance is the value for the cold lamp. The wire heats up only after current has flowed through it for a moment. The current follows from the definition of resistance.

          Formula

          $R_\text{cold}$$= \dfrac{U}{I_\text{cold}}$

          so

          $I_\text{cold}$$= \dfrac{U}{R_\text{cold}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the voltage is in volts and the resistance in ohms.

          Substitution

          $I_\text{cold}$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{V}}{1.0\,\Omega}$
          $= 3.0\,\text{A}$

          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{A}$

          Two significant figures like the voltage and the resistance.

          Reasonableness

          The resistance of the lit lamp is $3.0\,\text{V} : 0.25\,\text{A} = 12\,\Omega$, which is 12 times the cold value, so the switch-on current is 12 times the operating current $0.25\,\text{A}$. This large current heats the thin wire very quickly, and the thinnest part of the wire heats up most, because there the resistance per unit length is greatest. That is why the wire most often breaks right when the lamp is switched on.
