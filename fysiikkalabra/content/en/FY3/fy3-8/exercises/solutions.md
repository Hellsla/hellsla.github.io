## fy3-8-L1-01
**Given:** steel gutter, $L_0 = 6.0\,\text{m}$, $T_0 = 15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 55\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the change in length of the gutter $\Delta L$ in millimetres
**Principle:** The vibration of the particles of a solid gets stronger when it warms up and the average distance between the particles grows. The gutter is not jammed and its state of matter does not change, so the change in length is proportional to the initial length and the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T - T_0$. The quantity asked for, $\Delta L$, is already alone on the left side.
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins: $\Delta T = 55\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 15\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 40\,\text{K}$. The result comes out in metres, and $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta L = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{m} \cdot 40\,\text{K} = 0.00288\,\text{m} = 2.88\,\text{mm}$
**Answer:** $\Delta L \approx 2.9\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures. The limiting data are the length $6.0\,\text{m}$ and the coefficient $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (both have two figures); the temperature difference $\Delta T = 40\,\text{K}$, as a difference of whole degrees, also has two figures, so it does not limit the result any further.
**Reasonableness:** A rough estimate: $10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 6\,\text{m} \cdot 40\,\text{K} \approx 2.4\,\text{mm}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude. $\Delta L$ is positive because the gutter warms up. The change is only $\alpha \Delta T = 0.048\,\%$ of the length, but the fastenings must still let the gutter slide.

## fy3-8-L1-02
**Given:** aluminium wire, $L_0 = 50.0\,\text{m}$, $T_0 = 18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = -27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha = 23 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the change in length $\Delta L$ with its sign in millimetres
**Principle:** When it cools, the vibration of the particles gets weaker and their average distance decreases, so the wire gets shorter. The state of matter of the wire does not change, so the same formula $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$ describes both warming and cooling, and a negative $\Delta T$ gives a negative $\Delta L$.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T - T_0$ (final minus initial).
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is the same in kelvins: $\Delta T = -27\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 18\,{}^\circ\text{C} = -45\,\text{K}$. The result comes out in metres, and $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta L = 23 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{m} \cdot (-45\,\text{K}) = -0.05175\,\text{m} = -51.75\,\text{mm}$
**Answer:** $\Delta L \approx -52\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures. The limiting data are $\alpha = 23 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = -45\,\text{K}$ (both have two figures); the length $50.0\,\text{m}$ has three figures and does not limit the result.
**Reasonableness:** A rough estimate: $2 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50\,\text{m} \cdot 45\,\text{K} \approx 45\,\text{mm}$. The sign is correct because the temperature falls. The wire gets shorter by about $0.10\,\%$, so it must be installed slack in summer so that it does not become too tight in winter.

## fy3-8-L1-03
**Given:** copper tube, $L_0 = 500.0\,\text{mm}$ at the temperature $T_0 = 0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha = 17 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the change in length of the tube $\Delta L$ in millimetres, that is, the change in the reading of the dial gauge
**Principle:** One end of the tube is fixed, so the whole change in length shows at the free end and in the dial gauge. The copper warms up and its state of matter does not change, so the change in length is proportional to the initial length and the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T - T_0$. The new length is $L = L_0 + \Delta L$.
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 100\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. No unit conversion is needed: $L_0$ is in millimetres, so $\Delta L$ also comes out in millimetres.
**Substitution:** $\Delta L = 17 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 500.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 100\,\text{K} = 0.85\,\text{mm}$, and $L = 500.0\,\text{mm} + 0.85\,\text{mm} = 500.85\,\text{mm}$.
**Answer:** $\Delta L = 0.85\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures. The limiting datum is $\alpha = 17 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (two figures); the length $500.0\,\text{mm}$ and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = 100\,\text{K}$ are more precise.
**Reasonableness:** The relative change is $\alpha \Delta T = 17 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 100\,\text{K} = 0.17\,\%$, and $0.17\,\%$ of $500\,\text{mm}$ is $0.85\,\text{mm}$. The tube gets longer because it warms up, and a change of less than a millimetre shows on a dial gauge but not on a ruler.

## fy3-8-L1-04
**Given:** water, $V_0 = 200.0\,\textsf{l}$, $T_0 = 12\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 32\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\gamma = 0.21 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the increase in the volume of the water $\Delta V$ in litres
**Principle:** A liquid expands in all directions, so the change in its volume is proportional to the initial volume and the change in temperature. Water stays liquid below $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the coefficient of volume expansion $\gamma$ describes the situation. The tank does not limit the expansion, because the question is only about the growth of the volume of the water.
**Formula:** $\Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T - T_0$.
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 32\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 12\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 20\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. The litres do not need converting, because $V_0$ is in litres and $\Delta V$ also comes out in litres.
**Substitution:** $\Delta V = 0.21 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 200.0\,\textsf{l} \cdot 20\,\text{K} = 0.84\,\textsf{l}$
**Answer:** $\Delta V = 0.84\,\textsf{l}$, two significant figures. The limiting datum is $\gamma = 0.21 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (two figures); the volume $200.0\,\textsf{l}$ has four figures and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = 20\,\text{K}$ has two figures, so neither limits the result any further.
**Reasonableness:** The relative growth is $\gamma \Delta T = 0.21 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 20\,\text{K} = 0.42\,\%$, and $0.42\,\%$ of $200.0\,\textsf{l}$ is $0.84\,\textsf{l}$. The growth is positive because the water warms up. Almost a litre more would not fit in a tank filled to the brim and closed, so the tank has an overflow or an air space.

**Find:** the change in width $\Delta L$ in millimetres
**Principle:** Glass is a solid, and it expands in all directions in the same proportion when it warms up. The glass does not melt, so the change in width is proportional to the initial width and the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T - T_0$.
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 45\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 25\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. The result comes out in metres, and $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta L = 9.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 1.5\,\text{m} \cdot 25\,\text{K} = 0.0003375\,\text{m} = 0.3375\,\text{mm}$
**Answer:** $\Delta L \approx 0.34\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures. There are three limiting data: $\alpha = 9.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, the width $1.5\,\text{m}$ and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = 25\,\text{K}$ all have two figures.
**Reasonableness:** The relative change is $9.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{K} = 0.0225\,\%$, and $1500\,\text{mm} \cdot 0.000225 = 0.34\,\text{mm}$. The same situation with steel ($\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$) would give about $0.45\,\text{mm}$, so glass expands less than metal, as its smaller $\alpha$ promises.

## fy3-8-L2-01
**Given:** steel pipe, maximum allowance of the bellows section $\Delta L = 50.0\,\text{mm}$, $T_0 = 10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 90\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the greatest length $L_0$ of the straight section in metres
**Principle:** The change in the length of the pipe is proportional to its length and the change in temperature, and the bellows section takes up at most $50.0\,\text{mm}$. Steel stays solid, so the same expansion formula applies. The formula is rearranged for the initial length, because the change is known and the length is asked for.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, from which $L_0 = \dfrac{\Delta L}{\alpha \Delta T}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta L = 50.0\,\text{mm} = 0.0500\,\text{m}$, so that the result comes out in metres. $\Delta T = 90\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 10\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins.
**Substitution:** $L_0 = \dfrac{0.0500\,\text{m}}{12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 80\,\text{K}} = \dfrac{0.0500\,\text{m}}{9.6 \cdot 10^{-4}} = 52.083\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $L_0 \approx 52\,\text{m}$, two significant figures. The limiting data are $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = 80\,\text{K}$ (both have two figures); the allowed change $50.0\,\text{mm}$ has three figures and does not limit the result.
**Reasonableness:** Check forwards: $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 52\,\text{m} \cdot 80\,\text{K} = 0.0499\,\text{m} \approx 50\,\text{mm}$. A larger allowed change or a smaller temperature difference would allow a longer pipe, so the result goes in the right direction. If the millimetres were not converted to metres, the result would be a thousand times too large.

## fy3-8-L2-02
**Given:** steel kettlebell, $V_0 = 2.0\,\text{dm}^3$, $T_0 = -22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the increase in volume $\Delta V$ in cubic centimetres
**Principle:** The kettlebell expands in all three directions in the same proportion, so its volume grows in relative terms about three times as much as its length: $\gamma \approx 3\alpha$. This holds for a solid whose state of matter does not change. The change in volume is proportional to the initial volume and the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $\gamma \approx 3\alpha$ and $\Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$, that is, $\Delta V = 3\alpha V_0 \Delta T$.
**Conversions:** $V_0 = 2.0\,\text{dm}^3 = 2000\,\text{cm}^3$, because $1\,\text{dm}^3 = 1000\,\text{cm}^3$. $\Delta T = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-22\,{}^\circ\text{C}) = 42\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins.
**Substitution:** $\gamma \approx 3 \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} = 36 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, and $\Delta V = 36 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 2000\,\text{cm}^3 \cdot 42\,\text{K} = 3.024\,\text{cm}^3$
**Answer:** $\Delta V \approx 3.0\,\text{cm}^3$, two significant figures. There are three limiting data: the volume $2.0\,\text{dm}^3$, the coefficient $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ and the difference of whole degrees $\Delta T = 42\,\text{K}$ all have two figures. The factor $3$ ($\gamma \approx 3\alpha$) is an exact number and does not limit the result.
**Reasonableness:** The relative growth is $36 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 42\,\text{K} = 0.15\,\%$, and $0.15\,\%$ of $2000\,\text{cm}^3$ is $3.0\,\text{cm}^3$. The volume grows because the kettlebell warms up. The change is so small that you cannot notice it by hand.

## fy3-8-L2-03
**Given:** steel rail, $L_0 = 25.0\,\text{m}$, $T_0 = 12\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, gap $\Delta L = 7.2\,\text{mm}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the temperature $T$ at which the gap closes
**Principle:** The rail gets longer when it warms up, in proportion to its length and its change in temperature. According to the assumption in the problem, the gap closes when the rail has lengthened by the width of the gap, so $\Delta L$ is set equal to the gap and the required change in temperature is solved.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$, from which $\Delta T = \dfrac{\Delta L}{\alpha L_0}$, and finally $T = T_0 + \Delta T$.
**Conversions:** $\Delta L = 7.2\,\text{mm} = 0.0072\,\text{m}$, so that the lengths are in the same unit. The change in temperature $\Delta T$ comes out in kelvins, and it is the same in degrees Celsius, so it is added directly to the temperature $T_0$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta T = \dfrac{0.0072\,\text{m}}{12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 25.0\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{0.0072\,\text{m}}{3.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m/K}} = 24\,\text{K}$, and $T = 12\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 24\,\text{K} = 36\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Answer:** $T = 36\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures. The figures of the change in temperature $\Delta T = 24\,\text{K}$ are limited by the gap $7.2\,\text{mm}$ and the coefficient $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (both have two figures; the length $25.0\,\text{m}$ has three). The sum $12\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 24\,\text{K}$ is in whole degrees, so $36\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ keeps those two figures.
**Reasonableness:** Check: $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 25.0\,\text{m} \cdot 24\,\text{K} = 7.2\,\text{mm}$, so the rail fills the gap. The temperature is above the initial temperature, because the rail gets longer only when it warms up. The neighbouring rail also gets longer, but each rail lengthens in both directions: each edge of the gap moves $\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta L$, so in total the lengthening of one rail, $\Delta L$, is directed towards the gap, and the assumption of the problem holds. A dark rail easily heats above $36\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ in a heatwave.

The points of the graph give the change in temperature and the change in length, and from them $\alpha$, which can be compared with the table.

A fraction of a millimetre per metre of length is typical of metals.

## fy3-8-L3-01
**Given:** steel gear, diameter of the hole $d_0 = 75.000\,\text{mm}$ at the temperature $T_0 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, diameter of the axle $d = 75.090\,\text{mm}$, $\alpha = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the lowest temperature $T$ at which the hole is at least as big as the axle
**Principle:** An object expands in all directions in the same proportion, as when a photograph is enlarged, and so does a hole. The diameter of the hole is a dimension of the object itself, so its change follows the same formula $\Delta d = \alpha d_0 \Delta T$ as a change in length. So the hole does not get smaller even though the material around it expands. When it cools, the hole tries to return to the size $75.000\,\text{mm}$, but the axle is in the way, and the gear is pressed tightly in place.
**Formula:** $\Delta d = \alpha d_0 \Delta T$, from which $\Delta T = \dfrac{\Delta d}{\alpha d_0}$, and $T = T_0 + \Delta T$. Here $\Delta d = d - d_0$.
**Conversions:** All the dimensions are in millimetres, so $\Delta d$ and $d_0$ cancel in the same unit and no conversion is needed. $\Delta T$ comes out in kelvins and is the same as in degrees Celsius.
**Substitution:** $\Delta d = 75.090\,\text{mm} - 75.000\,\text{mm} = 0.090\,\text{mm}$, and $\Delta T = \dfrac{0.090\,\text{mm}}{12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 75.000\,\text{mm}} = \dfrac{0.090}{9.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\text{K} = 100\,\text{K}$. So $T = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 100\,\text{K} = 120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Answer:** $T = 120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures. The difference of the diameters $\Delta d = 0.090\,\text{mm}$ has two figures, because the dimensions are given to the nearest thousandth of a millimetre ($75.000$ and $75.090$), and the coefficient $12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ also has two figures; they limit the result $\Delta T = 100\,\text{K}$ and thus the temperature $120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check: $75.000\,\text{mm} \cdot (1 + 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 100\,\text{K}) = 75.090\,\text{mm}$. The temperature is clearly higher than room temperature but below the melting of steel. In practice the gear is heated a little above $120\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ so that a clearance is left between the hole and the axle.

## fy3-8-L3-02
**Given:** copper and steel strips, $L_0 = 50.0\,\text{mm}$, $T_0 = 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha_\text{Cu} = 17.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, $\alpha_\text{Fe} = 12.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the difference of the lengthenings $\Delta L_\text{Cu} - \Delta L_\text{Fe}$ in micrometres and the direction in which the strip bends
**Principle:** Both strips are solids and lengthen according to the formula $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$. The strips have the same initial length and the same change in temperature, so the difference in length comes only from the difference of the coefficients. Copper lengthens more because its $\alpha$ is greater.
**Formula:** $\Delta L_\text{Cu} - \Delta L_\text{Fe} = (\alpha_\text{Cu} - \alpha_\text{Fe}) L_0 \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T - T_0$.
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. $L_0$ is in millimetres, so the difference comes out in millimetres, and $1\,\text{mm} = 1000\,\mu\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta L_\text{Cu} = 17.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 80\,\text{K} = 0.068\,\text{mm}$ and $\Delta L_\text{Fe} = 12.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 80\,\text{K} = 0.048\,\text{mm}$. The difference is $0.068\,\text{mm} - 0.048\,\text{mm} = 0.020\,\text{mm} = 20\,\mu\text{m}$.
**Answer:** $20\,\mu\text{m}$, two significant figures. The limiting datum is the difference of the coefficients $17.0 - 12.0 = 5.0$ (in the unit $10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$), which has two figures because the coefficients are given to the nearest tenth; $\Delta T = 80\,\text{K}$ also has two figures. If the coefficients were given only as whole numbers, the difference $17 - 12 = 5$ would have one figure.
**Reasonableness:** The same result with a single multiplication: $(17.0 - 12.0) \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 50.0\,\text{mm} \cdot 80\,\text{K} = 0.020\,\text{mm}$. The difference is positive because copper lengthens more. The strips are fastened to each other, so the longer copper stays on the outer edge of the curve and the strip bends towards the steel side. The end of the strip moves much more than $20\,\mu\text{m}$ and opens the electric circuit.

## fy3-8-L3-03
**Given:** petrol $V_0 = 20.0\,\textsf{l}$, $T_0 = 10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T = 35\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $\gamma_\text{petrol} = 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$; steel can $\alpha_\text{steel} = 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (given in the problem)
**Find:** the volume of petrol that overflows $\Delta V_\text{over}$ in cubic centimetres and a justification for why the can is not filled to the brim
**Principle:** Both the petrol and the inner space of the can expand, and the inner volume of the can grows like that of a steel object of the same size ($\gamma \approx 3\alpha$). The can was full, so what overflows is the difference between the growth of the petrol and the growth of the inner volume. Both volumes are calculated with the same formula.
**Formula:** $\Delta V = \gamma V_0 \Delta T$ for both, $\gamma_\text{steel} \approx 3\alpha_\text{steel}$ and $\Delta V_\text{over} = \Delta V_\text{petrol} - \Delta V_\text{can}$.
**Conversions:** $V_0 = 20.0\,\textsf{l} = 20\,000\,\text{cm}^3$, because $1\,\textsf{l} = 1000\,\text{cm}^3$. $\Delta T = 35\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 10\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 25\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins.
**Substitution:** $\Delta V_\text{petrol} = 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 20\,000\,\text{cm}^3 \cdot 25\,\text{K} = 475\,\text{cm}^3$ and $\Delta V_\text{can} = 3 \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 20\,000\,\text{cm}^3 \cdot 25\,\text{K} = 18\,\text{cm}^3$. So $\Delta V_\text{over} = 475\,\text{cm}^3 - 18\,\text{cm}^3 = 457\,\text{cm}^3$.
**Answer:** $\Delta V_\text{over} \approx 460\,\text{cm}^3$, two significant figures. The growth of the petrol $475\,\text{cm}^3$ is limited by the coefficient $\gamma = 0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$ (two figures), so it is known only to the nearest tens of cubic centimetres. In the difference $475 - 18$ the less precise number determines the decimals, so the result is rounded to tens: $460\,\text{cm}^3$. The $\alpha$ of steel and the volume $20.0\,\textsf{l}$ affect only the small part $18\,\text{cm}^3$.
**Reasonableness:** The growth of the petrol is $0.95 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{K} = 2.4\,\%$ and that of the can only $0.09\,\%$, so the difference is almost the whole growth of the petrol, about $0.46\,\textsf{l}$. The expansion of the can is not enough to compensate, because the $\gamma$ of petrol is more than 25 times the $\gamma$ of steel. That is why an air space is left in the can.
