## fy8-10-L1-01
**Given:** mass of the carbon-12 atom $m_\text{atom} = 12.000000\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\,\text{u}$, $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\,\text{u}$; the nucleus ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ has $Z = 6$ protons and $N = 12 - 6 = 6$ neutrons
**Asked:** the mass defect $\Delta m$ in atomic mass units
**Principle:** A nucleus is lighter than its separate nucleons. When the masses of hydrogen atoms are used, they contain six electrons just like the carbon atom, so the masses of the electrons cancel and the difference is the mass defect of the nucleus.
**Formula:** $\Delta m = Z\,m_\text{H} + N\,m_\text{n} - m_\text{atom}$
**Unit conversions:** None needed: all masses are in atomic mass units.
**Substitution:** $\Delta m = 6 \cdot 1.007825\,\text{u} + 6 \cdot 1.008665\,\text{u} - 12.000000\,\text{u} = 6.046950\,\text{u} + 6.051990\,\text{u} - 12.000000\,\text{u} = 0.098940\,\text{u}$
**Answer:** $0.098940\,\text{u}$, six decimals like the given masses.
**Reasonableness:** The mass of the separate nucleons is about $12.10\,\text{u}$, so the mass defect is about $0.8\,\%$ of it, as it usually is for nuclei. The result is positive, because a bound nucleus is lighter than its parts.

## fy8-10-L1-06
**Asked:** what the binding energy of a nucleus means.
**Principle:** A nucleus is lighter than its separate protons and neutrons. By the equivalence of mass and energy, the mass defect $\Delta m$ corresponds to the energy $\Delta m\,c^2$, and this is the binding energy $E_\text{B}$ of the nucleus.
**Model answer:** The binding energy is the energy needed to break the nucleus into separate nucleons. The same energy was released when the nucleons bound together into the nucleus, which is why the nucleus is lighter than its parts. So a nucleus cannot be thought of as an energy store from which energy would be released when it is broken up: breaking it up takes energy. The binding energy is also not the kinetic energy of the nucleons, nor the binding energy of the atom's electrons, which is negligible compared with the binding energy of the nucleus.

**Principle:** The binding energy per nucleon $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ tells how tightly the nucleons are bound on average: the greater the value, the more energy is needed to remove a nucleon. ## fy8-10-L2-07
**Asked:** why no energy is obtained by combining iron nuclei with each other or by splitting them.
**Principle:** A nuclear reaction releases energy when the total binding energy of the nuclei formed is greater than that of the original ones. The number of nucleons is conserved, so the binding energy per nucleon $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ decides: energy is released when the nucleons move up along the binding energy curve.
**Model answer:** Iron-56 is at the peak of the binding energy curve together with nickel-62, and $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ is about $8.8\,\text{MeV}$. For both lighter and heavier nuclei the value is smaller, so in iron the nucleons are bound almost as tightly as in the most tightly bound nuclei at the peak. If an iron nucleus were combined with another iron nucleus or split, the nucleons in the nuclei formed would be on average more loosely bound, so the binding energy would decrease and the reaction would require energy. Chemical stability is irrelevant, because it concerns electrons and not nuclei. Electric repulsion affects only the starting of fusion and not the energy balance, and iron-56 has $26$ protons and $30$ neutrons. With other nuclei a reaction can still release energy: for example, iron-56 and helium-4 can form nickel-60 and release about $6.3\,\text{MeV}$.

**Formula:** $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$ and the energy needed is $E = -Q$, so $E = (m_\text{final} - m_\text{initial})\,c^2$, where $m_\text{final} = m({}^{3}\text{He}) + m_\text{n}$ and $m_\text{initial} = m({}^{4}\text{He})$
**Unit conversions:** $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ is put in place of the unit $\text{u}$, so $c^2$ cancels.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{final} - m_\text{initial} = 3.016029\,\text{u} + 1.008665\,\text{u} - 4.002603\,\text{u} = 0.022091\,\text{u}$, so $E = 0.022091 \cdot 931.5\,\text{MeV} = 20.578\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** $20.58\,\text{MeV}$, four significant figures like the conversion factor. This is almost three times the binding energy per nucleon of helium-4, $7.07\,\text{MeV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The energy must be positive, because a tightly bound nucleus is being broken up. It is greater than the average $7.07\,\text{MeV}$, because the helium-3 that is left is much more loosely bound: its binding energy is only about $7.7\,\text{MeV}$, that is, $2.6\,\text{MeV}$ per nucleon, so removing the neutron takes most of the binding energy $28.3\,\text{MeV}$ of helium-4. This is why helium-4 is exceptionally stable.

## fy8-10-L3-02
**Given:** for deuterium $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A} = 1.112\,\text{MeV}$ ($A = 2$), for helium-4 $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A} = 7.074\,\text{MeV}$ ($A = 4$)
**Asked:** the reaction energy $Q$ in megaelectronvolts
**Principle:** Let us imagine the reaction in two steps: the deuterium nuclei are first broken into nucleons, which requires their binding energy, and the nucleons are then assembled into a helium-4 nucleus, which releases its binding energy. The number of nucleons is conserved, so the energy released is the increase of the binding energy. By the conservation of energy, the intermediate steps do not matter.
**Formula:** $Q = E_\text{B}({}^{4}\text{He}) - 2\,E_\text{B}({}^{2}\text{H})$, where $E_\text{B} = A \cdot \tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$, so $Q = 4 \cdot \tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}({}^{4}\text{He}) - 2 \cdot 2 \cdot \tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}({}^{2}\text{H})$
**Unit conversions:** None needed: all energies are in megaelectronvolts.
**Substitution:** $Q = 4 \cdot 7.074\,\text{MeV} - 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1.112\,\text{MeV} = 28.296\,\text{MeV} - 4.448\,\text{MeV} = 23.848\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** $23.85\,\text{MeV}$, four significant figures like the given binding energies $1.112\,\text{MeV}$ and $7.074\,\text{MeV}$.
**Reasonableness:** From atomic masses the same result: $(2 \cdot 2.014102\,\text{u} - 4.002603\,\text{u})\,c^2 = 0.025601 \cdot 931.5\,\text{MeV} \approx 23.85\,\text{MeV}$. Energy is released because helium-4 is much higher in the graph than deuterium: this is the source of the energy of fusion.

## fy8-10-L3-03
**Given:** mass of the water $m = 1.00\,\text{kg}$, initial temperature $80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, final temperature $20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, specific heat capacity $c_\text{v} = 4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$, speed of light $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Asked:** the decrease $\Delta m$ of the mass of the water, in the unit $10^{-12}\,\text{kg}$
**Principle:** As it cools, the water gives off heat $Q = c_\text{v} m \Delta T$, and its internal energy decreases by the same amount, because the state of matter does not change. The equivalence of mass and energy applies to every change of energy, so the mass decreases by $\tfrac{Q}{c^2}$.
**Formula:** $Q = c_\text{v} m \Delta T$ and $Q = \Delta m\,c^2$, so $\Delta m = \dfrac{c_\text{v} m \Delta T}{c^2}$
**Unit conversions:** $c_\text{v} = 4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$; the change of temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins: $\Delta T = 80.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 60.0\,\text{K}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta m = \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 1.00\,\text{kg} \cdot 60.0\,\text{K}}{(2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2} = \dfrac{2.514 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{J}}{8.988 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 2.797 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $2.80 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{kg}$, three significant figures like the mass, the specific heat capacity and the change of temperature.
**Reasonableness:** The change of mass is about three trillionths of the mass of the water, far below the precision of any balance. This is why the equivalence of mass and energy shows in practice only in nuclear reactions, where the mass defect is almost one per cent.

## fy8-10-L3-04
**Given:** the atomic masses and the $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ values of the table, $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\,\text{u}$, $1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, $1\,\text{u} = 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, $1\,\text{MeV} = 1.602177 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{J}$, mass of the sample $m = 1.00\,\text{g}$
**Asked:** a) and b) the reaction energy $Q$ in two ways, c) the total energy released $E$ in the unit $10^{10}\,\text{J}$
**Principle:** In the reaction the mass number and the charge number are conserved, but the mass decreases, and the decrease in mass is released as energy $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$. The same energy is obtained from the binding energies: the binding energy of a free neutron is zero, so $Q$ is the sum of the binding energies of the nuclei formed minus the binding energy of uranium. When the energy of one nucleus is known, the energy of the sample is obtained by multiplying it by the number of nuclei.
**Formula:** a) $Q = (m_\text{U} + m_\text{n} - m_\text{Ba} - m_\text{Kr} - 3\,m_\text{n})\,c^2$; b) $Q = E_\text{B}(\text{Ba}) + E_\text{B}(\text{Kr}) - E_\text{B}(\text{U})$, where $E_\text{B} = A \cdot \tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$; c) the number of nuclei $N = \dfrac{m}{m_\text{U}}$ and $E = N\,Q$
**Unit conversions:** $931.5\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ is put in place of the unit $\text{u}$, so $c^2$ cancels. The mass of the uranium atom is $m_\text{U} = 235.043930 \cdot 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg} = 3.90302 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$ and $1.00\,\text{g} = 1.00 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$.
**Substitution:** a) $m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final} = 235.043930\,\text{u} - 140.914411\,\text{u} - 91.926156\,\text{u} - 2 \cdot 1.008665\,\text{u} = 0.186033\,\text{u}$, so $Q = 0.186033 \cdot 931.5\,\text{MeV} = 173.29\,\text{MeV}$. b) $Q = 141 \cdot 8.326\,\text{MeV} + 92 \cdot 8.513\,\text{MeV} - 235 \cdot 7.591\,\text{MeV} = 1173.97\,\text{MeV} + 783.20\,\text{MeV} - 1783.89\,\text{MeV} = 173.28\,\text{MeV}$. c) $N = \dfrac{1.00 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}}{3.90302 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}} = 2.562 \cdot 10^{21}$ and $E = 2.562 \cdot 10^{21} \cdot 173.29 \cdot 1.602177 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{J} = 7.113 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $7.11 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{J}$, three significant figures like the mass of the sample $1.00\,\text{g}$. The reaction energy is $173.3\,\text{MeV}$ both ways.
**Reasonableness:** The results a) and b) differ by only $0.01\,\text{MeV}$, because the $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ values of the table are rounded to thousandths and are multiplied by the mass numbers, so the rounding error can grow to at most about $0.2\,\text{MeV}$ ($\pm 0.0005\,\text{MeV} \cdot (141 + 92 + 235)$). The result of part a), calculated from the masses, is more accurate, because the masses are given to six decimals and are direct measurement results, whereas part b) is based on rounded derived values. The mass decreases by $0.186\,\text{u}$, that is, $0.079\,\%$ of the mass of the reactants $236.05\,\text{u}$, and $0.00079 \cdot 1.00 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot c^2 = 7.1 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{J}$ agrees. The energy corresponds to about $2.5$ tonnes of coal, when the heating value of coal is about $29\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$: $7.11 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{J} : 2.9 \cdot 10^{7}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} = 2450\,\text{kg}$.

## fy8-10-L3-05
**Given:** $L = 1.0 \cdot 10^{28}\,\text{W}$ (example data), $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\,\text{u}$, $m({}^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603\,\text{u}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $t = 1\,\text{yr} = 365.25 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$, mass of the star $M = 2.0 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg}$, share of hydrogen $70\,\%$
**Asked:** a) the decrease in mass in a year $\Delta m_\text{yr}$, b) the share that turns into energy $f$, c) the amount of hydrogen turned into helium in a year $m_\text{hydrogen}$ in the unit $10^{20}\,\text{kg}$, d) the share of hydrogen used up in a billion years
**Principle:** Power is energy per unit time, so the energy radiated in a year is $E = L\,t$. By the equivalence of mass and energy, the mass of a radiating star decreases by $\Delta m_\text{yr} = \tfrac{E}{c^2}$. The same decrease in mass arises in reactions where the mass of four hydrogen atoms is greater than the mass of one helium atom, so the amount of hydrogen used up is obtained from the decrease in mass and the share that turns into energy.
**Formula:** a) $\Delta m_\text{yr} = \dfrac{L\,t}{c^2}$; b) $f = \dfrac{4\,m_\text{H} - m({}^{4}\text{He})}{4\,m_\text{H}}$; c) $m_\text{hydrogen} = \dfrac{\Delta m_\text{yr}}{f}$; d) share $= \dfrac{10^{9} \cdot m_\text{hydrogen}}{0.70\,M}$
**Unit conversions:** $t = 365.25 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3.15576 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$ and $1\,\text{W} \cdot 1\,\text{s} = 1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{kg} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}$. The share $f$ is a ratio, so the atomic mass unit cancels.
**Substitution:** a) $\Delta m_\text{yr} = \dfrac{1.0 \cdot 10^{28}\,\text{W} \cdot 3.15576 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}}{(2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2} = \dfrac{3.15576 \cdot 10^{35}\,\text{J}}{8.988 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 3.511 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}$. b) $4\,m_\text{H} = 4.031300\,\text{u}$ and the decrease in mass is $4.031300\,\text{u} - 4.002603\,\text{u} = 0.028697\,\text{u}$, so $f = \dfrac{0.028697\,\text{u}}{4.031300\,\text{u}} = 0.007119 = 0.712\,\%$. c) $m_\text{hydrogen} = \dfrac{3.511 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}}{0.007119} = 4.932 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$. d) There is $0.70 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg} = 1.4 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg}$ of hydrogen, and in a billion years $10^{9} \cdot 4.932 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg} = 4.9 \cdot 10^{29}\,\text{kg}$ is used up, so the share is $\dfrac{4.9 \cdot 10^{29}\,\text{kg}}{1.4 \cdot 10^{31}\,\text{kg}} = 0.035 = 3.5\,\%$.
**Answer:** $4.9 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures like the power $1.0 \cdot 10^{28}\,\text{W}$. The mass decreases by $3.5 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}$ in a year, and only $0.71\,\%$ of the mass of the hydrogen atoms turns into energy.
**Reasonableness:** The power of the star is about $26$ Suns ($3.83 \cdot 10^{26}\,\text{W}$), and the Sun turns about $6 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{kg}$ of hydrogen into helium per second, that is, about $1.9 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{kg}$ per year; multiplying this by $26$ gives about $5 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{kg}$, which agrees with the result. We assume that all the hydrogen is available (in reality only the core fuses, so the share of the hydrogen of the core is greater). In a billion years only $3.5\,\%$ of the supply is used up, so the power does not change much because of the decrease in hydrogen, and the assumption of constant power is reasonable over this time span. The decrease in mass because of radiation, $3.5 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{kg}$, is only $1.8 \cdot 10^{-13}$ of the mass of the star.
