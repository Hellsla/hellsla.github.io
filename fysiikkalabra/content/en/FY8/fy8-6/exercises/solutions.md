## fy8-6-L1-01
**Given:** threshold voltage $U_\text{k} = 1.95\,\text{V}$, elementary charge $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the energy $E$ that the electron receives from the voltage source, in joules
**Principle:** When a charge $q$ passes across a voltage $U$, the electric field does work $qU$ on it. The magnitude of the charge of an electron is the elementary charge $e$, so it receives the energy $eU$ from the voltage source. This is exactly the energy available in the LED for creating a photon.
**Formula:** $E = eU_\text{k}$
**Conversions:** None are needed: $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $E = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1.95\,\text{V} = 3.1239 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $3.12 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, three significant figures like the voltage $1.95\,\text{V}$.
**Reasonableness:** In electronvolts the energy is exactly $1.95\,\text{eV}$, because $1\,\text{eV}$ is the energy that an electron receives in a voltage of $1\,\text{V}$. It is of the order of the energy of a photon of orange light: $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 605\,\text{nm} \approx 2.0\,\text{eV}$.

## fy8-6-L1-02
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 525\,\text{nm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the energy $E$ of the photon in electronvolts
**Principle:** The energy of light is quantised into photons, and the energy of one photon is $E = hf$. The frequency is obtained from the wavelength, because light travels at the speed $c = f\lambda$.
**Formula:** $E = hf$ and $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, so $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda = 525\,\text{nm} = 525 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; the result from joules to electronvolts by dividing by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}$
**Substitution:** $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $E = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{525 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3.7838 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = \dfrac{3.7838 \cdot 10^{-19}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV} = 2.362\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $2.36\,\text{eV}$, three significant figures like the wavelength $525\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** With round numbers $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 525\,\text{nm} \approx 2.4\,\text{eV}$. Green light is in the middle of the visible range, and the energy of its photon is between that of red, about $2.0\,\text{eV}$, and that of violet, about $3.1\,\text{eV}$.

## fy8-6-L1-03
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 650\,\text{nm}$, speed of light $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$
**Asked:** the frequency $f$ of the light in hertz
**Principle:** The light of a laser diode is electromagnetic wave motion that travels at the speed of light. The basic equation of wave motion, $c = f\lambda$, connects the frequency and the wavelength.
**Formula:** $c = f\lambda$, so $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda = 650\,\text{nm} = 650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $f = \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{650 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 4.612 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
**Answer:** $4.61 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, three significant figures like the wavelength $650\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The frequencies of visible light are about $4 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$–$8 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, and red light is at the low-frequency end of the range. An estimate: $3 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} : (6.5 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}) \approx 4.6 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$.

## fy8-6-L1-04
**Given:** energy gap $E_\text{g} = 1.42\,\text{eV}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the wavelength $\lambda$ of the light of the LED in nanometres
**Principle:** In an LED an electron falls from the conduction band into a hole of the valence band and gives up its energy as one photon. The energy of the photon is approximately equal to the energy gap, $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda} \approx E_\text{g}$.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = E_\text{g}$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{E_\text{g}}$
**Conversions:** $E_\text{g} = 1.42\,\text{eV} = 1.42 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 2.2748 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$; the result from metres to nanometres, $1\,\text{m} = 10^{9}\,\text{nm}$
**Substitution:** $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{2.2748 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} = 8.733 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m} = 873.3\,\text{nm}$
**Answer:** $873\,\text{nm}$, three significant figures like the energy gap $1.42\,\text{eV}$.
**Reasonableness:** With round numbers $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 1.42\,\text{eV} \approx 870\,\text{nm}$. The wavelength is over $700\,\text{nm}$, so the light is infrared. That fits, because the energy gap is smaller than the energy of a photon of red light, about $1.8\,\text{eV}$.

## fy8-6-L1-05
**Given:** slope of the fitted line $k = 4.10 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, elementary charge $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the Planck constant $h$ in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Principle:** At the threshold voltage the energy received by an electron is approximately the energy of a photon, $eU_\text{k} = hf$. So the threshold voltage is a line with respect to the frequency, $U_\text{k} = \tfrac{h}{e} f$, whose slope is $\tfrac{h}{e}$.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{h}{e}$, so $h = ek$
**Conversions:** None are needed: $\text{C} \cdot \text{V}\,\text{s} = \text{J}\,\text{s}$, because $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $h = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 4.10 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} = 6.568 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $6.57 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, three significant figures like the slope $4.10 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The accepted value is $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, and the result differs from it by $(6.568 - 6.626) : 6.626 \approx -0.9\,\%$. In a measurement based on threshold voltages a difference of a few per cent is usual, so the result is credible.

## fy8-6-L2-01
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 470\,\text{nm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the smallest voltage, that is the threshold voltage $U_\text{k}$, in volts
**Principle:** An LED emits light only when each electron receives from the voltage source at least approximately the energy of one photon. At the threshold voltage the energy $eU_\text{k}$ of an electron is equal to the energy $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ of a photon.
**Formula:** $eU_\text{k} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so $U_\text{k} = \dfrac{hc}{e\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda = 470\,\text{nm} = 470 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; $1\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{C}} = 1\,\text{V}$
**Substitution:** $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $e\lambda = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 470 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m} = 7.5294 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{C}\,\text{m}$ and $U_\text{k} = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{7.5294 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{C}\,\text{m}} = 2.638\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** about $2.64\,\text{V}$, three significant figures like the wavelength $470\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** With round numbers $1240\,\text{V}\,\text{nm} : 470\,\text{nm} \approx 2.6\,\text{V}$. The voltage is greater than that of a red LED, about $1.9\,\text{V}$, because a photon of blue light needs more energy. The measured threshold voltage is usually about $0.1\,\text{V}$ smaller than this estimate.

## fy8-6-L2-02
**Given:** peak wavelength $\lambda = 605\,\text{nm}$, threshold voltage $U_\text{k} = 1.95\,\text{V}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the value of the Planck constant $h$ from the measurement of one LED, in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Principle:** We assume that at the threshold voltage the energy $eU_\text{k}$ received by an electron is exactly the energy $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ of one photon. The Planck constant can be solved from the equation.
**Formula:** $eU_\text{k} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so $h = \dfrac{eU_\text{k}\lambda}{c}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda = 605\,\text{nm} = 605 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$
**Substitution:** $eU_\text{k} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1.95\,\text{V} = 3.1239 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ and $eU_\text{k}\lambda = 3.1239 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \cdot 605 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m} = 1.8900 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$, so $h = \dfrac{1.8900 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}} = 6.304 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $6.30 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, three significant figures like the voltage and the wavelength.
**Reasonableness:** The result is of the right order of magnitude but $(6.304 - 6.626) : 6.626 \approx -4.9\,\%$ smaller than the accepted value. The difference arises because the measured threshold voltage is slightly smaller than $\tfrac{hf}{e}$. This is why the Planck constant is determined from the slope of the line of many LEDs, which this almost constant difference does not affect.

## fy8-6-L2-03
**Given:** current $I = 20\,\text{mA}$, wavelength $\lambda = 630\,\text{nm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$; every electron creates one photon that gets out
**Asked:** the light power $P$ in milliwatts
**Principle:** The current tells how much charge passes every second, so the number of electrons per second is $N = \tfrac{I}{e}$. By the assumption, the same number of photons is created every second, and each carries away the energy $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. The light power is the energy carried away by the photons per second.
**Formula:** $N = \dfrac{I}{e}$ and $P = N \cdot \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so $P = \dfrac{Ihc}{e\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $I = 20\,\text{mA} = 0.020\,\text{A}$, $\lambda = 630\,\text{nm} = 630 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{W} = 1000\,\text{mW}$
**Substitution:** $N = \dfrac{0.020\,\text{C/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 1.2484 \cdot 10^{17}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, the energy of a photon is $\dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{630 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3.1532 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, so $P = 1.2484 \cdot 10^{17}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot 3.1532 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 0.03937\,\text{W} = 39.4\,\text{mW}$
**Answer:** about $39\,\text{mW}$, two significant figures like the current $20\,\text{mA}$.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of a photon is about $2\,\text{eV}$, so the light power is about $2\,\text{V} \cdot 0.020\,\text{A} = 40\,\text{mW}$, as was obtained. A real LED emits less than this, because not all the electrons create a photon and some of the photons are absorbed inside the LED.

## fy8-6-L2-04
**Given:** wavelength of the blue photon $\lambda_1 = 450\,\text{nm}$, wavelength of the yellow photon $\lambda_2 = 560\,\text{nm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the energy $\Delta E$ left as heat per photon, in electronvolts
**Principle:** In fluorescence (chapter *Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms*) a substance absorbs a photon and emits a photon of lower energy. Energy is conserved, so the difference between the energies of the absorbed and the emitted photon is left in the phosphor as heat.
**Formula:** $\Delta E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda_1} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda_2}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda_1 = 450 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$, $\lambda_2 = 560 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; the energies from joules to electronvolts by dividing by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}$
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_1} = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{450 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 4.4144 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 2.7556\,\text{eV}$ and $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_2} = \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{560 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 3.5473 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 2.2143\,\text{eV}$, so $\Delta E = 2.7556\,\text{eV} - 2.2143\,\text{eV} = 0.5413\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $0.541\,\text{eV}$, three significant figures like the wavelengths.
**Reasonableness:** With round numbers $1240 : 450 - 1240 : 560 \approx 2.76 - 2.21 = 0.55$, that is about $0.5\,\text{eV}$. The emitted photon has a longer wavelength than the absorbed one, so the difference must be positive, as it always is in fluorescence. About a fifth of the energy of the blue photon turns into heat.

## fy8-6-L3-01
**Given:** the peak wavelengths and threshold voltages of four LEDs: $625\,\text{nm}$ and $1.86\,\text{V}$, $590\,\text{nm}$ and $1.97\,\text{V}$, $520\,\text{nm}$ and $2.27\,\text{V}$, $465\,\text{nm}$ and $2.55\,\text{V}$; $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the Planck constant $h$ in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Principle:** At the threshold voltage $eU_\text{k} \approx hf$, so the points $(f, U_\text{k})$ are approximately on a line whose slope is $\tfrac{h}{e}$. The points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty, so the line is fitted to all the points. An almost equal deviation for all the LEDs shifts the line but does not change the slope.
**Reading the graph:** When the frequencies are calculated and the line is fitted in Graphical Analysis Pro with the choices **Curve Fit** and **Linear**, the result box shows $m = 4.212 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{Hz}}$ and $b = -0.164\,\text{V}$.
**Formula:** $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$ and $k = \dfrac{h}{e}$, so $h = ek$
**Conversions:** wavelengths to metres, for example $625\,\text{nm} = 625 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; $\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{Hz}} = \text{V}\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $f_1 = \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{625 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 4.797 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, in the same way $f_2 = 5.081 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, $f_3 = 5.765 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ and $f_4 = 6.447 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$. From the slope of the fit, $h = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 4.212 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} = 6.748 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $6.75 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, three significant figures like the threshold voltages.
**Reasonableness:** A check from the farthest points: $(2.55\,\text{V} - 1.86\,\text{V}) : (1.650 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}) = 4.18 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, close to the value of the fit. The result differs from the accepted value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ by about $+1.8\,\%$, which is a usual accuracy for this method.

## fy8-6-L3-02
**Given:** slope of the steepest line $k_\text{max} = 4.25 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, of the gentlest $k_\text{min} = 3.95 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the uncertainty $\Delta h$ of the Planck constant in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Principle:** Each slope gives a value for the Planck constant, $h = ek$. The steepest and the gentlest line that fit the points bound an *estimated* interval in which the slope probably lies; the uncertainty is estimated as half the width of the interval.
**Formula:** $h_\text{max} = ek_\text{max}$ and $h_\text{min} = ek_\text{min}$, so $\Delta h = \dfrac{h_\text{max} - h_\text{min}}{2} = \dfrac{e\,(k_\text{max} - k_\text{min})}{2}$
**Conversions:** None are needed: $\text{C} \cdot \text{V}\,\text{s} = \text{J}\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $k_\text{max} - k_\text{min} = 4.25 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} - 3.95 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} = 0.30 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, so $\Delta h = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 0.30 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} : 2 = 2.403 \cdot 10^{-35}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $\Delta h \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-35}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the difference of the slopes $0.30 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The middle value is $e \cdot 4.10 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} = 6.57 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, so the result is $h = (6.6 \pm 0.2) \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$. The uncertainty is about $4\,\%$, and the accepted value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ is within this interval.

## fy8-6-L3-03
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 365\,\text{nm}$, current $I = 20\,\text{mA}$, radiated power $P = 15\,\text{mW}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Asked:** the share of the number of photons that get out, of the number of electrons passing through the LED, as a percentage
**Principle:** The radiated power is the energy carried away by the photons per second, so the number of photons per second is the power divided by the energy of one photon. The current tells the number of electrons per second, $\tfrac{I}{e}$. One electron can create at most one photon, so the share is at most $100\,\%$.
**Formula:** $N_\text{f} = \dfrac{P}{hc/\lambda} = \dfrac{P\lambda}{hc}$ and $N_\text{e} = \dfrac{I}{e}$, so the share is $\dfrac{N_\text{f}}{N_\text{e}} = \dfrac{P\lambda e}{hcI}$
**Conversions:** $P = 15\,\text{mW} = 0.015\,\text{W}$, $I = 20\,\text{mA} = 0.020\,\text{A}$, $\lambda = 365 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$; finally the share to a percentage by multiplying by $100\,\%$
**Substitution:** $N_\text{f} = \dfrac{0.015\,\text{J/s} \cdot 365 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}} = 2.756 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$ and $N_\text{e} = \dfrac{0.020\,\text{C/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 1.2484 \cdot 10^{17}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, so $\dfrac{N_\text{f}}{N_\text{e}} = \dfrac{2.756 \cdot 10^{16}}{1.2484 \cdot 10^{17}} = 0.2208 = 22.1\,\%$
**Answer:** about $22\,\%$, two significant figures like the power $15\,\text{mW}$ and the current $20\,\text{mA}$.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of a photon is $1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm} : 365\,\text{nm} \approx 3.4\,\text{eV}$, so if every electron created a photon, the radiated power would be about $3.4\,\text{V} \cdot 0.020\,\text{A} = 68\,\text{mW}$. The measured $15\,\text{mW}$ is about a fifth of that, as was obtained. The share is less than $100\,\%$, as it must be.
