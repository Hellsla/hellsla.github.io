Run it: guide others

You run science workshops and help organise science events. Guiding a real workshop or event is the key evidence in the course.

What kind of events?

The events are agreed with the teacher during the school year. Possible events include, for example:

  • science workshops for primary pupils
  • visits by lower secondary students to the upper secondary school
  • Kiviruukki upper secondary school's open days
  • science festivals and science days
  • science clubs
  • joint events with LUMA centres
  • workshops for international visitors
  • science events planned by the students themselves
  • Kiviruukki's own STEAM and Makerspace events

The list gives possible kinds of events. The teacher tells you which events are on offer this school year.

At every event you know

Before every event, go through these six questions with the teacher and the other tutors. Write the answers on the event preparation checklist.

  1. What am I expected to do?

    Your own role: for example lead tutor, assistant, equipment manager or host. You know when you need to be there and what you do in each step.

  2. How do I prepare?

    You read the workshop card, try the workshop yourself, rehearse the start out loud and find out what kind of group is coming.

  3. What equipment is needed?

    You make an equipment list from the workshop card, set aside spares and check that everything works, the day before and just before the start.

  4. How are the responsibilities shared?

    You agree who guides, who assists, who is in charge of the equipment and who tidies up. The teacher is responsible for supervision and safety.

  5. How is safety ensured?

    You go through the safety checklist, you know where the first aid kit is and whom to contact if something happens.

  6. How is participation documented?

    You log the event, your role and your working time, collect anonymous participant feedback and fill in the tutor's self-assessment on the same day.

Roles at an event

  • Lead tutor

    Starts and ends the workshop, gives the instructions and keeps to the timetable.

  • Assistant

    Goes round the groups, helps those who are stuck and notices who needs support.

  • Equipment manager

    Prepares and hands out the equipment, keeps the spare parts safe and is in charge of tidying up.

  • Host

    Welcomes the group, shows them around and takes care of moving from place to place.

The roles change from event to event. If the events make it possible, everyone gets to try the lead tutor role at least once.

Responsibility

  • The teacher is responsible for proper supervision of the activity and for organising safety.
  • You are never alone the person responsible at an event for minors. The group's own teacher or another responsible adult is always there.
  • No personal data is collected from the participants. Feedback is anonymous, and identifiable photos are not taken or published without permission, following the school's practice.

SAFETY CHECK
============

Workshop: 

Checked together (teacher and tutors): 


IN EVERY WORKSHOP

[ ] The teacher in charge or another responsible adult is there the whole time.
[ ] I know where the first aid kit and the emergency exits are.
[ ] Walkways are clear, and there are no cables or things on the floor.
[ ] The equipment suits the participants' age.
[ ] Participants have been asked about allergies if food, latex or another allergen is used.
[ ] No personal data is collected from participants, and they are not photographed without permission.

ELECTRICITY

[ ] Only batteries or low voltage are used, never mains electricity.
[ ] Button cells are out of children's reach.
[ ] Participants are told not to short-circuit a battery: the wire can get hot.
[ ] Broken, leaking or hot batteries are taken out of use at once.

CHEMICALS AND WATER

[ ] Only substances the teacher has approved. The safety data has been read.
[ ] Safety glasses are worn when a substance can splash.
[ ] Nothing is tasted.
[ ] A wet floor is dried at once.

TOOLS AND LIGHT

[ ] Sharp tools only under guidance, not for young children.
[ ] No laser pointers are used.
[ ] No one looks straight at the sun or a bright lamp.
[ ] Lamps that get hot are placed where no one touches them.

IF SOMETHING HAPPENS

[ ] I stop the activity and call the responsible adult at once.
[ ] If a child may have swallowed a button cell or a magnet, call 112 at once. Do not wait for symptoms.
[ ] I tell the teacher what happened on the same day.

Peer tutoring

Peer tutoring can be part of the course, but it is not its main content. It is agreed with the teacher. Examples:

  • a voluntary maths problem-solving session
  • guiding experimental work in physics or chemistry
  • supporting students who are preparing for competitions
  • guiding small groups
  • organising science demonstrations

The same holds in peer tutoring as in workshops: guide the other person to think, do not do it for them. First ask what they have already tried.

Working time. A workload of about $9 \cdot 75\,\text{min}$ is set aside for preparing and running events. This could be, for example, two or three events with their preparation, or several short workshops. For each event, log the time spent on preparing and on guiding separately.

This part is done when

  • you have taken part in guiding at least one real science workshop or science event
  • you have practised the lead tutor role, if the events make it possible (agree on this with the teacher)
  • for every event, your role, working time, feedback and self-assessment are recorded
