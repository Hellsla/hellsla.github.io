## fy1-2-L1-01
**Given:** thickness of a hair $l = 82\,\mu\text{m}$
**Find:** the thickness $l$ in millimetres
**Principle:** A quantity does not change when its unit is changed; only the numerical value changes. The prefix micro means a factor of $10^{-6}$ and the prefix milli a factor of $10^{-3}$, so $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-3}\,\text{mm}$.
**Formula:** $1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-3}\,\text{mm}$
**Conversions:** In place of the micrometre we write its value in millimetres, $10^{-3}\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $l = 82\,\mu\text{m} = 82\cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{mm} = 0.082\,\text{mm}$
**Answer:** $0.082\,\text{mm}$, two significant figures like the initial value $82\,\mu\text{m}$ (the leading zero is not significant).
**Reasonableness:** A millimetre is a thousand times larger unit than a micrometre, so the numerical value must become a thousand times smaller: from 82 to 0.082. A hair is slightly less than a tenth of a millimetre thick, which fits experience.

**Given:** distance between the Earth and the Moon $s = 384\,400\,\text{km}$; $1\,\text{Mm} = 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

**Principle:** The distance is the same regardless of the unit. The prefix mega is $10^{6}$ and the prefix kilo $10^{3}$, so $1\,\text{Mm} = 10^{3}\,\text{km}$.
**Formula:** $1\,\text{km} = 10^{-3}\,\text{Mm}$
**Conversions:** A megametre is a thousand kilometres, so a kilometre is a thousandth of a megametre. In place of the kilometre we write $10^{-3}\,\text{Mm}$.
**Substitution:** $s = 384\,400\,\text{km} = 384\,400\cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{Mm} = 384.4\,\text{Mm}$
**Answer:** $384.4\,\text{Mm}$. In the tasks of this book the value $384\,400\,\text{km}$, given as a whole number ending in zeros, is exact, and $1\,\text{km} = 10^{-3}\,\text{Mm}$ is exact, so the numerical value is only divided by a thousand and the result is not rounded.
**Reasonableness:** A megametre is a thousand kilometres, and $384\,400\,\text{km}$ is about $400\,000\,\text{km}$, so the result is about $400\,\text{Mm}$. The distance lies between the powers of ten $10^{8}\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{Mm}$ and $10^{9}\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{Mm}$, as it should.

The base units are agreed in the SI system, and other units are derived from them as products and quotients, for example $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$. A prefix does not make a new unit but forms a part or a multiple of a unit.
**Model answer:** The kilogram is the SI base unit of mass. ## fy1-2-L2-01
**Given:** clock frequency $f = 3.2\,\text{GHz}$, time $t = 5.0\,\text{ms}$; $1\,\text{GHz} = 10^{9}\,\text{Hz}$ and $1\,\text{Hz} = 1/\text{s}$
**Find:** the number of clock cycles $N$
**Principle:** The frequency tells how many cycles occur in a second. When the frequency is constant, the number of cycles is the product of the frequency and the time. The frequency and the time must be given in the same unit of time, so both are converted to SI units.
**Formula:** $N = f\,t$
**Conversions:** $f = 3.2\,\text{GHz} = 3.2\cdot10^{9}\,\text{Hz} = 3.2\cdot10^{9}\,\text{1/s}$ and $t = 5.0\,\text{ms} = 5.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $N = 3.2\cdot10^{9}\,\dfrac{1}{\text{s}}\cdot 5.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\text{s} = 16\cdot10^{6} = 1.6\cdot10^{7}$
**Answer:** $16\,000\,000$, that is $1.6\cdot10^{7}$ cycles, two significant figures like the initial values $3.2\,\text{GHz}$ and $5.0\,\text{ms}$.
**Reasonableness:** Three gigahertz is about three billion cycles per second. A millisecond is a thousandth of a second, so in one millisecond there are about three million cycles, and in five about $5\cdot 3 = 15$ million. The result $16$ million agrees with the estimate.

## fy1-2-L3-04
**Given:** $T = k\,L^{a}g^{b}m^{c}$, where $k$ has no unit; $L_1 = 0.250\,\text{m}$, $T_1 = 1.00\,\text{s}$, $L_2 = 1.00\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the exponents $a$, $b$ and $c$ and the period $T_2$ in seconds
**Principle:** Both sides of an equation must have the same unit. The units therefore give conditions for the exponents. After this the form of the formula is known, and the dimensionless coefficient $k$ stays the same when the length of the pendulum changes in the same place, because $k$ is a constant of the swinging phenomenon itself and does not depend on the length or the place. A unit analysis does not, however, give the number $k$: it tells only how $T$ depends on the quantities, not the size of the coefficient.
**Formula:** Units: $\text{s} = \text{m}^{a}\cdot\left(\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\right)^{b}\cdot\text{kg}^{c} = \text{m}^{a+b}\,\text{s}^{-2b}\,\text{kg}^{c}$. The kilogram appears only in the mass, so $c = 0$. The seconds give $-2b = 1$, so $b = -\tfrac{1}{2}$, and the metres $a + b = 0$, so $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Thus $T = k\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$, and since $k$ and $g$ stay the same, $T_2 = T_1\sqrt{\dfrac{L_2}{L_1}}$.
**Conversions:** Not needed: the lengths are in metres and the time in seconds.

**Answer:** $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $b = -\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $c = 0$; $T_2 = 2.00\,\text{s}$, three significant figures like the measurement. The mass does not affect the period. Units do not give the coefficient: for small swings $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$, in which $2\pi$ is a number without a unit that a unit analysis cannot give. It is not needed in this task, because $k$ cancels from the ratio $T_2/T_1$.
**Reasonableness:** The length of the pendulum is four times as great and the period twice as great, because $\sqrt{4} = 2$. The period of a one-metre pendulum is about two seconds, which fits the pendulum of a wall clock.

**Principle:** The numerical values $0.65$ and $2.20$ cannot be compared, because the units $\text{kWh}$ and $\text{MJ}$ are of different sizes. A power bank runs for the time $t = \tfrac{E}{P}$ until the stored energy has been used up. Energy and power are therefore converted to the units J and W.
**Formula:** $t = \dfrac{E}{P}$
**Conversions:** In place of the kilowatt-hour we write $1000\,\text{W}\cdot3600\,\text{s}$, that is $1\,\text{kWh} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$. $E_\text{A} = 0.65\cdot1000\,\text{W}\cdot3600\,\text{s} = 2.34\cdot10^{6}\,\text{J}$, $E_\text{B} = 2.20\cdot10^{6}\,\text{J}$ and $P_\text{B} = 0.100\cdot1000\,\text{W} = 100\,\text{W}$. Seconds are converted to hours, $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $t_\text{A} = \dfrac{2.34\cdot10^{6}\,\text{J}}{85.0\,\text{W}} = 2.753\cdot10^{4}\,\text{s} = 7.647\,\text{h}$ and $t_\text{B} = \dfrac{2.20\cdot10^{6}\,\text{J}}{100\,\text{W}} = 2.20\cdot10^{4}\,\text{s} = 6.111\,\text{h}$. The difference is $7.647\,\text{h} - 6.111\,\text{h} = 1.54\,\text{h} \approx 1.5\,\text{h}$
**Answer:** power bank A runs longer, about $1.5\,\text{h}$, two significant figures like the energy $0.65\,\text{kWh}$. The energy of power bank B is only $0.61\,\text{kWh}$, and its power is greater. So the claim is not correct.
**Reasonableness:** The energy of power bank A, $650\,\text{Wh}$, divided by an estimate of $90\,\text{W}$ for the power $85\,\text{W}$ gives about $7\,\text{h}$. The energy of power bank B is $2.2:3.6 = 0.61\,\text{kWh}$, and $610\,\text{Wh} : 100\,\text{W}$ is about $6\,\text{h}$.
