## fy7-4-L1-01
**Given:** length of the wire in the field $l = 0.40\,\text{m}$, current $I = 12\,\text{A}$, magnetic flux density $B = 0.050\,\text{T}$, wire perpendicular to the field, so $\alpha = 90^\circ$
**Find:** force $F$ on the wire in newtons
**Principle:** A magnetic field exerts a force on a current-carrying wire, because a magnetic force acts on the charges moving in the wire. The field is uniform and the wire is straight, so the formula $F = I l B \sin\alpha$ can be used for the whole piece of wire.
**Formula:** $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, and when $\alpha = 90^\circ$, $F = I l B$
**Conversions:** None needed: all quantities are in SI units, and $\text{A} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{T} = \text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $F = 12\,\text{A} \cdot 0.40\,\text{m} \cdot 0.050\,\text{T} = 0.24\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $0.24\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** The force is about the weight of a $25\,\text{g}$ object, which suits a laboratory magnet: the wire swings clearly, but the force is small. Doubling the current would double the force.

## fy7-4-L1-02
**Given:** $l = 15\,\text{cm}$, $I = 8.0\,\text{A}$, $B = 0.25\,\text{T}$, angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 40^\circ$
**Find:** force $F$ on the piece of wire in newtons
**Principle:** The wire is oblique to the field, so only the component of the field perpendicular to the wire, $B \sin\alpha$, produces a force. The field is uniform, so the force is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$.
**Formula:** $F = I l B \sin\alpha$
**Conversions:** $l = 15\,\text{cm} = 15 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.15\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $F = 8.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.15\,\text{m} \cdot 0.25\,\text{T} \cdot \sin 40^\circ = 0.30\,\text{N} \cdot 0.6428 = 0.1928\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $0.19\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** For a perpendicular wire the force would be $0.30\,\text{N}$. For an oblique wire it must be smaller, and $\sin 40^\circ \approx 0.64$ reduces it to about two thirds. With the cosine the result would wrongly be $0.23\,\text{N}$.

## fy7-4-L1-03
**Given:** $l = 6.0\,\text{cm}$, $I = 4.5\,\text{A}$, $F = 27\,\text{mN}$, wire perpendicular to the field, so $\alpha = 90^\circ$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ in teslas
**Principle:** The force that a field exerts on a straight current-carrying wire is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. When the force, the current and the length are known, the magnitude of the field can be solved from the formula. A current balance is thus a way of measuring a magnetic field.
**Formula:** $F = I l B$, so $B = \dfrac{F}{I l}$
**Conversions:** $l = 6.0\,\text{cm} = 6.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.060\,\text{m}$ and $F = 27\,\text{mN} = 27 \cdot 0.001\,\text{N} = 0.027\,\text{N}$
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{0.027\,\text{N}}{4.5\,\text{A} \cdot 0.060\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{0.027\,\text{N}}{0.27\,\text{A}\cdot\text{m}} = 0.10\,\text{T}$
**Answer:** $0.10\,\text{T}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** Between the poles of a horseshoe magnet the field is typically from hundredths to tenths of a tesla, so $0.10\,\text{T}$ is plausible. Check: $4.5\,\text{A} \cdot 0.060\,\text{m} \cdot 0.10\,\text{T} = 0.027\,\text{N}$.

## fy7-4-L1-04
**Given:** $l = 100\,\text{m}$, $I = 200\,\text{A}$, the Earth's field $B = 53\,\mu\text{T}$, angle between the wire and the field $\alpha = 74^\circ$
**Find:** force $F$ that the Earth's magnetic field exerts on the piece of wire, in newtons
**Principle:** Over a region the length of the wire the Earth's magnetic field is practically uniform, so the force is $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. The wire runs north–south as shown by a compass, and the field points towards magnetic north at $74^\circ$ to the horizontal, so the angle between the wire and the field is $74^\circ$.
**Formula:** $F = I l B \sin\alpha$
**Conversions:** $B = 53\,\mu\text{T} = 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$
**Substitution:** $F = 200\,\text{A} \cdot 100\,\text{m} \cdot 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot \sin 74^\circ = 1.06\,\text{N} \cdot 0.9613 = 1.0189\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $1.0\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the magnetic flux density $53\,\mu\text{T}$.
**Reasonableness:** The force is about the weight of a $100\,\text{g}$ object over the whole $100\,\text{m}$, which is negligible compared with the weight of the wire: a power line carrying $200\,\text{A}$ weighs about $1\,\text{kg}$ per metre, so $100\,\text{m}$ of wire weighs about $1000\,\text{N}$. The Earth's field is weak, so the result is as expected.

## fy7-4-L1-05
**Given:** $l = 5.0\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.20\,\text{T}$, $F = 0.030\,\text{N}$, wire perpendicular to the field, so $\alpha = 90^\circ$
**Find:** current $I$ in the wire in amperes
**Principle:** The field exerts on a current-carrying wire a force $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, which is directly proportional to the current. When the force, the length and the field are known, the current can be solved.
**Formula:** $F = I l B$, so $I = \dfrac{F}{l B}$
**Conversions:** $l = 5.0\,\text{cm} = 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.050\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{0.030\,\text{N}}{0.050\,\text{m} \cdot 0.20\,\text{T}} = \dfrac{0.030\,\text{N}}{0.010\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}} = 3.0\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** A current of a few amperes is usual for a laboratory power supply. Units: $\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{T}\cdot\text{m}} = \tfrac{\text{N}}{\frac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{m}}\cdot\text{m}} = \text{A}$.

## fy7-4-L2-01
**Given:** currents of the wires $I_1 = I_2 = 10\,\text{A}$ in the same direction, distance $r = 5.0\,\text{mm}$, length of the parallel part $l = 0.80\,\text{m}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** force $F$ between the wires in millinewtons
**Principle:** The magnetic field of wire $1$ circles around it, so at wire $2$ it is perpendicular to wire $2$. The field at wire $2$ is the same along the whole length, because the distance stays the same. A force $F = I_2 l B_1$ therefore acts on wire $2$. Currents in the same direction attract each other.
**Formula:** $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ and $F = I_2 l B_1$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0\,\text{mm} = 5.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m} = 0.0050\,\text{m}$. Finally $1\,\text{mN} = 0.001\,\text{N}$.
**Substitution:** $B_1 = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 10\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.0050\,\text{m}} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T}$ and $F = 10\,\text{A} \cdot 0.80\,\text{m} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T} = 0.0032\,\text{N} = 3.2\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $3.2\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures like the distance and the length. The wires attract each other.
**Reasonableness:** The field at wire $2$ is $B_1 = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{T}$, less than a tenth of the field of a fridge magnet, so the force is small, about the weight of a $0.3\,\text{g}$ object. The force grows if the wires are brought closer together.

## fy7-4-L2-02
**Given:** length of the wire in the field $l = 4.0\,\text{cm}$, wire perpendicular to the field; in the graph the change $\Delta m$ of the balance reading as a function of the current $I$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ in teslas
**Principle:** The field exerts on the wire a force $F = I l B$, and by Newton's third law the wire exerts an equal and opposite force on the magnet. The reading of the balance therefore changes: the change $\Delta m$ corresponds to a force $F = \Delta m \cdot g$. The line passes through the origin, because without current there is no force.
**Reading the graph:** The data points deviate slightly from the line because of measurement uncertainty, so we use the line fitted to the points, which smooths out the deviations. The line passes through the origin and the grid point ($5.0\,\text{A}$; $1.50\,\text{g}$). We therefore read the current $I = 5.0\,\text{A}$ and the corresponding change $\Delta m = 1.50\,\text{g}$ from the line, not from a single data point: the last data point, $1.47\,\text{g}$, is slightly below the line.
**Formula:** $\Delta m \cdot g = I l B$, so $B = \dfrac{\Delta m \cdot g}{I l}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta m = 1.50\,\text{g} = 1.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.00150\,\text{kg}$ and $l = 4.0\,\text{cm} = 4.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.040\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{0.00150\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.040\,\text{m}} = \dfrac{0.014715\,\text{N}}{0.20\,\text{A}\cdot\text{m}} = 0.0736\,\text{T}$
**Answer:** $0.074\,\text{T}$, two significant figures like the length $4.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The value is typical of a horseshoe magnet, of the order of a tenth of a tesla. Check with another point of the line: at $3.0\,\text{A}$ the line gives $0.90\,\text{g}$, and $0.00090\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 : (3.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.040\,\text{m}) = 0.074\,\text{T}$.

## fy7-4-L2-03
**Given:** $B = 0.40\,\text{T}$, $l = 0.50\,\text{m}$, $I = 5.0\,\text{A}$, $F = 0.50\,\text{N}$
**Find:** angle $\alpha$ between the wire and the field in degrees
**Principle:** The force depends on the angle between the wire and the field: $F = I l B \sin\alpha$. The greatest possible force, $I l B$, would be obtained with a perpendicular wire, so the ratio of the measured force to the greatest force gives the sine of the angle.
**Formula:** $F = I l B \sin\alpha$, so $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{F}{I l B}$ and $\alpha = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{F}{I l B}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in SI units, and the expression for the sine has no unit.
**Substitution:** $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{0.50\,\text{N}}{5.0\,\text{A} \cdot 0.50\,\text{m} \cdot 0.40\,\text{T}} = \dfrac{0.50\,\text{N}}{1.0\,\text{N}} = 0.50$, so $\alpha = \sin^{-1} 0.50 = 30^\circ$
**Answer:** $30^\circ$, to two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** For a perpendicular wire the force would be $1.0\,\text{N}$. The measured force is half of that, and half of the greatest force is obtained at the angle $30^\circ$, because $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$.

## fy7-4-L2-04
**Given:** distance between the rails, that is the part of the rod in the path of the current, $l = 8.0\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.30\,\text{T}$ vertical, $I = 15\,\text{A}$, mass of the rod $m = 20\,\text{g}$, no friction
**Find:** acceleration $a$ of the rod in the unit $\text{m/s}^2$
**Principle:** The current flows through the rod from one rail to the other, so the $l = 8.0\,\text{cm}$ part of the rod is a current-carrying wire perpendicular to the vertical field. The force $F = I l B$ is horizontal and along the rails. The weight and the normal force of the rails cancel each other and there is no friction, so by Newton's second law $F = m a$.
**Formula:** $I l B = m a$, so $a = \dfrac{I l B}{m}$
**Conversions:** $l = 8.0\,\text{cm} = 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.080\,\text{m}$ and $m = 20\,\text{g} = 20 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.020\,\text{kg}$
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{15\,\text{A} \cdot 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot 0.30\,\text{T}}{0.020\,\text{kg}} = \dfrac{0.36\,\text{N}}{0.020\,\text{kg}} = 18\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $18\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** The acceleration is almost twice the acceleration of free fall, which is plausible for a light rod acted on by a force equal to the weight of a $36\,\text{g}$ object. In reality friction would reduce the acceleration.

## fy7-4-L3-01
**Given:** mass of the rod $m = 12\,\text{g}$, length in the field $l = 20\,\text{cm}$, $B = 0.25\,\text{T}$, rod perpendicular to the horizontal field; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** current $I$ at which the wires go slack
**Principle:** The wires are slack when their tension is zero. Then only the weight, downwards, and the magnetic force act on the rod. The rod is at rest, so by Newton's first law the forces must cancel: the magnetic force must point upwards and equal the weight. The field is horizontal and the rod is perpendicular to it, so the force $I l B$ is vertical; the direction of the current is chosen so that the force points upwards.
**Formula:** $I l B = m g$, so $I = \dfrac{m g}{l B}$
**Conversions:** $m = 12\,\text{g} = 12 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.012\,\text{kg}$ and $l = 20\,\text{cm} = 20 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.20\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{0.012\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.20\,\text{m} \cdot 0.25\,\text{T}} = \dfrac{0.11772\,\text{N}}{0.050\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}} = 2.354\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $2.4\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** A current of a few amperes is enough to support a light rod, which suits a laboratory experiment. If the direction of the current were reversed, the force would point downwards and the wires would tighten: the tension would then be twice the weight.

## fy7-4-L3-02
**Given:** distance between the busbars $r = 0.30\,\text{m}$, currents $I_1 = I_2 = 20\,\text{kA}$ in opposite directions, length considered $l = 1.0\,\text{m}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** force $F$ on a $1.0\,\text{m}$ length of a busbar and its direction; the force is given as positive if the busbars attract each other and negative if they repel each other
**Principle:** Each busbar is in the magnetic field of the other. The field of one busbar at the other is perpendicular to it and the same along its whole length, so the force is $F = I_2 l B_1$. Currents in opposite directions repel each other, so the force pushes the busbars apart.
**Formula:** $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r}$ and $F = I_2 l B_1$
**Conversions:** $I_1 = I_2 = 20\,\text{kA} = 20 \cdot 1000\,\text{A} = 20\,000\,\text{A}$
**Substitution:** $B_1 = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 20\,000\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.30\,\text{m}} = 0.013\,333\,\text{T}$ and $F = 20\,000\,\text{A} \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} \cdot 0.013\,333\,\text{T} = 266.7\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** The magnitude of the force is $270\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the current and the distance. The busbars repel each other, so by the sign rule of the task the answer is $-270\,\text{N}$.
**Reasonableness:** The field at the other busbar is $B_1 = 0.013\,\text{T}$ and the current is very large, so the force per metre corresponds to the weight of a $27\,\text{kg}$ object. This is why the busbars of a substation are fixed firmly: a short circuit could otherwise bend them.

## fy7-4-L3-03
**Given:** length of the wire $l = 1.0\,\text{m}$, mass $m = 2.7\,\text{g}$, wire horizontal and east–west by the compass; the Earth's field $B = 53\,\mu\text{T}$ towards magnetic north and downwards at $74^\circ$ to the horizontal; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** current $I$ in kiloamperes
**Principle:** Split the field into a horizontal and a vertical component. The wire runs east–west by the compass, so it is perpendicular to the horizontal component, which points to magnetic north, and also perpendicular to the vertical component. The vertical component exerts on the wire a horizontal force, which does not support the wire. The horizontal component $B_\text{horizontal} = B \cos 74^\circ$ points to magnetic north, and by the right-hand rule a current flowing east experiences from it a vertical force upwards. The wire stays in place when this force equals the weight.
**Formula:** $I l B \cos 74^\circ = m g$, so $I = \dfrac{m g}{l B \cos 74^\circ}$
**Conversions:** $m = 2.7\,\text{g} = 2.7 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 0.0027\,\text{kg}$ and $B = 53\,\mu\text{T} = 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$. Finally $1\,\text{kA} = 1000\,\text{A}$.
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{0.0027\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.0\,\text{m} \cdot 53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot \cos 74^\circ} = \dfrac{0.026487\,\text{N}}{1.4609 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}\cdot\text{m}} = 1813\,\text{A} = 1.813\,\text{kA}$
**Answer:** $1.8\,\text{kA}$, two significant figures like the data.
**Reasonableness:** The required current is enormous: a thin wire would melt at once. The result shows how weak the Earth's magnetic field is, about $0.05\,\text{mT}$, compared with a tenth of a tesla for a laboratory magnet. A compass needle still turns, because it is a magnet and not a current-carrying wire.
