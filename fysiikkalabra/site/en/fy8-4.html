Light and electrons as waves and particles

Why does very weak light or an electron beam draw first random single points on the screen, from which a fringe pattern arises only after thousands of hits?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain how the fringe pattern of the double-slit experiment arises from the hits of single photons and electrons.
  • describe the photon with different models and explain the Compton effect using the energy and momentum of the photon.
  • calculate the momentum of a photon and the number of photons of light per second.

Prior knowledge

  • Two waves strengthen or weaken each other depending on the path difference between themchapter When waves combine: superposition and interference
  • The energy of a photon is $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, and one photon can release at most one electron from a metalchapter The photoelectric effect and the photon
  • When an atom moves to a lower energy level, it emits a photon whose energy is the difference between the levelschapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 2.

1Think about it

In 1989 a Japanese research group sent electrons one at a time towards a device equivalent to a double slit and filmed the screen. The first electrons appeared as single points of light that flashed up at seemingly random places. When tens of thousands of points had accumulated, regular fringes could be seen on the screen. The same experiment can be done with light so weak that there is usually only one photon in the apparatus at a time.

Does each electron or photon pass through only one of the slits, and if it does, where do the fringes come from?

Review: the photoelectric effect

A metal plate is illuminated with light that releases electrons from it. The wavelength is kept the same, but the intensity of the light is doubled.What happens?

  • The energy of a photon is $hf$, and it depends only on the frequency. Increasing the intensity increases the number of photons, not the energy of a single photon.

  • Correct. Double the intensity means double the number of photons per second, and one photon releases at most one electron. The photon energy $hf$ does not change (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon). In this lesson, we look at how photons behave when there are very few of them.

  • The maximum kinetic energy depends on the energy $hf$ of one photon, not on the amount of light (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon). The same wavelength means the same photon energy, so each electron receives as much energy as before.

  • The intensity does have an effect: it tells how many photons hit the plate per second. That is why more electrons are released, although the maximum kinetic energy of each stays the same.

Assumptions of the model

The hit positions are drawn from the probability distribution of the model, so the numbers are values of the model, not measurements. The drawing starts from the same point after every clearing, so the same experiment always gives the same numbers. The patterns of photons and electrons are drawn to the same scale to make comparison easier; in real experiments the fringe spacing is quite different. The width and the distance of the slits are drawn enlarged. The path measurement is ideal: it always tells the right slit. There is only one quantum in the apparatus at a time, and no quanta are lost on the way. The faint wave illustrates the probability wave and is not a measurable quantity.

How the calculation works

When both slits are open and the path is not measured, the probability of hits at the point $x$ is proportional to the intensity of the wave model

$$I(x) \propto \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{s}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\sin(\pi x / a)}{\pi x / a}\right)^2$$

where the fringe spacing is $s = 2.0\,\text{mm}$ and the first zero of the one-slit pattern is at $a = 10\,\text{mm}$. With one slit or a measured path the first factor is missing, so there are no fringes. A dark fringe is where the $\cos^2$ factor is zero: $x = \pm 1.0\,\text{mm}, \pm 3.0\,\text{mm}, \ldots$. The share is $n : N$, where $n$ is the number of hits in the counting interval and $N$ the total number of hits. The dashed line is the expected number of hits: $N$ times the probability of the interval.

Model answer

With one slit, $43$ photons out of $1000$ hit the counting interval $x_0 = 1.0\,\text{mm}$, but when both slits were open, only $3$. If the photons passed through one of the slits as bullets, the distribution would be the sum of two one-slit patterns, and the share would stay the same as with one slit. Both slits affect every quantum: its wave passes through both slits, and at this point the waves weaken each other. The electrons behaved in the same way. When the path was measured, the dark fringe disappeared ($43$ hits), because information about the path removes the interference. A single hit is a random point, but the intensity of the wave tells the probability of a hit at each place. The numbers are values of the model.

    Compton's scattering experiment

    In 1923 Arthur Compton directed X-rays at graphite and measured the wavelength $\lambda'$ of the radiation scattered in different directions. The wavelength of the incident radiation was $\lambda = 0.0711\,\text{nm}$. The values in the table have been calculated from Compton's model, and Compton's measurements followed them.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Wave or particle?

    In the double-slit experiment, light passes through two narrow, closely spaced slits to a screen. A pattern of bright and dark fringes arises on the screen, which the wave model explains: the waves from the two slits strengthen each other where the path difference is a multiple of the wavelength, and weaken each other where the path difference is half a wavelength, one and a half wavelengths or another odd multiple of half a wavelength (chapter When waves combine: superposition and interference). In the photoelectric effect, however, light gives up its energy in quanta: one photon, whose energy is $hf$, can release at most one electron (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon). Both observations are true. How can the same light be both?

    One quantum at a time

    When the double-slit experiment is done with light so weak that there is on average less than one photon in the apparatus at a time, a sensitive detector registers each photon as a single point. The position of a single point cannot be predicted. When thousands of points have accumulated, they form the same fringe pattern as strong light. The same happens with electrons: in a double-slit experiment with an electron beam, each electron hits the screen at one place, but the hits pile up into fringes. So the electron, which we are used to thinking of as a small particle, behaves like a wave as it travels, and the photon, which the wave model describes well, is detected like a particle.

    The wave gives the probability

    The wave model does not tell where a single photon or electron will hit. It tells the probability with which the quantum is detected at each place: where the intensity of the wave is large, many hits arrive, and in the dark fringes hardly any. When only one slit is open, the fringes of the two slits disappear. When both slits are open, fewer hits arrive in the dark fringes than with one slit, so opening the second slit reduces the hits at these places. This cannot be explained by each quantum passing through only one slit like a small bullet. If the experiment measures through which slit each quantum passes, the fringe pattern disappears and the distribution is the sum of two one-slit patterns.

    The phenomenon in which the same object has both wave and particle properties is called wave-particle duality. It does not mean that a photon or an electron changes its form. Neither classical model, wave or bullet, describes a quantum object alone, and the experimental arrangement decides which property shows. While travelling, a quantum object behaves like a wave, and when it is detected, it is registered at one place like a particle.

    Four models of the photon

    The photon is described in physics with several models. Each works in its own range of phenomena, and none of them alone is the whole picture.

    • State of the field: light is an oscillation of the electric and magnetic field that propagates as a wave. The model explains interference and diffraction.
    • Quantum of the field: the energy of the radiation field changes only in whole quanta $hf$. The model explains the photoelectric effect and spectral lines.
    • Quantum particle: the photon is detected at one place, and it carries energy and momentum. The model explains single hits and the Compton effect.
    • Mediator of an interaction: the electromagnetic interaction between electric charges is mediated by photons. The model belongs to the Standard Model of particle physics.

    A photon has no rest mass, and in a vacuum it always travels at the speed of light $c$. So it is not a small bullet that could be at rest.

    Photon momentum

    Although a photon has no mass, it has a momentum that depends on its energy:

    $$p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}$$

    where $p$ is the momentum of the photon ($\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$), $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ the energy of the photon ($\text{J}$), $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$ the speed of light, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ the Planck constant and $\lambda$ the wavelength ($\text{m}$). The unit works out: $\tfrac{\text{J}\,\text{s}}{\text{m}} = \tfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \cdot \text{s}}{\text{m}} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. The momentum of a photon of visible light is small: at the wavelength $500\,\text{nm}$ it is ${1.3 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}$. The momentum of an X-ray photon of wavelength $0.0711\,\text{nm}$ is about $7000$ times as large, ${9.3 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}$. A short wavelength thus means a large energy and a large momentum.

    The Compton effect

    In 1923 Arthur Compton directed X-rays at graphite and measured the wavelength of the scattered radiation. The wavelength of the scattered radiation was longer than that of the incident radiation, and the difference grew as the scattering angle grew. This is called the Compton effect. The wave model does not explain it: electrons oscillating in time with the wave would radiate at the same frequency as the incident radiation. In the photon model the phenomenon is a collision. The photon gives part of its energy and momentum to an almost free electron, which starts to move. Energy is conserved:

    $$hf = hf' + E_\text{k}$$

    where $hf$ is the energy of the incident photon, $hf'$ the energy of the scattered photon and $E_\text{k}$ the kinetic energy received by the electron. Because $hf' < hf$, the frequency of the scattered photon is lower and its wavelength $\lambda'$ longer than those of the incident one. Momentum is also conserved as a vector quantity: $\overline{p} = \overline{p}' + \overline{p}_e$, where $\overline{p}_e$ is the momentum of the electron.

    Photon flow

    The power $P$ of light tells how much energy the light carries per second. Because each photon brings the energy $hf$, the number of photons passing per second is

    $$\frac{N}{t} = \frac{P}{hf}$$

    where $N$ is the number of photons, $t$ the time ($\text{s}$), $P$ the power ($\text{W}$) and $hf$ the energy of one photon ($\text{J}$). In ordinary light there are so many photons that the light looks like a continuous stream. Only in very weak light do single photons stand out.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the fringe pattern of the double-slit experiment arises when photons or electrons collide with each other, or that each quantum passes through just one of the slits like a small bullet. In the weak light of the example the photons arrive one at a time, and the pattern still arises. When the same total number of hits is counted, in the bullet model the share $n : N$ of hits at a given place would stay the same as with one slit, because the second slit would only add hits (if the source emits the same number of quanta per second for the same time). In reality the share of hits in the dark fringes is clearly smaller than with one slit.

    Another mistake is to think that a photon has no momentum because it has no mass. In the Compton effect the photon gives the electron both energy and momentum, and light that is reflected from a mirror or absorbed by a surface pushes on it. The momentum of a photon is $p = \tfrac{h}{\lambda}$.

    A third mistake is to think that wave-particle duality means that the object is sometimes a wave and sometimes a particle. A quantum object is the same all the time: the wave model gives the probability of detection, and when it is detected, the quantum is registered at one place.

    Summary

    • In the double-slit experiment, single photons and electrons hit the screen one at a time at random places, but thousands of hits form the fringe pattern of the wave model.
    • The wave gives the probability: where the intensity of the wave is large, many hits arrive. If it is measured through which slit the quantum passes, the fringes disappear.
    • Wave-particle duality: photons and electrons have both wave and particle properties, and the experimental arrangement decides which shows.
    • The photon is described by four models: state of the field, quantum of the field, quantum particle and mediator of an interaction.
    • The momentum of a photon is $p = \tfrac{E}{c} = \tfrac{h}{\lambda}$. In the Compton effect the photon gives the electron energy and momentum, so the wavelength of the scattered photon is longer: $hf = hf' + E_\text{k}$.
    • The number of photons per second is $\tfrac{N}{t} = \tfrac{P}{hf}$.

      Example 1: Photons in a double slit

      In a double-slit experiment, red laser light of wavelength $633\,\text{nm}$ is used. The light is dimmed with filters so that the power of the light arriving at the slits is $1.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{W}$. How many photons arrive at the slits per second, and how long is the distance between successive photons on average? Is there usually more than one photon at a time in an apparatus of length $1.2\,\text{m}$?

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. ${\lambda = 633\,\text{nm}}$, ${P = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{W}}$, ${h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}$ and ${c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}$. We are asked for the number of photons per second $\tfrac{N}{t}$ and the average distance $d$ between successive photons.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The energy of light travels as photons, so the power is the total energy of the photons arriving per second: $P = \tfrac{N}{t} \cdot hf$. The photons travel at the speed $c$. In one second the light advances the distance $c$, and $\tfrac{N}{t}$ photons fit in this distance, so the average gap is the distance divided by the number of photons.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} \frac{N}{t} &= \frac{P}{hf}, \quad hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \\ d &= \frac{c}{N/t} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The wavelength in metres: $633\,\text{nm} = 633 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. The power is already in watts, that is, joules per second.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate the energy of one photon, then the number of photons per second and finally the gap between them:

      $$\begin{aligned} hf &= \frac{hc}{\lambda} \\ &= \frac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{633 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} \\ &\quad \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \\ &= 3.138 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \\[6pt] \frac{N}{t} &= \frac{P}{hf} \\ &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{W}}{3.138 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \\ &= 3.187 \cdot 10^{5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \\[6pt] d &= \frac{c}{N/t} \\ &= \frac{2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{3.187 \cdot 10^{5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}} \\ &= 940.8\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. About $3.2 \cdot 10^5$ photons arrive at the slits per second, and the gap between successive photons is on average about $9.4 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$. Two significant figures, because the power $1.0 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{W}$ is known to two figures. The length of the apparatus, $1.2\,\text{m}$, is only about a thousandth ($0.13\,\%$) of the gap between the photons, so there is usually at most one photon in the apparatus at a time.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. On average there are $1.2\,\text{m} : 940.8\,\text{m} \approx 0.0013$ photons in the apparatus, so most of the time there is no photon at all and two at once is rare. An ordinary $1\,\text{mW}$ laser pointer emits about $3 \cdot 10^{15}$ photons per second, so the light has been dimmed by a factor of about $10^{10}$, which dark filters can do. The fringe pattern therefore cannot arise from interaction between the photons.

      Example 2: Find the error

      A student calculates the momentum of a photon of green light when the wavelength is $520\,\text{nm}$:

      $$\begin{aligned} p &= \frac{h}{\lambda} \\ &= \frac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{520} \\ &= 1.27 \cdot 10^{-36}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s} \end{aligned}$$

      Where is the error?

      • A photon has no mass, but it has the momentum $p = \tfrac{h}{\lambda}$. The transfer of momentum can be seen, for example, in the Compton effect. The formula is correct; the error is in the substitution.

      • $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ is the energy $E$ of the photon, whose unit is the joule. The momentum is $p = \tfrac{E}{c} = \tfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so the speed of light cancels out.

      • Correct. In SI units $\lambda = 520 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$, so $p = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}{520 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 1.27 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. The student's result is a billion times too small, and the unit would have revealed the error: $\tfrac{\text{J}\,\text{s}}{\text{nm}}$ is not $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      A solar sail is a thin, mirror-like film that sunlight pushes in space. At the Earth's distance from the Sun, the intensity of sunlight, that is the power per area, is $1361\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$. The area of the sail is $32\,\text{m}^2$, and it is perpendicular to the light. Assume that all the light is reflected straight back.

      How large a force does the light exert on the sail? Give the answer in micronewtons ($\mu\text{N}$).

      µN
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the connection between force and momentum? How much does the momentum of one photon change when it is reflected straight back?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Per second, the energy $P = IA$ arrives at the sail. The total momentum of all these photons is $\tfrac{P}{c}$, because for each photon $p = \tfrac{E}{c}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      In reflection the momentum changes twice as much:

      $F$$= \dfrac{2IA}{c}$
      $= \dfrac{2 \cdot 1361 \cdot 32}{2.998 \cdot 10^8}\,\text{N}$
      Show the solution

      Given

      the intensity of sunlight $I = 1361\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2}$, the area of the sail $A = 32\,\text{m}^2$, the light is reflected straight back; $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$

      Asked

      the force $F$ exerted by the light on the sail in micronewtons

      Principle

      Force is the change of momentum per unit time. The momentum of each photon is $p = \tfrac{E}{c}$. When a photon is reflected straight back, its momentum changes from $p$ to $-p$, so the sail receives the momentum $2p$. Per second, the energy $P = IA$ arrives at the sail, so the momentum of the photons arriving per second is $\tfrac{P}{c}$.

      Formula

      $F$$= \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$
      $= 2 \cdot \dfrac{P}{c}$

      where $P = IA$, so $F = \dfrac{2IA}{c}$

      Conversions

      $\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot \text{m}^2$$= \text{W}$
      $= \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}$
      $\tfrac{\text{J/s}}{\text{m/s}}$$= \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{m}}$
      $= \text{N}$

      Finally $1\,\text{N} = 10^6\,\mu\text{N}$.

      Substitution

      $F$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 1361\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot 32\,\text{m}^2}{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}$
      $= \dfrac{87\,104\,\text{W}}{2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}$
      $= 2.905 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$
      $= 290.5\,\mu\text{N}$

      Answer

      $2.9 \cdot 10^2\,\mu\text{N}$

      Two significant figures as in the area $32\,\text{m}^2$.

      Reasonableness

      The force is about $0.3\,\text{mN}$, roughly the weight of an object of $30\,\text{mg}$, for example a grain of rice. It is small, but in a vacuum it acts continuously, so a light probe accelerates to a high speed over months. If the sail absorbed the light, the force would be half, $145\,\mu\text{N}$.

        $E_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{2.480 \cdot 10^{-46}\,\text{kg}^2\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{2 \cdot 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}}$
        $= 1.361 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$
        $= 850\,\text{eV}$

        From conservation of energy,

        $E_\text{k}$$= 2.794 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} - 2.657 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$
        $= 1.368 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$
        $= 854\,\text{eV}$

        Answer

        $1.57 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$

        Three significant figures, because the change in wavelength $\Delta\lambda = 0.07476\,\text{nm} - 0.07110\,\text{nm} = 0.00366\,\text{nm} = 3.66\,\text{pm}$ is known to three digits. The two values of the kinetic energy, $850\,\text{eV}$ and $854\,\text{eV}$, differ from each other by only about $0.5\,\%$, so they agree: conservation of momentum and conservation of energy give the same result. The small difference is due to the change in wavelength $3.66\,\text{pm}$ being known to only three digits.

        Reasonableness

        The side $p_\text{e}$ of the triangle must be larger than the difference of the photon momenta and smaller than their sum:

        $0.456 \cdot 10^{-23}$
        $< 1.575 \cdot 10^{-23}$
        $< 1.818 \cdot 10^{-23}$

        (unit $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$). The angle $120\,{}^\circ$ is close to backscattering, so the result is closer to the sum, as it should be. The electron received about $850\,\text{eV}$, that is, about $5\,\%$ of the energy $17.4\,\text{keV}$ of the incident photon (

        $E$$= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$
        $= 2.794 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$

      3. Calculation task: Compton effect

      An X-ray photon of wavelength $0.0600\,\text{nm}$ scatters from an almost free electron. The wavelength of the scattered photon is $0.0624\,\text{nm}$.

      How much kinetic energy does the electron receive? Give the answer in electronvolts.

      eV
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Where does the energy lost by the photon go?

      Hint 2: what is the connection
      $E_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'}$

      when the wavelengths are in metres.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The energy of the incident photon is $3.3108 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$ and that of the scattered one $3.1835 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$. Divide the difference by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      the wavelength of the incident X-ray photon $\lambda = 0.0600\,\text{nm}$, of the scattered one $\lambda' = 0.0624\,\text{nm}$; $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

      Asked

      the kinetic energy $E_\text{k}$ received by the electron in electronvolts

      Principle

      In the Compton effect, the photon collides with an almost free electron. Energy is conserved, so the electron receives the energy that the photon loses: $hf = hf' + E_\text{k}$. The energy of each photon is obtained from its wavelength.

      Formula

      $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$$= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} + E_\text{k}$

      from which

      $E_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'}$

      Conversions

      $0.0600\,\text{nm} = 0.0600 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$ and $0.0624\,\text{nm} = 0.0624 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. Finally joules to electronvolts: $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

      Substitution

      $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$
      $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$
      $E_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0600 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} - \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{0.0624 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
      $= 3.3108 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J} - 3.1835 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$
      $= 1.273 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$

      In electronvolts:

      $E_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{1.273 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV}$
      $= 794.9\,\text{eV}$

      Answer

      $7.9 \cdot 10^2\,\text{eV}$

      Two significant figures, because the difference of the wavelengths $0.0024\,\text{nm}$ is known only to two figures.

      Reasonableness

      The energy of the incident photon is

      $\tfrac{3.31 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \cdot 1\,\text{eV}$
      $\approx 20.7\,\text{keV}$

      and the wavelength grows by $4\,\%$, so about $4\,\%$ of the energy, about $0.8\,\text{keV}$, is transferred to the electron. The electron gains energy and the photon loses it, so $E_\text{k}$ is positive.

      Optional 4. Explanation task: Evaluate the claim

      Your friend claims: “Light is really a wave. The photon is just a calculation aid needed in the photoelectric effect.” Evaluate the claim using the double-slit experiment and the Compton effect.

      Model answer

      The claim is only partly correct. The wave model explains the fringe pattern of the double-slit experiment, but in an experiment with weak light the light is detected as single photons that hit the screen one at a time at one place. In the Compton effect, the wavelength of the scattered X-rays grows with the scattering angle, which the wave model does not explain. In the photon model the photon gives the electron part of its energy and its momentum $p = \tfrac{h}{\lambda}$, as in a collision. So the photon is not merely a calculation aid. Light is neither only a wave nor only bullets: the wave gives the probability, and the photon is a quantum that is detected at one place. This is wave-particle duality.
