## fy6-1-L1-01
**Given:** charge $Q = 45\,\text{C}$, time $t = 30\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the electric current $I$ in $\text{A}$
**Principle:** Electric current tells how large a charge passes through the cross-section of a conductor per unit time. Both the charge that has passed and the time are known, so the current is obtained directly from the definition of current.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$
**Conversions:** The charge is in coulombs and the time in seconds, so the current comes directly in amperes, because $1\,\text{A} = 1\,\text{C/s}$.
**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{45\,\text{C}}{30\,\text{s}} = 1.5\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $1.5\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the charge $45\,\text{C}$ and the time $30\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The charge is one and a half times the number of seconds, so $1.5\,\text{C}$ passes every second. Check by multiplying: $1.5\,\text{A}\cdot 30\,\text{s} = 45\,\text{C}$. The current is greater than in a torch lamp ($0.3\,\text{A}$) and of the same order as in a car brake light bulb, where $21\,\text{W} : 12\,\text{V} = 1.75\,\text{A}$.

## fy6-1-L1-02
**Given:** current $I = 0.50\,\text{A}$, time $t = 40\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the charge $Q$ that passed through the motor, in $\text{C}$
**Principle:** Current tells how large a charge passes in a second. The current stays the same all the time, so the charge that has passed is obtained by solving it from the definition of current.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$, so $Q = It$
**Conversions:** The current is in amperes and the time in seconds, so the charge comes in coulombs, because $1\,\text{A}\cdot\text{s} = 1\,\text{C}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 0.50\,\text{A}\cdot 40\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{C}$
**Answer:** $20\,\text{C}$, two significant figures like the current $0.50\,\text{A}$ and the time $40\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Half a coulomb passes every second, so in $40\,\text{s}$ the charge is half of the number $40$, that is $20\,\text{C}$. The charge corresponds to about $20 \cdot 6.2 \cdot 10^{18} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{20}$ electrons. For comparison, the current $0.50\,\text{A}$ is a usual current of a small motor, and it is greater than the $0.3\,\text{A}$ of a torch lamp.

## fy6-1-L1-03
**Given:** energy transferred $E = 54\,\text{J}$, charge $Q = 6.0\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the voltage $U$ of the battery in $\text{V}$
**Principle:** Voltage tells how much energy is transferred per unit of charge. The energy transferred from the battery to the charge and the charge are known, so the voltage is obtained as their quotient.
**Formula:** $U = \dfrac{E}{Q}$
**Conversions:** The energy is in joules and the charge in coulombs, so the voltage comes in volts, because $1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J/C}$.
**Substitution:** $U = \dfrac{54\,\text{J}}{6.0\,\text{C}} = 9.0\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $9.0\,\text{V}$, two significant figures like the charge $6.0\,\text{C}$ and the energy $54\,\text{J}$.
**Reasonableness:** Every coulomb gets $9\,\text{J}$ from the battery, and for six coulombs that makes $6 \cdot 9\,\text{J} = 54\,\text{J}$. Shops sell exactly $9\,\text{V}$ batteries, so the result is believable.

## fy6-1-L2-02

## fy6-1-L3-01

**Find:** the time $t$ for which the lamp is lit, in hours

## fy6-1-L3-02
**Given:** the $9.0\,\text{V}$ battery: $U_1 = 9.0\,\text{V}$, $Q_1 = 0.50\,\text{Ah}$; the AA battery: $U_2 = 1.5\,\text{V}$, $Q_2 = 2.5\,\text{Ah}$

**Principle:** The energy transferred by a battery depends on both the charge and the voltage, because voltage tells the energy per unit of charge. We calculate the energy of each, $E = UQ$, and compare them with one division. A larger charge alone does not decide, because each coulomb of the AA battery transfers less energy.

**Reasonableness:** The voltage is $9.0\,\text{V}$ : $1.5\,\text{V}$, that is six times greater, but the charge is five times smaller. Six fifths is $1.2$, the same result without conversions. The smaller battery can thus transfer slightly more energy, although its charge is much smaller.

## fy6-1-L1-06
**Find:** which statement about the conventional direction of current and the movement of electrons is correct
**Principle:** The direction of current was agreed before electrons were known: in the external circuit the current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. The charge of the electron is negative, so the electrons move in the circuit in the opposite direction, from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. Current is not used up in the lamp, because the charge circulates in a closed circuit, and the electrons move slowly everywhere in the circuit at the same time.
**Model answer:** The correct statement is the one according to which the conventional direction of current is from the positive terminal to the negative terminal and the electrons move in the opposite direction. The statement that the electrons move in the same direction as the conventional current is wrong, because the charge of the electron is negative. The statement that current is used up in the lamp is wrong, because the same charge returns from the lamp to the battery and the current is equally large on both sides of the lamp. The statement that the lamp lights up only after the electrons have arrived at the speed of light is wrong, because the wire is already full of free electrons that move everywhere at the same time, and they do not travel at the speed of light.

## fy6-1-L3-04
**Given:** for each hour $U$ and $I$ from the table: $4.0\,\text{V}$ and $0.80\,\text{A}$; $3.8\,\text{V}$ and $0.75\,\text{A}$; $3.7\,\text{V}$ and $0.70\,\text{A}$; $3.6\,\text{V}$ and $0.60\,\text{A}$; $3.4\,\text{V}$ and $0.50\,\text{A}$; each interval $t = 1.0\,\text{h}$
**Find:** a) the total energy $E$ transferred from the battery, in $\text{Wh}$; b) the total charge $Q$ in $\text{Ah}$, the average voltage $U_{\text{avg}}$ and the energy in $\text{kJ}$; c) the estimate calculated with the initial voltage and its error in percent
**Principle:** Voltage is the energy transferred per unit of charge, $U = \tfrac{E}{Q}$, and current is the charge per unit of time, $Q = It$. Thus $E = UQ = UIt$. The voltage and the current change during the discharge, so the energy is calculated separately for each hour, when both are constant, and the energies are added. Energies and charges are additive, because energy is transferred and charge passes through the battery all the time.
**Formula:** $E_k = U_k I_k t$ for each hour $k$, $E = \sum E_k$; $Q = \sum I_k t$ and $U_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{E}{Q}$; estimate $E_{\text{estimate}} = U_0 Q$
**Conversions:** The time interval is $t = 1.0\,\text{h}$, so the product $U I t$ is directly in watt-hours, because $1\,\text{V}\cdot 1\,\text{A}\cdot 1\,\text{h} = 1\,\text{Wh}$. Watt-hour to joules: $1\,\text{Wh} = 1\,\text{V}\cdot 1\,\text{A}\cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3600\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** The energies of the hours are $4.0\,\text{V}\cdot 0.80\,\text{A}\cdot 1.0\,\text{h} = 3.20\,\text{Wh}$, $3.8\cdot 0.75 = 2.85\,\text{Wh}$, $3.7\cdot 0.70 = 2.59\,\text{Wh}$, $3.6\cdot 0.60 = 2.16\,\text{Wh}$ and $3.4\cdot 0.50 = 1.70\,\text{Wh}$. The sum is $12.5\,\text{Wh}$, so $E = 12.5\,\text{Wh}$. The charge is $Q = (0.80 + 0.75 + 0.70 + 0.60 + 0.50)\,\text{A}\cdot 1.0\,\text{h} = 3.35\,\text{Ah}$, the average voltage $U_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{12.5\,\text{Wh}}{3.35\,\text{Ah}} = 3.73\,\text{V}$ and the energy $E = 12.5 \cdot 3600\,\text{J} = 45\,000\,\text{J} = 45\,\text{kJ}$. The estimate is $E_{\text{estimate}} = 4.0\,\text{V}\cdot 3.35\,\text{Ah} = 13.4\,\text{Wh}$, so it is $\dfrac{13.4\,\text{Wh} - 12.5\,\text{Wh}}{12.5\,\text{Wh}} = 0.072 = 7.2\,\%$ too large.
**Answer:** a) $12.5\,\text{Wh}$, three significant figures, because the energies of the hours are exactly the sum of the numbers $3.20$, $2.85$, $2.59$, $2.16$ and $1.70$. b) The charge is $3.35\,\text{Ah}$, the average voltage $3.7\,\text{V}$ and the energy $45\,\text{kJ}$. c) The estimate $13.4\,\text{Wh}$ is about $7\,\%$ too large, because the voltage falls from the initial value $4.0\,\text{V}$ all the time; the real average voltage is smaller than the initial voltage.
**Reasonableness:** The voltage is all the time between $3.4\,\text{V}$ and $4.0\,\text{V}$ and the charge is $3.35\,\text{Ah}$, so the energy is between $3.4\cdot 3.35 = 11.4\,\text{Wh}$ and $4.0\cdot 3.35 = 13.4\,\text{Wh}$. The result $12.5\,\text{Wh}$ is between these. The energy of a real phone battery is of the same order, about $10\,\text{Wh}$–$15\,\text{Wh}$.

## fy6-1-L3-05
**Given:** current $I = 0.40\,\text{A}$, free electrons per metre $n = 1.3 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{1/m}$, elementary charge $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, distance $s = 1.0\,\text{m}$
**Find:** a) the speed $v$ of the electrons, in $\text{mm/s}$; b) the time $t$ in hours for an electron to move a distance of $1.0\,\text{m}$; c) why the lamp lights up immediately
**Principle:** Current tells how large a charge passes through a cross-section in a second, and the charge is the number of electrons times $e$. When the electrons move at the speed $v$, those electrons pass through the cross-section in a second that are in the wire within a distance $v \cdot 1\,\text{s}$. Their number is $n v \cdot 1\,\text{s}$. From this we get the speed. The time needed to move a distance is the distance divided by the speed.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{Ne}{t}$, so the number of electrons passing in a second is $\dfrac{N}{t} = \dfrac{I}{e}$; on the other hand $\dfrac{N}{t} = n v$, so $v = \dfrac{I}{n e}$ and $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$
**Conversions:** The speed is first calculated in $\text{m/s}$ and converted: $1\,\text{m/s} = 1000\,\text{mm/s}$. Time from seconds to hours: $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** In a second $\dfrac{N}{t} = \dfrac{0.40\,\text{A}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 2.50 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{1/s}$ electrons pass, so $v = \dfrac{2.50 \cdot 10^{18}\,\text{1/s}}{1.3 \cdot 10^{23}\,\text{1/m}} = 1.92 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{m/s} = 1.92 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\text{mm/s}$. The time is $t = \dfrac{1.0\,\text{m}}{1.92 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{m/s}} = 5.2 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{s} = \dfrac{5.2 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{s}}{3600\,\text{s/h}} = 14.5\,\text{h}$.
**Answer:** a) $0.019\,\text{mm/s}$, two significant figures like the current $0.40\,\text{A}$ and the density of the electrons. b) Moving a distance of $1.0\,\text{m}$ takes about $14\,\text{h}$. c) The lamp lights up immediately, because the wire is already full of free electrons, which start to move everywhere in the circuit at the same time. Energy is transferred to the lamp immediately although each electron advances slowly, because the electrons of the filament of the lamp itself move immediately and the lamp does not wait for the electrons of the battery.
**Reasonableness:** The speed $0.019\,\text{mm/s}$ means about $0.019\,\text{mm/s}\cdot 3600\,\text{s} \approx 69\,\text{mm}$ in an hour, so a metre takes about $14\,\text{h}$ as calculated. The $2.5 \cdot 10^{18}$ electrons passing in a second are a negligibly small part of the $1.3 \cdot 10^{23}$ electrons of the wire per metre, which fits slow advance. A current of $0.40\,\text{A}$ means a charge of $0.40\,\text{C}$ every second, which corresponds to about $2.5 \cdot 10^{18}$ electrons.

## fy6-1-L1-09
**Find:** which of the five statements about the current, the charge and the energy in a flashlight are true.
**Principle:** Current is not used up in the lamp: the same charge circulates in a closed circuit, so the current is equally large at every point of the circuit. The charge is obtained from the current, $Q = It$. Voltage is the transferred energy per unit of charge, $U = \tfrac{E}{Q}$, so the energy is $E = UQ$. The wire is already full of free electrons, which start to move everywhere in the circuit at the same time.
**Model answer:** The charge passing through the lamp in a minute and the energy transferred to the lamp are

$Q = It = 0.30\,\text{A} \cdot 60\,\text{s} = 18\,\text{C}$

$E = UQ = 3.0\,\text{V} \cdot 18\,\text{C} = 54\,\text{J}$

1 false: current is not used up in the lamp. Between the lamp and the negative terminal the current is the same $0.30\,\text{A}$ as before the lamp, because the same charge returns to the battery.

2 true: $18\,\text{C}$ passes through the lamp in a minute.

3 true: $3.0\,\text{V} = 3.0\,\text{J/C}$, so each coulomb brings $3.0\,\text{J}$ to the lamp when the resistance of the wires and of the battery itself is neglected.

4 false: the wire already contains free electrons, and they start to move everywhere in the circuit at the same time, also in the filament. The lamp lights up at once even though the electrons advance slowly.

5 false: $3.0\,\text{J}$ is the energy per one coulomb. In a minute $54\,\text{J}$ is transferred to the lamp.
