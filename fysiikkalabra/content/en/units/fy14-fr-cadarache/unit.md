On the second day of your (imagined) visit, your class meets researchers at work. You will need to introduce
yourself at the reception, answer and ask a few simple questions, and find your way through a large building. In
this lesson you also find out who does research in this part of Provence.

## Science in France: CEA, CNRS and Cadarache

France has two large public research organisations:

- **The CEA** (<span lang="fr">Commissariat à l'énergie atomique et aux énergies alternatives</span>) was created in 1945 under Charles de
  Gaulle, first for atomic energy. Since 2010 its name has included *alternative energies*. It has about 21,000
  staff in ten centres in France.
- **The CNRS** (<span lang="fr">Centre national de la recherche scientifique</span>) was founded in 1939. It is the largest public research body in
  Europe, with about 33,000 staff (2021), and does fundamental research in every field.

**Cadarache** is one of the CEA's centres. Research there began in 1959, and over 5,000 people work on energy:
fission reactors and their fuel, nuclear waste, solar energy and fusion. Its tokamak **Tore Supra** (1988–2010)
was rebuilt as **WEST**. On 12 February 2025, WEST held a plasma for 1,337 seconds, a world record for a tokamak at
the time.

**ITER** is not part of the CEA: it is a separate international organisation, built next to the Cadarache centre.
On 28 June 2005, in Moscow, the ITER partners chose Cadarache instead of Rokkasho in Japan, and the ITER Agreement
was signed in Paris on 21 November 2006. Today seven members (China, the European Union, India, Japan, Korea, Russia
and the United States) work together there. As of November 2026, the start of research operation is planned for 2034.

## Cadarache, ITER and Provence

Cadarache and ITER are in the commune of **Saint-Paul-lez-Durance**, in the department of Bouches-du-Rhône, in the
region **Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur**. The site lies by the river **Durance**, among dry hills and oak woods, about 35 km north of Aix-en-Provence. Because the staff come from many countries, an **international school**
opened in nearby Manosque in 2007 for their children.

## Asking for help and finding the way

At a research centre you speak to adults at work, so you use **<span lang="fr">vous</span>**. A polite request has four parts:

1. get attention: **<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">Excusez-moi</span></span>** or **<span lang="fr">Pardon</span>**, with <span lang="fr">madame</span> or <span lang="fr">monsieur</span>;
2. say what you need: **<span lang="fr">Je cherche …</span>** (I am looking for) or **<span lang="fr">Où est … ?</span>** (Where is …?);
3. be polite: **<span lang="fr">s'il vous plaît</span>**;
4. thank: **<span lang="fr">Merci beaucoup, c'est très gentil.</span>** The answer is often <span lang="fr">Je vous en prie</span> (You are welcome).

The directions you hear most often:

| French | English |
|---|---|
| <span lang="fr">à gauche</span> | on the left, to the left |
| <span lang="fr">à droite</span> | on the right, to the right |
| <span lang="fr">tout droit</span> | straight on |
| <span lang="fr">au bout du couloir</span> | at the end of the corridor |
| <span lang="fr">à côté de …</span> | next to … |
| <span lang="fr">en face de …</span> | opposite … |
| <span lang="fr">au <span class="nobr">rez-de-chaussée</span></span> | on the ground floor |
| <span lang="fr">au premier étage</span> | on the first floor (one level up) |

**Careful with floors.** In France the ground floor is the *<span lang="fr"><span class="nobr">rez-de-chaussée</span></span>*, and the *<span lang="fr">premier étage</span>* is
the floor above it. In Finland that floor is the *toinen kerros*.
