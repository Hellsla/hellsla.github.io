Building nuclei: isotopes and nuclear notation

Why do the protons in a nucleus not fly apart, although they all repel each other?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • describe a nucleus with $Z$, $N$ and $A$ and write it in nuclear notation.
  • explain what isotopes are and name the isotopes of hydrogen.
  • compare the strong nuclear force with the electric repulsion of protons.

Prior knowledge

  • An atom has a tiny positive nucleus with electrons around itchapter From Rutherford's nucleus to Bohr's atom
  • Charges of the same sign repel each other, and Coulomb's law $F = k q_1 q_2 / r^2$ gives the force (earlier physics courses)

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 2.

1Think about it

Imagine an atom as large as a football stadium, about $100\ \text{m}$ across. Its nucleus would then be a small seed of a few millimetres in the middle, and yet it would hold almost all of the atom's mass. Inside that seed, the protons are all positive and packed very close together. Charges of the same sign repel, and the closer they are, the stronger the push.

What could keep the protons of a nucleus together?

Review: Rutherford's nucleus

In Rutherford's gold-foil experiment, almost all alpha particles went straight through the foil, but a very few bounced back. What did this show?

  • Right. A few alpha particles bounced back because they came very close to a small, heavy, positive nucleus. Most passed through the empty space around it. Today you look inside that nucleus.

  • An electron is about $7300$ times lighter than an alpha particle, so it cannot turn it back. Only a heavy nucleus can.

  • This is Thomson's model, which the experiment rejected: a spread-out charge could not turn an alpha particle back.

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 3 questions

Prediction 1: one more neutron

A hydrogen-1 nucleus is a single proton. Suppose you add one neutron to it. What do you have now?

  • A neutron has no charge, so it cannot cancel the proton's charge: the nucleus still has the charge $+e$. It is deuterium, $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$.

  • Right: $Z = 1$ still, so it is hydrogen, but $A = 2$. This isotope is deuterium, $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$, and you built it in the Isotope Builder.

  • The element is decided by the number of protons, $Z$, not by the number of particles. With one proton the nucleus is still hydrogen: deuterium, $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$. Helium needs two protons.

Prediction 2: what holds the nucleus together?

Protons in a nucleus repel each other strongly. Which explanation of why the nucleus holds together seems most likely to you?

  • Gravity grows at short distance too, but in the same way as the electric force, so the ratio stays the same: gravity between two protons is about $10^{36}$ times weaker than their electric repulsion.

  • A neutron has no charge, so it cannot cancel anything. Neutrons help because the strong force attracts them too, without adding any repulsion.

  • Before 1932 some physicists thought so, but there are no electrons in a nucleus. Chadwick's neutron replaced this idea, and the binding comes from the strong nuclear force.

  • This is the strong nuclear force. It attracts protons and neutrons almost equally and wins over the electric repulsion when nucleons nearly touch, but it fades out by about $2.5\ \text{fm}$.

Your prediction is saved. Feedback appears when you have made the measurements.

Prediction 3: light and heavy nuclei

A light stable nucleus such as carbon-12 has as many neutrons as protons, $N = Z = 6$.What do you expect for a heavy stable nucleus such as lead-208, with $Z = 82$?

  • The table of stable nuclei shows otherwise: lead-208 has $N = 126$, so $N/Z = 1.54$. The more protons there are, the more extra neutrons are needed.

  • Right: lead-208 has $126$ neutrons and $82$ protons. Every proton pushes every other proton, but the strong force acts only between neighbours, so a large nucleus needs extra neutrons for extra attraction.

  • No stable heavy nucleus has $N < Z$. Lead-208 has $N = 126$ and $Z = 82$. Neutrons add attraction without repulsion, so heavy nuclei need more of them.

Lead-208 is $^{208}_{82}\text{Pb}$. Calculate its neutron number $N$ and then the ratio $N/Z$ for the last row. Type the ratio.

Hint 1: a guiding question

The upper number is $A$, the lower number is $Z$.

Hint 2: what is the connection

$N = A - Z$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$N = 208 - 82 = 126$; now divide by $Z$.

Show the solution

Given

lead-208, $A = 208$, $Z = 82$

Find

the neutron number $N$ and the ratio $N/Z$

Principle

The mass number counts all nucleons, $A = Z + N$, so the neutron number is the difference of the two numbers in the notation.

Formula

$N = A - Z$, and then the ratio $N/Z$

Unit conversions

None: $N$, $Z$ and their ratio are pure numbers.

Substitution

$N = 208 - 82 = 126$, and $N/Z = 126 : 82 = 1.537$

Answer

$N = 126$

And $N/Z \approx 1.54$, to two decimal places like the other rows of the table.

Reasonableness

The ratio continues the trend of the table: $1.00$ for light nuclei, $1.15$ for iron, $1.40$ for tin, $1.49$ for gold. The heaviest stable nucleus has the most extra neutrons.

    Review the theory summary.

    4Theory summary

    Inside the atom

    An atom has a tiny nucleus with electrons around it (Rutherford, 1911). The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, together called nucleons. Rutherford identified the proton in experiments from 1917 to 1920; James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.

    Particle Charge Mass (u) Where
    proton $+e$ $1.00728$ nucleus
    neutron $0$ $1.00866$ nucleus
    electron $-e$ $0.00055$ around it

    Here $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ is the elementary charge and $1\ \text{u} = 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ the atomic mass unit. A proton is $1836$ times as heavy as an electron, so almost all the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, although its diameter is only $1/60\,000$ (hydrogen) to $1/27\,000$ (uranium) of the atom's.

    Key idea

    The number of protons is the atomic number $Z$. It decides the element: every carbon nucleus has $Z = 6$, every uranium nucleus $Z = 92$. The number of neutrons is the neutron number $N$. Nuclei of one element with different numbers of neutrons are isotopes of the element, from the Greek for "same place": they share a place in the periodic table. The Scottish doctor Margaret Todd suggested the word to the chemist Frederick Soddy in 1913.

    Isotopes have practically the same chemistry but different masses. Some are stable; others are radioactive and change into another nucleus after some time. Hydrogen has three isotopes, and two of them are the fuel of fusion reactors:

    About one hydrogen atom in $6400$ on Earth is deuterium, so every litre of water contains some. Tritium is radioactive, with a half-life of $12.3$ years, so there is almost none in nature.

    Mass number and nuclear notation

    The mass number $A$ is the total number of nucleons:

    $$A = Z + N$$

    A nucleus is written in nuclear notation as $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where $\text{X}$ is the chemical symbol. The symbol and $Z$ always belong together, so $Z$ is sometimes left out: $^{14}\text{C}$ or "carbon-14" means the same as $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$.

    The mass number is a count, not a mass, although it is close to the mass in $\text{u}$.

    Words of the lesson

    The force that holds the nucleus

    Inside a nucleus, protons are only about $1\text{–}2\ \text{fm}$ apart ($1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$). There the electric repulsion of two protons is huge, about $58\ \text{N}$ at $2\ \text{fm}$. Gravity between them is about $10^{36}$ times weaker.

    The nucleus is held by a different force, the strong nuclear force. It attracts any two nucleons, protons and neutrons alike, and it beats the electric repulsion when they nearly touch. But it has a very short range: it fades out by about $2.5\ \text{fm}$. Below about $0.7\ \text{fm}$ it repels, so nucleons cannot be squeezed together.

    So each nucleon is held only by its nearest neighbours, but each proton is pushed by every other proton in the nucleus. Neutrons add attraction without adding repulsion. This is why light stable nuclei have $N \approx Z$, but heavy stable nuclei need more neutrons than protons. Above lead, no amount of neutrons makes a nucleus fully stable.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      An iron-56 nucleus, $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$, has the mass $m = 9.29 \cdot 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$. Its radius can be estimated with $R = r_0 A^{1/3}$, where $r_0 = 1.2\ \text{fm}$. How many protons and neutrons does it have, how large is it, and what is the density of the nucleus?

      Step 1/7

      Given: mass number $A = 56$, atomic number $Z = 26$, mass ${m = 9.29 \cdot 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}}$, ${r_0 = 1.2\ \text{fm}}$. Find: the number of protons $Z$, the number of neutrons $N$, the radius $R$ and the density $\rho$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle: The lower number in the notation is the number of protons, the upper one the number of nucleons, $A = Z + N$. A nucleus is close to a sphere, so its volume is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$, and the density is mass divided by volume.

      Step 3/7

      Formula: Solved in symbols:

      $$\begin{aligned} N &= A - Z \\ R &= r_0 A^{1/3} \\ \rho &= \frac{m}{V} = \frac{3m}{4\pi R^3} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions: ${r_0 = 1.2\ \text{fm} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m}}$, so that the density comes out in $\text{kg/m}^3$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution: $$\begin{aligned} N &= 56 - 26 = 30 \\[4pt] R &= 1.2 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m} \cdot 56^{1/3} \\ &= 1.2 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m} \cdot 3.826 \\ &= 4.59 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m} \\[4pt] \rho &= \frac{3 \cdot 9.29 \cdot 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}}{4\pi \cdot (4.59 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m})^3} \\ &= 2.29 \cdot 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer: The nucleus has $26$ protons and $30$ neutrons. Its radius is about $4.6\ \text{fm}$ and its density about $2 \cdot 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$. The density is given to one significant figure, because $R = r_0 A^{1/3}$ is only an estimate: a nucleus has no sharp surface.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness: The density of solid iron is about $7900\ \text{kg/m}^3$. The nucleus is about $3 \cdot 10^{13}$ times denser, which fits: almost all of the mass of an atom sits in a volume $10^{13}\text{–}10^{14}$ times smaller than the atom. A teaspoon ($5\ \text{cm}^3$) of nuclear matter would have a mass of about $10^{12}\ \text{kg}$.

      How hard do two protons push?

      Two protons in a nucleus are $r = 2.0\ \text{fm}$ apart, centre to centre. Calculate the electric force between them with Coulomb's law $F = k q_1 q_2 / r^2$, where $k = 8.99 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$ and the charge of a proton is $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. Give the force in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Both charges are $e$, so $q_1 q_2 = e^2$.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Convert $2.0\ \text{fm} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ before you square it.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $e^2 = 2.566 \cdot 10^{-38}\ \text{C}^2$ and $r^2 = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-30}\ \text{m}^2$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      distance $r = 2.0\ \text{fm}$, charges $q_1 = q_2 = e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, $k = 8.99 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$

      Find

      the electric force $F$ between the protons

      Principle

      Two charges of the same sign repel each other with the force of Coulomb's law. At this distance the protons can be treated as point charges for an estimate.

      Formula

      $$F = k\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} = \frac{k e^2}{r^2}$$

      Unit conversions

      $r$$= 2.0\ \text{fm}$
      $= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$

      Substitution

      $$\begin{aligned} F &= 8.99 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{C}^2 \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{(1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{C})^2}{(2.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m})^2} \\ &= 57.7\ \text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Answer

      $F \approx 58\ \text{N}$

      Two significant figures as in the distance $2.0\ \text{fm}$.

      Reasonableness

      $58\ \text{N}$ is about the weight of a $6\ \text{kg}$ bag, acting on a particle of only $1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$: it would give the proton an acceleration of

      $58\ \text{N} : 1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$
      $\approx 3 \cdot 10^{28}\ \text{m/s}^2$

      Without a much stronger attraction, the nucleus would fly apart at once.

      Optional Find the error
      A student writes: "Oxygen-17, $^{17}_{8}\text{O}$, has $8$ protons and $17$ neutrons." What is wrong?

      • Right. The upper number is the mass number $A = Z + N$, so $N = A - Z = 9$.

      • Isotopes of one element have the same $Z$ but different $N$. Oxygen-16 has $8$ neutrons; oxygen-17 has $9$.

      • The upper number is the mass number: protons and neutrons together. $N = A - Z = 17 - 8 = 9$.

      Common misconception

      "Isotopes are different elements." No: isotopes have the same $Z$ and so belong to the same element. Only the number of neutrons, and so the mass, is different. If the number of protons changes, the element changes.

      "The mass number is the mass of the nucleus." The mass number is a whole number that counts nucleons. The real mass of $^{56}\text{Fe}$ is not $56\ \text{u}$, and in the next unit you will see why the difference matters.

      "The neutrons hold the nucleus together by cancelling the charge of the protons." A neutron has no charge, so it cannot cancel anything. It helps because the strong force also acts on it.

      Optional The discovery of the neutron

      The people behind the neutron

      James Chadwick (1891–1974)

      James Chadwick was an English physicist who studied under Ernest Rutherford in Manchester and followed him to the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. Rutherford had suggested that the nucleus might contain a neutral particle, but nobody had found it. In 1932 Chadwick aimed the radiation from polonium and beryllium at paraffin wax and measured the protons that it knocked out. Only a neutral particle with about the mass of a proton explained the results. In February 1932 he reported "the possible existence of a neutron". He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1935.

      1. Concept: isotopes

      Which pair of nuclei are isotopes of the same element?

      • They have the same mass number, but $Z = 6$ and $Z = 7$: two different elements. Isotopes need the same $Z$.

      • Right. Both have $Z = 6$, so both are carbon; they have $6$ and $7$ neutrons.

      • Same $A = 3$, but different $Z$: hydrogen and helium. The mass number does not decide the element.

      • Beryllium has twice as many of everything, but $Z$ is different, so these are different elements.

      Suggestion: one answer does not show everything you can do, but review the theory summary.

      2. Notation: uranium-235

      The fuel of most fission reactors is uranium-235, $^{235}_{92}\text{U}$. How many neutrons does its nucleus have?

      • You added $A$ and $Z$. But $A$ already includes the protons: $N = A - Z$.

      • $235$ is the mass number $A$: all nucleons together. Subtract the protons: $N = A - Z$.

      • $92$ is the number of protons, $Z$. The neutrons are $N = 235 - 92$.

      • Right: $N = A - Z = 235 - 92 = 143$.

      Suggestion: one answer does not show everything you can do, but review the theory summary.

      3. Forces: heavy nuclei

      Lead-208 has $44$ more neutrons than protons. What is the best explanation?

      • A neutron is neutral, so it cannot cancel charge. The lead nucleus still has the charge $+82e$.

      • Right. Each proton repels every other proton across the whole nucleus, while the strong force acts only between neighbours. Extra neutrons add attraction without adding repulsion.

      • Gravity between nucleons is about $10^{36}$ times weaker than their electric repulsion. It plays no part inside a nucleus.

      Suggestion: one answer does not show everything you can do, but review the theory summary.

      4. Explain: tritium and helium-3

      Tritium, $^{3}_{1}\text{H}$, and helium-3, $^{3}_{2}\text{He}$, have the same mass number. Your friend says they are isotopes. Do you agree? Explain with $Z$, $N$ and $A$, and say what would be an isotope of tritium.

      Sentence starters

      • I agree / I disagree, because …
      • Tritium has … protons and … neutrons, while …
      • The element is decided by …

      Model answer

      I disagree. Tritium has $Z = 1$ and $N = 2$, while helium-3 has $Z = 2$ and $N = 1$. They have the same mass number $A = 3$, but the element is decided by the number of protons, so they are two different elements. An isotope of tritium must also have one proton, for example hydrogen-1, $^{1}_{1}\text{H}$, or deuterium, $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$.

        Review the theory summary.

        Summary

        • A nucleus contains $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons; the mass number is $A = Z + N$, written $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$.
        • $Z$ decides the element. Isotopes of an element have the same $Z$ but different $N$.
        • Hydrogen-1, deuterium $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$ and tritium $^{3}_{1}\text{H}$ are the isotopes of hydrogen; tritium is radioactive.
        • The nucleus is about $10^{-15}\text{–}10^{-14}\ \text{m}$ across, and nuclear matter has a density of about $2 \cdot 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$.
        • The strong nuclear force attracts all nucleons but only over about $2.5\ \text{fm}$; the electric repulsion of protons reaches across the whole nucleus. That is why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons.
