## fy8-15-L1-01
**Given:** $\Delta^{++} = u\,u\,u$, charge of the $u$ quark $q_u = +\tfrac{2}{3}e$
**Asked:** the charge $q$ of the particle as a multiple of the elementary charge $e$
**Principle:** The charge of a hadron is the sum of the charges of its quarks, because charge is an additive and conserved quantity. All three quarks are of the same kind.
**Formula:** $q = q_u + q_u + q_u$, so $q = 3\,q_u$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because the charges are given as multiples of the elementary charge $e$.
**Substitution:** $q = 3 \cdot \left(+\tfrac{2}{3}e\right) = +2\,e$
**Answer:** $+2\,e$. The result is exact because the charges of the quarks are exact fractions.
**Reasonableness:** The charge is a whole-number multiple of the elementary charge, like that of every hadron, and it fits the notation $\Delta^{++}$, where the two plus signs mean the charge $+2e$. In the proton one $u$ has been replaced by a $d$, and the charge is one smaller.

## fy8-15-L1-02
**Given:** $\bar{p} = \bar{u}\,\bar{u}\,\bar{d}$; the charge of the $u$ quark is $+\tfrac{2}{3}e$ and that of the $d$ quark $-\tfrac{1}{3}e$
**Asked:** the charge $q$ of the antiproton as a multiple of the elementary charge
**Principle:** The charge of an antiquark is opposite to that of the corresponding quark, so the charge of $\bar{u}$ is $-\tfrac{2}{3}e$ and that of $\bar{d}$ is $+\tfrac{1}{3}e$. The charge of a hadron is the sum of the charges of its parts.
**Formula:** $q = 2\,q_{\bar{u}} + q_{\bar{d}}$, so $q = -2\,q_u - q_d$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because the charges are multiples of the elementary charge.
**Substitution:** $q = -2 \cdot \tfrac{2}{3}e - \left(-\tfrac{1}{3}e\right) = -\tfrac{4}{3}e + \tfrac{1}{3}e = -1\,e$
**Answer:** $-1\,e$. The result is exact because the charges of the quarks are exact fractions.
**Reasonableness:** The charge of the antiproton must be opposite to the $+e$ of the proton, because an antiparticle has the same mass but the opposite charge. The result agrees.

## fy8-15-L1-03
**Given:** antineutron $\bar{n} = \bar{u}\,\bar{d}\,\bar{d}$: quarks $n_\text{q} = 0$, antiquarks $n_{\bar{\text{q}}} = 3$
**Asked:** the baryon number $B$
**Principle:** The baryon number is calculated from the quarks: each quark gives $+\tfrac{1}{3}$ and each antiquark $-\tfrac{1}{3}$. The antineutron has only antiquarks.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{n_\text{q} - n_{\bar{\text{q}}}}{3}$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because the numbers of quarks are counts.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{0 - 3}{3} = -1$
**Answer:** $-1$. The result is exact because the antiquarks are counted as items.
**Reasonableness:** The baryon number of the neutron is $1$, and an antiparticle has the opposite baryon number. The charge of the antineutron is $-\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} = 0$, like that of the neutron, so the antineutron differs from the neutron precisely in its baryon number.

## fy8-15-L1-04
**Given:** an electron and a positron at rest, the rest energy of each $m_e c^2 = 511.0\,\text{keV}$; two photons are produced
**Asked:** the energy $E_\gamma$ of one photon in kiloelectronvolts
**Principle:** In annihilation all the energy of the particles turns into the energy of the photons, so energy is conserved. The momentum of particles at rest is zero, so the two photons must leave in opposite directions with equal momentum and therefore equal energy.
**Formula:** $2\,m_e c^2 = 2\,E_\gamma$, so $E_\gamma = m_e c^2$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because the rest energy is given in kiloelectronvolts.
**Substitution:** $E_\gamma = 511.0\,\text{keV}$
**Answer:** $511.0\,\text{keV}$, four significant figures like the given rest energy.
**Reasonableness:** The combined energy of the two photons, $1022\,\text{keV}$, is the sum of the rest energies of the electron and the positron. The photons are gamma radiation, whose energies are of the order of kiloelectronvolts or megaelectronvolts, like the gamma photons of a nucleus.

## fy8-15-L1-05
**Given:** a helium-4 nucleus has two protons ($uud$) and two neutrons ($udd$)
**Asked:** the number of $u$ quarks $N_u$
**Principle:** The quarks of a nucleus are the quarks of its nucleons. A proton has two $u$ quarks and a neutron has one.
**Formula:** $N_u = 2 \cdot n_p + 1 \cdot n_n$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because we are counting items.
**Substitution:** $N_u = 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 2 = 6$
**Answer:** $6$. The result is an exact whole number.
**Reasonableness:** The nucleus has $4 \cdot 3 = 12$ quarks, of which the number of $d$ quarks is $2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 6$, and $6 + 6 = 12$. The charge is $6 \cdot \tfrac{2}{3}e - 6 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}e = +2e$, as it must be for a nucleus of two protons.

## fy8-15-L2-01
**Given:** nucleus ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$: atomic number $Z = 6$, mass number $A = 12$; proton $uud$, neutron $udd$
**Asked:** the number of $d$ quarks $N_d$
**Principle:** First the numbers of protons and neutrons are calculated from the symbol of the nucleus, then their $d$ quarks: a proton has one $d$ quark and a neutron has two.
**Formula:** $N = A - Z$ and $N_d = 1 \cdot Z + 2 \cdot N$, so $N_d = Z + 2\,(A - Z)$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because we are counting items.
**Substitution:** $N_d = 6 + 2 \cdot (12 - 6) = 6 + 12 = 18$
**Answer:** $18$. The result is an exact whole number.
**Reasonableness:** The nucleus has $12 \cdot 3 = 36$ quarks, of which the number of $u$ quarks is $2 \cdot 6 + 6 = 18$, and $18 + 18 = 36$. The charge is $18 \cdot \tfrac{2}{3}e - 18 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}e = +6e$, as for a nucleus of six protons.

## fy8-15-L2-02
**Given:** $\Lambda^0 \to p + \pi^-$, $m_\Lambda = 1115.683\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, $m_p = 938.272\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, $m_\pi = 139.570\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$
**Asked:** the energy $Q$ released in the decay, in megaelectronvolts
**Principle:** The decaying particle is heavier than the products together, and the energy corresponding to the mass difference is released as the kinetic energy of the products ($E = mc^2$). The decay is allowed: the charge $0 = 1 - 1$, the baryon number $1 = 1 + 0$ and the lepton number $0 = 0 + 0$ are conserved.
**Formula:** $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2$, so $Q = (m_\Lambda - m_p - m_\pi)\,c^2$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the masses are in the unit $\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, so $c^2$ cancels.
**Substitution:** $Q = (1115.683 - 938.272 - 139.570)\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 37.841\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** $37.84\,\text{MeV}$; the masses are given to the precision of a thousandth, so $37.841\,\text{MeV}$ is accepted too.
**Reasonableness:** Rounded, $1116 - 938 - 140 = 38$, close to the result. The energy is positive, so the decay is possible as far as energy is concerned, and it is only about $3\,\%$ of the rest energy of the lambda, because the products are almost as heavy.

## fy8-15-L2-03
**Given:** the smallest energy of the photon $E = 2 \cdot 0.511\,\text{MeV}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Asked:** the greatest wavelength $\lambda$ in picometres
**Principle:** In pair production the masses of the electron and the positron are made from the energy of the photon, so the energy of the photon must be at least the sum of their rest energies. The energy of a photon is $\tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so the smallest energy corresponds to the greatest wavelength.
**Formula:** $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{E}$
**Conversions:** $E = 2 \cdot 0.511 \cdot 10^{6} \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 1.6372 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{J}$; finally $1\,\text{m} = 10^{12}\,\text{pm}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{1.6372 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{J}} = 1.2133 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{m} = 1.2133\,\text{pm}$
**Answer:** $1.21\,\text{pm}$, three significant figures like the rest energy $0.511\,\text{MeV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The wavelength of a gamma photon is of the order of picometres, like that of the gamma radiation in the chapter *The atomic nucleus and radioactive decay*. A photon with a longer wavelength is not enough to make a pair, because its energy is smaller than $1.022\,\text{MeV}$.

## fy8-15-L2-04
**Given:** neutron $udd$, $m_u = 2.16\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, $m_d = 4.70\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, $m_n = 939.565\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$
**Asked:** the share $x$ of the mass of the neutron that is the sum of the masses of the quarks, as a percentage
**Principle:** First the sum of the masses of the three quarks is calculated, then it is compared with the mass of the neutron by one division. The masses are in the same unit, so the share has no unit.
**Formula:** $m_\text{quarks} = m_u + 2\,m_d$, so $x = \dfrac{m_u + 2\,m_d}{m_n} \cdot 100\,\%$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because all the masses are in the unit $\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ and the unit cancels.
**Substitution:** $x = \dfrac{2.16\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} + 2 \cdot 4.70\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}}{939.565\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}} \cdot 100\,\% = \dfrac{11.56}{939.565} \cdot 100\,\% = 1.2304\,\%$
**Answer:** $1.23\,\%$, three significant figures like the masses of the quarks.
**Reasonableness:** The masses of the quarks are a few $\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$ and that of the neutron almost $1000\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, so the share is of the order of one percent. The remaining about $99\,\%$ of the mass of the neutron is the energy of the strong interaction that binds the quarks.

## fy8-15-L3-01
**Given:** $\Omega^- = s\,s\,s$, $m_\Omega = 1672.45\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$; $\Lambda^0 = u\,d\,s$, $m_\Lambda = 1115.683\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$; $\Sigma^0 = u\,d\,s$, $m_\Sigma = 1192.642\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$; $K^- = s\,\bar{u}$, $m_K = 493.677\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$; $\pi^0$ an uncharged meson, $m_\pi = 134.977\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$
**Asked:** the possible reaction and the energy $Q$ released in it, in megaelectronvolts
**Principle:** A reaction is possible only if the charge, the baryon number and the lepton number are conserved and the energy released is positive, because a particle at rest cannot produce products heavier than itself. The charges and baryon numbers are calculated from the quarks. There are no leptons, so $L = 0$ everywhere. Charges: $\Omega^-$: $3 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)e = -e$; $\Lambda^0$ and $\Sigma^0$: $\left(\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3}\right)e = 0$; $K^-$: $\left(-\tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{2}{3}\right)e = -e$. The baryon number is $\tfrac{3 - 0}{3} = 1$ for baryons and $\tfrac{1 - 1}{3} = 0$ for mesons.
**Formula:** $q_\text{initial} = q_\text{final}$, $B_\text{initial} = B_\text{final}$ and $Q = (m_\text{initial} - m_\text{final})\,c^2 > 0$; for reaction (3) $Q = (m_\Omega - m_\Lambda - m_K)\,c^2$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the masses are in the unit $\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, so $c^2$ cancels.
**Substitution:** (1) the charge $-1 = -1 + 0$ is conserved, but the baryon number $1 \ne 0 + 0$, so the reaction is forbidden. (2) the charge $-1 = 0 - 1$ and the baryon number $1 = 1 + 0$ are conserved, but $Q = (1672.45 - 1192.642 - 493.677)\,\text{MeV} = -13.869\,\text{MeV} < 0$, so the energy is not enough. (3) the charge $-1 = 0 - 1$ and the baryon number $1 = 1 + 0$ are conserved, and $Q = (1672.45 - 1115.683 - 493.677)\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2} \cdot c^2 = 63.090\,\text{MeV}$
**Answer:** Reaction (3) $\Omega^- \to \Lambda^0 + K^-$ is possible, and it releases $63.09\,\text{MeV}$, to the precision of a hundredth like the mass of the omega.
**Reasonableness:** Rounded, $1672 - 1116 - 494 = 62$, close to the result. Reactions (2) and (3) differ only in the mass of the baryon: the lighter $\Lambda^0$ leaves energy over, the heavier $\Sigma^0$ does not. The charge alone does not tell the kind of quarks: $\Delta^- = d\,d\,d$ also has the charge $-e$, but its mass is only about $1232\,\tfrac{\text{MeV}}{c^2}$, because the $d$ quark is lighter than the $s$ quark. The observed $\Omega^-$ most often decays precisely by reaction (3).

## fy8-15-L3-02
**Given:** the mass of antimatter $1.0\,\text{g}$ and the mass of ordinary matter $1.0\,\text{g}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
**Asked:** the energy released $E$ in the unit $10^{14}\,\text{J}$
**Principle:** In annihilation the mass of both the antimatter and the ordinary matter turns into the energy of radiation, so the whole mass $2.0\,\text{g}$ takes part. The equivalence of mass and energy gives the energy.
**Formula:** $E = mc^2$, where $m = m_\text{anti} + m_\text{matter}$, so $E = (m_\text{anti} + m_\text{matter})\,c^2$
**Conversions:** $1.0\,\text{g} + 1.0\,\text{g} = 2.0\,\text{g} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$, and $1\,\text{kg} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} = 1\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $E = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot (2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s})^2 = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot 8.988 \cdot 10^{16}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} = 1.798 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $1.8 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{J}$, two significant figures like the masses $1.0\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** By estimate $2 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot (3 \cdot 10^{8})^2 = 1.8 \cdot 10^{14}$. A common mistake would be to use only the mass of the antimatter, which would give half of the result. The energy corresponds to about $50\,\text{GWh}$, since $1\,\text{GWh} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{12}\,\text{J}$.

## fy8-15-L3-03
**Given:** the reaction $\bar{\nu}_e + p \to n + X$; from the table $\bar{\nu}_e$: $q = 0$, $B = 0$, $L = -1$; $p$: $q = +1$, $B = 1$, $L = 0$; $n$: $q = 0$, $B = 1$, $L = 0$
**Asked:** the lepton number $L_X$ of the particle $X$
**Principle:** The charge, the baryon number and the lepton number are conserved in all particle reactions, so the numbers of the particle $X$ follow from the difference between the initial and final states. They also tell which particle $X$ is.
**Formula:** $L_{\bar{\nu}} + L_p = L_n + L_X$, so $L_X = L_{\bar{\nu}} + L_p - L_n$
**Conversions:** Not needed, because the numbers have no units.
**Substitution:** $L_X = -1 + 0 - 0 = -1$; in the same way $q_X = 0 + 1 - 0 = +1$ and $B_X = 0 + 1 - 1 = 0$
**Answer:** $L_X = -1$. The result is an exact whole number. The particle with $q = +1$, $B = 0$ and $L = -1$ is an antilepton, that is the positron $e^+$: $\bar{\nu}_e + p \to n + e^+$.
**Reasonableness:** The reaction is like beta plus decay: a proton turns into a neutron and a positron is produced. The experiment of Cowan and Reines observed precisely the $511\,\text{keV}$ photons produced in the annihilation of the positron and the capture of the neutron.
