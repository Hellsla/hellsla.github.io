**Principle:** One ball is chosen as the object. The forces on it are the weight $\overline{G}$ down, the tension $\overline{T}$ of the thread along the thread towards the hook, and the electric force $\overline{F}_\text{s}$ of the other ball. The balls are at the same height, so the electric force is horizontal and points away from the other ball: charges of the same sign repel. The ball is at rest, so the sum of the forces is zero. The horizontal component of the force of the thread cancels the electric force and the vertical component cancels the weight. The angle $\alpha$ of the thread from the vertical comes from the geometry, because the ball is half the distance away from the vertical through the hook (chapter *Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces*).
**Formula:** $T\sin\alpha = F_\text{s}$ and $T\cos\alpha = mg$, so $F_\text{s} = mg\tan\alpha$, where $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{r/2}{L}$
**Conversions:** $m = 0.50\,\text{g} = 0.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{kg}$. The ratio of the lengths needs no conversion. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.
**Substitution:** $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{4.0\,\text{cm}}{40.0\,\text{cm}} = 0.100$, so $\alpha = 5.74^\circ$ and $\tan\alpha = 0.1005$. The weight is $mg = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 4.905 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$, so $F_\text{s} = 4.905 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N} \cdot 0.1005 = 4.930 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N} = 0.493\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $F_\text{s} \approx 0.49\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures, as in the mass $0.50\,\text{g}$ and the distance $8.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The threads are almost vertical, so the horizontal force must be small compared with the weight: it is about $10\,\%$ of the weight $4.9\,\text{mN}$. The force of the thread $T = \tfrac{mg}{\cos\alpha} \approx 4.93\,\text{mN}$ is only slightly larger than the weight, as it should be at a small angle.

### b)
**Given:** from part a $F_\text{s} = 0.493\,\text{mN}$; $r = 8.0\,\text{cm}$; $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the charge $q$ of each ball and the number $n$ of electrons moved
**Principle:** The balls are small compared with the distance, so they can be treated as point charges, and Coulomb's law gives the force. The charges are equal, so the product of the charges is $q^2$. Charge consists of elementary charges, so the positive balls lack $n = \tfrac{q}{e}$ electrons (chapter *Electric charge and Coulomb's law*).
**Formula:** $F_\text{s} = k\dfrac{q^2}{r^2}$, so $q = r\sqrt{\dfrac{F_\text{s}}{k}}$ and $n = \dfrac{q}{e}$
**Conversions:** $r = 8.0\,\text{cm} = 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.080\,\text{m}$ and $F_\text{s} = 0.493\,\text{mN} = 0.493 \cdot 0.001\,\text{N} = 4.93 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$. Finally $1\,\text{nC} = 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$.
**Substitution:** $q = 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{4.93 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}}{8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2}} = 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot 2.342 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{C}}{\text{m}} = 1.874 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{C} = 18.7\,\text{nC}$ and $n = \dfrac{1.874 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{C}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 1.17 \cdot 10^{11}$
**Answer:** $q \approx 19\,\text{nC}$, and about $1.2 \cdot 10^{11}$ electrons have moved away from each ball. Two significant figures, as in the data.
**Reasonableness:** Check: $k\tfrac{q^2}{r^2} = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \tfrac{(1.874 \cdot 10^{-8})^2}{0.080^2}\,\text{N} = 4.93 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$. Tens of nanocoulombs is the order of magnitude of the charge obtained by rubbing, and $10^{11}$ electrons are a negligibly small fraction of all the electrons of the ball.

### c)
**Given:** the charges of the balls before touching $q$ and $q$; the third identical ball uncharged; $r = 8.0\,\text{cm}$; from part a $F_\text{s} = 0.493\,\text{mN}$
**Find:** the electric force $F'$ at the same distance and a comparison of the forces
**Principle:** Charge is conserved. When two identical conducting balls touch, they share their common charge equally, so the charge of the touched ball is $\tfrac{q + 0}{2} = \tfrac{q}{2}$. By Coulomb's law the force is proportional to the product of the charges. The forces on the balls are force and counterforce, so they are equal and opposite (chapters *Electric charge and Coulomb's law* and *Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws*).
**Formula:** $F' = k\dfrac{q \cdot \tfrac{q}{2}}{r^2} = \tfrac12 \cdot k\dfrac{q^2}{r^2} = \tfrac12 F_\text{s}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the force is obtained as a fraction of the force of part a.
**Substitution:** $F' = \tfrac12 \cdot 0.493\,\text{mN} = 0.2465\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $F' \approx 0.25\,\text{mN}$, two significant figures. An equal force acts on each ball, although the charges are $q$ and $\tfrac{q}{2}$.
**Reasonableness:** The charges appear symmetrically in the formula, so the same number gives the force on each ball. When the forces and the masses are the same, the threads still stay symmetrical, although the charges are different.

### d)
**Given:** the charges of part c, $q$ and $\tfrac{q}{2}$; the original distance $r_0 = 8.0\,\text{cm}$; $L = 40.0\,\text{cm}$; the masses the same as before
**Find:** the new distance $r$ between the balls
**Principle:** In the new position of rest the same equilibrium condition $F = mg\tan\alpha$ holds, but the electric force and the angle change together. The angle is small, so $\tan\alpha \approx \sin\alpha = \tfrac{r}{2L}$. Then the equation contains only powers of the distance, and the old and the new situation can be compared as a ratio (chapters *Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces* and *Electric charge and Coulomb's law*).
**Formula:** Before $k\dfrac{q^2}{r_0^2} = mg\dfrac{r_0}{2L}$ and now $k\dfrac{q^2/2}{r^2} = mg\dfrac{r}{2L}$, so $r^3 = \tfrac12 r_0^3$ and $r = \dfrac{r_0}{\sqrt[3]{2}}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the distance is obtained in centimetres directly from $r_0$.
**Substitution:** $r = \dfrac{8.0\,\text{cm}}{\sqrt[3]{2}} = \dfrac{8.0\,\text{cm}}{1.260} = 6.35\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $r \approx 6.3\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures, as in the distance $8.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** A smaller force gives a smaller angle, so the balls approach each other. The distance does not, however, decrease as much as the force: as the balls approach each other, the electric force grows again. The small-angle assumption is good, since $\tfrac{3.2\,\text{cm}}{40.0\,\text{cm}} = 0.08$, and the exact calculation gives $6.35\,\text{cm}$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The whole distance $8.0\,\text{cm}$ is used in the sine of the angle instead of half of it, which makes the force twice as large.
- The electric force is drawn along the thread or towards the other ball, although charges of the same sign repel.
- The square of the distance is forgotten in Coulomb's law, or the distance is substituted in centimetres.
- In part c the charge moves entirely to the third ball, or it is thought to stay unchanged, although identical balls share the charge equally.
- In part c a larger force is thought to act on the ball with the larger charge.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a the small-angle approximation $\tan\alpha \approx \sin\alpha = 0.100$, which gives $F_\text{s} \approx 0.491\,\text{mN}$.
- In part d the exact solution without the small-angle approximation, $r \approx 6.35\,\text{cm}$.

### a)
**Given:** the side of a plate $a = 25.0\,\text{cm}$, the distance between the plates $d = 3.00\,\text{mm}$, the voltage $U = 4.50\,\text{kV}$, for air $\varepsilon_\text{r} = 1.0$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$
**Find:** the capacitance $C$ and the charge $Q$
**Principle:** The distance between the plates is small compared with the size of the plates, so there is a uniform field between the plates and the expression for the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor holds. The charge is directly proportional to the voltage, and the capacitance is their ratio (chapter *The capacitor and its energy*).
**Formula:** $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_\text{r}A}{d}$, where $A = a^2$, and $Q = CU$
**Conversions:** $A = 0.250\,\text{m} \cdot 0.250\,\text{m} = 0.0625\,\text{m}^2$, $d = 3.00\,\text{mm} = 3.00 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m} = 0.00300\,\text{m}$ and $U = 4.50\,\text{kV} = 4.50 \cdot 1000\,\text{V} = 4500\,\text{V}$. Finally $1\,\text{pF} = 10^{-12}\,\text{F}$ and $1\,\mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}\,\text{C}$.
**Substitution:** $C = \dfrac{8.854 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 0.0625\,\text{m}^2}{0.00300\,\text{m}} = 1.845 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{F} = 184.5\,\text{pF}$ and $Q = 1.845 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{F} \cdot 4500\,\text{V} = 8.30 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{C} = 0.830\,\mu\text{C}$
**Answer:** $C \approx 184\,\text{pF}$ and $Q \approx 0.830\,\mu\text{C}$, three significant figures, as in the side of the plate, the distance and the voltage. The relative permittivity of air is very precisely $1$, so the $1.0$ of the table does not limit the precision.
**Reasonableness:** The capacitance of an air-filled capacitor is small, only hundreds of picofarads, although the plates are larger than a palm. The unit is correct: $\tfrac{\text{F}}{\text{m}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{m}} = \text{F}$. The charge is less than a microcoulomb, of the same order of magnitude as the charge discharged in a spark.

### b)
**Given:** from part a $C = 1.845 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{F}$; $U = 4500\,\text{V}$; $d = 0.00300\,\text{m}$; the dielectric strength of air $E_\text{max} = 3\,\text{MV/m}$
**Find:** the stored energy $W$ and the strength $E$ of the field between the plates compared with the dielectric strength
**Principle:** During charging the voltage grows from zero to $U$ in the same ratio as the charge, so the energy $\tfrac12 QU = \tfrac12 CU^2$ is stored in the capacitor. Between the plates the field is uniform, so its strength is the voltage divided by the distance. Breakdown does not occur if the field is smaller than the dielectric strength of air (chapters *The capacitor and its energy* and *Uniform electric field*).
**Formula:** $W = \tfrac12 CU^2$, $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$ and $U_\text{max} = E_\text{max} d$
**Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{J} = 1000\,\text{mJ}$ and $1\,\text{MV/m} = 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}$.
**Substitution:** $W = \tfrac12 \cdot 1.845 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{F} \cdot (4500\,\text{V})^2 = 1.868 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{J} = 1.87\,\text{mJ}$, $E = \dfrac{4500\,\text{V}}{0.00300\,\text{m}} = 1.50 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}$ and $U_\text{max} = 3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m} \cdot 0.00300\,\text{m} = 9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}$
**Answer:** $W \approx 1.87\,\text{mJ}$ and $E \approx 1.50\,\text{MV/m}$, three significant figures. The field is half of the dielectric strength of air, so the air gap withstands it: breakdown would occur only at a voltage of about $9\,\text{kV}$.
**Reasonableness:** The other form gives the same: $\tfrac12 QU = \tfrac12 \cdot 8.30 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{C} \cdot 4500\,\text{V} = 1.87\,\text{mJ}$. The energy is small, but the margin to breakdown is only a factor of two, and humid air, dust or a sharp edge can trigger a spark at a lower voltage too.

### c)
**Given:** from part a $Q = 8.30 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{C}$ and $C = 1.845 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{F}$; for glass $\varepsilon_\text{r} = 5.6$; the glass fills the gap; the source disconnected
**Find:** the new voltage $U'$ and the new energy $W'$
**Principle:** From the plates of a disconnected capacitor the charge cannot go anywhere, so $Q$ stays the same. A dielectric that fills the gap makes the capacitance $\varepsilon_\text{r}$ times as large. Then the voltage decreases, because $U = \tfrac{Q}{C}$, and the energy $\tfrac12 QU$ decreases in the same ratio (chapter *The capacitor and its energy*).
**Formula:** $C' = \varepsilon_\text{r}C$, $U' = \dfrac{Q}{C'}$ and $W' = \tfrac12 QU'$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in SI units, and finally $1\,\text{J} = 1000\,\text{mJ}$.
**Substitution:** $C' = 5.6 \cdot 1.845 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\text{F} = 1.033 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{F}$, $U' = \dfrac{8.30 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{C}}{1.033 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{F}} = 803.6\,\text{V}$ and $W' = \tfrac12 \cdot 8.30 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{C} \cdot 803.6\,\text{V} = 3.335 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{J} = 0.334\,\text{mJ}$
**Answer:** $U' \approx 8.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{V}$ and $W' \approx 0.33\,\text{mJ}$, two significant figures, as $\varepsilon_\text{r} = 5.6$.
**Reasonableness:** Both the voltage and the energy decrease by a factor of $5.6$: $\tfrac{4500\,\text{V}}{5.6} \approx 804\,\text{V}$ and $\tfrac{1.87\,\text{mJ}}{5.6} \approx 0.33\,\text{mJ}$. Inside the glass the field is $\tfrac{804\,\text{V}}{0.00300\,\text{m}} \approx 0.27\,\text{MV/m}$, far below the dielectric strength of glass, $14\,\text{MV/m}$.

### d)
**Find:** where the energy went, and whether the plates of a disconnected capacitor can be touched
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: if the energy of the capacitor decreases, it has done work or turned into another form of energy. The charge and the voltage of a disconnected capacitor stay until it is discharged (chapter *The capacitor and its energy*).
**Model answer:** The energy of the capacitor decreased from about $1.87\,\text{mJ}$ to $0.33\,\text{mJ}$, so about $1.5\,\text{mJ}$ went elsewhere. The glass becomes polarised in the field of the plates: its surface against the positive plate gets a negative charge and the opposite surface a positive charge. The charges of the plates attract these surface charges, so the field pulls the glass plate into the gap between the plates and does positive work on it. If the glass is pushed in slowly, the hand has to hold it back, and the energy goes to the hand and finally into internal energy; if the glass can move freely, it gets kinetic energy, which turns into internal energy through friction. The student's claim is wrong. Disconnecting the source does not discharge the capacitor: the plates still have the charge $0.83\,\mu\text{C}$, and there is a voltage of about $800\,\text{V}$ between them, and before the glass was pushed in as much as $4.5\,\text{kV}$. When you touch the plates, the charge is discharged through the body. The energy of this capacitor is small, but the shock of larger capacitors can be dangerous. That is why a capacitor is always first discharged with an insulated discharge rod or through a resistor before the plates are touched.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The length of the side is taken as the area of the plate, or centimetres and millimetres are not converted to metres.
- In part b the energy is calculated as $QU$ and not $\tfrac12 QU$.
- In part c the voltage is thought to stay the same, as when the source is connected, in which case the energy would increase.
- In part c the capacitance is divided by the relative permittivity instead of multiplied by it.
- In part d it is assumed that disconnecting the source removes the voltage.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b the energy from the form $\tfrac12 QU$, the same result.
- In part c the direct reasoning: when $Q$ stays the same and $C$ becomes $5.6$ times as large, both $U$ and $W = \tfrac{Q^2}{2C}$ decrease by a factor of $5.6$.

**Model answer:** The points lie on a downward-curving curve that levels off as the distance increases. ### b)

### c)
**Given:** the points of the fitted line $(0.20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}; 93\,\text{pF})$ and $(1.00\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}; 376\,\text{pF})$; the side of a plate $20.0\,\text{cm}$; for air $\varepsilon_\text{r} \approx 1.0$
**Find:** the slope $k$ of the line and the electric constant $\varepsilon_0$ determined from it
**Principle:** According to the model $C = \varepsilon_0 A \cdot \tfrac{1}{d} + b$, so the slope of the line is $k = \varepsilon_0 A$. The slope is read from two points of the fitted line far apart, not from the measurement points (chapters *The capacitor and its energy* and *Graphing and fitting a straight line*).
**Reading the graph:** The points $\tfrac{1}{d} = 0.20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}$, $C = 93\,\text{pF}$ and $\tfrac{1}{d} = 1.00\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}$, $C = 376\,\text{pF}$, which are circled in the graph, are read from the line.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{\Delta C}{\Delta(1/d)}$ and $k = \varepsilon_0 A$, so $\varepsilon_0 = \dfrac{k}{A}$
**Conversions:** $A = 0.200\,\text{m} \cdot 0.200\,\text{m} = 0.0400\,\text{m}^2$. The unit of the slope is $\text{pF} \cdot \text{mm} = 10^{-12}\,\text{F} \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} = 10^{-15}\,\text{F}\cdot\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{376\,\text{pF} - 93\,\text{pF}}{1.00\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}} - 0.20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}} = \dfrac{283\,\text{pF}}{0.80\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}} = 353.8\,\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm} = 3.538 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{F}\cdot\text{m}$ and $\varepsilon_0 = \dfrac{3.538 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\text{F}\cdot\text{m}}{0.0400\,\text{m}^2} = 8.84 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$
**Answer:** $k \approx 354\,\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm}$ and $\varepsilon_0 \approx (8.8 \pm 0.3) \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$. The calculator gives the intermediate result $8.84 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$, but the result is given to two significant figures, because the thickness of the spacers alone is uncertain by a few per cent. The result agrees with the table value $8.854 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The unit is correct: $\tfrac{\text{F}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{m}^2} = \tfrac{\text{F}}{\text{m}}$. The intermediate result differs from the table value by only about $0.1\,\%$, which is much less than the uncertainty of the measurement. Such good agreement is partly chance.

### d)
**Given:** the point of the line of part c $(0.20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}; 93\,\text{pF})$ and the slope $k = 353.8\,\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm}$; the side of a plate $200\,\text{mm}$; the distances $1.0\text{–}6.0\,\text{mm}$
**Find:** the point $b$ where the line crosses the vertical axis, its interpretation and the range of validity of the model
**Principle:** The intercept is the value of the line when $\tfrac{1}{d} \to 0$, that is, when the plates are very far apart. Then the mutual capacitance of the plates vanishes, and what is left is the part that the meter measures in any case. The parallel-plate capacitor model requires a uniform field, so the distance must be small compared with the size of the plates (chapters *Graphing and fitting a straight line* and *The capacitor and its energy*).
**Reading the graph:** The dashed extension of the line crosses the vertical axis at about $22\,\text{pF}$, which is marked in the graph with an orange point.
**Formula:** $C = k \cdot \tfrac{1}{d} + b$, so $b = C_1 - k \cdot \tfrac{1}{d_1}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the slope and the value of the quantity $\tfrac{1}{d}$ are in the units $\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm}$ and $\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}}$, so the product is in picofarads.
**Substitution:** $b = 93\,\text{pF} - 353.8\,\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm} \cdot 0.20\,\tfrac{1}{\text{mm}} = 93\,\text{pF} - 70.8\,\text{pF} = 22.2\,\text{pF}$
**Answer:** $b \approx 22\,\text{pF}$, two significant figures, because it is the difference of two almost equal numbers.
**Reasonableness:** The constant term is the capacitance of the leads and the meter themselves: the leads are close to each other and to the terminals of the meter, and the meter measures their capacitance in addition to the capacitance of the plates. Tens of picofarads is the usual order of magnitude for test leads. The model works in the measured range, because even the largest distance, $6.0\,\text{mm}$, is only $3\,\%$ of the side of the plate, $200\,\text{mm}$. If the distance were centimetres, the curving field at the edges would increase the capacitance above the value of the model. At the smallest distances, on the other hand, the uncertainty of the thickness of the spacers and the bending of the plates, for example $\pm 0.1\,\text{mm}$, is $10\,\%$ of the distance $1.0\,\text{mm}$. That is why the slope is best determined from the whole line and not from a single point.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The capacitance is plotted as a function of the distance, and the slope is read from the curved graph.
- The line is forced through the origin, which makes the slope and $\varepsilon_0$ too large.
- The unit of the slope, $\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm}$, is not converted, or the millimetres are converted the wrong way round, which makes the result a thousand times too large or a thousandth.
- The area is taken as $20.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 20.0\,\text{cm} = 400\,\text{m}^2$ without converting the centimetres.
- In part d the constant term is explained as a measurement error without saying where it comes from.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b the graph of $C$ as a function of the quantity $\tfrac{1}{d}$ in the unit $\tfrac{1}{\text{m}}$; the slope is then in the unit $\text{pF}\cdot\text{m}$.
- In part c the fit given by a calculator or a spreadsheet, about $354\,\text{pF}\cdot\text{mm}$, and $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.8 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$.

## fy9-sahkokentta-s4

### a)
**Find:** an assessment of the claim that the bird on the conductor is safe because its feet insulate
**Principle:** A current flows through the body only if there is a voltage, that is, a potential difference, between two points of the body. The voltage between different points of a conductor comes from Ohm's law when the resistance of the conductor between them and the current are known (chapters *Electrical safety at home and at work* and *Potential energy and potential in an electric field*).
**Model answer:** The bird's feet are about $5\,\text{cm}$ apart. The resistance of the conductor between the feet is $0.30\,\tfrac{\Omega}{\text{km}} \cdot 0.05 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{km} = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\Omega$, so the voltage between the feet is $U = RI = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\Omega \cdot 100\,\text{A} = 1.5\,\text{mV}$. Such a small voltage does not drive a noticeable current through the bird's body. The bird is indeed at almost the same potential as the conductor, about $12\,\text{kV}$ relative to earth (the $20\,\text{kV}$ of the line is the voltage between the phase conductors), but potential alone is not dangerous: a current needs two points at different potentials. The bird does not touch the ground, the pole or another conductor, so no current flows through its body. The conclusion of the claim is right, but the justification is wrong: the bird would be safe even if its feet conducted electricity well. The bird is in danger if it touches another conductor or an earthed part of the pole with its wing at the same time.

### b)
**Find:** why the angler can get a shock without touching the conductor, and the smallest air gap that can break down
**Principle:** Air is an insulator only when the electric field is smaller than the dielectric strength of air. In a uniform field an air gap $d$ withstands the voltage $E_\text{max} d$ (chapters *The capacitor and its energy* and *Electrical safety at home and at work*).
**Model answer:** Carbon fibre conducts electricity. When the angler stands on the ground, the tip of the rod is almost at earth potential, so the whole voltage between the conductor and earth, about $12\,\text{kV}$, is across the air gap between the tip and the conductor. The nominal voltage of the line, $20\,\text{kV}$, is between two phase conductors, and one conductor is at a voltage of $\tfrac{20\,\text{kV}}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 12\,\text{kV}$ relative to earth. With the uniform field model the smallest gap that breaks down is $d = \tfrac{U}{E_\text{max}} = \tfrac{12 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}}{3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}} \approx 4\,\text{mm}$. The estimate is a lower limit for the safety distance, not a safe distance: near a thin conductor and the tip of the rod the field is much stronger than $\tfrac{U}{d}$, and humid air, dirt and the fishing line weaken the insulation, so an arc can strike across a clearly longer gap too. After the breakdown the air conducts, and the current flows through the arc, the rod and the angler's body to earth. If the resistance of the current path is, for example, $10\,\text{k}\Omega$, $12\,\text{kV}$ drives a current of about $1.2\,\text{A}$ through the body, tens of times larger than the about $50\,\text{mA}$ that is enough for ventricular fibrillation. That is why you do not fish or fly a kite under power lines, and you do not approach the conductors with any object.

### c)
**Find:** why you must not go close to a conductor that has fallen to the ground, how to leave the area, and why a cow is in greater danger than a person
**Principle:** A current conducted into the ground spreads into the soil, and the potential of the ground decreases as the distance from the point of contact increases. The voltage between two points is the difference of their potentials (chapter *Electrical safety at home and at work*).
**Model answer:** When the conductor touches the ground, the current flows through the soil. The potential of the ground is highest at the point of contact, almost the voltage of the conductor, and decreases further away. There is therefore a voltage between two points, and it is largest near the point of contact, where the potential changes most steeply. If a person stands with the feet apart, there is a step voltage between the feet, and the current flows up one leg and down the other. The step voltage is the larger, the closer you are to the point of contact and the longer the step is. That is why you do not approach a fallen conductor. If you are already close, you leave with short steps taken with the feet together or by jumping with both feet together, so that the feet are at almost the same potential. The damage is reported by calling the emergency number $112$, and you do not return to the area before the network company has cut the power. The distance between the front and hind legs of a cow is over a metre, so the voltage between them is much larger than across the short step of a person. In addition, the current flows from the front legs of the cow to the hind legs through the chest and the heart, so even a smaller voltage is dangerous.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The safety of the bird is explained by the bird not being earthed, without mentioning that there is no voltage between its feet.
- A high potential as such is interpreted as dangerous, although the danger comes only from a potential difference.
- In part b the voltage of the air gap is taken as the $20\,\text{kV}$ between the phase conductors, although between the tip of the rod and the conductor there is the about $12\,\text{kV}$ between the conductor and earth.
- The $4\,\text{mm}$ of part b is presented as a safe distance, although it is the lower limit of a uniform field.
- In part c the advice is to run away with long steps, which increases the step voltage.

## fy9-sahkokentta-s5

### a)
**Given:** the table of the report: the voltages $U$ and the currents $I$ with seven loads; the area of the panel $A = 60\,\text{cm}^2$; the light intensity assumed in the report $G = 1000\,\text{W/m}^2$
**Find:** the largest power $P_\text{max}$ of the panel and the efficiency $\eta$
**Principle:** The power given by the panel is the product of the voltage and the current measured at the same time with the same load. The power is zero both in a short circuit and with no load, and between them it is largest at the maximum power point. The efficiency is the largest electric power divided by the power of the light falling on the panel (chapter *Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell*).
**Formula:** $P = UI$ with each load and $\eta = \dfrac{P_\text{max}}{GA}$
**Conversions:** The currents from milliamperes to amperes, for example $199\,\text{mA} = 199 \cdot 0.001\,\text{A} = 0.199\,\text{A}$, and $A = 60\,\text{cm}^2 = 60 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2 = 0.0060\,\text{m}^2$.
**Substitution:** The powers are in order $2.10\,\text{V} \cdot 0.210\,\text{A} = 0.441\,\text{W}$, $0.872\,\text{W}$, $4.97\,\text{V} \cdot 0.199\,\text{A} = 0.989\,\text{W}$, $0.959\,\text{W}$, $0.805\,\text{W}$, $0.574\,\text{W}$ and $0.358\,\text{W}$, so $P_\text{max} = 0.989\,\text{W}$ and $\eta = \dfrac{0.989\,\text{W}}{1000\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot 0.0060\,\text{m}^2} = \dfrac{0.989\,\text{W}}{6.0\,\text{W}} = 0.165 = 16.5\,\%$
**Answer:** $P_\text{max} \approx 0.99\,\text{W}$ with the load $25\,\Omega$ and $\eta \approx 16\,\%$, two significant figures, as in the area $60\,\text{cm}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** The largest power is smaller than the $U_\text{oc}I_\text{sc} = 1.28\,\text{W}$ of the report, as it should be, because the panel does not give the open-circuit voltage and the short-circuit current at the same time. Every row of the table satisfies Ohm's law for the load, for example $\tfrac{4.97\,\text{V}}{0.199\,\text{A}} \approx 25\,\Omega$, so the measurements are consistent. The efficiency of ordinary silicon cells is about $15\text{–}22\,\%$.

### b)
**Find:** an assessment of the conclusion of the report and the reliability of the measurement
**Principle:** A conclusion is reliable only if the power has been measured correctly, the power of the light is known and the uncertainty of the result has been estimated (chapters *Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell* and *Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting*).
**Model answer:** The most important mistake of the report is systematic. With no load the current is zero and in a short circuit the voltage is zero, so the power of the panel is zero in both. The product $U_\text{oc}I_\text{sc} = 1.28\,\text{W}$ is larger than any power the panel really gives. The student's own table shows $0.99\,\text{W}$ as the largest power, so the efficiency is about $16\,\%$ and not $21\,\%$. The second shortcoming is that the light intensity was not measured. The value $1000\,\text{W/m}^2$ is only an assumption, and the real value depends on the height of the sun, the angle of the panel relative to the rays of the sun and thin clouds; in May in Finland it can be clearly smaller. The report also lacks information on the orientation and the temperature of the panel. Thirdly, the report has no uncertainty: $21.35\,\%$ gives four figures, although the area is given to two figures and the light intensity is an estimate. The conclusion that the panel is better than the manufacturer states is wrong: the correctly calculated efficiency, about $16\,\%$, agrees with the manufacturer's value $15\,\%$ within the precision of the measurements.

### c)
**Find:** an improved experiment that gives the efficiency reliably
**Principle:** The efficiency needs the largest electric power and, at the same time, the power of the light falling on the panel. The largest power is found reliably only when the power is measured with many loads and the peak is found from a graph (chapters *Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell* and *Graphing and fitting a straight line*).
**Model answer:** The panel is placed perpendicular to the rays of the sun, and the light intensity is measured with an irradiance meter in the plane of the panel just before and after the measurement series. A variable resistor is connected to the panel, and the voltage and the current are measured with, for example, $15$–$20$ loads, most densely around the power peak, in the range of about $20$–$35\,\Omega$. The measurement is made quickly so that the light intensity and the temperature of the panel stay as constant as possible, and the series is repeated. $P = UI$ is calculated for each point, and the graph of the power is plotted as a function of the voltage. The largest power is read from the peak of the graph. The efficiency is calculated as $\eta = \tfrac{P_\text{max}}{GA}$, with the area of the cells measured with a ruler. The uncertainty is estimated from the uncertainties of the light intensity, the area and the power and from the spread of the repetitions, and the result is compared with the manufacturer's value with its uncertainties.

- The largest power is looked for at the largest voltage or the largest current and not at their largest product.
- The area $60\,\text{cm}^2$ is converted wrongly, for example to $0.60\,\text{m}^2$, which makes the efficiency a hundred times too small.
- In part b it is only stated that the measurement has errors, without naming the systematic error and its direction.

## fy9-sahkokentta-s6

### a)
**Given:** the accelerating voltage $U = 1.10\,\text{kV}$, the distance between the grids $d = 1.0\,\text{mm}$, the mass of an ion $m = 2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$ and its charge $+e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, the initial speed almost zero
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the ions and the strength $E$ of the field between the grids
**Principle:** Between the grids only the electric force acts on an ion: the weight of the ions is negligible, and there is no air resistance in space. The electric force is conservative, so the decrease $eU$ in the potential energy of the ion turns into kinetic energy. The grids are close to each other, so the field between them is uniform (chapters *Potential energy and potential in an electric field* and *Uniform electric field*).
**Formula:** $\tfrac12 mv^2 = eU$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m}}$, and $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$
**Conversions:** $U = 1.10\,\text{kV} = 1.10 \cdot 1000\,\text{V} = 1100\,\text{V}$ and $d = 1.0\,\text{mm} = 1.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m} = 0.0010\,\text{m}$. In addition $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$, and finally $1\,\text{km/s} = 1000\,\text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1100\,\text{V}}{2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{1.617 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m/s} = 40.2\,\text{km/s}$ and $E = \dfrac{1100\,\text{V}}{0.0010\,\text{m}} = 1.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 40.2\,\text{km/s}$, three significant figures, as in the voltage $1.10\,\text{kV}$, and $E \approx 1.1\,\text{MV/m}$, two significant figures, as in the distance $1.0\,\text{mm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The kinetic energy of the ion is $1100\,\text{eV}$. An electron would get the same energy, but a xenon ion is about $2.4 \cdot 10^{5}$ times heavier, so its speed is $\sqrt{2.4 \cdot 10^{5}} \approx 490$ times smaller. Still, the speed is about ten times the exhaust speed of a chemical rocket engine, about $3\text{–}4\,\text{km/s}$. In air the field $1.1\,\text{MV/m}$ would come close to breakdown, but in a vacuum there is no gas that could conduct.

### b)
**Given:** the current of the ion beam $I = 1.60\,\text{A}$; from part a $v = 4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m/s}$; $m = 2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$; $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
**Find:** the thrust $F$ that the ion beam gives the satellite
**Principle:** Current is the charge flowing per unit time, so it tells how many ions the thruster sends out per second. The mass $\Delta m$ of the ions leaving in the time $\Delta t$ gets the speed $v$ from almost rest. By the impulse principle their change in momentum $\Delta m \cdot v$ is the impulse $F\,\Delta t$ of the force the thruster exerts on them. By Newton's third law the ions exert an equal and opposite force on the thruster, which pushes the satellite (chapters *Momentum and impulse* and *Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws*).
**Formula:** $N = \dfrac{I}{e}$, $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = Nm$ and $F\,\Delta t = \Delta m \cdot v$, so $F = \dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,v = \dfrac{Imv}{e}$
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{A} = 1\,\text{C/s}$. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.
**Substitution:** $N = \dfrac{1.60\,\text{C/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 9.988 \cdot 10^{18}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$, $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = 9.988 \cdot 10^{18}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot 2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg} = 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{kg/s}$ and $F = 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{kg/s} \cdot 4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m/s} = 0.08754\,\text{N} = 87.5\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $F \approx 87.5\,\text{mN}$, three significant figures, as in the current and the voltage.
**Reasonableness:** The force is as large as the weight of an object of about $9\,\text{g}$ at the surface of the Earth, that is, very small. The unit is correct: $\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = \text{kg} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = \text{N}$.

### c)
**Given:** from part b $F = 0.08754\,\text{N}$ and $\tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{kg/s}$; the mass of the satellite $M = 650\,\text{kg}$; the time $30$ days
**Find:** the change $\Delta v$ in the speed of the satellite and the xenon used, $m_\text{Xe}$
**Principle:** The thrust is constant, so by Newton's second law the acceleration of the satellite is constant, and the change in speed is the acceleration times the time. The thrust is assumed to be along the orbit, and only the change in speed it causes is considered. The mass of the satellite stays almost constant if little xenon is used; this is checked at the end (chapters *Newton's second law* and *Momentum and impulse*).
**Formula:** $a = \dfrac{F}{M}$, $\Delta v = a\,\Delta t$ and $m_\text{Xe} = \dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} \cdot \Delta t$
**Conversions:** $\Delta t = 30 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 2.592 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $a = \dfrac{0.08754\,\text{N}}{650\,\text{kg}} = 1.347 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m/s}^2$, $\Delta v = 1.347 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 2.592 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{s} = 349\,\text{m/s}$ and $m_\text{Xe} = 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{kg/s} \cdot 2.592 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{s} = 5.64\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $\Delta v \approx 349\,\text{m/s}$ and $m_\text{Xe} \approx 5.64\,\text{kg}$, three significant figures, as in the thrust.
**Reasonableness:** Less than $1\,\%$ of the mass of the satellite is used as xenon, so the constant-mass assumption is good. By the impulse principle a chemical engine with an exhaust speed of about $3\,\text{km/s}$ would need about $\tfrac{650\,\text{kg} \cdot 349\,\text{m/s}}{3000\,\text{m/s}} \approx 76\,\text{kg}$ of fuel for the same change in speed, over ten times as much.

### d)
**Find:** whether the solar panels are enough, why the thrust is small despite the large power, and why an ion thruster is used anyway
**Principle:** A current flowing across the accelerating voltage gets the power $P = UI$. When only the electric force does work, the same power goes into the kinetic energy of the ions. The thrust is the change in momentum of the ions per second (chapters *Potential energy and potential in an electric field* and *Momentum and impulse*).
**Model answer:** The ion beam needs the power $P = UI = 1100\,\text{V} \cdot 1.60\,\text{A} = 1.76\,\text{kW}$. It is as large as the kinetic energy of the ions per second, $\tfrac12 \cdot 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot (4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}})^2 = 1.76\,\text{kW}$, as the conservation of energy requires. The $2.5\,\text{kW}$ of the panels is therefore enough for the beam, and the remaining $0.7\,\text{kW}$ goes to ionisation, neutralisation and the other devices of the satellite. The thrust is $F = \tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,v$ and the power $P = \tfrac12 \tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,v^2$, so $F = \tfrac{2P}{v}$. With a given power a large ion speed gives a small thrust, only $87.5\,\text{mN}$. On the other hand, a large speed means that each kilogram of propellant gives a lot of momentum, so little xenon is used. An ion thruster is not suitable for launch or fast manoeuvres, but for long orbit changes of weeks or months it is good, because it saves tens of kilograms of mass.

- In part b the current is interpreted directly as the number of ions per second without dividing it by the elementary charge.
- In part b the thrust is calculated from the electric force $eE$ on one ion and not from the momentum of all the ions leaving per second.
- In part c the days are converted to seconds wrongly, for example $30 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$.
- In part d a large power is assumed to mean a large thrust.
