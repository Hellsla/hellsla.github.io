When classical physics ran out of answers

Why could the best physics of 1900 not explain the glow of a hot piece of metal?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why classical physics could not explain the radiation of hot objects.
  • calculate the peak wavelength or the temperature of a black body with Wien's law.
  • describe Planck's energy packets and place the main steps of modern physics on a timeline.

Prior knowledge

  • Light is an electromagnetic wave, and its speed, frequency and wavelength are linked by $c = f\lambda$ (earlier physics courses)
  • The absolute temperature in kelvins is $T = (t + 273.15)\ \text{K}$, where $t$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius (earlier physics courses)

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 1.

1Think about it

On 27 April 1900 the famous physicist Lord Kelvin gave a lecture at the Royal Institution in London. Physics seemed almost complete: Newton's mechanics described the motion of planets and machines, Maxwell's theory described electricity, magnetism and light, and thermodynamics described heat. Kelvin praised this beautiful theory, but he warned of two "clouds" over it, two problems it could not solve. One cloud was about the rule for how energy is shared among the parts of a body. The same rule, applied to the light inside a hot oven, ran into trouble with something very ordinary: the glow of hot objects. Within thirty years both clouds had turned into new physics.

How can something as ordinary as a glowing piece of hot metal break the physics of 1900?

Light is an electromagnetic wave

In 1864 James Clerk Maxwell published a theory that joined electricity, magnetism and light. Light, he said, is an electromagnetic wave: changing electric and magnetic fields that travel through space at the speed $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Between 1886 and 1889 Heinrich Hertz made such waves with electric sparks and detected them: radio waves. For every wave, the speed, the frequency $f$ and the wavelength $\lambda$ are linked by $c = f\lambda$.

Visible light is only a narrow band of the electromagnetic spectrum. Waves shorter than violet light are ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays; waves longer than red light are infrared, microwaves and radio waves.

Prediction 2: sharing the energy

Think of the radiation in a hot oven as standing waves, like the vibrations of a guitar string. There is no limit to how short a wave can be. Classical physics gives every wave pattern the same average energy. What does this idea predict for the radiation at very short wavelengths?

  • Right: this is the classical prediction of Rayleigh and Jeans. Ever more patterns, each with the same share, give ever more power at short wavelengths, and an infinite total. The measured curve falls to zero instead: this failure was later called the ultraviolet catastrophe.

  • Each pattern gets the same share, but the number of patterns grows as the wavelength gets shorter. So the predicted power grows without limit towards short wavelengths, which real ovens do not show.

  • That is what real hot objects do, but not what the classical idea predicts. If every pattern gets the same share and there are ever more short-wave patterns, the predicted power at short wavelengths grows. The classical model and the measurements disagree.

3Investigate

Reading the curves

Every object sends out thermal radiation: electromagnetic radiation produced by the heat motion of its particles. A cold object sends out mostly invisible infrared. A hot one also glows. To compare objects fairly, physicists study an ideal object called a black body. It absorbs all radiation that falls on it, so all the radiation that leaves it is its own thermal radiation. A small hole in the wall of a hot oven is a very good black body, and the Sun and a lamp filament are fairly close to one.

Around 1900, careful measurements in Berlin showed how the power of this radiation is spread over the wavelengths. The figure shows the result as model values from the law that fits those measurements. Study it carefully: how do the curves change when the temperature rises?

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A black body sends out radiation at all wavelengths, but not equally. Its spectrum has one peak. When the temperature rises, the body radiates more power at every wavelength, and the peak moves to shorter wavelengths. This is why a heated iron bar first glows dull red, then orange, and finally white: at first only the long-wave edge of its spectrum reaches the visible band, and later the whole band is covered.

    The Sun's surface, at about $5800\ \text{K}$, has its peak near $500\ \text{nm}$, in the middle of the visible band. Your skin, at about $300\ \text{K}$, has its peak near $10\ \mu\text{m}$, in the infrared: thermal cameras see it.

    The ultraviolet catastrophe

    Classical physics tried to explain the curve. In an oven, the radiation forms standing waves between the walls, like the vibrations of a guitar string. There is no limit to how short a standing wave can be, and the shorter the wavelength, the more different wave patterns fit into the oven. Classical physics says that at a temperature $T$ every wave pattern gets the same average share of the energy.

    Lord Rayleigh worked out what this means in 1900, and Rayleigh and James Jeans completed the calculation in 1905. At long wavelengths their result agrees with the measurements. At short wavelengths it does not: the predicted power grows without limit, and the total energy of the radiation would be infinite. Every warm oven would flood the room with ultraviolet and X-rays. In 1911 Paul Ehrenfest named this failure the ultraviolet catastrophe.

    The measured curve falls to zero at short wavelengths. Classical physics was not just a little inaccurate here; its basic idea gave an impossible answer.

    Wien's law and Planck's energy packets

    The peak wavelength $\lambda_\text{max}$ is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature $T$ (Wilhelm Wien, 1893):

    $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_\text{max} T &= b \\ b &= 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K} \end{aligned}$$

    so $\lambda_\text{max} = b/T$. The temperature must be in kelvins.

    On 19 October 1900 Max Planck presented a formula that fitted the Berlin measurements at every wavelength. To derive it, on 14 December 1900, he assumed that the walls of the oven can give and take radiation energy only in packets, or quanta, of size

    $$E = hf$$

    where $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}$ is Planck's constant. A short wave has a high frequency, so its packet is large. At a temperature $T$ the typical energy available from the heat motion is small, of the order of $k_\text{B}T$, where $k_\text{B} = 1.381 \cdot 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ is the Boltzmann constant. Very few short-wave patterns ever receive a whole packet, so they carry almost no energy. The catastrophe disappears.

    Planck called his assumption "an act of despair" and saw it as a mathematical step. Five years later Einstein took the packets seriously as particles of light.

      Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

      Example 1

      A filament lamp has a tungsten filament at a temperature of about $2700\ \text{K}$. At which wavelength does the filament radiate most strongly? How large is one energy packet $hf$ at that wavelength, in electronvolts? Is the peak in the visible band?

      Step 1/7

      Given: ${T = 2700\ \text{K}}$, ${b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K}}$, ${h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s}}$, ${c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}$. Find the peak wavelength $\lambda_\text{max}$ and the packet energy $E$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle: The filament is close to a black body, so Wien's law gives the peak. The energy of one packet is $E = hf$, and the frequency follows from $c = f\lambda$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula: Solved in symbols:

      $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_\text{max} &= \frac{b}{T} \\ E &= hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda_\text{max}} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions: The temperature is already in kelvins. The energy in joules is changed to electronvolts with ${1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{J}}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution: $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_\text{max} &= \frac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{2700\ \text{K}} \\ &= 1.073 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} \\[6pt] hc &= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{s} \\ &\quad \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \\ &= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{m} \\[6pt] E &= \frac{hc}{\lambda_\text{max}} \\ &= \frac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\ \text{J} \cdot \text{m}}{1.073 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}} \\ &= 1.851 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \\ &= \frac{1.851 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{J}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \text{J/eV}} \\ &= 1.155\ \text{eV} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer: The filament radiates most strongly at ${\lambda_\text{max} \approx 1.07\ \mu\text{m}}$, that is, ${1070\ \text{nm}}$, and one packet there has ${E \approx 1.16\ \text{eV}}$. Three significant figures, because the temperature is a rounded value. The peak is in the infrared, not in the visible band ($380\text{–}750\ \text{nm}$).

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness: The filament is about half as hot as the Sun's surface, so its peak should be at about twice the Sun's $500\ \text{nm}$: $1070\ \text{nm}$ fits. A visible photon has $1.6\text{–}3.3\ \text{eV}$, and $1.16\ \text{eV}$ is less, as it should be for infrared. This is why an old filament lamp turns most of its electrical energy into infrared radiation (and heat), not visible light.

      Example 2: Apply: a thermal camera

      A thermal camera shows that the radiation from a person's forehead has its peak at $\lambda_\text{max} = 9.4\ \mu\text{m}$. Treat the skin as a black body and calculate its temperature in kelvins.

      K
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which law links the peak wavelength and the temperature?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $T = b/\lambda_\text{max}$ with $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Write the wavelength in metres: $9.4\ \mu\text{m} = 9.4 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      $\lambda_\text{max} = 9.4\ \mu\text{m}$, $b = 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K}$. Find the temperature $T$.

      Principle

      The skin behaves almost like a black body, so Wien's law links the peak and the temperature.

      Formula

      $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$, so $T = b/\lambda_\text{max}$.

      Unit conversions

      $\lambda_\text{max}$$= 9.4\ \mu\text{m}$
      $= 9.4 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$

      Substitution

      $$T = \frac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{9.4 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}} = 308.3\ \text{K}$$

      Answer

      $T \approx 308\ \text{K}$

      Which is about $35\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The wavelength has two significant figures, so the answer is good to a few kelvins.

      Reasonableness

      The skin of the forehead is a little cooler than the inside of the body ($37\ ^\circ\text{C} = 310\ \text{K}$), so $308\ \text{K}$ is very reasonable.

      Optional Example 3: Find the error
      A student calculates the peak wavelength of a pizza oven at $400\ ^\circ\text{C}$:

      $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_\text{max} &= \frac{2.898 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \cdot \text{K}}{400} \\ &= 7.2\ \mu\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      What is wrong?

      • Look at the units: $b$ is in $\text{m} \cdot \text{K}$, so the temperature must be in kelvins. $400\ ^\circ\text{C} = 673\ \text{K}$, and the peak is at $4.3\ \mu\text{m}$, not $7.2\ \mu\text{m}$.

      • Then a hotter oven would have a longer peak wavelength, but the curves show the opposite. $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$ gives $\lambda_\text{max} = b/T$. The real error is the temperature scale.

      • Every body has a peak in its thermal radiation, even when it is not visible. The oven's peak is in the infrared. The error is that $400$ is in degrees Celsius, not kelvins.

      • Right. Wien's law uses the absolute temperature: $T = (400 + 273)\ \text{K} = 673\ \text{K}$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = b/T$ gives ${\lambda_\text{max} \approx 4.3\ \mu\text{m}}$, still in the infrared.

      Common misconception

      "Red hot is the hottest." In everyday life red means danger, but in thermal radiation red is the colour of the coolest glow. A hotter body has its peak at a shorter wavelength, so its glow moves from red through orange and yellow to white, and very hot stars look bluish.

      A second mistake is to put degrees Celsius into Wien's law. The law needs the absolute temperature: $T = (t + 273.15)\ \text{K}$. A third is to think that Planck discovered photons. Planck quantised the exchange of energy in the oven walls and did not believe that light itself is made of particles; that was Einstein's step in 1905.

        • White light contains all visible wavelengths, including blue and violet. A body that sends out enough of these must be hotter, because its peak lies at a shorter wavelength.

        • The colour of a glow depends on the temperature of the body (Wien's law), not on the room lighting. The white glow belongs to the higher temperature.

        • Right. At a higher temperature the peak moves to shorter wavelengths and the bar radiates strongly over the whole visible band, so the glow looks white.

          Summary

          • Light is an electromagnetic wave, $c = f\lambda$; visible light is a narrow part of the electromagnetic spectrum.
          • Every object sends out thermal radiation. The spectrum of a black body has one peak, and Wien's law $\lambda_\text{max} T = b$ says that the peak moves to shorter wavelengths when the temperature rises.
