Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs

Why can the distance a cyclist has travelled be four kilometres, even though her displacement is zero?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • describe motion as position as a function of time.
  • calculate displacement and velocity with their signs.
  • read the velocity of uniform motion from the slope of a graph of position against time.

Prior knowledge

  • Velocity from lower secondary school: distance divided by time, $v = s/t$
  • Reading a coordinate system: axes, units and the steepness of a line

1Think about it

A cyclist leaves home and rides steadily $2.0\,\text{km}$ along a straight road to the shop. She buys milk and rides straight back home. At the end the odometer shows $4.0\,\text{km}$, but a tracking app that measures the position from home shows $0\,\text{m}$. The app also draws a graph with time on the horizontal axis and the position of the cyclist on the vertical axis.

What would such a graph look like, and what could be read from it about the cyclist's velocity and direction of travel?

Review from lower secondary school

A runner covers $150\,\text{m}$ in $20\,\text{s}$ at a uniform speed. What is her velocity?

  • Correct: $v = \dfrac{150\,\text{m}}{20\,\text{s}} = 7.5\,\text{m/s}$. In this chapter the same idea gets a more exact form: the change in position takes the place of the distance, and the velocity gets a sign.

  • You divided the time by the distance. The result 0.13 is seconds per metre, not metres per second. Velocity is distance divided by time.

  • You multiplied the distance and the time. Velocity tells how much distance is covered in a second, so the distance is divided by the time: $v = \dfrac{150\,\text{m}}{20\,\text{s}}$.

  • You subtracted the time from the distance, but different quantities cannot be subtracted from each other: metres and seconds are not the same unit. Velocity is a ratio $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$.

How to use

Press Start or drag the Moment $t$ slider and watch what happens on the track and in the graph. Choose a Case and try how the graphs change. The direction and length of the arrow show the velocity, and the points on the track mark whole seconds. The line of the graph is drawn for the moments you have visited.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation, not measurements of real carts. The carts move along a straight track at a constant velocity and are at full speed right away. Friction, acceleration and the size of the carts are ignored. The exception is case 4: cart $\text{E}$ moves forward for $4\,\text{s}$, stands still for $2\,\text{s}$ and returns for $4\,\text{s}$ at the same speed, so it ends up at its starting position.

How the calculation works

The velocities are not given as numbers. Read the positions from the track, the clock or the graph and calculate the velocity as the ratio of the change in position to the change in time $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$. The distance $s$ is the length of the route travelled and the displacement $\Delta x$ is the change in position, so in case 4 $\Delta x = 0\,\text{m}$ even though $s \neq 0$.

4Theory summary

Key idea

Describing motion begins with a convention. We choose the straight line along which the object moves, and an origin and a positive direction on it: this is the $x$-axis. The position $x$ tells where the object is relative to the origin. The sign tells which side: the position $x = -3\,\text{m}$ is three metres from the origin in the negative direction. Time $t$ is measured with a clock, and the motion is described by giving the position at every moment.

When an object moves from the position $x_1$ to the position $x_2$, its displacement is the change in position $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$. Displacement is a vector quantity: it has a magnitude and a direction, and on a line the direction is given by the sign. Plus means the positive direction and minus the opposite one. Displacement is not the same thing as the distance travelled $s$. The distance is the length of the route, and it cannot be negative. If a cyclist rides to the shop and back, the distance is four kilometres, but the displacement is zero, because the final position is the same as the initial position.

Velocity tells how fast and in which direction the position changes. It is also a vector quantity, and its sign tells the direction. The absolute value of the velocity $|v|$ is the speed. In uniform motion the velocity stays constant: the position changes by the same amount in every time interval of equal length.

Motion can be shown as a $(t,x)$ graph, with time on the horizontal axis and position on the vertical axis. The graph of uniform motion is a straight line. The steepness of the line, that is its slope, is the velocity: a steeper line means a greater velocity, a horizontal line means being at rest and a falling line means motion in the negative direction. The graph is not a picture of the path of the object: a rising line does not mean uphill, but that the position increases.

Average velocity and the equation of uniform motion

When the position changes by $\Delta x$ during the time interval $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$, the average velocity is the displacement divided by the time:

$$v_\text{avg} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}$$

where $v_\text{avg}$ is the average velocity (m/s), $\Delta x$ the displacement (m), $\Delta t$ the elapsed time (s) and $x_1$ and $x_2$ the positions (m) at the moments $t_1$ and $t_2$. As vectors, the velocity is written $\overline{v}$ and the displacement $\Delta\overline{x}$. On a line we use their components $v$ and $\Delta x$, whose sign tells the direction.

In uniform motion the average velocity is the same over any time interval, and it is called the velocity $v$. In non-uniform motion the average velocity only tells the constant velocity at which the same displacement would be covered in the same time. If the object is at the position $x_0$ at the moment $t = 0$, solving the formula for the position gives the position as a function of time:

$$x = x_0 + v\,t$$

where $x$ is the position at the moment $t$ (m), $x_0$ the initial position (m), $v$ the velocity (m/s) and $t$ the time (s). This is the equation of the straight line in the $(t,x)$ graph: $x_0$ is where the line crosses the position axis and $v$ is its slope.

The slope is calculated by choosing two points far apart on the line and dividing the change in position by the change in time. In a graph drawn from measured points, a line is fitted through the points so that the points lie evenly on both sides of it. The slope is calculated from the line, not from two neighbouring measured points, because every measurement has a small error.

Common misconception

A $(t,x)$ graph is not a picture of the path of the object: a rising line does not mean uphill, but that the position increases. Another mistake is to think of a negative velocity as a slow velocity. The sign only tells the direction: the velocity $-3\,\text{m/s}$ is greater in magnitude than $+2\,\text{m/s}$. A third mistake is to draw a conclusion about the velocity from the position: an object can be ahead and still move more slowly.

Summary

  • The position $x$ tells where the object is relative to the origin. The displacement $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ is the change in position, and its sign tells the direction. The distance is the length of the route and is not negative.
  • The velocity $v = \Delta x / \Delta t$ is a vector quantity. In uniform motion it is constant, and the position is $x = x_0 + v\,t$.
  • In a $(t,x)$ graph the line of uniform motion is straight. Its slope is the velocity, and its sign tells the direction.
  • A line is fitted to measured data, and the velocity is calculated from points chosen far apart on the line.
  • The average velocity of a back-and-forth motion can be zero even though the distance is not zero.

    Worked example

    Problem

    A jogger runs at a uniform velocity. From the graph we read that at the moment $t = 0$ she is at the position $20\,\text{m}$ and at the moment $t = 40\,\text{s}$ at the position $120\,\text{m}$. What is the jogger's velocity, and where is she at the moment $t = 60\,\text{s}$?

    Step 1/7

    Given and find. Two points are read from the graph: $(t_1$; $x_1) = (0\,\text{s}$; $20\,\text{m})$ and $(t_2$; $x_2) = (40\,\text{s}$; $120\,\text{m})$. We are asked for the velocity $v$ and the position $x$ at the moment $t = 60\,\text{s}$.

    Step 2/7

    Principle. The jogger's motion is uniform, so the velocity stays constant and the $(t, x)$ graph is a straight line. Then the velocity is the slope of the line, that is the change in position divided by the change in time. Once the velocity is known, the position is obtained by adding the product of the velocity and the time to the initial position.

    Step 3/7

    Formula solved. The velocity and the position are solved symbolically before using numbers:

    $$\begin{aligned} v &= \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1} \\ x &= x_1 + v\,t \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4/7

    Unit conversions. The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so the velocity comes directly in the unit $\text{m/s}$. No conversions are needed. The initial position is $x_1 = 20\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t_1 = 0$, so $60\,\text{s}$ can be used directly as the time $t$.

    Step 5/7

    Substitution. Substitute the numerical values with their units:

    $$\begin{aligned} v &= \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1} \\ &= \frac{120\,\text{m} - 20\,\text{m}}{40\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} \\ &= 2.5\,\text{m/s} \\[4pt] x &= x_1 + v\,t \\ &= 20\,\text{m} + 2.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 60\,\text{s} \\ &= 20\,\text{m} + 150\,\text{m} \\ &= 170\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 6/7

    Result. The velocity is $v = 2.5\,\text{m/s}$ and the position at the moment $60\,\text{s}$ is $x = 170\,\text{m}$ or $1.7 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$. The velocity has two significant figures, because the time interval $40\,\text{s}$ has two. In the position, the product $2.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 60\,\text{s} = 150\,\text{m}$ also has two figures, so the sum $20\,\text{m} + 150\,\text{m}$ is known only to the accuracy of tens of metres: $x = 1.7 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$.

    Step 7/7

    Reasonableness. During the first 40 seconds the position grew by $100\,\text{m}$, so during the next 20 seconds it grows by half of that, $50\,\text{m}$. The position is $120\,\text{m} + 50\,\text{m} = 170\,\text{m}$, the same result. The velocity $2.5\,\text{m/s}$ is about $9\,\text{km/h}$, which is the speed of jogging. The velocity is positive, because the graph rises.

    • A change in position divided by time is metres per second. The unit m/s is correct; the error is in the sign of the result.

    • Velocity is a vector quantity, and the sign tells the direction. A negative velocity means motion in the negative direction of the $x$-axis, and here the position really does decrease.

    • Time moves forward, so $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1 = 6.0\,\text{s}$ is positive. The error is in the calculation of the displacement.

    A sled slides down a straight slope at a uniform velocity. On the slope there is a marker post at which the position is 0. At the moment $t = 3.0\,\text{s}$ the sled is at the position $4.0\,\text{m}$ and at the moment $t = 7.0\,\text{s}$ at the position $24\,\text{m}$.

    m

    First find the velocity using the two given moments.

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The velocity is $v = \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}$. After that, the later position is the position at one moment plus the velocity times the elapsed time: $x = x_2 + v\,\Delta t$.

    Given

    positions of the sled: $x_1 = 4.0\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t_1 = 3.0\,\text{s}$ and $x_2 = 24\,\text{m}$ at the moment $t_2 = 7.0\,\text{s}$; the moment asked for $t = 10.0\,\text{s}$; the velocity is uniform

    The sled slides at a uniform velocity, so its velocity is obtained from two known points and its position at a later moment from a known point using the velocity and the elapsed time. The position at the starting moment is not zero, so $v\,t$ alone is not valid.

    $39\,\text{m}$

    $x_0$$= 4.0\,\text{m} - 5.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 3.0\,\text{s}$

      1. A cyclist rides east along a straight road $6.0\,\text{km}$ in $20\,\text{min}$ and then turns straight back, riding the same $6.0\,\text{km}$ west in $30\,\text{min}$. Calculate the average speed over the whole trip, that is the distance travelled divided by the elapsed time. Give the answer in the unit m/s.

        m/s
        Answer

        $4.0\,\text{m/s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        east $s_1 = 6.0\,\text{km}$ in the time $\Delta t_1 = 20\,\text{min}$ and west $s_2 = 6.0\,\text{km}$ in the time $\Delta t_2 = 30\,\text{min}$

        Find

        the average speed $v_\text{speed}$ in the unit $\text{m/s}$

        Principle

        The average speed is the whole distance travelled divided by the elapsed time. Both parts are included in the distance, because the distance does not depend on the direction. The average velocity would be a different thing, because it would use the displacement, in which the direction matters.

        Formula

        $v_\text{speed} = \dfrac{s}{\Delta t}$, where $s = s_1 + s_2$ and $\Delta t = \Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2$. So

        $v_\text{speed}$$= \dfrac{s_1 + s_2}{\Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2}$

        Conversions

        $6.0\,\text{km} = 6000\,\text{m}$ and $20\,\text{min} = 1200\,\text{s}$, $30\,\text{min} = 1800\,\text{s}$.

        Substitution

        $v_\text{speed}$$= \dfrac{6000\,\text{m} + 6000\,\text{m}}{1200\,\text{s} + 1800\,\text{s}}$
        $= \dfrac{12\,000\,\text{m}}{3000\,\text{s}}$
        $= 4.0\,\text{m/s}$

        Answer

        $4.0\,\text{m/s}$

        Two significant figures, because the times $20\,\text{min}$ and $30\,\text{min}$ (the sum $50\,\text{min}$) have two figures; the distances $6.0\,\text{km}$ also have two, and their sum $12.0\,\text{km}$ limits less.

        Reasonableness

        The speed on the way out is

        $\dfrac{6000\,\text{m}}{1200\,\text{s}}$$= 5.0\,\text{m/s}$

        and the speed on the way back

        $\dfrac{6000\,\text{m}}{1800\,\text{s}}$$\approx 3.3\,\text{m/s}$

        and the value lies between them, as an average speed should. The cyclist returned to the starting point, so the displacement is $0$ and the average velocity $0\,\text{m/s}$, even though the average speed is not zero. About $14\,\text{km/h}$ is relaxed cycling.

      2. Aino and Eeva start walking towards each other at the same time along the same line. Aino starts from the position 0 and walks at a velocity $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ in the positive direction. Eeva starts from the position $90\,\text{m}$ and walks at a velocity $-1.2\,\text{m/s}$.

        At what position do they meet?

        m
        Answer

        $50\,\text{m}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        Aino: $x_{0\text{A}} = 0$, $v_\text{A} = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$; Eeva: $x_{0\text{E}} = 90\,\text{m}$, $v_\text{E} = -1.2\,\text{m/s}$; both start at the same time and the motion is uniform

        Find

        the meeting position $x$ in the unit $\text{m}$

        Principle

        The motion of both is uniform, so the position of each is of the form $x_0 + v\,t$. They meet when their positions are the same at the same moment, and the time is solved first from this condition.

        Formula

        $x_\text{A} = x_{0\text{A}} + v_\text{A}\,t$ and $x_\text{E} = x_{0\text{E}} + v_\text{E}\,t$. From the condition $x_\text{A} = x_\text{E}$ it follows that $x_{0\text{A}} + v_\text{A}\,t = x_{0\text{E}} + v_\text{E}\,t$, so $(v_\text{A} - v_\text{E})\,t = x_{0\text{E}} - x_{0\text{A}}$ and

        $$t = \dfrac{x_{0\text{E}} - x_{0\text{A}}}{v_\text{A} - v_\text{E}}$$

        The position is $x = x_{0\text{A}} + v_\text{A}\,t$

        Conversions

        All quantities are in the units $\text{m}$ and $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed. Eeva's velocity is substituted as negative, because she walks in the negative direction.

        Substitution

        $$\begin{aligned} t &= \dfrac{90\,\text{m} - 0}{1.5\,\text{m/s} - (-1.2\,\text{m/s})} \\ &= \dfrac{90\,\text{m}}{2.7\,\text{m/s}} \\ &= 33.33\ldots\,\text{s} \end{aligned}$$

        $$\begin{aligned} x &= 0 + 1.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 33.33\ldots\,\text{s} \\ &= 50\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

        Answer

        $50\,\text{m}$

        Or $5.0 \cdot 10^1\,\text{m}$, two significant figures, because the velocities $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ and $-1.2\,\text{m/s}$ have two figures and so does their difference $2.7\,\text{m/s}$.

        Reasonableness

        Eeva walks $1.2\,\text{m/s} \cdot 33.33\,\text{s} = 40\,\text{m}$, so her position is $90\,\text{m} - 40\,\text{m} = 50\,\text{m}$, the same as Aino's. The position is between the starting and finishing points, and because Aino is faster, the meeting is closer to Eeva's starting position than halfway ($45\,\text{m}$).

      3. The $(t, x)$ graph of a robot consists of three straight parts: between 0 and $10\,\text{s}$ the position grows from 0 to $30\,\text{m}$, between 10 and $20\,\text{s}$ the robot is at rest and between 20 and $40\,\text{s}$ the position decreases to $10\,\text{m}$.

        What is the average velocity of the robot over the whole $40\,\text{s}$? Give the answer with its sign.

        m/s
        Answer

        $0.25\,\text{m/s}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        parts of the $(t, x)$ graph: $x = 0$ at $0\,\text{s}$, $x = 30\,\text{m}$ at $10\,\text{s}$, $x = 30\,\text{m}$ at $20\,\text{s}$ and $x = 10\,\text{m}$ at $40\,\text{s}$

        Find

        the average velocity $v_\text{avg}$ over the whole $40\,\text{s}$ with its sign in the unit $\text{m/s}$

        Principle

        The average velocity is the whole displacement divided by the whole time. Only the initial and final positions affect the displacement, so the intermediate stages, that is moving forward, stopping and coming back, do not affect the calculation.

        Reading the graph

        From the given points we read the starting point $(0\,\text{s}$; $0\,\text{m})$ and the end point $(40\,\text{s}$; $10\,\text{m})$. Between $10\text{–}20\,\text{s}$ the graph is horizontal, because the robot stands still, and its points are not needed. The average velocity is the slope of the line between these two points.

        Formula

        $v_\text{avg}$$= \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$
        $= \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

        Conversions

        The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $v_\text{avg}$$= \dfrac{10\,\text{m} - 0\,\text{m}}{40\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
        $= 0.25\,\text{m/s}$

        Answer

        $0.25\,\text{m/s}$

        Two significant figures, because the final position $10\,\text{m}$ and the time $40\,\text{s}$ are given with two figures.

        Reasonableness

        The robot went $30\,\text{m}$ forward and $20\,\text{m}$ back, so the displacement is $30\,\text{m} - 20\,\text{m} = 10\,\text{m}$ even though the distance was $50\,\text{m}$. The sign is positive, because at the end the robot is at a greater $x$ than at the beginning. The mean of the partial velocities $3.0\,\text{m/s}$, $0$ and $-1.0\,\text{m/s}$,

        $\dfrac{3.0 + 0 - 1.0}{3}$$= 0.67\,\text{m/s}$

        would be wrong, because the sub-intervals are not of equal length.
