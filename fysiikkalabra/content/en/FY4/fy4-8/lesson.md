## Key idea

### Mechanical energy changes its form
In the chapter *Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy* you learned that the mechanical energy of an object is the sum of its kinetic energy and potential energy, $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$. When a roller coaster car rolls into a valley, its height decreases and its speed increases: potential energy turns into kinetic energy. When the car climbs the opposite slope, kinetic energy turns back into potential energy. If there is no friction or air resistance, the car rises exactly to its starting height and stops there for a moment. The mechanical energy is then equal at every moment, even though its form changes. This is called **conservation of mechanical energy**.

Not all the forces acting on the car change its energy. The weight does work when the car goes down or up, and it is exactly this work that turns potential energy into kinetic energy and back. The normal force of the track is perpendicular to the surface of the track and therefore also to the motion, so it does no work. That is why the mechanical energy is conserved when only the weight does work, however twisting the track is. The speed of the car at any point then depends only on how much lower it has descended from the starting point, not on the shape of the track or the mass of the car.

```pysahdy
Two cars, one twice as heavy as the other, start from rest at the top of the same hill on a frictionless track. Which one is faster at the bottom of the valley?
---
They are equally fast. The speed depends only on how far down the car has descended, not on its mass or the shape of the track.
```

### Work transfers energy
In the chapter *Work and energy* you learned that a force does work when an object moves in the direction of the force. Now we also need the sign of the work. A force does positive work when it has a component in the direction of the motion: it increases the energy of the object. A force that opposes the motion, such as friction, does negative work: it decreases the energy of the object. A force perpendicular to the motion, such as the normal force, does no work at all. The unit of work is the same as that of energy, the joule.

A child slides down a slope on a sled. Does the normal force of the snow do work? What about friction?

The normal force is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work. Friction opposes the motion, so it does negative work and decreases the mechanical energy.

### Friction turns mechanical energy into internal energy
In a real roller coaster the rolling resistance of the wheels and the air resistance oppose the motion the whole way. They do negative work, and the mechanical energy of the car decreases by exactly this work. Energy does not disappear but turns into internal energy of the wheels, the rails and the air, which warm up slightly. The total energy is always conserved. Mechanical energy is conserved only when there is no friction or air resistance, or when they are so small that they can be ignored.

You remember the difference between the weight, that is gravity, and friction from the chapter *Kinetic energy, potential energy and mechanical energy*: the weight is a **conservative force**. What is new is what follows from this for calculating energy: the work done by the weight depends only on the difference in height, so when an object returns to the same height, it gets back the same energy, whatever route it took. That is why a potential energy can be associated with the weight. The negative work done by friction and air resistance grows with every metre travelled, so it depends on the route. For such a **non-conservative force** a potential energy cannot be defined.

```pysahdy
A car brakes to a stop. What does its kinetic energy turn into?
---
Into internal energy of the brakes, the tyres and the road, which warm up. The energy does not disappear.
```

### Answer to the opening question
A chain lift lifts the train to the top of the first hill. After that the train has only the mechanical energy it got during the lift, and nothing adds to it. The resistive forces decrease it with every metre. If the next hill were as high as the first, the train would not reach its top. Every later hill must be lower than the first and lower than the height that the remaining energy would reach, so that the train still has some kinetic energy at the top.

## Formula: the work–energy principle

### Work and kinetic energy
When an object moves in a straight line in one direction a distance $s$ and a constant force $\overline{F}$ acts on it, the work done by the force is
```kaavat
work done by a force :: W = F\, s
```
where $F$ is the component of the force in the direction of the motion ($\text{N}$), negative when the component points against the motion, and $s$ is the distance travelled ($\text{m}$). The unit of work is $1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\,\text{J}$.

When an object moves along the $x$-axis and a constant net force $\Sigma F_x$ along the axis acts on it, it accelerates uniformly, and its acceleration is $a = \tfrac{\Sigma F_x}{m}$. In the chapter *Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion* you learned that in uniformly accelerated motion the acceleration is $a = \tfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t}$ and the displacement is the average velocity times the time, $\Delta x = \tfrac{v_1 + v_2}{2}\,t$. Here the velocities $v_1$ and $v_2$ are signed: a negative velocity means motion in the negative direction of the axis. When these equations are multiplied together, the time cancels:
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&a\,\Delta x = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t} \cdot \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2}\,t \\
&\phantom{a\,\Delta x} = \tfrac12 \left(v_2^2 - v_1^2\right)
\end{aligned}
```
When the equation is multiplied on both sides by the mass $m$ and $ma = \Sigma F_x$ is substituted, we get $\Sigma F_x\, \Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} m v_1^2$, that is, $\Sigma F_x\, \Delta x = \Delta E_\text{k}$.

The displacement $\Delta x$ is not the same as the distance travelled $s$. If the object moves in one direction all the time, the magnitude of the displacement equals the distance travelled, and the work of the net force is $\Sigma F\, s$. If the object turns back, like a ball thrown upwards, the distance travelled is longer than the displacement. The work of a constant force is then calculated from the displacement, $W = F_x\, \Delta x$, or in parts so that on each part the motion is in one direction. When friction and air resistance oppose the motion of the object, the work they do is negative, and its magnitude grows with the distance travelled. In both cases the work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy:
```kaavat
work principle :: E_{\text{k}0} + W = E_\text{k}
```
where $E_{\text{k}0}$ is the kinetic energy at the start and $E_\text{k}$ at the end ($\text{J}$) and $W$ the work done together by all the forces acting on the object ($\text{J}$). This is the **work principle**: the total work is the change in kinetic energy, $W = \Delta E_\text{k}$. For example, when the mass of a cart is $2.0\,\text{kg}$ and a net force of $12\,\text{N}$ accelerates it from rest over a distance of $3.0\,\text{m}$, the force does the work $36\,\text{J}$, and the speed of the cart at the end is $6.0\,\text{m/s}$.

### The work–energy principle
The total work is divided into two parts: the work done by the weight $W_G$ and the work done by all the other forces. Other forces include, for example, friction, air resistance, a pulling rope and a pushing hand. When an object descends a height $h$, the weight does the work $mgh$ and the potential energy decreases by the same amount, so $W_G = -\Delta E_\text{p}$. When the work of the weight is written in this way as a change in potential energy, only the work done by forces other than the weight is left as the work $W$ of the work principle. This gives the **work–energy principle of mechanics**:
```kaavat
work-energy principle of mechanics :: E_{\text{p}0} + E_{\text{k}0} + W = E_\text{p} + E_\text{k}
```
where the index $0$ means the initial situation and a quantity without an index the final situation, $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ is the kinetic energy, $E_\text{p} = mgh$ is the potential energy at a height $h$ from the chosen zero level, and $W$ is the work done by forces other than the weight ($\text{J}$). In words: the change in mechanical energy equals the work done by forces other than the weight, $\Delta E_\text{mech} = W$. The zero level can be chosen freely, for example at the lowest point of the situation, because only the change in height affects the result.

When an object moves against a stationary surface or air, friction and air resistance oppose the motion and do negative work, so they decrease the mechanical energy. (A moving surface or the wind can also do positive work, for example a conveyor belt carrying a box.) A pulling rope or a motor can do positive work and increase it, like the chain lift that lifts the train. If the other forces do no work, $W = 0$, and the work–energy principle becomes the **law of conservation of mechanical energy**:
```kaavat
law of conservation of mechanical energy :: E_{\text{p}0} + E_{\text{k}0} = E_\text{p} + E_\text{k}
```
When an object starts from rest at a height $h$ and descends to the zero level, the conservation law $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} mv^2$ gives $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. The mass cancels, so the speed does not depend on the mass.

## Worked example
A child slides down a hill on a sled. The combined mass of the child and the sled is $32\,\text{kg}$. The sled starts from rest at the top of the hill, which is $5.0\,\text{m}$ higher than the foot of the hill. At the foot of the hill the speed of the sled is measured to be $7.6\,\text{m/s}$. How much work do friction and air resistance together do on the sled during the descent?

<!-- vaihe -->

**Given and find.** $m = 32\,\text{kg}$, difference in height $h = 5.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $v_1 = 0$, final speed $v_2 = 7.6\,\text{m/s}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the work $W$ done together by friction and air resistance.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Principle.** The weight, the normal force of the snow, friction and air resistance act on the sled. The normal force is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work. The work of the weight is taken into account as the change in potential energy. Friction and air resistance are other forces, so we use the work–energy principle. The zero level is chosen at the foot of the hill.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Formula solved.** At the start $E_{\text{k}0} = 0$ and $E_{\text{p}0} = mgh$, at the end $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} mv_2^2$ and $E_\text{p} = 0$:
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&mgh + W = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 \\
&W = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 - mgh
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Unit conversions.** Not needed: all quantities are in SI units, and $1\,\text{kg}\cdot\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} = 1\,\text{J}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Substitution.** First we calculate the kinetic energy at the end and the potential energy at the start, and then their difference:
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&\tfrac{1}{2} m v_2^2 \\
&= \tfrac12 \cdot 32\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(7.6\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right)^2 \\
&= 924.16\,\text{J} \\[6pt]
&mgh \\
&= 32\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{m} \\
&= 1569.6\,\text{J} \\[6pt]
&W \\
&= 924.16\,\text{J} - 1569.6\,\text{J} \\
&= -645.44\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Result.** Friction and air resistance together do the work $W \approx -6.5 \cdot 10^2\,\text{J}$. The result is given with two significant figures like the height $5.0\,\text{m}$ and the speed $7.6\,\text{m/s}$. The work is negative, because friction and air resistance oppose the motion: about $650\,\text{J}$ of mechanical energy turned into internal energy of the snow, the runners and the air.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Reasonableness.** Without friction and air resistance the speed of the sled at the foot of the hill would be $\sqrt{2gh} \approx 9.9\,\text{m/s}$, so the measured $7.6\,\text{m/s}$ is smaller, as it should be. About $41\,\%$ of the initial potential energy turned into internal energy. If the slope is $24\,\text{m}$ long, the average resistive force is $645\,\text{J} : 24\,\text{m} \approx 27\,\text{N}$, less than a tenth of the weight $314\,\text{N}$, which is believable for a sliding sled.

## Common misconception
Many people think that a heavier object is faster at the bottom of a hill. When friction and air resistance can be ignored, the mass cancels from the conservation law: a full and an empty car are equally fast at the bottom of the valley. The heavier car has more kinetic energy at the bottom, but it also had more potential energy at the start.

Another mistake is to say that friction destroys energy. Friction decreases the mechanical energy, but an equal amount of energy turns into internal energy, and the total energy is conserved. A third mistake is to calculate the work of friction from the difference in height. The work of friction is the frictional force times the distance travelled along the track. When the resistive force is the same, on a long gentle track more mechanical energy turns into internal energy than on a short steep one, even if the difference in height is the same.

## Summary
- The mechanical energy $E_\text{mech} = E_\text{k} + E_\text{p}$ is conserved when only the weight does work, that is, when friction and air resistance can be ignored and nothing pulls or pushes. The normal force does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion.
- A force does work with its component in the direction of the motion, $W = F s$ when the object moves in one direction: positive in the direction of the motion and negative against it.
- The work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy, $W = \Delta E_\text{k}$.
- The work–energy principle: the change in mechanical energy is the work done by forces other than the weight, $\Delta E_\text{mech} = W$. Friction and air resistance turn mechanical energy into internal energy, and the total energy is conserved.
- The weight is a conservative force, because its work depends only on the difference in height. Friction and air resistance are non-conservative forces, because their work grows with the distance travelled.
