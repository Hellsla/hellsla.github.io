## Lesson plan (75 min)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** The electric car's battery and ten litres of petrol. Students first estimate in words with a partner which holds more energy and why charging takes so long. The answer is not given yet: it is at the end of the Key idea part of the theory summary ($40\,\text{kWh} : 2.3\,\text{kW} \approx 17\,\text{h}$, in the petrol $7.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 43\,\text{MJ/kg} \approx 320\,\text{MJ} \approx 90\,\text{kWh}$).
- **5–15 min, Predict (starting-level test).** Six questions individually, without notes. Take a show of hands or ask students to write the numbers of the questions they got wrong in their notebooks.
- **15–25 min, Investigate.** The bar chart of the condensing power plant in pairs: each pair calculates the efficiencies of all three stages and checks the product. Discuss together: why the steam cycle is the weakest stage.
- **25–35 min, Theory summary.** Go through the concept map and ask the students for one example for each branch. Return to Think about it.
- **35–55 min, Apply.** Four multiple-choice questions in pairs (about $6\,\text{min}$), then four calculations individually. Fast students continue without hints, slower ones use the hints one at a time.
- **55–65 min, Check your understanding and exit ticket.** Individually. The explanation task is self-assessed with the criteria.
- **65–75 min, Problems.** Ten minutes is enough for the start of one long problem in exam conditions, without the model answer: recommended *The wood chip power plant* a–b (a calculation problem) or *The pumped-storage plant in the mine* a–b (combining modules). The rest of the problem and the other five problems are homework, two or three at a time before the next lesson.

## Using the starting-level test
The Predict questions are a diagnostic test, not a grade. Each question corresponds to one chapter:

- Question 1: conservation of energy (chapter *Forms of energy and conservation of energy*).
- Questions 2 and 3: power or energy, and the kilowatt-hour in joules (chapter *Power*).
- Question 4: the efficiency of a chain (chapter *Energy production and the efficiency of power plants*).
- Question 5: heat of combustion (chapter *Fuels and heat of combustion*).
- Question 6: rated power and average power (chapter *Ways of producing energy and how they compare*).

If more than a third of the group answers questions 2 or 3 wrongly, go through power and energy on the board before the Apply part: one example in which the same energy is obtained with a large power in a short time and with a small power in a long time. Mistakes in question 4 are usually corrected in the Investigate task.

## Pair work
- Investigate: one reads from the chart and says the values aloud, the other calculates; swap after each stage.
- Apply multiple-choice questions: each first chooses alone and only then justifies the choice to the partner. Answer only when the pair agrees.
- In the long problem *The island's electricity* (written), pairs can write part b together and assess it with another pair using the scoring guide.

## Fast and slower students
- **Fast students** do the Apply calculations without hints and move straight on to the long problem *Frost and the electricity bill* (graph, $18$ points), which has the most steps. An extra question: what is the house's heating energy for the whole of January if the average temperature is $-8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$? (From the line about $132\,\text{kWh}$ a day, in January about $4.1\,\text{MWh}$, of which heating is about $3.7\,\text{MWh}$.)
- **Slower students** do the Apply calculations *The electric sauna stove* and *The pellet boiler* with hints and leave *From fuel to wheels* for home. The *Review if needed* links of the theory summary lead straight to the original chapters, which have basic-level exercises.

## Assessment
The long problems are self-assessed with the scoring guide. If the teacher assesses them, the same criteria work: in a calculation part the justification of the principle, the symbolic solution, unit conversions by substitution, precision and reasonableness; in a written part the evaluation of the claim with the numbers of the data. All data are invented examples, not statistics.
