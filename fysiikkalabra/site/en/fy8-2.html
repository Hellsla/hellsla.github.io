Spectra reveal the energy levels of atoms

Why do narrow dark lines cross the rainbow colours of sunlight, and how can they tell us which elements there are in the Sun?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why the emission and absorption spectra of a gas have lines at the same wavelengths.
  • calculate the wavelength of a spectral line from the difference between energy states, and the difference from the wavelength of the line.
  • identify an element from its spectral lines and explain fluorescence with energy states.

Prior knowledge

  • The energy of a photon is $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so a shorter wavelength means a greater energychapter The photoelectric effect and the photon
  • The electronvolt is a unit of energy: $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 3.

1Think about it

When sunlight is directed through a narrow slit and a prism or a grating, it forms a band of rainbow colours. Looked at closely, hundreds of narrow dark lines cross the band. The same lines appear every day in the same places, and some of them are exactly where hot hydrogen gas or sodium vapour in the laboratory emits bright light.

Why are certain wavelengths missing from the light of the Sun, and why exactly the wavelengths that a hot gas emits?

Review: the energy of a photon

The wavelength of red light is $650\,\text{nm}$ and that of violet light $410\,\text{nm}$. Which statement about their photons is correct?

  • The energy is inversely proportional to the wavelength: $E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon). A longer wavelength means a lower frequency and a lower energy, so the energy of a red photon is only about $1.91\,\text{eV}$.

  • Correct. The energy of a photon is $E = hf = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon): the energy of a violet photon is about $3.02\,\text{eV}$ and that of a red photon about $1.91\,\text{eV}$. Today we will see that an atom emits only photons of certain energies.

  • Intensity tells the number of photons, not the energy of a single photon (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon). The energy of one photon is $E = hf$, so the colour, that is the frequency, decides it.

  • All photons travel in a vacuum at the speed of light, but the energy depends on the frequency: $E = hf$. The frequency of violet light is higher, so the energy of its photon is greater.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Continuous spectrum and line spectrum

    A spectrum is formed when light is separated into its wavelengths with a grating or a prism. The light of a hot solid, such as a filament, gives a continuous spectrum: all colours from red to violet as one unbroken band. The light of a hot, thin gas has only a few bright colours. Its spectrum is a line spectrum, and more precisely an emission spectrum: separate bright lines on a dark background. For example, the visible lines of hydrogen gas are at the wavelengths $656\,\text{nm}$, $486\,\text{nm}$, $434\,\text{nm}$ and $410\,\text{nm}$. Every element has its own lines, so the lines are like the fingerprint of the element.

    When light with a continuous spectrum passes through a gas, dark lines are left in the spectrum. This is an absorption spectrum. Every dark line is at exactly the same wavelength as one of the bright emission lines of the same gas. The dark lines of the Sun are formed in this way: the continuous light from the surface of the Sun passes through its atmosphere, and the atoms of the atmosphere absorb their own wavelengths.

    Energy states explain the lines

    The electron shell of an atom can only be in certain energy states. The lowest one is the ground state, and the higher ones are excited states. The energies are given as negative numbers, because the zero is chosen to be the situation in which the electron has left the atom. When an atom moves from a higher energy state to a lower one, it emits one photon. Energy is conserved, so the energy of the photon is the difference between the energies of the states. Because an atom has only certain energy states, there are only a certain number of differences, and the spectrum has only certain lines.

    Absorption is the reverse process. An atom absorbs a photon only if the energy of the photon is exactly the difference between two states; other photons pass through the gas. An excited atom soon returns to a lower state and emits a photon, but in a random direction. Therefore exactly those wavelengths are missing from the light travelling in the original direction, and a dark line is left in the spectrum. A gas absorbs only transitions that start from states in which its atoms are. In a cold gas almost all atoms are in the ground state, so there are fewer absorption lines than emission lines. The atmosphere of the Sun is so hot that a small fraction of the hydrogen atoms are in the first excited state. They absorb wavelengths of visible light, and that is why the red line $656\,\text{nm}$ of hydrogen appears dark in the spectrum of the Sun. The energy states of electrons are quantised, and spectra are the most direct evidence of this.

    The energy difference between the states is a necessary condition, but not all transitions are equally probable, and some of them are very weak. Therefore not every possible difference between energy levels is necessarily observed as a strong spectral line. Real spectral lines are not infinitely narrow either: the photon energy must match the difference between the states to within the small width of the line.

    You can measure a spectrum yourself. In a spectroscope, light passes through a narrow slit and a grating, and the wavelengths of the lines are read from a scale. A grating fixed in front of a phone camera or a ready-made spectrometer works in the same way: in the light of a gas discharge tube, a fluorescent tube or an LED lamp you can see at once whether the spectrum is continuous or made of lines. A gas discharge tube works at a high voltage and gets hot, so it is used as the teacher instructs and is not touched while it is on.

    Fluorescence

    In fluorescence, a substance absorbs short-wavelength light, usually ultraviolet, and at once emits longer-wavelength visible light. The ink of a highlighter glows green in the light of a UV lamp: a molecule of the dye becomes excited, gives away part of its energy in small steps as heat to the vibrations of the molecule, and then returns to the ground state by emitting a photon of visible light. The energy of the emitted photon is smaller than that of the absorbed one, so its wavelength is longer. The spectrum is therefore shifted towards red.

    Energy level difference and photon energy

    When an atom moves from one energy state to another, it emits or absorbs a photon whose energy is the difference between the energies of the states:

    $$\begin{aligned} hf &= E_\text{u} - E_\text{l} \\ \lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}} \end{aligned}$$

    where $E_\text{u}$ is the energy of the upper and $E_\text{l}$ the energy of the lower energy state (J or eV), ${h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}}$ is the Planck constant, $f$ the frequency of the photon (Hz), ${c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}$ the speed of light and $\lambda$ the wavelength of the photon (m). The lower line is obtained by substituting $f = \tfrac{c}{\lambda}$ and solving for $\lambda$. The same difference gives an emission line and an absorption line, so they are at the same wavelength. A large energy difference gives a short wavelength.

    The energies of atoms are often given in electronvolts. Before substituting into an equation that has $h$ and $c$, the electronvolts are converted to joules: the value ${1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}$ is substituted in place of the electronvolt.

    The energy states of the hydrogen atom have been measured from its spectrum. In the chapter Energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the Bohr model we see where these values come from.

    The strongest visible lines of hydrogen are formed when a hydrogen atom moves from level $3$, $4$, $5$ or $6$ to level $2$. From still higher levels come weak lines at the violet edge, for example from the transition $7 \to 2$ at the wavelength $397\,\text{nm}$. Transitions to the ground state $n = 1$ are much larger, and their photons are ultraviolet.

    Common misconception

    In the photoelectric effect it is enough that the energy of the photon is at least as great as the work function. Therefore many people think that an atom also absorbs every photon whose energy is enough for an excitation, and that the extra energy is left over. This is not so: when the electron stays in the atom, the energy of the photon must be exactly the difference between two energy states. A photon with slightly too much or too little energy passes through the gas. The only exception is a photon whose energy is enough to free the electron completely, that is, to ionise the atom; then the extra energy remains as the kinetic energy of the freed electron.

    Another mistake is to think that the dark line of an absorption spectrum is completely dark or that the absorbed energy disappears. The atoms emit the energy they absorbed again, but in all directions, so the line is only darker than its surroundings.

    Summary

    • A hot solid gives a continuous spectrum. A hot, thin gas gives an emission spectrum with separate bright lines, and white light that has passed through a gas gives an absorption spectrum whose dark lines are at the same wavelengths as some of the emission lines.
    • The electron shell of an atom can only be in certain energy states. The energy of the photon emitted or absorbed in a transition is the difference between the states: $hf = E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$ and $\lambda = \tfrac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}}$.
    • Every element has its own lines, so the spectrum identifies which elements there are in a light source or in a gas on the path of the light.
    • In fluorescence, a substance absorbs short-wavelength light and emits longer-wavelength light, because part of the energy is turned into heat.

      Optional Greenhouse gases absorb infrared

      Greenhouse gases and infrared

      Molecules also have quantised energy states: their atoms vibrate relative to each other only at certain energies. The differences between the vibrational states of carbon dioxide and water vapour are about $0.08$–$0.5\,\text{eV}$. They correspond to infrared radiation: for example, the strong absorption band of carbon dioxide is at the wavelength $15\,\mu\text{m}$, where the energy of a photon is about $0.083\,\text{eV}$. Photons of visible light have no such differences to match, so a large part of visible sunlight passes through the atmosphere and reaches the ground, even though part of the radiation is absorbed and scattered.

      The warmed ground radiates infrared. Carbon dioxide and water vapour molecules absorb part of it. The energy is also transferred to the rest of the air in collisions, and the gases of the atmosphere radiate infrared in all directions, also back down. Therefore the ground stays warmer than it would without these gases. When the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere grows, absorption becomes stronger and the energy balance of the Earth changes: this is the physical core of climate change.

      Example 1: The red line of neon

      The spectrum of a neon sign has a strong red-orange line at the wavelength $640\,\text{nm}$. What is the difference between the energies of the neon atom's states in the transition that produces this light? Give the answer in electronvolts.

      Step 1/7

      Given and asked. The wavelength of the photon is $\lambda = 640\,\text{nm}$, the Planck constant is $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ and the speed of light is $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. The energy difference $\Delta E = E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$ is asked, in electronvolts.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. An atom emits a photon when it moves from a higher energy state to a lower one. Energy is conserved, so the energy $hf$ of the photon is the difference between the energies of the states. The frequency is obtained from the wavelength, because $c = f\lambda$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\Delta E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The wavelength in metres: $640\,\text{nm} = 640 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. The result is obtained in joules, and it is converted to electronvolts by substituting $1\,\text{J} = \tfrac{1}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we calculate the product

      $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
      $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

      and then the energy difference:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta E &= \frac{hc}{\lambda} \\ &= \frac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{640 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} \\ &= 3.1039 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \\ &= \frac{3.1039 \cdot 10^{-19}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV} \\ &= 1.9375\,\text{eV} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The difference between the energies of the states is about $1.94\,\text{eV}$. The result is given to three significant figures, like the wavelength $640\,\text{nm}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The energies of photons of visible light are about $1.7$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$, and photons at the red end have the smallest energy, so $1.94\,\text{eV}$ fits red-orange light. The result is of the same order of magnitude as the energy difference $1.89\,\text{eV}$ of the red line $656\,\text{nm}$ of hydrogen, and because $640\,\text{nm}$ is a slightly shorter wavelength, its energy is slightly greater.

      Example 2: Find the mistake

      A student calculates the wavelength of the photon produced in the transition $n = 3 \to 2$ of the hydrogen atom.She calculates the energy difference $\Delta E = -1.51\,\text{eV} - (-3.40\,\text{eV}) = 1.89\,\text{eV}$ and then writes

      $$\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{hc}{\Delta E} \\ &= \frac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}}{1.89}\,\text{m} \\ &\approx 1.05 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      She first calculated $hc = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8} = 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}$ in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{m}$.

      Where is the mistake?

      • The energy difference $E_\text{u} - E_\text{l} = 1.89\,\text{eV}$ is correct and positive: the energy of the upper level, $-1.51\,\text{eV}$, is greater than that of the lower one, $-3.40\,\text{eV}$. The mistake is that the electronvolts were not converted to joules before substituting.

      • Correct. Substituting $\Delta E = 1.89 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 3.028 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$ gives $\lambda \approx 656\,\text{nm}$. The unit of the Planck constant is $\text{J}\,\text{s}$, so the energy must be in joules for the units to cancel to metres. The result could have been seen to be wrong at once: $10^{-25}\,\text{m}$ is far smaller than an atomic nucleus, while the wavelength of visible light is hundreds of nanometres.

      • The energy of the photon is the difference of the levels, because energy is conserved: the energy of the atom decreases by as much as the photon takes away. The difference $1.89\,\text{eV}$ is correct; the mistake is that it was not converted to joules.

      • The formula $\lambda = \tfrac{hc}{\Delta E}$ is correct: a greater energy difference gives a shorter wavelength. The wrong formula would give the unit $\tfrac{1}{\text{m}}$. The mistake is in the unit: $\Delta E$ was substituted in electronvolts, although $h$ is in the unit $\text{J}\,\text{s}$.

      Given

      $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$

      1. Reflection task

      Answer on your own first and only then open the answer.

      1. The atom of a gas has three energy states: $0$, $2.0\,\text{eV}$ and $3.0\,\text{eV}$. How many different spectral lines can the hot gas emit, and what are the energies of their photons?
        Answer

        Three lines, one for each pair of states. The energies of the photons are the differences between the states: $3.0\,\text{eV} - 2.0\,\text{eV} = 1.0\,\text{eV}$, $2.0\,\text{eV} - 0 = 2.0\,\text{eV}$ and $3.0\,\text{eV} - 0 = 3.0\,\text{eV}$.

      2. In ordinary fluorescence a substance absorbs one photon and emits one photon, and it gets no extra energy from elsewhere. A fluorescent substance absorbs blue light with a wavelength of $450\,\text{nm}$. Can it emit violet light with a wavelength of $400\,\text{nm}$ in such fluorescence? Justify using energy.
        Answer

        It cannot. The energy of a photon is $E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so a photon of $400\,\text{nm}$ has more energy than a photon of $450\,\text{nm}$. In ordinary fluorescence no energy comes from elsewhere, and part of the energy of the absorbed photon is turned into heat, so the emitted photon has less energy and a longer wavelength than the absorbed one. Emission at a shorter wavelength is possible only in special cases where extra energy comes from elsewhere, for example from two absorbed photons.

      2. Concept task

      Why does the spectrum of a hot, thin gas consist of separate lines and not of an unbroken band of colours like the spectrum of a filament?

      • Correct. The energy of the photon produced in a transition is $hf = E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$. Because there are only certain states, there are only certain differences, and each difference gives one line.

      • In an emission spectrum the gas itself emits the light; it does not filter the light of any source. The lines are formed because an atom can emit only photons whose energy is the difference $E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$ between two energy states.

      • The number of photons affects the brightness of the lines, not the place in the spectrum where they appear. Even if there were billions of atoms, all the atoms of the same element emit the same energies, because they have the same energy states.

      • The speeds of atoms in a gas are distributed continuously, and the colour of a line does not come from the motion of the atom. The colour is decided by the energy of the photon, which is the difference between two energy states of the electron shell.

      3. Calculation task: Calculate the energy difference

      A fluorescent tube contains mercury vapour, whose spectrum has a bright green line at the wavelength $546\,\text{nm}$.

      What is the difference between the energies of the mercury atom's states in the transition that produces this light? Answer in electronvolts.

      eV
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      The energy of the photon is the difference between the energies of the states.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $\Delta E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, when $\lambda$ is in metres.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The result in joules is $3.638 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$; convert it to electronvolts.

      Show the solution

      Given

      wavelength of the mercury line $\lambda = 546\,\text{nm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$

      Find

      the energy difference $\Delta E = E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}$ in electronvolts

      Principle

      A mercury atom emits a photon when it moves from a higher energy state to a lower one. Energy is conserved, so the energy of the photon is the difference between the states, and the energy of the photon is obtained from the wavelength.

      Formula

      $\Delta E = hf$ and $f = \tfrac{c}{\lambda}$, so $\Delta E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

      Conversions

      $546\,\text{nm} = 546 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$. The result in joules is converted to electronvolts by dividing by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}$.

      Substitution

      $hc$$= 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
      $= 1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}$
      $\Delta E$$= \dfrac{1.9865 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{J}\,\text{m}}{546 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
      $= 3.6383 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
      $= \dfrac{3.6383 \cdot 10^{-19}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$
      $= 2.2711\,\text{eV}$

      Answer

      $2.27\,\text{eV}$

      Three significant figures like the wavelength $546\,\text{nm}$.

      Reasonableness

      With the estimate $hc \approx 1240\,\text{eV}\,\text{nm}$ we get $\tfrac{1240}{546}\,\text{eV} \approx 2.3\,\text{eV}$. Green light is in the middle of the visible range, and $2.3\,\text{eV}$ is between the $1.9\,\text{eV}$ of red and the $3.0\,\text{eV}$ of violet.

      Optional 4. Explanation task: Explain and transfer

      Helium was discovered in 1868 in the spectrum of the Sun before it had been found on Earth: the spectrum had a yellow line at a wavelength that was not in the spectrum of any known element. Explain with energy states why this line was evidence of a new element.

      Model answer

      The atoms of every element have their own energy states, and the wavelength of a spectral line is decided by the difference between two states: $\lambda = \tfrac{hc}{E_\text{u} - E_\text{l}}$. All the atoms of the same element always produce the same lines, and the states, and therefore the lines, of different elements are different. When the spectrum of the Sun had a line that was not among the lines of any known element, it had to be produced by atoms whose energy states differ from all the known ones. Later helium gas was found also on Earth, and its spectrum had the same line.
