Bonjour ! Introducing yourself

Why can one small word, bonjour, decide how a whole conversation in France begins?

Imagine that your class visits ITER, the international fusion experiment that is under construction (as of November 2026) next to the Cadarache research centre in Provence, in the south of France. You would meet engineers and researchers from many countries there, and a few French phrases, said politely, open doors. This is a scenario for practice: no visit has been booked.

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • greet people in French, choosing between Bonjour, Bonsoir and Salut and between tu and vous.
  • introduce yourself: your name, where you come from and live, and which languages you speak.
  • pronounce silent final letters, the French r, nasal vowels and the liaison in vous êtes understandably.

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 1.

Model answer

We choose the greeting by the person and the situation: friends get moi or terve, adults we do not know get hei or päivää, and very formal situations hyvää päivää. French works the same way, but the polite form is used more. French we already recognise: bonjour, merci, au revoir, oui, non, s'il vous plaît, croissant, café, restaurant, bon appétit.

    4Theory

    Tu or vous?

    French has two words for you. Choosing the right one is part of being polite.

    • tu is for one person you know well: a friend, a family member, a classmate, a child. Young people of the same age usually say tu to each other from the first moment.
    • vous is for one adult you do not know, or someone in a formal or work situation: a receptionist, an engineer, a professor. Vous is also the only word for two or more people, whoever they are.

    Finnish speakers know the difference from sinä and te (teitittely), but in France the polite vous is much more common than te is in Finland today. A safe rule for a visit: start with vous with every adult. Change to tu only when the other person offers it, for example On peut se tutoyer ? (Shall we say tu?). People often move from vous to tu quickly, for example among colleagues in science and technology; moving back from tu to vous is rare.

    The greeting follows the same idea. Bonjour works with anyone during the day; add madame or monsieur to be polite. Bonsoir is for the evening, both when you meet and when you leave. Salut (hi, bye) is only for friends and people your age. In France you say bonjour before anything else: before you ask a question, when you enter a shop, when you meet the guide. Starting without it can sound rude.

    Sounds for Finnish speakers

    In Finnish, you say every letter you write. In French you do not, and that is the first surprise. Four tips help you most at the start. The pronunciation hints in italics are written with Finnish letters: capitals show the stressed syllable (in French usually the last one), õ, ã and ẽ are nasal vowels, è is an open e and ž is the s of English pleasure.

    1. Silent final letters. Most consonants at the end of a word are not pronounced, and neither is a final -e: salut is sa-LY, vous is VU, français is frã-SÈ, appelles is a-PEL. A final c, r, f or l is often heard, like the r of bonsoir. This is why finlandais (boy) and finlandaise (girl) sound different: in finlandaise the s is no longer at the end, so it is heard, as a z.
    2. The French r is made at the back of the throat, a little like a soft gargle. It is not the rolled Finnish r. Practise with Bonjour, merci and au revoir.
    3. Nasal vowels. In an, en, on, in, un at the end of a word or before another consonant, the air goes through the nose and you do not say the n: bonjour, bien, Finlande, enchanté, un peu. A Finnish speaker easily says a clear n (bon-ŽUR); try bõ-ŽUR instead.
    4. Liaison. A silent final consonant is linked to the next word when that word starts with a vowel or a silent h: vous‿êtes is vu-ZÈT, vous‿habitez is vu-za-bi-TE. After vous before a verb the liaison is compulsory, and the s sounds like a z.

    Some letters are friendly for a Finn: the French u (salut) is the Finnish y, ou (vous) is the Finnish u, eu (un peu) is close to ö, and é is a clear e. The j of je and bonjour is like the s in English pleasure.

    Summary

    The first minute of a meeting. Use tu with friends and people your age, vous with adults you do not know and with any group. Comment ça va ? also works with vous. With tu, a question can simply rise at the end (Tu parles anglais ?); the inverted form (Parles-tu anglais ?) is more careful. With vous, the table gives the inverted form.

        2. Saying your nationality

        Aino, a Finnish girl, wants to say I am Finnish. Which sentence is correct?

        • Yes. Finlandaise is the feminine form, and in French a nationality is written with a lower-case letter.

        • Finlande is the country. With je suis you need the nationality: Je suis finlandaise. The country goes with venir de: Je viens de Finlande.

        • Je viens means I come, and it needs de and the country: Je viens de Finlande. For the nationality, use je suis: Je suis finlandaise.

        • This is the form for a boy or a man. A girl or a woman adds -e: Je suis finlandaise. You can hear the difference: the s is silent in finlandais but sounds like z in finlandaise.

        Suggestion: one answer does not show everything you can do, but review the theory.

        3. Pronouncing vous êtes

        How do you pronounce vous êtes (you are)?

        • You made the liaison, well done, but in liaison the s is voiced: it sounds like z, as in English zoo. Say vu-ZÈT.

        • This is the Finnish habit of saying every letter. The final -es of êtes is silent, and the s of vous sounds like z when it is linked: vu-ZÈT.

        • People will understand you, but it sounds wrong: the liaison after vous before a verb is compulsory. Link the words: vu-ZÈT.
