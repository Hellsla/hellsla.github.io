§ 01 — About

Lauri Hellsten

🇫🇮

I am a physics and mathematics teacher, teacher trainer and educational content author based in Espoo, Finland. I teach at Espoo Yhteislyseo upper secondary school, teach the Dynamic Geometry course at the University of Helsinki, and serve as an assistant member of the Matriculation Examination Board.

My work focuses on STEAM education, formative assessment, flipped learning and meaningful uses of technology and AI in teaching. I am especially interested in designing learning environments where students can experiment, receive feedback within their zone of proximal development, and gradually take more responsibility for their own learning.

I have also taken part in planning Kiviruukin lukio, my school’s new name and new home in Kivenlahti from 2027, and its focus on STEAM and the Ahjo makerspace.

I also coordinate my school’s international work — Erasmus+, Nordplus and other projects — because together we are more than the sum of our parts.

§ 03 — From the classroom
Case study · Introductory physics · Autumn 2026

Flipped Learning and Self-Assessment

In my introductory physics course, I used a flipped and flexible learning approach that combined independent work, weekly deadlines, collaborative learning, and regular feedback. Students could plan their work within each week, while I monitored their progress and provided short individual feedback to help them reflect on their learning.

Student feedback was very positive: 28 respondents gave the course an overall average of 4.33/5. In particular, students valued the learning materials, the supportive classroom atmosphere, flexible pacing, and assessment that was not centred solely on the final exam. Many students said that weekly progress checks helped them study more regularly and understand how their learning was developing.

Randomly changing table groups also supported participation and community building. Some students reported that working with different classmates helped them become more active, confident, and socially connected.

The experience strengthened my view that learner autonomy works best when it is supported by clear structure, regular feedback, and opportunities for self-assessment and reflection.

Case study · Force and motion (FY04) · Autumn 2026

Knowledge Checks and Weekly Feedback

In my force and motion course, taught to two groups, I kept the same flipped and flexible structure of weekly deadlines, independent work, group tasks, and short individual feedback, and added regular knowledge checks. Their purpose was to help students see what they had already mastered and what they still needed to practise, while I followed their progress each week.

Student feedback was positive: 47 respondents across the two groups gave the course an overall average of 4.05/5. Students especially enjoyed coming to lessons and felt that the teaching, guidance, and materials met their needs. Most said the knowledge checks and weekly task follow-up helped them see what they already knew and what they still needed to practise, and many wrote that the quick feedback helped them study regularly and revise.

The feedback also pointed to clear areas for development. Individual students found the tasks within a chapter too similar, wished for more time to practise, or felt that feedback on the knowledge checks did not always explain why points were lost. These are concrete things I will change in the next implementation.

The course showed me that regular self-assessment helps students see their own progress, but it works best when the tasks around it are varied and there is time to act on what the checks reveal.

79% of 47 students said knowledge checks and task follow-up helped them see what they know and what to practise
Students who agreed

Next time
  • More varied tasks within each chapter
  • Clearer explanations in knowledge-check feedback
  • More time for practice at the end of lessons
  • Group-task instructions in Wilma for absent students
