Debate: should governments invest more in fusion?

Why do well-informed people disagree about spending public money on fusion?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • argue for or against public money for fusion with claim, evidence and reasoning.
  • evaluate the strength of evidence and rebut the strongest argument of the other side.
  • use polite English debate language to state, concede, question and sum up.

Prior knowledge

  • Established results, institutional plans and corporate claims are different kinds of statementschapter Fusion in the energy system
  • The fusion gain $Q$ compares fusion power with heating power, not with electricitychapter Inside ITER: the machine and fusion gain

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 1.

1Think about it

Imagine that your country's parliament is planning next year's research budget. One member wants to double the money for fusion research: "It is the energy source of the future." Another wants to move the money to solar power and batteries: "We need solutions now, not in 2050." Both are well informed. Today you will take part in their debate.

Should governments invest more public money in fusion research, and how can you decide?

Review: what kind of statement?

In the chapter Fusion in the energy system, you sorted statements into established results, institutional plans and corporate claims. Which of these is an institutional plan?

  • A measured property of nature: an established result, not a plan.

  • This is about the future, but it comes from a private company, so it is a corporate claim, not an institutional plan.

  • This has already happened and was measured: an established result. A plan is about the future.

  • Right. An intergovernmental organisation's schedule is a plan: it says what it intends, and it can change, as ITER's schedule has before.

Sentence starters

  • At the moment, I am for / against / undecided, because …
  • I think public money should go to …
  • What worries me most is …
  • I would change my mind if …

Think about it: your first opinion

Discuss with a partner, then write two or three sentences.

Are you for, against or undecided about the motion "Governments should invest more public money in fusion research"? Give one reason, and say what kind of evidence could change your mind.

Pair work: tell your partner your first opinion in one minute, then each of you writes it down. Keep it: you will compare it with your opinion after the debate.

Model answer

At the moment, I am undecided. I think fusion could give clean energy in the future, but what worries me most is that it has taken so long and cost so much. I would change my mind if I saw evidence that fusion can produce electricity at a reasonable price, or that other energy sources need the money more.

    3Investigate

    Evidence cards

    Each card gives verified facts with their source and date (all checked on 1 November 2026). Read them carefully: a card is evidence, but you decide what it shows.

    Card A: Records in the laboratory

    In its final experiments in autumn 2023, the JET tokamak in the UK released $69.26\ \text{MJ}$ of fusion heat in one pulse of about six seconds (EUROfusion, 8 February 2024). On 5 December 2022 the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in California released $3.15\ \text{MJ}$ from a fuel target hit by $2.05\ \text{MJ}$ of laser light, and in 2025 it reached $8.6\ \text{MJ}$.

    Card B: The fuel

    About one hydrogen atom in 6420 in sea water is deuterium, roughly $0.035\ \text{g}$ in every litre of water. The ITER Organization states that lithium reserves on land would allow fusion power plants to operate for more than 1000 years.

    Card C: Waste and safety

    According to the ITER Organization, fusion produces no high-activity, long-lived waste; the materials made radioactive by neutrons could be recycled within about 100 years. A fusion plasma holds only a few grams of fuel, and it cools within seconds if anything goes wrong.

    Card D: A changing electricity system

    In 2025, renewable sources gave $33.8\ \%$ of the world's electricity, more than coal ($33.0\ \%$). Wind and solar together gave $17.3\ \%$; their output depends on the weather (Ember, Global Electricity Review 2026). A grid with much variable power also needs storage, flexible demand or firm low-carbon power.

    Card E: Private money

    A survey of 56 fusion companies by the Fusion Industry Association (July 2026) found that they raised USD 4.48 billion in the year to July 2026 and USD 14.24 billion in total, of which about USD 0.98 billion came from public sources. The association said that the companies still need government support (World Nuclear News, 14 July 2026).

    Card F: ITER's cost and schedule

    When ITER was agreed in 2006, its construction was estimated at $5.9$ billion euros over ten years. Later estimates of construction and operation together are $18\text{–}22$ billion euros; other, higher estimates of up to $65$ billion US dollars are disputed by ITER. The 2024 baseline plans deuterium–deuterium plasmas from 2035 and deuterium–tritium operation from 2039, and added an estimated $5$ billion euros, partly for repairing faulty parts (Wikipedia summary of the 2024 baseline).

    Card G: Gain is not electricity

    NIF's lasers needed about $300\ \text{MJ}$ of electricity to produce the $2.05\ \text{MJ}$ of laser light. ITER aims for $500\ \text{MW}$ of fusion heat from $50\ \text{MW}$ of heating, but it will use over $300\ \text{MW}$ of electricity and will not produce any. As of November 2026, no fusion device has delivered electricity to a grid.

    Card H: The climate clock

    The IPCC concluded in 2018 that, to limit warming to $1.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$, net carbon dioxide emissions must fall by about $45\ \%$ from 2010 levels by 2030 and reach net zero around 2050. The UK's prototype fusion plant STEP targets first operations in 2040 (UK government, 12 June 2025).

    Card I: From public laboratory to company

    Commonwealth Fusion Systems was founded in 2018 as a spin-off from the Plasma Science and Fusion Center of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). There, the Alcator C-Mod tokamak (1991–2016), proposed to the US Department of Energy in 1985, set a record plasma pressure of $2.05$ atmospheres in 2016; in 2014 a US federal spending law gave it 22 million dollars to keep running. In May 2023 the US Department of Energy gave the company, with seven others, milestone-based funding.

    Discuss: the strongest card for each side

    Discuss in your group, then write four sentences.

    Which card is the strongest for each side, and why? Which card is the weakest, and why? Use the words result, plan or claim at least once.

    Group work in fours: two of you look for the proposition's best card, two for the opposition's; then compare.

    Model answer

    The strongest card for the proposition is card A, because it reports measured results: JET's $69\ \text{MJ}$ in 2023 and NIF's $3.15\ \text{MJ}$ in 2022 show that the science is improving. The strongest card for the opposition is card F, because ITER's construction estimate of $5.9$ billion euros (2006) has become $18\text{–}22$ billion euros for construction and operation, and its schedule has slipped; this is a documented history of official estimates, not a company's guess. Card H is weaker for both sides as evidence about fusion itself, because STEP's 2040 date is only a plan. Card E could help both sides: investors believe in fusion, but that may mean the state does not need to pay.

        4Theory

        Key idea

        A good debate argument has three parts: a claim (what you say), evidence (a fact with its source and date) and reasoning (why the evidence supports the claim). The same evidence can support both sides: private investment can show that fusion is attractive, or that governments can leave it to companies. So reasoning, not the fact alone, wins the argument.

        Check the kind of evidence. An established result is stronger than an institutional plan, and a plan is usually stronger than a corporate claim, although a plan's own record matters too: ITER's schedule has slipped several times. Never use scientific gain $Q$ as if it were electricity. A strong speaker also rebuts: answers the best argument of the other side instead of ignoring it, and concedes a fair point when it is true.

        How a structured debate works

        The motion is "This house believes that governments should invest more public money in fusion research." The proposition (the team for) argues for it, the opposition (the team against) argues against it; the fact-checkers check numbers. Your role in the debate is not necessarily your own opinion: arguing the other side helps you understand it.

        Role Who Task
        chair 1 opens, gives the floor, keeps time
        for 3 opening, rebuttal, closing
        against 3 opening, rebuttal, closing
        checkers 2 check numbers against the cards
        judges the rest score, then vote

        The order and the time limits are:

        1. The chair opens and the audience votes once (for, against, undecided), 1 minute.
        2. Opening speeches: proposition, then opposition, 2 minutes each.
        3. Rebuttals: opposition, then proposition, 2 minutes each.
        4. Open floor: questions from the judges to both teams, 5 minutes.
        5. Fact-check report: correct facts and any mistakes, 2 minutes.
        6. Closing speeches: opposition, then proposition, 90 seconds each.
        7. Second vote and the judges' scores, 2 minutes.

        The judges score each team from 0 to 3 on each criterion: evidence (at least two cards, with source and date), accuracy (results, plans and claims kept apart; gain is not electricity), reasoning (evidence linked to the motion; the other side answered) and language (clear, polite debate language). The scores give each team feedback and are used for assessment; the winner is decided by the votes: the team that changes more votes, not the team that started with more support, wins.

        Debate language

        Learn the phrases that do the work in a debate.

        Sentence starters for every speech

        • Chair: Welcome to the debate. I now give the floor to … · You have 30 seconds left. · Thank you; time is up.
        • Opening: Madam / Mr Chair, we believe that … Our first argument is … Our second argument is …
        • Evidence: According to … (date), … · The figures on card … show that …
        • Rebuttal: Our opponents claim that …, but … · This argument ignores the fact that …
        • Conceding: We accept that …; however, … · That is a fair point, but …
        • Question: Could you explain how …? · Where does that number come from?
        • Closing: To sum up, … · The strongest reason is … · For these reasons, we urge you to vote for / against the motion.

          5Examples

          Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

          Your teacher gives each student a role: proposition, opposition, chair, fact-checker or judge (see the table above). Teams have 12 minutes to prepare. Plan three arguments with claim, evidence and reasoning, decide who speaks when, and predict the other side's strongest argument so that you can rebut it. Rebuttal speakers write two lines of the form Our opponents will say …, but …; closing speakers choose the one card they will repeat at the end. The chair learns the chair's phrases below. Fact-checkers prepare by noting the key numbers and dates of every card; judges read the four criteria.

          Sentence starters

          • We believe that … Our first argument is that …
          • According to card …, … This shows that …
          • Our opponents will probably say that …, but …
          • We accept that …; however, …

          Write your team's opening speech

          Write the opening speech of your team (about 100–150 words, about 2 minutes when spoken). State your position, give two arguments, each with a card (source and date) and your reasoning, and end with one sentence that the listeners will remember. Fact-checkers and judges: write the opening speech of the side you find less convincing.

          Team work: plan together, then each speaker writes their own part; the first speaker reads the opening speech in the debate.

          Model answer

          Proposition: Madam Chair, we believe that governments should invest more public money in fusion research. Our first argument is that the science is working. According to EUROfusion, JET released $69\ \text{MJ}$ of fusion heat in one pulse in 2023, and in 2022 the National Ignition Facility released more energy than its laser light delivered. These are measured results, not promises. Our second argument is that public research builds industries: Commonwealth Fusion Systems began in 2018 as a spin-off of a publicly funded MIT laboratory, and companies themselves still ask for government support. We accept that ITER is over budget; however, every first-of-a-kind machine is. Fusion fuel would last for centuries. Let us not stop halfway to a new source of energy.

          Opposition (alternative model): Madam Chair, we believe that governments should not invest more public money in fusion. Our first argument is cost and delay. ITER's construction was estimated at $5.9$ billion euros in 2006; later estimates of construction and operation are $18\text{–}22$ billion euros, and its deuterium–tritium experiments are planned only from 2039. Our second argument is that fusion has not yet made any electricity: NIF's lasers used about $300\ \text{MJ}$ of electricity for one shot, and ITER will produce none. Meanwhile, the IPCC says emissions must fall by about $45\ \%$ by 2030, and renewables already gave $33.8\ \%$ of the world's electricity in 2025. We accept that fusion research is exciting; however, public money should go where it cuts emissions soonest.

            Review the theory.

            Choose the best rebuttal

            The opposition says: "ITER's cost has tripled. Fusion is a waste of public money." Which reply by the proposition is the best rebuttal?

            • The Sun shows that fusion is possible, but not that a power plant on Earth will be affordable. It does not answer the argument about cost.

            • Right. It concedes a true point, then answers with evidence (results such as card A) and reasoning. Conceding makes the speaker more credible.

            • This attacks the speakers, not the argument, and gives no evidence. Judges score it low on reasoning and language.

            • This mixes up gain and electricity: $Q = 10$ compares fusion power with heating power, and ITER will produce no electricity (card G).

            Run the debate

            Follow the order and the time limits in How a structured debate works. The chair keeps time and gives the floor. While the teams speak:

            • Fact-checkers write down every number and date a speaker uses and compare it with the cards. In their report they say which facts were correct and correct any mistakes politely: "The speaker said …; card … says …."
            • Judges give each team 0–3 points for evidence, accuracy, reasoning and language, and note one strong argument from each side.
            • Everyone votes twice, before the opening speeches and after the closing speeches. The team that changes more votes wins.

            Common misconception

            "The side with more facts wins." Facts without reasoning do not persuade. A single strong card, well explained and defended against the rebuttal, is worth more than five cards read aloud.

            "Disagreeing means attacking the other speaker." In a debate you attack arguments, never people. Phrases such as "I see your point, but …" keep the discussion respectful.

            A speaker says: "In 2022 the National Ignition Facility proved that fusion produces more energy than it uses." What should the fact-checker say?

            • No: the whole facility used far more. The lasers alone drew about $300\ \text{MJ}$ of electricity to make $2.05\ \text{MJ}$ of light (card G).

            4. Explanation task: Write: your position after the debate

            Write a paragraph of 120–150 words in your own name (not your debate role).

            Is your opinion now for, against or undecided? Compare it with your first opinion. Give your main argument with one card (source and date) and your reasoning, and answer the strongest argument of the other side.

            Model answer

            Before the debate I was undecided; now I am, on balance, for more public money, but only for research with clear milestones. The most convincing argument was that public research created the knowledge that companies now use: Commonwealth Fusion Systems began in 2018 as a spin-off of a publicly funded MIT laboratory (card I). Without that money, the private investment of USD 4.48 billion in 2025–2026 (card E) would probably not exist. I accept that ITER's estimates have grown to $18\text{–}22$ billion euros and that no fusion device has yet made electricity (cards F and G); however, this is a reason to fund research carefully, not to stop it. Because fusion is not planned to deliver electricity before 2030 and the public prototype STEP targets 2040, I think most climate money should still go to renewables and grids, and fusion should get a smaller, long-term share.

              Summary

              • A debate argument has a claim, evidence (with source and date) and reasoning.
              • Established results are stronger evidence than plans, and plans are usually stronger than corporate claims (check their record); scientific gain is not electricity.
              • The same evidence can support both sides; the reasoning decides.
              • Rebut the strongest argument of the other side and concede what is true.
              • Whether to spend more public money on fusion is a question of values and priorities as well as of physics: well-informed people can disagree.
