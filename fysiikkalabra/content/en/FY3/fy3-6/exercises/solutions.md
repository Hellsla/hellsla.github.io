## fy3-6-L1-01
**Given:** water, $m = 1.8\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 12\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 96\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the water, in megajoules
**Principle:** The water warms and its phase does not change, so the heat transferred to it is proportional to the mass and to the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $Q = c m \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** The change in temperature is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, so $\Delta T = 96\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 12\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 84\,\text{K}$. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1.8\,\text{kg} \cdot 84\,\text{K} = 633\,528\,\text{J} = 0.633\,528\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $0.63\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures as in the mass $1.8\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** Heating one kilogram by one kelvin takes about 4 kJ, so heating almost two kilograms by about 80 K takes about $4\,\text{kJ} \cdot 2 \cdot 80 \approx 0.6\,\text{MJ}$. $Q$ is positive because the water warms.

## fy3-6-L1-02
**Given:** iron, $m = 2.4\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 220\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 25\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 450\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat given out by the pan, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The pan cools and its phase does not change, so it gives out a quantity of heat that is proportional to the mass and to the drop in temperature. The heat given out is stated as a positive number.
**Formula:** Heat given out $Q_\text{out} = c m (T_\text{initial} - T_\text{final})$
**Conversions:** The drop in temperature is $220\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 25\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 195\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q_\text{out} = 450\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 2.4\,\text{kg} \cdot 195\,\text{K} = 210\,600\,\text{J} = 210.6\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $210\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures as in the mass $2.4\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The $c$ of iron is small, so even a heavy pan, despite the large cooling, gives out only about a third of the energy needed to heat the 1.8 kg of water in the coffee maker ($0.63\,\text{MJ}$).

## fy3-6-L1-03
**Given:** sand, $m = 50\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 14\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 32\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the sand, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The sand warms and its phase does not change, so the heat transferred to it is proportional to the mass and to the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $Q = c m \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 32\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 14\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 18\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 50\,\text{kg} \cdot 18\,\text{K} = 720\,000\,\text{J} = 720\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $720\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures as in the mass $50\,\text{kg}$ and in the change $18\,\text{K}$.
**Reasonableness:** The same heat would raise the temperature of 50 kg of water by only about 3.4 K, because the $c$ of water is five times as large. That is why sand on a beach gets hot faster than water.

## fy3-6-L1-04
**Given:** water, $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 38.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 31.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the water, with its sign, in megajoules
**Principle:** The water cools and its phase does not change. When $\Delta T$ is calculated as final minus initial, the formula gives a negative $Q$ for cooling: the water gives out heat.
**Formula:** $Q = c m \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 31.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 38.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} = -7.0\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. Finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^6\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg} \cdot (-7.0\,\text{K}) = -35\,196\,000\,\text{J} \approx -35.2\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $-35\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures, because the change in temperature $7.0\,\text{K}$ is known to two figures.
**Reasonableness:** $Q$ is negative because the water cools. The tub holds a lot of water, so even a cooling of a few kelvins releases tens of megajoules: $4\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg} \cdot 7\,\text{K} \approx 34\,\text{MJ}$.

## fy3-6-L1-05
**Given:** ice, $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = -22\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = -4.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the quantity of heat $Q$ transferred to the ice, in kilojoules
**Principle:** The ice warms but does not melt, because the final temperature is below $0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. So $Q = c m \Delta T$ with the specific heat capacity of ice is enough, and no latent heat of fusion is needed.
**Formula:** $Q = c m \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = -4.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} - (-22\,{}^\circ\text{C}) = 18\,\text{K}$, the same in kelvins. Finally $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 2100\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.50\,\text{kg} \cdot 18\,\text{K} = 18\,900\,\text{J} = 18.9\,\text{kJ}$
**Answer:** $19\,\text{kJ}$, two significant figures as in the mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The temperature rises, so $Q$ is positive. Be careful when subtracting readings with minus signs: the change is 18 K, not 26 K. The $c$ of ice is about half that of water, so making the same change in temperature in 0.50 kg of liquid water would take about twice the heat.

## fy3-6-L2-01
**Given:** hot water $m_1 = 160\,\text{g}$, $T_1 = 95\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; cold water $m_2 = 40\,\text{g}$, $T_2 = 8.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; both are water, no heat losses
**Find:** the equilibrium temperature $T$
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: when no heat is transferred to the mug or the air, the heat given out by the hot water is equal to the heat received by the cold water. At the end both have the same temperature $T$.
**Formula:** $c m_1 (T_1 - T) = c m_2 (T - T_2)$. The same $c$ cancels, and $T$ can be solved: $T = \dfrac{m_1 T_1 + m_2 T_2}{m_1 + m_2}$
**Conversions:** The grams do not need to be converted to kilograms, because the mass is in both the numerator and the denominator and the unit cancels. The temperatures can be substituted in degrees Celsius: the equilibrium equation contains only temperature differences, and in the solved formula the sum of the weights is in the denominator, so shifting the scale shifts the result by the same amount.
**Substitution:** $T = \dfrac{160\,\text{g} \cdot 95\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 40\,\text{g} \cdot 8.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{160\,\text{g} + 40\,\text{g}} = \dfrac{15\,200 + 320}{200}\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 77.6\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $78\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures as in the temperature $95\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and the mass $40\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** The result is between the initial temperatures and close to 95 °C, because there is four times as much hot water. The energy balance holds: $160\,\text{g} \cdot 17.4\,\text{K} = 40\,\text{g} \cdot 69.6\,\text{K}$.

## fy3-6-L2-02
**Given:** $P = 2.4\,\text{kW}$, water $m = 1.5\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\text{final} = 95\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the heating time $t$ in seconds
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: when all the energy of the kettle is transferred to the water, the energy $Pt$ supplied by the kettle in the time $t$ is equal to the heat needed to heat the water, $c m \Delta T$.
**Formula:** $P t = c m \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$, so $t = \dfrac{c m \Delta T}{P}$
**Conversions:** $P = 2.4\,\text{kW} = 2400\,\text{W} = 2400\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}$. $\Delta T = 95\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 15\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 80\,\text{K}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 80\,\text{K}}{2400\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}} = \dfrac{502\,800\,\text{J}}{2400\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}} = 209.5\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $210\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the power $2.4\,\text{kW}$ and the mass $1.5\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** About 3.5 minutes matches experience with a kettle. In reality the time is slightly longer, because some of the energy heats the kettle and the air. Units: $\dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{J/s}} = \text{s}$.

## fy3-6-L2-03
**Given:** water $m = 80\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{initial} = 9.0\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $Q = 15\,\text{MJ}$; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; no heat losses
**Find:** the final temperature $T_\text{final}$
**Principle:** All the heat given out by the resistor stays in the water, and the water warms without boiling, so $Q = c m \Delta T$. First solve the change in temperature, then add it to the initial temperature.
**Formula:** $\Delta T = \dfrac{Q}{c m}$, so $T_\text{final} = T_\text{initial} + \dfrac{Q}{c m}$
**Conversions:** $Q = 15\,\text{MJ} = 15 \cdot 10^6\,\text{J} = 15\,000\,000\,\text{J}$. The change obtained in kelvins is added directly to the Celsius reading, because the steps of the scales are the same.
**Substitution:** $\Delta T = \dfrac{15\,000\,000\,\text{J}}{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 80\,\text{kg}} = 44.75\,\text{K}$, so $T_\text{final} = 9.0\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 44.75\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 53.75\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $54\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures as in the heat $15\,\text{MJ}$ and the mass $80\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The change in temperature is added to the initial temperature, so 45 K alone is not an acceptable answer. About 54 °C is a typical temperature for a hot-water tank, and the water stays liquid.

## fy3-6-L2-04
**Given:** aluminium $m_1 = 0.25\,\text{kg}$, $T_1 = 95\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; water $m_2 = 0.40\,\text{kg}$, $T_2 = 18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$; from the table $c_1 = 900\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$, $c_2 = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; no heat losses
**Find:** the common final temperature $T$
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: the heat given out by the ladle is equal to the heat received by the water, and at the end both have the same temperature. The substances are different, so the specific heat capacities do not cancel.
**Formula:** $c_1 m_1 (T_1 - T) = c_2 m_2 (T - T_2)$, from which $T = \dfrac{c_1 m_1 T_1 + c_2 m_2 T_2}{c_1 m_1 + c_2 m_2}$
**Conversions:** The masses are already in kilograms. The temperatures can be substituted in degrees Celsius: the equilibrium equation contains only temperature differences, and in the solved formula the sum of the weights is in the denominator, so shifting the scale shifts the result by the same amount.
**Substitution:** $c_1 m_1 = 900\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.25\,\text{kg} = 225\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}$ and $c_2 m_2 = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 0.40\,\text{kg} = 1676\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}$, so $T = \dfrac{225\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}} \cdot 95\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 1676\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}} \cdot 18\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{1901\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{K}}} = 27.11\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Answer:** $27\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, two significant figures as in the masses.
**Reasonableness:** The heat capacity of the water is about 7.5 times that of the ladle, so the ladle cools by about 68 K but the water warms by only about 9 K. The mean of the temperatures, 56.5 °C, would be wrong.

## fy3-6-L3-01
**Given:** water $m_\text{w} = 20\,\text{kg}$, $T_\text{w} = 10\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, target $T = 40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c_\text{w} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; stones $T_\text{s} = 300\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c_\text{s} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; no losses and no vaporisation
**Find:** the mass of the stones $m_\text{s}$
**Principle:** Energy is conserved: the heat given out by the stones is equal to the heat received by the water. In equilibrium the stones and the water are at the same temperature, so the stones cool down to 40 °C, not to 0 °C.
**Formula:** $c_\text{s} m_\text{s} (T_\text{s} - T) = c_\text{w} m_\text{w} (T - T_\text{w})$, from which $m_\text{s} = \dfrac{c_\text{w} m_\text{w} (T - T_\text{w})}{c_\text{s} (T_\text{s} - T)}$
**Conversions:** The temperature differences are the same in kelvins: $T - T_\text{w} = 30\,\text{K}$ and $T_\text{s} - T = 260\,\text{K}$. The other quantities are in SI units.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{s} = \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 20\,\text{kg} \cdot 30\,\text{K}}{800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 260\,\text{K}} = \dfrac{2\,514\,000\,\text{J}}{208\,000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}} = 12.09\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $12\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures as in the mass $20\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The temperature of the stones changes 8.7 times as much as that of the water, which reduces the mass needed, and the 5.2 times smaller $c$ of the stones increases it; together $\dfrac{m_\text{s}}{m_\text{w}} = \dfrac{5.2}{8.7} \approx 0.60$, and $0.60 \cdot 20\,\text{kg} = 12\,\text{kg}$.

## fy3-6-L3-02
**Given:** $A = 2000\,\text{m}^2$, $h = 1.2\,\text{m}$, energy $18\,\text{MJ}$ for each square metre, $1000\,\text{kg}$ of water in a cubic metre; from the table $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$
**Find:** the rise in temperature $\Delta T$
**Principle:** The energy stays in the water and the water mixes evenly, so $Q = c m \Delta T$. It is enough to consider the column of water under one square metre, because the energy is given per square metre.
**Formula:** $\Delta T = \dfrac{Q}{c m}$, where the mass of the water column of one square metre is $m = \rho\, h \cdot 1\,\text{m}^2$
**Conversions:** $Q = 18\,\text{MJ} = 18\,000\,000\,\text{J}$. Under a square metre there is $1.2\,\text{m} \cdot 1\,\text{m}^2 = 1.2\,\text{m}^3$ of water, so $m = 1.2\,\text{m}^3 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} = 1200\,\text{kg}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta T = \dfrac{18\,000\,000\,\text{J}}{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg}} = 3.58\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $3.6\,\text{K}$, two significant figures as in the energy $18\,\text{MJ}$ and the depth $1.2\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** For the whole bay both the energy and the mass are 2000 times as large, so the area cancels. A rise of a few kelvins in a day fits experience of a shallow bay. The result is an upper limit: the real rise is smaller, because some of the energy goes into evaporation and radiates away at night.

## fy3-6-L3-03
**Given:** water $V_\text{w} = 1.0\,\text{m}^3$, $m_\text{w} = 1000\,\text{kg}$, $c_\text{w} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; sand $1600\,\text{kg}$ per cubic metre, $c_\text{s} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}$; both between $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and $70\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** the volume of sand $V_\text{s}$ that stores the same quantity of heat
**Principle:** The store takes up the heat $Q = c m \Delta T$. When $Q$ and $\Delta T$ are the same, the heat capacities $c m$ are the same. The volume is obtained from the mass with the help of the density.
**Formula:** $c_\text{s} m_\text{s} = c_\text{w} m_\text{w}$, so $m_\text{s} = \dfrac{c_\text{w} m_\text{w}}{c_\text{s}}$ and $V_\text{s} = \dfrac{m_\text{s}}{\rho_\text{s}}$
**Conversions:** The density of the sand is $\rho_\text{s} = 1600\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}$. The temperature range $\Delta T = 50\,\text{K}$ cancels, so it is not needed.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{s} = \dfrac{4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} \cdot 1000\,\text{kg}}{800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}}} = 5237.5\,\text{kg}$, so $V_\text{s} = \dfrac{5237.5\,\text{kg}}{1600\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}} = 3.27\,\text{m}^3$
**Answer:** $3.3\,\text{m}^3$, two significant figures as in the volume $1.0\,\text{m}^3$.
**Reasonableness:** Per kilogram, water stores about 5.2 times as much as sand, but sand is denser, so a cubic metre holds 1.6 times as much mass. So the ratio of the volumes is $\dfrac{5.2}{1.6} \approx 3.3$ and not 5.2.
