Energy transformations and the joule

Why does the motion of a bicycle ultimately come from the Sun, and how can the amount of energy be given as a single number?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain an energy chain and what the energy finally turns into.
  • give energy in joules and with the prefixes kJ, MJ, GJ and TJ.
  • compare everyday and societal energies by order of magnitude.

Prior knowledge

  • Energy appears in different forms and is conservedchapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy
  • The prefixes kilo, mega, giga and tera are powers of tenchapter Quantities, units and the SI system
  • Powers of ten and orders of magnitudechapter Physics as a science and the scales of the universe

1Think about it

In the morning you eat oat porridge and set off to school by bike. The pedals turn, your speed stays up and the tyres warm up. When you arrive, you brake and stop. The oats grew in a field through the summer, and the Sun shone on them the whole time.

Where does the energy of the bicycle's motion come from, and where does it finally go when you stop?

Optional Review: conservation of energy
A car brakes and stops. Which description of its kinetic energy is correct (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy)?

  • Correct. Energy is conserved and changes form: in the brakes, tyres and road, kinetic energy becomes thermal energy, which is transferred as heat to the surroundings and spreads out. Today we examine such chains and calculate energy in joules.

  • The road does not start moving, so the energy does not stay there as kinetic energy. It turns into thermal energy, which warms the brakes, tyres and road.

  • Energy does not disappear. When the car stops, its kinetic energy turns into other energy, and there is as much energy in total as before braking.

Assumptions of the model

The values are rounded orders of magnitude, not exact measurements: the energy of a lightning strike varies a lot, and the energy of a day's food depends on the person. Each item has a source (a table value, a statistic or a calculation) or is marked as an estimate. The scale is logarithmic, so the same distance on the ladder always means the same number of times more energy.

How the calculation works

The energy of each item is written as a number times a power of ten, for example $3.2 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{J}$. The prefix is obtained by replacing the power with its value: $10^{6}\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{MJ}$, so $3.2 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{J} = 32 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J} = 32\,\text{MJ}$. When two items are compared, the larger energy is divided by the smaller: $32\,\text{MJ} : 10\,\text{MJ} = 3.2$.

Hint 1: a guiding question

Energy is conserved. How much energy was there at the start, and how much of it is in the water at the end?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The energy that did not end up in the water is the difference between the initial energy and the energy transferred to the water: $E_\text{other} = E_\text{start} - E_\text{water}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

$E_\text{other} = 100\,\text{MJ} - 34\,\text{MJ}$.

Show the solution

Given

chemical energy of the fuel $E_\text{start} = 100\,\text{MJ}$ and energy transferred to the water $E_\text{water} = 34\,\text{MJ}$

Find

energy that ended up somewhere other than the water $E_\text{other}$ in megajoules

Principle

Energy is conserved, so the initial energy is divided entirely between the water and elsewhere. The other energy turned into thermal energy in the power plant, the grid and the kettle and was transferred as heat to the surroundings.

Formula

$E_\text{start} = E_\text{water} + E_\text{other}$, so $E_\text{other} = E_\text{start} - E_\text{water}$

Conversions

Not needed: all energies are given in megajoules.

Substitution

$E_\text{other}$$= 100\,\text{MJ} - 34\,\text{MJ}$
$= 66\,\text{MJ}$

Answer

$66\,\text{MJ}$

Two significant figures as in the data $34\,\text{MJ}$.

Reasonableness

The heat at the steps is $100\,\text{MJ} - 40\,\text{MJ} = 60\,\text{MJ}$, $40\,\text{MJ} - 38\,\text{MJ} = 2\,\text{MJ}$ and $38\,\text{MJ} - 34\,\text{MJ} = 4\,\text{MJ}$, and together they make $60\,\text{MJ} + 2\,\text{MJ} + 4\,\text{MJ} = 66\,\text{MJ}$. This is more than the energy that remained in the water, as in power plants.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Energy changes from one form to another

    Energy is a quantity that describes what can happen in a system. It appears in different forms, such as mechanical, chemical, electrical, thermal energy, radiation and nuclear energy (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy). In everyday life, energy seldom stays in one form: it changes from one form to another in a chain of steps.

    The cyclist's chain begins with the Sun. Radiation energy is stored in a plant as chemical energy, and it ends up in oats and in morning porridge. The muscles turn part of the chemical energy of the porridge into the motion of the bicycle, and when you stop, the kinetic energy finally turns into thermal energy of the tyres, the brakes and the air.

    At every step, part of the energy turns into thermal energy, which is transferred as heat to the surroundings and spreads out. Even so, no energy disappears: as stated in chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy, there is the same amount of energy at the start and at the end of the chain. In the example in the figure, the $800\,\text{kJ}$ of the porridge is divided into motion and thermal energy, and the parts add up to the same amount as at the start. Thermal energy spreads so widely that it is no longer easy to put to use, and this is why energy seems to "run out".

    Joule

    The symbol of energy is $E$ and its unit is the joule, whose symbol is J. One joule is a small amount of energy: it is roughly what is needed to lift an apple of about $0.1\,\text{kg}$ by one metre. In this chapter you learn the size of the joule through examples.

    Unit of energy and its multiples

    Everyday energies are large numbers when given in joules, so we use prefixes (chapter Quantities, units and the SI system): kilo (k), mega (M), giga (G) and tera (T). Each of them is a thousand times the previous one:

    $$\begin{aligned} 1\,\text{kJ} &= 1000\,\text{J} \\ 1\,\text{MJ} &= 1000\,\text{kJ} \\ 1\,\text{GJ} &= 1000\,\text{MJ} \\ 1\,\text{TJ} &= 1000\,\text{GJ} \end{aligned}$$

    where J is the joule, kJ the kilojoule, MJ the megajoule, GJ the gigajoule and TJ the terajoule. So a megajoule contains $1000 \cdot 1000\,\text{J} = 1\,000\,000\,\text{J}$.

    A conversion is done by replacing the prefix with its value: $2.5\,\text{kJ} = 2.5 \cdot 1000\,\text{J} = 2500\,\text{J}$. In the other direction, the number is written using the prefix: $3000\,\text{J} = 3 \cdot 1000\,\text{J} = 3\,\text{kJ}$. Energies are added together only in the same unit, so they are first converted to the same unit.

    The orders of magnitude of everyday energies are collected in the figure, where each step is a factor of ten (chapter Physics as a science and the scales of the universe). When the orders of magnitude of two energies are very different, one division is enough for the comparison: the ratio of the $32\,\text{MJ}$ of a litre of petrol to the $10\,\text{MJ}$ of a day's food is $32\,\text{MJ} : 10\,\text{MJ} \approx 3$.

    The amount of energy does not yet tell how fast the energy is used. That is described in the next chapter, Power.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that energy runs out when the speed slows down or the battery goes flat. Energy does not run out and does not disappear. It changes form, and a large part ends up as heat in the surroundings, from where it can no longer easily be collected back for use.

    Another mistake is to add energies in different units, for example $1.5\,\text{MJ} + 300\,\text{kJ} = 301.5$. A prefix is a multiplier, so the energies are first converted to the same unit.

    Summary

    • Energy changes from one form to another in a chain, for example radiation, chemical energy, kinetic energy and thermal energy.
    • Energy is conserved (chapter Forms of energy and conservation of energy). At every step part of it turns into thermal energy, which is transferred as heat to the surroundings.
    • The unit of energy is the joule (J). Prefixes: $1\,\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$, $1\,\text{MJ} = 1000\,\text{kJ}$, $1\,\text{GJ} = 1000\,\text{MJ}$ and $1\,\text{TJ} = 1000\,\text{GJ}$.
    • A prefix is replaced by its value: $2.5\,\text{kJ} = 2.5 \cdot 1000\,\text{J}$. Energies are added in the same unit.
    • Everyday energies range from joules to terajoules, and they are compared using powers of ten.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      A cyclist eats a banana whose chemical energy is $0.45\,\text{MJ}$. The muscles turn $110\,\text{kJ}$ of it into the motion of cycling, and the rest turns into thermal energy. How much energy turns into thermal energy in the muscles and the body? Give the answer in kilojoules.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The chemical energy at the start is $E_\text{start} = 0.45\,\text{MJ}$ and the energy that turned into motion is $E_\text{motion} = 110\,\text{kJ}$. The thermal energy $E_\text{heat}$ is to be found in kilojoules.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Energy is conserved: the chemical energy of the banana does not disappear but turns entirely into motion and thermal energy. So the energy at the start is equal to the sum of the energies that turned into motion and thermal energy.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{start} &= E_\text{motion} + E_\text{heat} \\ E_\text{heat} &= E_\text{start} - E_\text{motion} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The energies are calculated in the same unit. Replace the prefix mega with its value:

      $0.45\,\text{MJ}$$= 0.45 \cdot 1000\,\text{kJ}$
      $= 450\,\text{kJ}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{heat} &= E_\text{start} - E_\text{motion} \\ &= 450\,\text{kJ} - 110\,\text{kJ} \\ &= 340\,\text{kJ} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The thermal energy produced is $340\,\text{kJ}$. The result is rounded to two significant figures, because the initial energy $0.45\,\text{MJ}$ and the motion $110\,\text{kJ}$ are given with two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. More energy turns into thermal energy than into motion, as is usual in human muscles: the body warms up when you cycle. The sum checks out: $110\,\text{kJ} + 340\,\text{kJ} = 450\,\text{kJ}$, which is the same as at the start.

      Optional Find the error
      A student converts the energy $2.5\,\text{kJ}$ to joules and writes $2.5\,\text{kJ} = 2.5 : 1000\,\text{J} = 0.0025\,\text{J}$. Where is the error?

      • On the contrary: the joule is a smaller unit than the kilojoule, so the same energy needs more joules, that is, a larger number.

      • Kilo is a thousand, not a hundred. The conversion is done by replacing the prefix with its value, $\text{kJ} = 1000\,\text{J}$.

      • Correct. The prefix kilo means a thousand, so a kilojoule contains a thousand joules, and in joules the number is a thousand times larger. You can check the reasonableness from the order of magnitude: the joule is a smaller unit than the kilojoule, so the number must grow.

      Apply to a new situation

      A wind farm produces electrical energy of $360\,\text{GJ}$ in a day. In the grid, $9\,\text{GJ}$ turns into thermal energy on the way, and consumers use $0.300\,\text{TJ}$ of electricity. The rest is stored in a battery.

      How many gigajoules are stored in the battery?

      GJ
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Where does all the electrical energy that was produced go? Energy does not disappear.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Write the conservation law: the energy produced equals the energy that turned into thermal energy, the energy used by consumers and the stored energy, added together. Solve for the stored energy.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      First convert to the same unit: $0.300\,\text{TJ} = 0.300 \cdot 1000\,\text{GJ}$. Then $E_\text{battery} = 360\,\text{GJ} - 9\,\text{GJ} - 300\,\text{GJ}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      electrical energy produced $E_\text{produced} = 360\,\text{GJ}$, turned into thermal energy in the grid $E_\text{heat} = 9\,\text{GJ}$, consumers $E_\text{use} = 0.300\,\text{TJ}$

      Find

      energy stored in the battery $E_\text{battery}$ in gigajoules

      Principle

      Energy is conserved: the energy produced is divided into thermal energy, the consumers and the battery. Nothing disappears, so the parts together equal the energy produced.

      Formula

      $E_\text{produced} = E_\text{heat} + E_\text{use} + E_\text{battery}$, so $E_\text{battery} = E_\text{produced} - E_\text{heat} - E_\text{use}$

      Conversions

      Replace the terajoule with its value:

      $0.300\,\text{TJ}$$= 0.300 \cdot 1000\,\text{GJ}$
      $= 300\,\text{GJ}$

      Substitution

      $E_\text{battery}$$= 360\,\text{GJ} - 9\,\text{GJ} - 300\,\text{GJ}$
      $= 51\,\text{GJ}$

      Answer

      $51\,\text{GJ}$

      To the accuracy of a whole gigajoule: in a subtraction the least precise value decides the accuracy, and all the data ($360\,\text{GJ}$, $9\,\text{GJ}$ and $0.300\,\text{TJ} = 300\,\text{GJ}$) are known to the accuracy of a gigajoule.

      Reasonableness

      A small part of the energy produced is left for the battery, and the sum is correct: $9\,\text{GJ} + 300\,\text{GJ} + 51\,\text{GJ} = 360\,\text{GJ}$. The energy that turned into thermal energy in the grid is the smallest item, which fits the fact that transmission losses are small.

      3. Comparison

      A lightning strike releases on average about $1.0\,\text{GJ}$ of energy, and the energy of a day's food is $10\,\text{MJ}$.

      How many days' food does the energy of a lightning strike correspond to?

      kpl
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Both energies must be in the same unit so that they can be compared.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $1.0\,\text{GJ} = 1.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{MJ}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The number is the energy of the lightning divided by the energy of a day's food: $1000\,\text{MJ} : 10\,\text{MJ}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      lightning $E_\text{lightning} = 1.0\,\text{GJ}$ and a day's food $E_\text{food} = 10\,\text{MJ}$

      Find

      number of days' food $N$

      Principle

      The number is obtained by dividing the energy of the lightning by the energy of one day's food, when both are in the same unit.

      Formula

      $N = E_\text{lightning} : E_\text{food}$

      Conversions

      $1.0\,\text{GJ}$$= 1.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{MJ}$
      $= 1000\,\text{MJ}$

      Substitution

      $N$$= E_\text{lightning} : E_\text{food}$
      $= 1000\,\text{MJ} : 10\,\text{MJ}$
      $= 100$

      Answer

      About $100$ days' food, two significant figures as in the data $1.0\,\text{GJ}$ and $10\,\text{MJ}$.

      Reasonableness

      A gigajoule is a thousand megajoules and a day's food is ten, so the ratio is a thousand divided by ten, about a hundred. The energy of the lightning would last a person over three months, but the lightning releases it in a fraction of a second.

      Optional 4. Explain and transfer

      Explain why we say that energy cannot be used up, even though a petrol tank becomes empty. Use driving a car as an example and use the words energy chain and thermal energy.

      Model answer

      In the engine of a car, the chemical energy of petrol turns into motion and thermal energy. When driving, air resistance, the tyres and the brakes turn the energy of the motion into thermal energy, which spreads into the air and the road. So no energy disappears: there is the same amount of energy at the start and at the end of the chain. The tank becomes empty because the energy has changed into a form that can no longer easily be collected back for use. That is why new energy is needed all the time, for example from fuel or from the Sun.

          $\text{TJ}$ is replaced with the value $1000\,\text{GJ}$.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 0.14 \cdot 1000\,\text{GJ}$
          $= 140\,\text{GJ}$

          Answer

          $140\,\text{GJ}$

          Two significant figures as in the given value $0.14\,\text{TJ}$.

          Reasonableness

          The terajoule is a larger unit than the gigajoule, so the number grows. A tank of petrol is about $1.6\,\text{GJ}$, so $140\,\text{GJ} : 1.6\,\text{GJ} \approx 90$, that is, $140\,\text{GJ}$ corresponds to about $90$ tanks. That is the daily output of a small wind farm; a nuclear power plant unit produces about a thousand times more in a day, $140\,\text{TJ}$.

        4. Gas with a chemical energy of $12\,\text{MJ}$ is burned on a gas cooker. Of this, $9.0\,\text{MJ}$ is transferred to the water in the pan, and the rest heats the air of the kitchen.

          How much energy is transferred to the air of the kitchen? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          $3\,\text{MJ}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          chemical energy $E_\text{start} = 12\,\text{MJ}$ and energy transferred to the water $E_\text{water} = 9.0\,\text{MJ}$

          Find

          energy transferred to the air $E_\text{air}$ in megajoules

          Principle

          Energy is conserved, so the chemical energy of the gas is transferred entirely to the water and the air. The chemical energy does not disappear anywhere.

          Formula

          $E_\text{start} = E_\text{water} + E_\text{air}$, so $E_\text{air} = E_\text{start} - E_\text{water}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: both energies are given in megajoules.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{air}$$= 12\,\text{MJ} - 9.0\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 3.0\,\text{MJ}$
          $\approx 3\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $3\,\text{MJ}$

          To the accuracy of a whole megajoule: in a subtraction the least precise value decides the accuracy, and the energy of the gas, $12\,\text{MJ}$, is given only in whole megajoules.

          Reasonableness

          Most goes to the water, $9.0\,\text{MJ}$ out of twelve. Check: $9.0\,\text{MJ} + 3\,\text{MJ} = 12\,\text{MJ}$, which is the same total as at the start.

        5. The figure shows the order of magnitude of the energy of three things on a power-of-ten scale, and each small step is a factor of ten. Read the energy of an AA battery and of a lightning flash from the figure in joules.

          How many AA batteries correspond in energy to one lightning flash?

          kpl
          Answer

          $100\,000$

          Show the solution

          Given

          energies read from the figure: AA battery $E_\text{b} = 10^{4}\,\text{J}$ and lightning $E_\text{l} = 10^{9}\,\text{J}$ (orders of magnitude)

          Find

          number of batteries $N$ whose total energy equals that of the lightning flash

          Principle

          Every battery has the same energy, so the number is obtained by dividing the energy of the lightning flash by the energy of one battery. On a power-of-ten scale every step is a factor of ten, so the power of ten of an energy is read by counting steps.

          Reading the graph

          The AA battery point is one small step to the right of $1\,\text{kJ} = 10^{3}\,\text{J}$, so its energy is $10 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{J} = 10^{4}\,\text{J}$. The lightning point is at $1\,\text{GJ} = 10^{9}\,\text{J}$. There are five small steps between the points.

          Formula

          $N \cdot E_\text{b} = E_\text{l}$, so $N = E_\text{l} : E_\text{b}$

          Conversions

          Both energies were read in joules, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $N$$= 10^{9}\,\text{J} : 10^{4}\,\text{J}$
          $= 10^{9-4}$
          $= 10^{5}$
          $= 100\,000$

          Answer

          About $100\,000$ batteries. The result is an order of magnitude, because the energies in the figure are orders of magnitude.

          Reasonableness

          Five steps of a factor of ten means multiplying by ten five times: $10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 100\,000$. The energy of lightning is large, but it is released in an instant; the same energy takes an enormous number of small batteries.

        Level 2 · applied practice 4 exercises

        Two steps, a unit conversion or rearranging a formula. Core exercises first, the rest is extra practice.

        1. Core

          On a hike you eat a $100\,\text{g}$ chocolate bar with an energy of $2.2\,\text{MJ}$ and a bag of nuts with an energy of $800\,\text{kJ}$.

          How much energy do you get in total? Give the answer in megajoules.

          MJ
          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{MJ}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          chocolate bar ($100\,\text{g}$) $E_1 = 2.2\,\text{MJ}$ and nuts $E_2 = 800\,\text{kJ}$

          Find

          total energy $E$ in megajoules

          Principle

          Energies are added together, because energy is additive. Addition works only in the same unit, so the kilojoules are converted to megajoules.

          Formula

          $E = E_1 + E_2$

          Conversions

          Write the number using a thousand and replace $1000\,\text{kJ}$ with a megajoule:

          $800\,\text{kJ}$$= 0.80 \cdot 1000\,\text{kJ}$
          $= 0.80\,\text{MJ}$

          Substitution

          $E$$= 2.2\,\text{MJ} + 0.80\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 3.0\,\text{MJ}$

          Answer

          $3.0\,\text{MJ}$

          To the accuracy of tenths of a megajoule, because the energy of the chocolate, $2.2\,\text{MJ}$, is given to tenths.

          Reasonableness

          The result is greater than either part. The wrong result $802.2$ would come from adding the numbers of different units. Beside the $10\,\text{MJ}$ of a day's food, $3.0\,\text{MJ}$ is a third, which suits a hiking snack.

        2. Core

          A car has $40\,\textsf{l}$ of petrol, and one litre of petrol contains $32\,\text{MJ}$ of energy. How much energy is there in the tank? Give the answer in gigajoules.

          GJ
          Answer

          $1.3\,\text{GJ}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          petrol $V = 40\,\textsf{l}$ and the energy of one litre $E_\text{litre} = 32\,\text{MJ}/\textsf{l}$

          Find

          energy of the tank $E$ in gigajoules

          Principle

          Every litre contains the same energy, so the total energy is the number of litres multiplied by the energy of one litre.

          Formula

          $E = V \cdot E_\text{litre}$

          Conversions

          Finally the megajoules are converted to gigajoules by writing the number using a thousand and replacing $1000\,\text{MJ}$ with a gigajoule.

          Substitution

          $E$$= 40\,\textsf{l} \cdot 32\,\text{MJ}/\textsf{l}$
          $= 1280\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 1.28 \cdot 1000\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 1.28\,\text{GJ}$

          Answer

          $1.3\,\text{GJ}$

          Two significant figures as in the data $40\,\textsf{l}$ and $32\,\text{MJ}/\textsf{l}$.

          Reasonableness

          The litres cancel, and the unit of energy remains. Estimate with round numbers:

          $40 \cdot 30\,\text{MJ}$$= 1200\,\text{MJ}$
          $\approx 1\,\text{GJ}$

          The result is of the same order of magnitude as the $1.6\,\text{GJ}$ of a $50$-litre tank.

        3. An LED lamp receives electrical energy of $6.0\,\text{kJ}$. It turns $1.5\,\text{kJ}$ of it into light, and the rest turns into thermal energy in the lamp. The light hits the walls and surfaces of the room and is absorbed by them.

          How much energy has finally turned into thermal energy in the room? Give the answer in kilojoules.

          kJ
          Answer

          $6.0\,\text{kJ}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          electrical energy $E_\text{electric} = 6.0\,\text{kJ}$, energy turned into light $E_\text{light} = 1.5\,\text{kJ}$; the light is absorbed by the surfaces of the room

          Find

          energy that turned into thermal energy in the room $E_\text{heat}$ in kilojoules

          Principle

          Energy is conserved. The thermal energy of the lamp is $E_\text{electric} - E_\text{light}$, and when the light is absorbed, it also turns into thermal energy. No energy leaves the room as light.

          Formula

          $E_\text{heat}$$= (E_\text{electric} - E_\text{light}) + E_\text{light}$
          $= E_\text{electric}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: all energies are given in kilojoules.

          Substitution

          $E_\text{heat}$$= (6.0\,\text{kJ} - 1.5\,\text{kJ}) + 1.5\,\text{kJ}$
          $= 4.5\,\text{kJ} + 1.5\,\text{kJ}$
          $= 6.0\,\text{kJ}$

          Answer

          $6.0\,\text{kJ}$

          Two significant figures as in the given electrical energy.

          Reasonableness

          All the electrical energy ends up as thermal energy, as at the end of a chain whenever nothing is stored. The light is not stored anywhere but heats the surfaces.

        4. The energy of explosives is often given in tonnes of TNT: by convention, one tonne of TNT corresponds to about $4.2\,\text{GJ}$. The energy of a chocolate bar ($200\,\text{g}$) is $4.4\,\text{MJ}$.

          How many chocolate bars correspond in energy to one tonne of TNT?

          kpl
          Answer

          $950$

          Show the solution

          Given

          a tonne of TNT $E_\text{TNT} = 4.2\,\text{GJ}$ and a chocolate bar ($200\,\text{g}$) $E_\text{chocolate} = 4.4\,\text{MJ}$

          Find

          number of chocolate bars $N$

          Principle

          Every bar has the same energy, so the number is obtained by dividing the energy of a tonne of TNT by the energy of one bar, when both are in the same unit.

          Formula

          $N \cdot E_\text{chocolate} = E_\text{TNT}$, so $N = E_\text{TNT} : E_\text{chocolate}$

          Conversions

          Replace the prefix giga with its value:

          $4.2\,\text{GJ}$$= 4.2 \cdot 1000\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 4200\,\text{MJ}$

          Substitution

          $N$$= 4200\,\text{MJ} : 4.4\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 954.5$

          Answer

          About $950$ chocolate bars, two significant figures as in the data $4.2\,\text{GJ}$ and $4.4\,\text{MJ}$.

          Reasonableness

          A gigajoule is a thousand megajoules, and the energy of a bar is roughly the same number as the gigajoules of a tonne of TNT ($4.4$ and $4.2$), so the ratio is a little under a thousand. An explosive does not contain an especially large amount of energy, but it releases it very quickly.

        Level 3 · challenging 3 exercises

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. In Finland, on average about $790\,\text{TJ}$ of electrical energy is consumed per day (calculated from Statistics Finland data for 2023). An Olkiluoto 3 nuclear power plant unit produces electrical energy of $140\,\text{TJ}$ per day.

          What is the smallest number of such units whose output would cover the whole consumption? Give the answer as a whole number.

          kpl
          Answer

          $6$

          Show the solution

          Given

          electricity consumption of Finland $E_\text{Finland} = 790\,\text{TJ}$ per day and the output of one unit $E_\text{unit} = 140\,\text{TJ}$ per day

          Find

          the smallest number of units $N$, as a whole number

          Principle

          Identical units together produce the number of units times the energy of one unit. The output must be at least as large as the consumption, so the result of the division is rounded up to the next whole number: less than a whole unit is not enough.

          Formula

          At the limit the output equals the consumption, $N \cdot E_\text{unit} = E_\text{Finland}$, so $N = E_\text{Finland} : E_\text{unit}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: both energies are given in terajoules, and the unit cancels.

          Substitution

          $N$$= 790\,\text{TJ} : 140\,\text{TJ}$
          $= 5.64$

          so at least the next whole number of units is needed.

          Answer

          At least $6$ units. Five units would produce only $5 \cdot 140\,\text{TJ} = 700\,\text{TJ}$, which is less than the consumption; six produce $6 \cdot 140\,\text{TJ} = 840\,\text{TJ}$. The number is a whole number, so rounding to significant figures does not apply to it.

          Reasonableness

          Estimate: $800 : 150 \approx 5.3$, so a little over five are needed, that is, six units. One Olkiluoto 3 therefore covers about a sixth of Finland's electricity, which is a realistic share for one large power plant unit.

        2. In the reactor of a nuclear power plant unit, thermal energy of $370\,\text{TJ}$ is released per day. Of this, $140\,\text{TJ}$ turns into electricity, and the rest is carried into the sea by the cooling water. Heating a detached house takes, for example, $72\,\text{GJ}$ per year.

          The energy transferred to the sea in one day corresponds to a year's heating for how many detached houses?

          kpl
          Answer

          $3200$

          Show the solution

          Given

          energy released in the reactor $E_\text{reactor} = 370\,\text{TJ}$ and electricity $E_\text{electric} = 140\,\text{TJ}$ per day; heating a detached house for a year $E_\text{house} = 72\,\text{GJ}$ (an example)

          Find

          number of detached houses $N$

          Principle

          First conservation of energy: the energy of the reactor is divided into electricity and the heat that goes to the sea, so the energy going to the sea is their difference. Then the comparison: the number is obtained by dividing the energy going to the sea by the yearly heating energy of one house, in the same unit.

          Formula

          $E_\text{reactor} = E_\text{electric} + E_\text{sea}$, so $E_\text{sea} = E_\text{reactor} - E_\text{electric}$, and $N = E_\text{sea} : E_\text{house}$

          Conversions

          Replace the prefix tera with its value:

          $230\,\text{TJ}$$= 230 \cdot 1000\,\text{GJ}$
          $= 230\,000\,\text{GJ}$

          Substitution

          $E_\text{sea}$$= 370\,\text{TJ} - 140\,\text{TJ}$
          $= 230\,\text{TJ}$
          $= 230\,000\,\text{GJ}$

          and

          $N$$= 230\,000\,\text{GJ} : 72\,\text{GJ}$
          $= 3194$

          Answer

          About $3200$ detached houses, two significant figures as in the data $72\,\text{GJ}$, $140\,\text{TJ}$ and $370\,\text{TJ}$.

          Reasonableness

          More energy goes to the sea than into electricity, as is usual in power plants that run on heat: $140\,\text{TJ} + 230\,\text{TJ} = 370\,\text{TJ}$. Estimate: $200\,000 : 70 \approx 3000$, so thousands of houses is the right order of magnitude. This amount of energy therefore corresponds to heating a small town for a year, but the heat going to the sea is too cool for district heating; in combined heat and power production the steam is condensed at a higher temperature, so the heat can be recovered and slightly less electricity is produced.

        3. In the reactor of a nuclear power plant unit, energy of $370\,\text{TJ}$ is released per day, and about $4.5\,\text{kg}$ of uranium-235 undergoes fission for it.

          How many litres of petrol would have to be burned to get the same energy, when a litre of petrol contains $32\,\text{MJ}$ of energy? Compare the result with the amount of uranium.

          l
          Answer

          $1.2 \cdot 10^{7}\,\textsf{l}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          energy of the reactor per day $E = 370\,\text{TJ}$, $4.5\,\text{kg}$ of uranium-235 and the energy of petrol per litre $E_\text{litre} = 32\,\text{MJ}/\textsf{l}$

          Find

          volume of petrol $V$ in litres and a comparison with the amount of uranium

          Principle

          Every litre of petrol gives the same energy, so the number of litres that gives the same energy is obtained by dividing the total energy by the energy of one litre. The energies must first be given in the same unit.

          Formula

          $E = V \cdot E_\text{litre}$, so $V = E : E_\text{litre}$

          Conversions

          Replace the prefixes with their values:

          $370\,\text{TJ}$$= 370 \cdot 1000\,\text{GJ}$
          $= 370 \cdot 1000 \cdot 1000\,\text{MJ}$
          $= 3.7 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{MJ}$

          Substitution

          $V$$= 3.7 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{MJ} : 32\,\text{MJ}/\textsf{l}$
          $= 1.16 \cdot 10^{7}\,\textsf{l}$

          Answer

          About $1.2 \cdot 10^{7}\,\textsf{l}$, that is, about $12$ million litres of petrol, two significant figures as in the data $370\,\text{TJ}$ and $32\,\text{MJ}/\textsf{l}$. Comparison: only about $4.5\,\text{kg}$ of uranium-235 gives the same energy in a day, which fits in the palm of a hand, whereas the petrol would fill hundreds of tanker lorries.

          Reasonableness

          Estimate: $400\,000\,000 : 40 = 10\,000\,000$ litres, so about ten million litres is the right order of magnitude. The litres remain in the result, because the megajoules cancel. The difference from uranium is huge, which fits the fact that much more nuclear energy is released from the same amount of matter than in burning.
