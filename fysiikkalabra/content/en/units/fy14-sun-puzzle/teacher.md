## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–6 min, Think about it.** The hook read aloud and the review question on mass and energy. Ask: "Which fuels do you know? How long would a fire the size of the Sun burn?"
- **6–10 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, each with a short written reason.
- **10–35 min, Investigate.** Individually: the coal-Sun calculation. In pairs: read the Eddington extracts aloud (one reads, the other explains each extract in their own words), answer the source question, put the timeline in order. Then the pair discussion *Kelvin against the geologists* with the word bank, and each student writes their own answer.
- **35–48 min, Theory summary.** The three candidate explanations, the key idea and the lifetime formula; the vocabulary match; back to the predictions.
- **48–62 min, Apply.** The worked example (the Sun's lifetime with its three assumptions) on the board, step by step; then the mass-loss task individually.
- **62–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks, the explanation for a younger student, the summary and the French corner.

## Organisation and preparation
- The lesson is a historical investigation: the source extracts are short, but read them aloud. *Synthesis*, *reservoir* and *sub-atomic* are explained in the text; check that students understand them.
- Point out that the extracts are quoted from 1920, when "atomic weight" and "sub-atomic energy" were the words used; our *nuclear energy* came later.
- For the timeline: the dates are in the items, so the task trains reading and the logic of the story, not memory. Ask students to say *why* each step followed the one before.
- Fast pairs: the optional Kelvin estimate (gravitational contraction gives about $30$ million years), the error-spotting task ($Mc^2/L$) and the biography of Eddington. Ask: "Which of our three assumptions makes the estimate too long, and which too short?"
- Running out of time: skip the reading of the misconceptions in class (they are short; students can read them at home) and do the worked example together without the slow stage-by-stage reveal. Keep the explanation for a younger student: it shows whether the main idea is in place.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade: the discussion answer identifies the conflict between Kelvin's timescale and geology/biology, explains that Kelvin's calculation was right for the energy sources he knew, and names nuclear energy as the missing piece; the explanation for a younger student rejects the fire model with the $5000$-year estimate and explains fusion with the loss of mass.
- Calculation: $t = E/L$ with consistent units, and an answer rounded to the precision the assumptions allow.
- Source work: the student distinguishes Eddington's evidence (a mass measurement) from what was not known in 1920 (the reactions and how they can happen).
- Language: correct use of at least three key words (luminosity, contraction, estimate, assumption, evidence) and a sentence starter.

## Common difficulties
- Thinking that the Sun "burns". Ask: "What would it burn with? How long would it last?"
- Thinking that the whole mass of the Sun turns into energy ($Mc^2$). The error-spotting task and the third misconception address this.
- Overprecise answers: an estimate built on a $10\ \%$ assumption deserves one significant figure. Discuss which digits mean something.
- Eddington's "1 part in 120" ($0.83\ \%$) differs from today's $0.71\ \%$. The cause is his rounded helium value: hydrogen $1.008$ against helium "just $4$" gives $0.79\ \%$, about 1 part in 126, which he rounded to 1 in 120. With helium's measured $4.003$ (or today's AME2020 masses) the loss is about $0.71\ \%$. The older oxygen-16 mass scale is not the cause: a change of scale multiplies all masses by almost the same factor, so the fraction stays the same. A good example of how a rounded value can hide in a famous number.

## Extension ideas
- Read more of Eddington's 1920 address (link in the sources): what did he say about using sub-atomic energy on Earth, "for the well-being of the human race — or for its suicide"?
- Compare Eddington's argument with Kelvin's: both used the best physics of their time. What makes a scientific estimate trustworthy?
- Estimate how much hydrogen the Sun fuses every second ($6 \cdot 10^{11}\ \text{kg}$) from $L$ and the $0.71\ \%$.
