Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion

Why does a stone dropped from a bridge travel a longer distance during each second than during the second before?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain what acceleration tells you about the change in velocity, and interpret its sign.
  • calculate the velocity, position and time of uniformly accelerated motion, and read them from $(t,v)$ and $(t,x)$ graphs.
  • determine the acceleration of free fall by fitting a quadratic polynomial to measured points.

1Think about it

You drop a stone from a footbridge into a river, and your friend films the fall with a phone. The video shows that during the first half second the stone falls about $1.2\,\text{m}$. After one whole second it has already fallen almost $5\,\text{m}$. So during the second half second it falls a much longer distance than during the first, even though the time is the same.

What changes in the stone's motion so that in every time interval of equal length it travels a longer distance than in the one before?

  • The number $12\,\text{m}$ is the change in position $\Delta x$, not a velocity. You get the velocity by dividing the change in position by the time it took, $4.0\,\text{s}$.

  • You divided the final position by the time: $\dfrac{14\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$. The cart does not start from the origin but from the position $2.0\,\text{m}$, so the velocity is calculated from the change in position $\Delta x = 14\,\text{m} - 2.0\,\text{m}$.

Simulation

How to use

Choose the initial velocity $v_0$ and the acceleration $a$ with the sliders. Press Start or drag the Time $t$ slider. The faint carts show the position at whole seconds. The blue arrow is the velocity and the orange arrow the acceleration, and the length of an arrow shows the magnitude: the scale of the velocity arrow adapts to the greatest velocity of the motion, so the longest arrow is always visible on the road, while the acceleration arrow has its own fixed scale. The blue dashed line in the $(t,x)$ graph is the tangent of the curve at the present moment, and the shaded area in the $(t,v)$ graph extends from time 0 to the present moment; the part below the time axis is darker.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation, not measurements. The cart starts at $t = 0$ from the position $x_0 = 0$, and its acceleration stays the same all the time.     4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Acceleration tells you how the velocity changes

    In the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs the velocity stayed constant. In most motions, however, it changes: a car starts from a traffic light, a cyclist brakes and a dropped stone falls faster and faster. Acceleration $a$ tells you how much the velocity changes per unit of time. Its unit is $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}} = \text{m/s}^2$. An acceleration of $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ means that the velocity grows by $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ every second.

    Acceleration is a vector quantity $\overline{a}$, like velocity $\overline{v}$, and in rectilinear motion its direction is given by its sign. When the acceleration and the velocity point in the same direction, the speed increases. When they point in opposite directions, the speed decreases. So a negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down: if an object moves in the negative direction and $a < 0$, its speed increases. Acceleration can exist even when the velocity is zero for a moment.

    Uniformly accelerated motion and the $(t,v)$ graph

    Motion is uniformly accelerated when the acceleration stays constant: the velocity changes by the same amount in every time interval of equal length. Then the $(t,v)$ graph, with time on the horizontal axis and velocity on the vertical axis, is a straight line. The slope of the line is the acceleration. A rising line means a positive acceleration, a horizontal line uniform motion and a falling line a negative acceleration.

    Instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent

    When the velocity grows, the position changes more in each second than in the one before. That is why the $(t,x)$ graph is not a straight line but a curve that gets steeper. The velocity at a given moment, the instantaneous velocity, is found from the tangent of the curve: the line that just touches the curve at that point. Two points far apart are chosen on the tangent, and the instantaneous velocity is the change in position between them divided by the change in time. A ratio calculated through two points of the curve would give only the average velocity between them.

    Free fall

    When air resistance is small, all objects fall with the same acceleration regardless of mass. This acceleration of free fall is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ at the Earth's surface, directed downwards. The velocity of a dropped stone therefore grows by about $9.8\,\text{m/s}$ every second. That is why the stone travels a longer distance in each second than in the one before: the distances that an object starting from rest covers in successive intervals of equal length are in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5 : 7$.

    Equations of uniformly accelerated motion

    Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it took:

    $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$

    where $a$ is the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$), $\Delta v = v_2 - v_1$ the change in velocity ($\text{m/s}$) and $\Delta t$ the time it took ($\text{s}$). When the acceleration is constant and timing starts at $t = 0$, when the velocity is $v_0$, the velocity changes by the amount $a$ every second:

    $$v = v_0 + a\,t$$

    where $v$ is the velocity at time $t$ ($\text{m/s}$), $v_0$ the initial velocity ($\text{m/s}$), $a$ the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$) and $t$ the time ($\text{s}$).

    The displacement is the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis

    In uniform motion the displacement $v\,t$ is the area of the rectangle under the $(t,v)$ graph. The same holds when the velocity changes: the displacement is the area between the graph and the time axis, where the part below the time axis ($v < 0$) is counted as negative. In uniformly accelerated motion the region divides into a rectangle whose area is $v_0\,t$ and a triangle whose height is the growth of velocity $a\,t$ and whose width is $t$. The area of the triangle is $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot a\,t \cdot t = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$.

    When the object is at the position $x_0$ at time $t = 0$, its position is the initial position plus the displacement:

    $$x = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$$

    where $x$ is the position at time $t$ ($\text{m}$) and $x_0$ the initial position ($\text{m}$); the other symbols are the same as above. When the time is asked for, this becomes a quadratic equation, and of its solutions you choose the one that fits the situation.

    Determining the acceleration of free fall from measurements

    The position of a falling object is measured at many moments, for example from a video or with light gates. A quadratic polynomial $s = A\,t^2 + B\,t + C$ is fitted to the measured points with a calculator or a spreadsheet. In real series of measurements the points do not usually lie exactly on one curve because of measurement uncertainty, so the fit uses all the points at once. When the polynomial is compared with the equation of position, the coefficients of $t^2$ are the same, $A = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a$:

    $$a = 2A$$

    where $A$ is the coefficient of the $t^2$ term of the fitted polynomial ($\text{m/s}^2$). The coefficient $B$ is the initial velocity and $C$ the initial position.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the acceleration is zero whenever the velocity is zero. A ball thrown up is at rest for a moment at the highest point, but its velocity changes all the time: just before the highest point it moves up and just after it moves down. The acceleration is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards during the whole flight. Another mistake is to take a negative acceleration to always mean slowing down: the speed decreases only when the acceleration and the velocity point in opposite directions. A third mistake is to think that a heavy object falls faster than a light one. When air resistance is small, both fall with the same acceleration.

    Summary

    • Acceleration $a = \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ tells you the change in velocity per unit of time, with the unit $\text{m/s}^2$. It is a vector quantity, and on a straight line its sign tells the direction.
    • The speed increases when the acceleration and the velocity point in the same direction, and decreases when they point in opposite directions.
    • In uniformly accelerated motion the $(t,v)$ graph is a straight line whose slope is the acceleration and whose area against the time axis is the displacement; the part below the time axis ($v < 0$) is counted as negative.
    • $v = v_0 + a\,t$ and $x = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. The $(t,x)$ graph is a curve, and the instantaneous velocity is the slope of its tangent.
    • In free fall the acceleration is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards. It can be determined by fitting a quadratic polynomial to the measured points: $a = 2A$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A cyclist reaches the top of a hill at $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ and stops pedalling. On the downhill her acceleration is $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the hill is $60\,\text{m}$ long. How long does the descent take, and what is her velocity at the bottom?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The initial velocity is $v_0 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, the acceleration $a = 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the displacement $\Delta x = x - x_0 = 60\,\text{m}$ in the direction of motion, which is chosen as positive. We are asked for the time $t$ and the velocity $v$ at the bottom.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The acceleration is constant, so the motion is uniformly accelerated and the displacement is $\Delta x = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. The time is the unknown, and it appears in both the first and the second power, so we get a quadratic equation. Once the time is known, the velocity comes from the equation $v = v_0 + a\,t$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. Write the equation in the form $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 + v_0\,t - \Delta x = 0$ and solve it with the quadratic formula. The positive solution is

      $$t = \frac{-v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x}}{a}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. All quantities are in SI units (m, m/s, m/s²), so no conversions are needed. Under the root the unit is $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so the root is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time comes out in seconds.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the expression under the root and its square root, then the time and the velocity:

      $$\begin{aligned} &v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x \\ &= (4.0\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &\quad + 2 \cdot 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 60\,\text{m} \\ &= 76\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \\[4pt] &\sqrt{76\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} \approx 8.72\,\text{m/s} \\[4pt] &t = \frac{-4.0\,\text{m/s} + 8.72\,\text{m/s}}{0.50\,\text{m/s}^2} \\ &\approx 9.44\,\text{s} \\[4pt] &v = v_0 + a\,t \\ &= 4.0\,\text{m/s} + 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 9.44\,\text{s} \\ &\approx 8.72\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The descent takes about $9.4\,\text{s}$, and the velocity at the bottom is about $8.7\,\text{m/s}$. Two significant figures, like the initial velocity $4.0\,\text{m/s}$, the acceleration $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the distance $60\,\text{m}$. The other solution of the formula, $t \approx -25\,\text{s}$, is rejected because the motion starts at $t = 0$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The velocity grows steadily, so the average velocity is the mean of the initial and final velocities, $\tfrac{4.0\,\text{m/s} + 8.72\,\text{m/s}}{2} \approx 6.36\,\text{m/s}$, and $6.36\,\text{m/s} \cdot 9.44\,\text{s} \approx 60\,\text{m}$, the length of the hill.           Three significant figures like the initial velocity $15.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          During the rise the velocity decreases steadily from $15.0\,\text{m/s}$ to zero, so the average velocity is $7.5\,\text{m/s}$ and $7.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.529\,\text{s} \approx 11.5\,\text{m}$. A height of about three storeys is plausible for a hard throw.

        4. On the Moon an astronaut drops a hammer from rest from a height of $1.60\,\text{m}$, and the hammer hits the surface $1.41\,\text{s}$ later. The Moon has no atmosphere.

          What is the acceleration of free fall on the Moon according to this measurement?

          m/s²
          Answer

          $1.61\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          drop height $h = 1.60\,\text{m}$, fall time $t = 1.41\,\text{s}$, initial velocity $v_0 = 0$; there is no air resistance on the Moon

          Find

          the Moon's acceleration of free fall $g_\text{M}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          There is no air resistance, so the hammer falls with constant acceleration. It starts from rest, so the drop height is $\tfrac{1}{2}\,g_\text{M}\,t^2$. Solve the acceleration from this.

          Formula

          $h = \tfrac{1}{2}\,g_\text{M}\,t^2$, so $g_\text{M} = \dfrac{2h}{t^2}$

          Conversions

          The height is in metres and the time in seconds, so the result comes out in $\text{m/s}^2$. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $g_\text{M}$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 1.60\,\text{m}}{(1.41\,\text{s})^2}$
          $= \dfrac{3.20\,\text{m}}{1.9881\,\text{s}^2}$
          $\approx 1.610\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $1.61\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Three significant figures like the height $1.60\,\text{m}$ and the time $1.41\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The table value for the Moon's acceleration of free fall is $1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$, about one sixth of the Earth's value, so the result is close to it. On Earth the same drop would take only $\sqrt{2 \cdot 1.60\,\text{m} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 0.57\,\text{s}$; the slower fall on the Moon corresponds to the smaller acceleration.

        1. A ball is thrown straight up from a balcony at $8.00\,\text{m/s}$. The point of release is $6.00\,\text{m}$ above the ground, and the ball finally lands on the ground next to the balcony. Air resistance is small.

          How long after the throw does the ball hit the ground?

          s
          Answer

          $2.19\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          initial velocity $v_0 = 8.00\,\text{m/s}$ upwards, point of release $6.00\,\text{m}$ above the ground, acceleration of free fall from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$; air resistance is small

          Find

          the time $t$ from the throw to hitting the ground, in seconds

          Principle

          The ball's acceleration is $g$ downwards during the whole flight, also at the highest point, so the whole flight is one uniformly accelerated motion. Place the origin at the point of release and take upwards as positive. The ground is then at the position $y = -6.00\,\text{m}$, and the time comes from a quadratic equation.

          Formula

          $y = v_0\,t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^2$. When $y = -h$: $\tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^2 - v_0\,t - h = 0$, whose positive solution is

          $t$$= \dfrac{v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          Under the root

          $v_0^2 + 2gh$$= (8.00\,\text{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 6.00\,\text{m}$
          $= 181.72\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          and $\sqrt{181.72\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} \approx 13.48\,\text{m/s}$. So

          $t$$= \dfrac{8.00\,\text{m/s} + 13.48\,\text{m/s}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $\approx 2.19\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $2.19\,\text{s}$

          Three significant figures like the initial velocity and the height. The other solution, $t \approx -0.56\,\text{s}$, is rejected because it is before the throw.

          Reasonableness

          The rise takes $8.00\,\text{m/s} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.82\,\text{s}$ and lifts the ball $3.26\,\text{m}$, so from the highest point to the ground it is $9.26\,\text{m}$. A fall from rest takes $\sqrt{2 \cdot 9.26\,\text{m} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 1.37\,\text{s}$, and $0.82\,\text{s} + 1.37\,\text{s} = 2.19\,\text{s}$, the same result.

        2. A metro train starts from rest at a station and accelerates uniformly to $15.0\,\text{m/s}$ in $20.0\,\text{s}$. Then it drives at this speed for $60.0\,\text{s}$ and finally brakes uniformly to a stop at the next station in $15.0\,\text{s}$.

          How long is the distance between the stations, in metres?

          m
          Answer

          $1160\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          acceleration $0 \to 15.0\,\text{m/s}$ in $t_1 = 20.0\,\text{s}$, constant speed $v = 15.0\,\text{m/s}$ for $t_2 = 60.0\,\text{s}$, braking to zero in $t_3 = 15.0\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the distance between the stations $s$ in metres

          Principle

          The train always moves in the same direction, so the distance is the area under the $(t,v)$ graph. The graph is a triangle (accelerating), a rectangle (constant speed) and a triangle (braking). The accelerations do not need to be calculated, because the areas come directly from the velocity and the times.

          Formula

          $s = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v\,t_1 + v\,t_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\,v\,t_3$

          Conversions

          The velocity is in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in seconds, so the result comes out in metres. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $s$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 15.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20.0\,\text{s} + 15.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 60.0\,\text{s} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 15.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 15.0\,\text{s}$
          $= 150\,\text{m} + 900\,\text{m} + 112.5\,\text{m}$
          $= 1162.5\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $1160\,\text{m}$

          Or $1.16\,\text{km}$, three significant figures like the velocity and the times. The result $1162.5\,\text{m}$ is rounded to three significant figures, so the zero at the end of the number is not significant; in kilometres this is clearer.

          Reasonableness

          If the train travelled the whole $95\,\text{s}$ at $15.0\,\text{m/s}$, the distance would be $1425\,\text{m}$; the acceleration and the braking take away half of their own shares, $150\,\text{m} + 112.5\,\text{m}$, so $1425\,\text{m} - 262.5\,\text{m} = 1162.5\,\text{m}$. A distance of a little over a kilometre between stations is usual for a metro.

        3. A ball is thrown straight up, and its height $y$ is measured as a function of time by video analysis. The quadratic polynomial fitted to the measured points is $y = -4.86\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot t^2 + 3.10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot t + 1.20\,\text{m}$, with the upward direction positive.

          What is the ball's velocity at $t = 0.500\,\text{s}$? Give the answer with its sign.

          m/s
          Answer

          $-1.76\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the fitted polynomial $y = -4.86\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot t^2 + 3.10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot t + 1.20\,\text{m}$, upwards positive; the moment $t = 0.500\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the ball's velocity $v$ with its sign in $\text{m/s}$ at $0.500\,\text{s}$

          Principle

          The polynomial is compared with the equation of position of uniformly accelerated motion, $y = y_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. From the coefficients we read the initial velocity $v_0 = 3.10\,\text{m/s}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a = -4.86\,\text{m/s}^2$, so $a = -9.72\,\text{m/s}^2$. The velocity then comes from the equation $v = v_0 + a\,t$.

          Formula

          From the coefficients of the polynomial $y = A\,t^2 + B\,t + C$ we get $v_0 = B$ and $a = 2A$, so $v = B + 2A\,t$

          Conversions

          The coefficients are in SI units and the time in seconds. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $v$$= 3.10\,\text{m/s} + 2 \cdot (-4.86\,\text{m/s}^2) \cdot 0.500\,\text{s}$
          $= 3.10\,\text{m/s} - 4.86\,\text{m/s}$
          $= -1.76\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $-1.76\,\text{m/s}$

          Three significant figures, because the coefficients of the polynomial and the moment $0.500\,\text{s}$ are given to three significant figures. The negative sign means that the ball is already moving downwards.

          Reasonableness

          The acceleration obtained from the measurement, $-9.72\,\text{m/s}^2$, is close to $-g$, as it should be. The ball is at its highest point at the time $3.10\,\text{m/s} : 9.72\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 0.32\,\text{s}$, so at $0.500\,\text{s}$ it is already descending and the velocity must be negative. The descent has lasted $0.18\,\text{s}$, and $9.72\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.18\,\text{s} \approx 1.8\,\text{m/s}$.
