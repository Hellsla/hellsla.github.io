## fy1-5-L1-01
**Given:** speed $v = 5.5\,\text{m/s}$, time $t = 20\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the zero point, so $s_0 = 0$
**Find:** distance travelled $s$ in metres
**Principle:** The speed is constant, so the motion is uniform, and the distance is directly proportional to time.
**Formula:** $s = s_0 + vt$, and since $s_0 = 0$, $s = vt$
**Conversions:** None needed: the speed is in m/s and the time in seconds, so the product is in metres.
**Substitution:** $s = 5.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 110\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $110\,\text{m}$, two significant figures as in the speed $5.5\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** About $5\,\text{m}$ every second for twenty seconds is about $5 \cdot 20 = 100\,\text{m}$, and the unit $\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m}$ is the unit of distance.

## fy1-5-L1-02
**Given:** distance $s = 360\,\text{m}$, time $t = 45\,\text{s}$; the motion is uniform and starts from the zero point
**Find:** speed $v$ in m/s
**Principle:** In uniform motion, the speed is the distance travelled divided by the time it took.
**Formula:** $s = vt$, so $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the distance is in metres and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{360\,\text{m}}{45\,\text{s}} = 8.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
**Answer:** $8.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the time $45\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check backwards: $8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 45\,\text{s} = 360\,\text{m}$. The speed corresponds to about $29\,\text{km/h}$, which matches the pace of a good runner in a 400-metre race.

## fy1-5-L1-03
**Given:** speed $v = 1.4\,\text{m/s}$, distance $s = 560\,\text{m}$; the motion is uniform and starts from the zero point
**Find:** time $t$ in seconds
**Principle:** The speed is constant, so the distance is proportional to time, and the time is obtained by dividing the distance by the speed.
**Formula:** $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the distance is in metres and the speed in m/s, so the metres cancel and the second remains.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{560\,\text{m}}{1.4\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}} = 400\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $400\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the speed $1.4\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The speed $1.4\,\text{m/s}$ is about $5\,\text{km/h}$, at which $0.56\,\text{km}$ takes about $0.11\,\text{h} \approx 6.7\,\text{min} \approx 400\,\text{s}$.

## fy1-5-L1-04
**Given:** $s_1 = 18\,\text{m}$ in time $t_1 = 4.0\,\text{s}$; the time asked about is $t_2 = 10\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the starting point, so $s_0 = 0$
**Find:** distance $s_2$ in time $t_2$ in metres
**Principle:** The motion is uniform and starts from the zero point, so the distance is directly proportional to time: the ratio $s : t$ is the same for both times.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{s_2}{t_2} = \dfrac{s_1}{t_1}$, so $s_2 = s_1\,\dfrac{t_2}{t_1}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the times are in seconds, and the seconds cancel.
**Substitution:** $s_2 = 18\,\text{m} \cdot \dfrac{10\,\text{s}}{4.0\,\text{s}} = 45\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $45\,\text{m}$, two significant figures as in the distance $18\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The time $10\,\text{s}$ is $2.5$ times the time $4.0\,\text{s}$, so the distance must also be $2.5$ times as large: $2.5 \cdot 18\,\text{m} = 45\,\text{m}$. The speed is $18\,\text{m} : 4.0\,\text{s} = 4.5\,\text{m/s}$, and $4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 10\,\text{s} = 45\,\text{m}$.

## fy1-5-L1-05
**Given:** at time $t_1 = 2.0\,\text{s}$ the position is $s_1 = 7.0\,\text{m}$, and at time $t_2 = 6.0\,\text{s}$ the position is $s_2 = 21\,\text{m}$; the motion is uniform
**Find:** speed $v$ in m/s
**Principle:** In uniform motion the speed is constant, so it equals the change in position divided by the time interval. The initial position does not matter, because it cancels in the difference.
**Formula:** $v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the positions are in metres and the times in seconds.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{21\,\text{m} - 7.0\,\text{m}}{6.0\,\text{s} - 2.0\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{14\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s}} = 3.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$
**Answer:** $3.5\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures as in the positions $7.0\,\text{m}$ and the times $2.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The quotients $7.0\,\text{m} : 2.0\,\text{s}$ and $21\,\text{m} : 6.0\,\text{s}$ are both $3.5\,\text{m/s}$, so the position is proportional to time and the initial position is zero. The speed $3.5\,\text{m/s}$ corresponds to slow cycling, about $13\,\text{km/h}$.

## fy1-5-L2-01
**Given:** speed $v = 54\,\text{km/h}$, time $t = 14\,\text{s}$; the motion starts from the zero point
**Find:** distance $s$ in metres
**Principle:** The car drives at constant speed, so the motion is uniform and $s = vt$. The speed and the time must be in compatible units.
**Formula:** $s = vt$
**Conversions:** substitute $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$: $v = 54\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{54\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 15\,\text{m/s}$.
**Substitution:** $s = 15\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 14\,\text{s} = 210\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $210\,\text{m}$, two significant figures as in the speed $54\,\text{km/h}$ and the time $14\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The speed $54\,\text{km/h}$ is $0.9\,\text{km}$ per minute, and $14\,\text{s}$ is about $0.23\,\text{min}$, so the distance is about $0.9 \cdot 0.23\,\text{km} \approx 0.21\,\text{km} = 210\,\text{m}$.

## fy1-5-L2-02
**Given:** speed $v = 36\,\text{km/h}$, distance $s = 4.5\,\text{km}$; the motion is uniform and starts from the zero point
**Find:** time $t$ in seconds
**Principle:** The bus drives at constant speed, so the distance is proportional to time and the time is the distance divided by the speed.
**Formula:** $s = vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s}{v}$
**Conversions:** $v = 36\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{36\cdot1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 10\,\text{m/s}$ and $s = 4.5\,\text{km} = 4500\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{4500\,\text{m}}{10\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}} = 450\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $450\,\text{s}$, two significant figures as in the data $36\,\text{km/h}$ and $4.5\,\text{km}$.
**Reasonableness:** In kilometres and hours: $4.5\,\text{km} : 36\,\text{km/h} = 0.125\,\text{h} = 7.5\,\text{min} = 450\,\text{s}$, which is the same result.

## fy1-5-L2-03
**Given:** initial position $s_0 = 1.5\,\text{km}$ from the cabin, speed $v = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$ away from the cabin, time $t = 10\,\text{min}$
**Find:** distance from the cabin $s$ in kilometres
**Principle:** The hiker walks at constant speed, so the motion is uniform. The initial position is not zero, so the position is the initial position plus the distance travelled.
**Formula:** $s = s_0 + vt$
**Conversions:** $s_0 = 1.5\,\text{km} = 1500\,\text{m}$ and $t = 10\,\text{min} = 600\,\text{s}$. Finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $s = 1500\,\text{m} + 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 600\,\text{s} = 1500\,\text{m} + 900\,\text{m} = 2400\,\text{m} = 2.4\,\text{km}$
**Answer:** $2.4\,\text{km}$, two significant figures as in the data $1.5\,\text{km}$ and $1.5\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The speed $1.5\,\text{m/s}$ is about $5.4\,\text{km/h}$, so in ten minutes, which is one sixth of an hour, the hiker advances about $0.9\,\text{km}$. Added to the initial position, $1.5\,\text{km} + 0.9\,\text{km} = 2.4\,\text{km}$, and the result is greater than the initial position, as it should be.

## fy1-5-L2-04
**Given:** time $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$; the travel time of light is ignored
**Find:** distance of the lightning $s$ in kilometres
**Principle:** Sound travels at constant speed, so the distance it travels is $s = vt$. Light arrives practically at once, so the delay of the thunder is the travel time of sound over the whole distance.
**Formula:** $s = vt$
**Conversions:** The distance is calculated in metres, and finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $s = 343\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 1715\,\text{m} = 1.7\,\text{km}$
**Answer:** $1.7\,\text{km}$, two significant figures as in the time $5.0\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Rule of thumb: three seconds corresponds to about a kilometre, so five seconds corresponds to about $1.7\,\text{km}$. Light would travel this distance in $1715\,\text{m} : (3.0 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}) \approx 6\,\mu\text{s}$, which is negligible compared with the delay $5.0\,\text{s}$.

## fy1-5-L3-01
**Given:** $s_0 = 119.7\,\text{AU}$ at time $t = 0$ (5 November 2018), $v = 15.341\,\text{km/s}$, $s = 150\,\text{AU}$, $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$, $1\,\text{day} = 86\,400\,\text{s}$
**Find:** time $t$ in days when the probe is at a distance of $150\,\text{AU}$
**Principle:** The speed is assumed constant, so the uniform motion model $s = s_0 + vt$ applies. The result is a model value, because the Sun's gravity slows the probe slightly and the real time is therefore a little longer.
**Formula:** $s = s_0 + vt$, so $t = \dfrac{s - s_0}{v}$
**Conversions:** The speed in astronomical units per day: $v = 15.341\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}} \cdot 86\,400\,\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{day}} = 1\,325\,462\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{day}}$, and $\dfrac{1\,325\,462\,\text{km/day}}{149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km/AU}} = 0.0088602\,\tfrac{\text{AU}}{\text{day}}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{150\,\text{AU} - 119.7\,\text{AU}}{0.0088602\,\text{AU/day}} = \dfrac{30.3\,\text{AU}}{0.0088602\,\text{AU/day}} = 3419.8\,\text{day}$
**Answer:** $3420\,\text{day}$, three significant figures, when $150\,\text{AU}$ is interpreted as accurate to three figures and the difference is $30.3\,\text{AU}$.
**Reasonableness:** The rate is about $3.2\,\text{AU}$ per year, so $30.3\,\text{AU}$ takes about $30.3 : 3.2 \approx 9.5$ years. The result $3420\,\text{day} \approx 9.4$ years fits this and falls in March 2028. The model differs from the JPL value by about 0.2 AU, which corresponds to an error of about 20 days in the date.

## fy1-5-L3-02
**Given:** distance $d = 150\,\text{AU}$, speed of the signal $c = 299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{AU} = 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km}$
**Find:** round-trip travel time $t$ in hours
**Principle:** A radio signal travels in vacuum at constant speed, so the distance it travels is $s = ct$. The command and the reply each travel the distance $d$, so the total distance is $2d$. During this time the probe moves about $15\,\text{km/s} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{km} \approx 0.015\,\text{AU}$, which is only $0.01\,\%$ of the distance $150\,\text{AU}$. In the same time the Earth moves about $30\,\text{km/s} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} \approx 0.03\,\text{AU}$, only about $0.02\,\%$, so neither motion needs to be taken into account.
**Formula:** $2d = ct$, so $t = \dfrac{2d}{c}$
**Conversions:** $d = 150 \cdot 149\,597\,870.7\,\text{km} = 2.2440 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{km} = 2.2440 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}$. Finally $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{2 \cdot 2.2440 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}}{299\,792\,458\,\text{m/s}} = 1.497 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s} = \dfrac{1.497 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{s}}{3600\,\text{s/h}} = 41.58\,\text{h}$
**Answer:** $41.6\,\text{h}$, three significant figures, when the distance $150\,\text{AU}$ is interpreted as accurate to three figures.
**Reasonableness:** Light travels one AU in about $499\,\text{s} \approx 8.3\,\text{min}$, so $150\,\text{AU}$ takes about $150 \cdot 8.3\,\text{min} \approx 1250\,\text{min} \approx 20.8\,\text{h}$, and there and back $41.6\,\text{h}$. A delay of more than a day is reasonable, because the probe is tens of billions of kilometres away.

## fy1-5-L3-03
**Given:** speed of the walker $v_1 = 1.5\,\text{m/s}$, speed of the cyclist $v_2 = 4.5\,\text{m/s}$, delay of the cyclist $t_0 = 40\,\text{s}$; both start from the door, so $s_0 = 0$
**Find:** distance from the door $s$ at the point where the cyclist catches up with the walker
**Principle:** Both move uniformly. They are at the same position when their distances from the door are equal. The time $t$ is measured from the start of the walker, so the cyclist has been moving for the time $t - t_0$.
**Formula:** $s_1 = v_1 t$ and $s_2 = v_2 (t - t_0)$. From the condition $s_1 = s_2$ we get $v_1 t = v_2 (t - t_0)$, so $t = \dfrac{v_2 t_0}{v_2 - v_1}$ and $s = v_1 t$.
**Conversions:** None needed: the speeds are in m/s and the delay in seconds.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{4.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 40\,\text{s}}{4.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} - 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}} = 60\,\text{s}$ and $s = 1.5\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 60\,\text{s} = 90\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $90\,\text{m}$, two significant figures as in the speeds.
**Reasonableness:** The cyclist rides for $60\,\text{s} - 40\,\text{s} = 20\,\text{s}$, and $4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 20\,\text{s} = 90\,\text{m}$, the same result. Another check: the walker's lead after $40\,\text{s}$ is $1.5 \cdot 40 = 60\,\text{m}$, and the cyclist closes it at the speed difference $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ in $60 : 3.0 = 20\,\text{s}$.
