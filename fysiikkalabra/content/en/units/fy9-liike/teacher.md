## Lesson plan (75 min)

- **0–5 min, Think about it.** The tram's velocity graph. The students discuss with a partner how the distance can be worked out from a graph that has no metres. The answer is not given yet: the Investigate task solves it, and the end of the Key idea section sums it up.
- **5–15 min, Predict (starting-level test).** Six questions alone, without notes. The students write the numbers of the questions they got wrong in their notebooks.
- **15–25 min, Investigate.** The tram's $(t,v)$ graph in pairs: each student calculates the distance of one stage, and the pair puts together the whole distance and the accelerations of the stages. Discuss together: why the area is the distance and the slope the acceleration.
- **25–35 min, Theory summary.** Go through the concept map on the board: draw the $(t,x)$ and $(t,v)$ graphs of one motion one above the other and show the slope downwards and the area upwards. Return to Think about it.
- **35–55 min, Apply.** Four multiple-choice questions in pairs (about $6\,\text{min}$), then four calculations alone. If time runs short, *A moose on the road* and *The dog and the jogger* (about $5\,\text{min}$ each) are left as homework. Fast students continue without hints, slower ones use the hints one at a time.
- **55–63 min, Check your understanding and exit ticket.** Alone. The students assess the explanation task themselves with the criteria.
- **63–75 min, Exercises.** Twelve minutes is enough for the beginning of one long task in exam conditions, without the model answer: recommended *The acceleration lane* a–b (a calculation task) or *A local train arrives at the station* part a (a graph on squared paper). The rest of the task and the other tasks are homework, two or three at a time before the next lesson.

## Using the starting-level test
The Predict questions are a diagnostic test, not a grade. Each question corresponds to one chapter:

- Questions 1 and 2: reading a $(t,x)$ graph, and displacement and distance (chapter *Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs*).
- Question 3: the initial position and direct proportionality (chapter *Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs*).
- Questions 4–6: the sign of the acceleration, the highest point and the area (chapter *Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion*).

If more than a third of the group answers questions 4 or 5 wrongly, go through the signs on the board before the Apply section: draw the $(t,v)$ graph of a ball thrown upwards and ask the direction of the velocity and the acceleration at each stage. Mistakes in question 6 are usually corrected in the Investigate task.

## Pair work
- Investigate: one student reads the graph and says the values aloud, the other calculates; they swap after each stage.
- Apply multiple-choice questions: each student first chooses alone and only then justifies the choice to the partner. They answer only when the pair agrees.
- In the long task *Average speed enforcement* (written answers) pairs can write part a together and assess it with another pair using the marking.

## Differentiation
- **Fast students** do the Apply calculations without hints and move on to the long task *Two balls in the lobby*, whose part c needs an insight. Extra question: draw the velocities of both balls in the same $(t,v)$ graph and show that the area between the lines up to the moment of passing is the initial distance between the balls. (The graphs are parallel, so the region is a parallelogram whose height is the difference of the velocities.)
- **Slower students** do the calculations *The kick scooter* and *The pesäpallo pitch* of the Apply section with the hints and leave *The dog and the jogger* for home. The *Review if needed* links of the theory summary lead straight to the original chapters, which have basic-level exercises.

## Assessment
The students assess the long tasks themselves with the marking. If the teacher assesses them, the same criteria work: the choice of coordinate system and signs, a symbolic solution, unit conversions by substituting the units, precision and reasonableness; in a graph part the axes, the points and the fit; in an assessment part the evaluation of a claim with the numbers of the data. All the data are invented examples, not measurements.
