## fy8-16-L1-01
**Given:** wavelength of the $\text{H}\beta$ line in the laboratory $\lambda_0 = 486.1\,\text{nm}$ and in the spectrum of the galaxy $\lambda = 492.4\,\text{nm}$
**Asked:** the redshift $z$, a pure number without a unit
**Principle:** In the light of the galaxy we see the same hydrogen line as in the laboratory, but shifted to a longer wavelength. Redshift is the change in wavelength relative to the laboratory wavelength of the line.
**Formula:** $z = \dfrac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$
**Conversions:** None needed: both wavelengths are in nanometres, and the unit cancels.
**Substitution:** $z = \dfrac{492.4\,\text{nm} - 486.1\,\text{nm}}{486.1\,\text{nm}} = \dfrac{6.3\,\text{nm}}{486.1\,\text{nm}} = 0.01296$
**Answer:** $0.013$, two significant figures, as in the wavelength difference $6.3\,\text{nm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The line shifted by about $6\,\text{nm}$, a little over one percent of its wavelength, so $z \approx 0.01$ is of the right order of magnitude. The redshift is positive because the line shifted to a longer wavelength.

**Asked:** the recession speed $v$ in kilometres per second
**Principle:** When the redshift is small, about $z < 0.1$, the recession speed of a galaxy is the product of the redshift and the speed of light. Here $z = 0.0250$ is small, so the equation holds.
**Formula:** $v = c\,z$
**Conversions:** None needed: the speed of light is already in kilometres per second, and the redshift is a pure number.
**Substitution:** $v = 2.998 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s} \cdot 0.0250 = 7495\,\text{km/s}$
**Answer:** $7.50 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{km/s}$, three significant figures, as in the redshift $0.0250$.
**Reasonableness:** A redshift of $0.025$ means that the speed is $2.5\,\%$ of the speed of light: $0.025 \cdot 300\,000\,\text{km/s} = 7500\,\text{km/s}$. The speed is much smaller than the speed of light, as it should be for a small redshift.

## fy8-16-L1-05
**Given:** wavelength $\lambda = 1.06\,\text{mm}$, $h = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$, $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$
**Asked:** the energy of the photon $E$ in the unit $10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$
**Principle:** The background radiation is electromagnetic radiation whose energy is quantised into photons (chapter *The photoelectric effect and the photon*). The energy of a photon is inversely proportional to its wavelength.
**Formula:** $E = hf$ and $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, so $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $1.06\,\text{mm} = 1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$. To change from joules to electronvolts, substitute $1\,\text{J} = \dfrac{1}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV}$.
**Substitution:** $E = 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{1.06 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}} = 1.8740 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\text{J} = \dfrac{1.8740 \cdot 10^{-22}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\text{eV} = 1.1698 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$
**Answer:** $1.17 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$, three significant figures, as in the wavelength $1.06\,\text{mm}$.
**Reasonableness:** A photon of visible light has an energy of $1.7$–$3.3\,\text{eV}$ and a wavelength of about $500\,\text{nm}$. The wavelength of the background radiation is about $2000$ times longer, so the energy is about $2000$ times smaller: $2\,\text{eV} : 2000 = 1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{eV}$.

## fy8-16-L2-02
**Given:** Hubble constant $H_0 = 67.4\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$, $1\,\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{km}$, $1\,\text{a} = 3.156 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{s}$
**Asked:** the estimate of the age of the universe $t$ in billions of years
**Principle:** If the speed of the expansion has stayed the same, a galaxy that is now at distance $d$ and recedes at the speed $v = H_0\,d$ was in the same place as we were a time $t = \tfrac{d}{v}$ ago. The distance cancels, so the time is the same for all galaxies.
**Formula:** $t = \dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{d}{H_0\,d}$, so $t = \dfrac{1}{H_0}$
**Conversions:** For the megaparsec, substitute $1\,\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{km}$ so that the kilometres cancel, and change seconds into years by substituting $1\,\text{s} = \dfrac{1}{3.156 \cdot 10^{7}}\,\text{a}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{1\,\text{Mpc}}{67.4\,\text{km/s}} = \dfrac{3.086 \cdot 10^{19}\,\text{km}}{67.4\,\text{km/s}} = 4.5786 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{s} = \dfrac{4.5786 \cdot 10^{17}}{3.156 \cdot 10^{7}}\,\text{a} = 1.4508 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{a}$
**Answer:** $14.5 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{a}$, that is, about $14.5$ billion years, three significant figures, as in the Hubble constant $67.4\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$.
**Reasonableness:** With $70\,\tfrac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}$ the estimate is about $14.0$ billion years; a smaller Hubble constant means slower expansion and therefore a longer time. The accurate age of $13.8$ billion years is close, because the expansion first slowed down and then accelerated.

**Given:** seven protons for every neutron, all neutrons in helium-4 nuclei (two protons and two neutrons), the masses of the proton and the neutron equal
**Asked:** the fraction of helium in the total mass of the nuclei, as a percentage
**Principle:** Because the masses of the proton and the neutron are assumed equal, the mass fraction is the same as the fraction of nucleons. For every two neutrons of a helium nucleus, two protons are needed in the helium nucleus, so helium contains twice as many nucleons as there are neutrons. The total number of nucleons does not change in fusion.
**Formula:** When there are $n$ neutrons and $p$ protons, $2n$ nucleons bind into helium, so the mass fraction is $Y = \dfrac{2n}{n + p}$

**Substitution:** Consider a group with $n = 1$ and $p = 7$: $Y = \dfrac{2 \cdot 1}{1 + 7} = \dfrac{2}{8} = 0.25 = 25\,\%$
**Answer:** $25\,\%$; the ratio $1 : 7$ is approximate, so the result is about a quarter.
**Reasonableness:** For example, of $16$ nucleons, $2$ are neutrons and $14$ protons: one helium nucleus ($4$ nucleons) and $12$ hydrogen nuclei form, and $\tfrac{4}{16} = 25\,\%$. The result matches the helium fraction observed in the oldest stars and gas clouds, about a quarter of the mass.

## fy8-16-L1-06
**Asked:** what causes the shift of the spectral lines of distant galaxies to longer wavelengths, that is, the redshift.
**Principle:** The space of the universe expands, and the expansion stretches light travelling in space. The wavelength of the light grows in the same proportion as space stretches during the journey, so the redshift $z = \tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0}$ is the same fraction of each line's own wavelength.
**Model answer:** The cause of the redshift is the expansion of space: when space stretches during the journey of the light, the wavelength of the light stretches too, and all spectral lines shift to longer wavelengths. The colour of the galaxy or the temperature of its stars does not explain it, because the shift applies as an equal fraction to every line. Dust and gas remove light only at certain wavelengths, but they do not move the positions of the lines. The motion of galaxies through space (the Doppler effect) does not explain the shift of distant galaxies, because it describes only the galaxy's own motion, whereas the shift arises from the expansion of space and grows with distance.

## fy8-16-L1-08
**Asked:** where the almost uniform radiation of the cosmic background radiation, arriving from every direction, comes from.
**Principle:** About $380\,000$ years after the Big Bang, protons and electrons formed atoms. The photons no longer scattered continuously from free electrons, so the universe became transparent and the radiation could travel freely. It arrives from every direction because the hot, dense universe was the same everywhere. The expansion has since stretched its wavelengths about $1100$-fold, so the radiation is now microwaves with a temperature of $2.725\,\text{K}$.
**Model answer:** The background radiation is radiation that was released when atoms formed, and the expansion of space has stretched it into microwaves. It is not the light of stars and galaxies, because they are only in certain directions and formed only after the background radiation was released. Nor is it radiation from dark matter, because dark matter does not emit radiation. Supernovae do not explain it, because they are individual events and do not produce almost exactly the same radiation from every direction.

Space expands everywhere, so viewed from any galaxy the others recede in the same way and there is no centre. If the explosion had a location, the recession would look different from different galaxies, but observations do not support that. According to the Hubble–Lemaître law, distant galaxies recede faster, not slower, so the claim of slower recession far away is wrong. The claim of tired light is also wrong, because the distances between galaxies really do grow as space expands.

## fy8-16-L2-08
**Asked:** how many times the distance of galaxy A, $d_\text{A}$, the distance of galaxy B, $d_\text{B}$, is.
**Principle:** The redshift $z = \tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0}$ is the same fraction of the wavelength of each line itself, so the redshifts calculated from different lines are comparable, but the shifts in nanometres are not. At a small redshift $v = cz$, and by the Hubble–Lemaître law $d = \tfrac{v}{H_0}$, so $d = \tfrac{cz}{H_0}$ is directly proportional to the redshift. In the ratio $c$ and $H_0$ cancel.
**Model answer:** The redshift of galaxy A:

$$\begin{aligned}
&z_\text{A} = \frac{662.9\,\text{nm} - 656.3\,\text{nm}}{656.3\,\text{nm}} \\
&\phantom{z_\text{A}} = \frac{6.6\,\text{nm}}{656.3\,\text{nm}} = 0.010056
\end{aligned}$$

The redshift of galaxy B:

$$\begin{aligned}
&z_\text{B} = \frac{500.7\,\text{nm} - 486.1\,\text{nm}}{486.1\,\text{nm}} \\
&\phantom{z_\text{B}} = \frac{14.6\,\text{nm}}{486.1\,\text{nm}} = 0.030035
\end{aligned}$$

The ratio of the distances:

$$\begin{aligned}
&\frac{d_\text{B}}{d_\text{A}} = \frac{c z_\text{B} : H_0}{c z_\text{A} : H_0} = \frac{z_\text{B}}{z_\text{A}} \\
&\phantom{\frac{d_\text{B}}{d_\text{A}}} = \frac{0.030035}{0.010056} = 2.987
\end{aligned}$$

The distance of galaxy B is about $3.0$ times the distance of galaxy A, two significant figures like the shift $6.6\,\text{nm}$. Both redshifts are below $0.1$, so $v = cz$ holds. The wrong options: comparing the shifts in nanometres gives $14.6 : 6.6 \approx 2.2$, comparing the measured wavelengths gives $500.7 : 662.9 \approx 0.76$, and the inverse dependence gives $0.010056 : 0.030035 \approx 0.33$. Reasonableness: the line of galaxy B has shifted about $3\,\%$ and the line of galaxy A about $1\,\%$ of its own wavelength, so B recedes about three times faster and is therefore about three times farther away.

## fy8-16-L3-04
**Given:** sample data: orbital speeds $v_\text{obs}$ and model speeds $v_\text{vis}$ at seven distances $r = 2$–$30\,\text{kpc}$ in the table; $r = 10\,\text{kpc}$: $v_\text{obs} = 207\,\text{km/s}$, $v_\text{vis} = 185\,\text{km/s}$; $r = 30\,\text{kpc}$: $v_\text{obs} = 213\,\text{km/s}$, $v_\text{vis} = 113\,\text{km/s}$

## fy8-16-L3-05
**Given:** the Lyman-alpha line in the laboratory $\lambda_0 = 121.6\,\text{nm}$ and in the spectrum of the quasar $\lambda = 486.4\,\text{nm}$; present temperature of the background radiation $T_\text{now} = 2.725\,\text{K}$; the temperature is inversely proportional to the stretching of space
**Asked:** a) the redshift $z$; b) why $v = c\,z$ does not hold; c) the temperature $T$ of the background radiation when the light set out, in kelvins
**Principle:** The wavelength of the light has stretched in the same proportion as space, so the stretching factor is $k = \tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda_0} = 1 + z$. The formula $v = c\,z$ is an approximation only for a small redshift, about $z < 0.1$. The temperature of the background radiation was then $k$ times the present one. The rule checks out: $2.725\,\text{K} \cdot 1100 \approx 3000\,\text{K}$.
**Formula:** $z = \dfrac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$, $k = 1 + z$ and $T = k\,T_\text{now}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the wavelengths are in nanometres, and the unit cancels.
**Substitution:** a) $z = \dfrac{486.4\,\text{nm} - 121.6\,\text{nm}}{121.6\,\text{nm}} = \dfrac{364.8\,\text{nm}}{121.6\,\text{nm}} = 3.000$. b) $v = c\,z = 3.000\,c = 8.99 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km/s}$. c) $k = 1 + 3.000 = 4.000$ and $T = 4.000 \cdot 2.725\,\text{K} = 10.9\,\text{K}$
**Answer:** $10.9\,\text{K}$, three significant figures, as in the redshift $z = 3.00$. a) $z = 3.00$. b) The formula $v = c\,z$ is an approximation for a small redshift ($z < 0.1$), and here $z = 3.00$ is much larger, so the result cannot be regarded as the speed of the galaxy.
**Reasonableness:** A common slip is to use $z = 3$ as the stretching factor instead of $1 + z = 4$, which would wrongly give the temperature $8.2\,\text{K}$. The temperature $10.9\,\text{K}$ is greater than the present $2.725\,\text{K}$ but much smaller than the $3000\,\text{K}$ when the background radiation was released, as it should be, because the light of the quasar set out much later than the background radiation. The typical wavelength was then $1.06\,\text{mm} : 4 = 0.265\,\text{mm}$, and the product $T \cdot \lambda$ is the same as now: $10.9\,\text{K} \cdot 0.265\,\text{mm} = 2.89\,\text{K mm}$ and $2.725\,\text{K} \cdot 1.06\,\text{mm} = 2.89\,\text{K mm}$.
