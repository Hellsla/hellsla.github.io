## fy3-1-L1-01
**Given:** $m = 4.0\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ (Earth)
**Find:** the weight $G$ of the object on Earth in newtons
**Principle:** Weight is a measure of the strength of the interaction between the Earth and the object, and it is directly proportional to mass. The object is on the surface of the Earth, so the constant is the Earth's acceleration of free fall $g$.
**Formula:** $G = m g$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side, so the formula does not need rearranging: $G = m g$.
**Conversions:** The mass is already in kilograms and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the product comes out directly in newtons: $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2$.
**Substitution:** $G = 4.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 39.24\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $39\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $4.0\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of a 1 kg mass is about $10\,\text{N}$, so the weight of a $4.0\,\text{kg}$ mass is about $4 \cdot 10\,\text{N} = 40\,\text{N}$. The result is positive and in newtons, as a force should be.

## fy3-1-L1-02
**Given:** $F_1 = 4.0\,\text{N}$ and its extension $x_1 = 2.0\,\text{cm}$; a second force $F = 12\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the extension $x$ in centimetres
**Principle:** The extension of a force meter's spring is directly proportional to the force stretching it (the spring is not stretched too far), so when the force is multiplied, the extension is multiplied by the same number. That is why we can use a ratio.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{x}{x_1} = \dfrac{F}{F_1}$, so $x = \dfrac{x_1 F}{F_1}$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed: the extension comes out in the same unit as $x_1$ (centimetres), and the newtons cancel in the ratio $\dfrac{F}{F_1}$.
**Substitution:** $x = \dfrac{2.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 12\,\text{N}}{4.0\,\text{N}} = 6.0\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $6.0\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the given values.
**Reasonableness:** The force is $\dfrac{12}{4.0} = 3$ times as great, so the extension must be $3 \cdot 2.0\,\text{cm} = 6.0\,\text{cm}$. The spring constant agrees too: $\dfrac{2.0\,\text{cm}}{4.0\,\text{N}} = 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{N}}$ and $0.50 \cdot 12 = 6.0$.

## fy3-1-L1-03
**Given:** $m = 30\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the weight on the Moon $G$ in newtons
**Principle:** Weight is a measure of the strength of the interaction between the Moon and the instrument, and it is directly proportional to mass. The instrument is on the surface of the Moon, so the constant is the Moon's $g$, not the Earth's.
**Formula:** $G = m g_\text{Moon}$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side.
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes out directly in newtons.
**Substitution:** $G = 30\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2 = 48.6\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $49\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $30\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** On Earth the instrument's weight would be $30\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 294.3\,\text{N}$. The Moon's $g$ is about one sixth of the Earth's value, and $\dfrac{294.3\,\text{N}}{6} \approx 49\,\text{N}$, so the result agrees.

## fy3-1-L1-04
**Given:** the measurement points of the graph at grid crossings; the extension of the spring with the object $x = 3.5\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** the force $F$ in newtons
**Principle:** The extension of the spring is directly proportional to the force (the spring is not stretched too far), so the points lie on a straight line through the origin and the ratio $x/F$ is the same everywhere. The unknown force does not have to be read between the points by eye: it is calculated from the ratio.
**Reading the graph:** Read two points from the grid crossings: $(4\,\text{N};\ 2\,\text{cm})$ and $(10\,\text{N};\ 5\,\text{cm})$. In both, $x/F = 0.5\,\text{cm/N}$, so the points lie on the same straight line through the origin. The extension $3.5\,\text{cm}$ is between the points $(6\,\text{N};\ 3\,\text{cm})$ and $(8\,\text{N};\ 4\,\text{cm})$, so the force must be between $6\,\text{N}$ and $8\,\text{N}$. An answer read by eye, $6.8$–$7.2\,\text{N}$, is accepted.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{x}{F} = \dfrac{x_1}{F_1}$, so $F = \dfrac{F_1 x}{x_1}$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed: the centimetres cancel in the ratio $\dfrac{x}{x_1}$ and the force comes out in newtons.
**Substitution:** $F = \dfrac{4\,\text{N} \cdot 3.5\,\text{cm}}{2\,\text{cm}} = 7.0\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $F = 7.0\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the extension $3.5\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The extension $3.5\,\text{cm}$ is $1.75$ times that of the point $(4\,\text{N};\ 2\,\text{cm})$, and $1.75 \cdot 4\,\text{N} = 7.0\,\text{N}$. The result is between $6\,\text{N}$ and $8\,\text{N}$, as it should be.

## fy3-1-L1-05
**Given:** $m = 2.5\,\text{kg}$, $g_\text{Jupiter} = 24.8\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the weight on Jupiter $G$ in newtons
**Principle:** Weight is directly proportional to mass, and the constant is the $g$ of the celestial body on whose surface (here the cloud tops) the object is. Mass is the same everywhere, and only $g$ changes.
**Formula:** $G = m g_\text{Jupiter}$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side.
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes out directly in newtons.
**Substitution:** $G = 2.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 24.8\,\text{m/s}^2 = 62.0\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $62\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $2.5\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** On Earth the weight would be $2.5\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 24.5\,\text{N}$. Jupiter's $g$ is $\dfrac{24.8}{9.81} \approx 2.5$ times the Earth's value, and $2.5 \cdot 24.5\,\text{N} \approx 62\,\text{N}$, so $62\,\text{N}$ agrees.

## fy3-1-L2-01
**Given:** the force meter reading, that is, the weight $G = 49\,\text{N}$ on Earth; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the mass $m$ of the object in kilograms
**Principle:** A force meter measures force, and the object hangs from the meter on Earth, so the reading is the object's weight $G = m g$. The mass is obtained by dividing the weight by the Earth's $g$.
**Formula:** $G = m g$, so $m = \dfrac{G}{g}$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed: $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2$, so $\dfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m/s}^2}$ is a kilogram.
**Substitution:** $m = \dfrac{49\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = 4.995\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $5.0\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures like the weight $49\,\text{N}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of a 1 kg mass is about $10\,\text{N}$, so $49\,\text{N}$ corresponds to a mass of about $5\,\text{kg}$. Check by calculating back: $5.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 49.05\,\text{N} \approx 49\,\text{N}$.

## fy3-1-L2-02
**Given:** $m = 60\,\text{g}$; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ (Earth)
**Find:** the weight $G$ in newtons
**Principle:** Weight is directly proportional to mass, $G = m g$. The egg is on the surface of the Earth, so we use the Earth's $g$. The formula needs the mass in kilograms, so the grams must be converted.
**Formula:** $G = m g$. The weight we want is already alone on the left-hand side.
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{g} = 0.001\,\text{kg}$, so $m = 60\,\text{g} = 0.060\,\text{kg}$. This way the result comes out in newtons.
**Substitution:** $G = 0.060\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 0.5886\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $0.59\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $60\,\text{g}$.
**Reasonableness:** The weight of an object of about $100\,\text{g}$ is $1\,\text{N}$, so the weight of $60\,\text{g}$ is about $0.6\,\text{N}$. If the mass had not been converted, we would have got $60 \cdot 9.81 \approx 589$, a value a thousand times too large, which is impossible for an egg.

## fy3-1-L2-03
**Given:** $m = 145\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the decrease in weight $\Delta G = G_\text{Earth} - G_\text{Moon}$ in newtons
**Principle:** The mass is the same in both places, and the weight in each is $G = m g$ with the $g$ of that place. The difference is obtained by subtracting the weight on the Moon from the weight on Earth.
**Formula:** $\Delta G = m g_\text{Earth} - m g_\text{Moon}$, so $\Delta G = m (g_\text{Earth} - g_\text{Moon})$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and the $g$ values in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes out in newtons and no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $\Delta G = 145\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2) = 145\,\text{kg} \cdot 8.19\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1187.55\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $1190\,\text{N}$, three significant figures like the mass $145\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** On Earth the weight is about $145 \cdot 9.81\,\text{N} \approx 1422\,\text{N}$ and on the Moon about $145 \cdot 1.62\,\text{N} \approx 235\,\text{N}$, and the difference is $1422\,\text{N} - 235\,\text{N} \approx 1187\,\text{N}$. On the Moon the weight is $\dfrac{1.62}{9.81} \approx \dfrac{1}{6}$ of the weight on Earth, so it decreases by about $\dfrac{5}{6}$, that is, $83\,\%$.

## fy3-1-L2-04
**Given:** $m = 0.20\,\text{kg}$ and its extension $x_1 = 1.5\,\text{cm}$ on Earth; a new extension $x = 4.5\,\text{cm}$; from the table $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the stretching force $F$ in newtons
**Principle:** The weight of the object $G = m g$ is the force that stretches the spring by $x_1$. The extension is directly proportional to the force (the spring is not stretched too far), so the force at the new extension is obtained from the ratio.
**Formula:** $G = m g$ and $\dfrac{F}{G} = \dfrac{x}{x_1}$, so $F = \dfrac{m g x}{x_1}$
**Conversions:** The mass is already in kilograms. The centimetres cancel in the ratio $\dfrac{x}{x_1}$, so the extensions do not need to be converted to metres.
**Substitution:** $F = \dfrac{0.20\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 4.5\,\text{cm}}{1.5\,\text{cm}} = 1.962\,\text{N} \cdot \dfrac{4.5}{1.5} = 5.886\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $5.9\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the mass $0.20\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The extension is $\dfrac{4.5}{1.5} = 3$ times as great, so the force must be three times as great too: $3 \cdot 1.962\,\text{N} = 5.886\,\text{N}$. Rough estimate: $0.2\,\text{kg}$ weighs about $2\,\text{N}$ and $3 \cdot 2\,\text{N} = 6\,\text{N}$.

## fy3-1-L3-01
**Given:** $G_\text{Earth} = 18\,\text{N}$; from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Mars} = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the meter reading on Mars $G_\text{Mars}$ in newtons
**Principle:** The meter shows the weight. The mass of the object stays the same on Mars and only $g$ changes, so the weight changes in the same proportion as $g$. The mass is therefore constant here, and it links the two places.
**Formula:** $m = \dfrac{G_\text{Earth}}{g_\text{Earth}}$ and $G_\text{Mars} = m g_\text{Mars}$, so $G_\text{Mars} = \dfrac{G_\text{Earth} g_\text{Mars}}{g_\text{Earth}}$
**Conversions:** No conversions are needed: $\dfrac{\text{m/s}^2}{\text{m/s}^2}$ cancels, and the weight stays in newtons.
**Substitution:** $m = \dfrac{18\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = 1.835\,\text{kg}$, so $G_\text{Mars} = 1.835\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2 = 6.807\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $6.8\,\text{N}$, two significant figures like the weight $18\,\text{N}$.
**Reasonableness:** Mars's $g$ is $\dfrac{3.71}{9.81} \approx 0.38$ of the Earth's value, and $0.38 \cdot 18\,\text{N} \approx 6.8\,\text{N}$. The weight decreases because the interaction on Mars is weaker, so the meter must show less than on Earth.

## fy3-1-L3-02
**Given:** $m_\text{Earth} = 22.0\,\text{kg}$; from the table $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the mass that can be lifted on the Moon $m_\text{Moon}$ in kilograms
**Principle:** The lifter can produce the same force $F$ in both places, and the weight of the mass lifted must be at most this force. On the Moon $g$ is smaller, so the same force is enough for a greater mass.
**Formula:** $F = m_\text{Earth} g_\text{Earth}$ and $F = m_\text{Moon} g_\text{Moon}$, so $m_\text{Moon} = \dfrac{m_\text{Earth} g_\text{Earth}}{g_\text{Moon}}$
**Conversions:** The mass is in kilograms and the $g$ values in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the force comes out in newtons and the mass in kilograms without conversions.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{Moon} = \dfrac{22.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{215.82\,\text{N}}{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2} = 133.2\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $133\,\text{kg}$, three significant figures like the mass $22.0\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The Moon's $g$ is about one sixth of the Earth's value, so the mass must be about $6 \cdot 22.0\,\text{kg} = 132\,\text{kg}$. Check by calculating back: $133.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2 = 215.8\,\text{N}$, the same as the weight of $22.0\,\text{kg}$ on Earth.

## fy3-1-L3-03
**Given:** the meter reading $L = 26\,\text{kg}$ on Mars; from the table $g_\text{Mars} = 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2$; the scale shows the force divided by the Earth's value $g_\text{Earth} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the true mass $m$ of the object in kilograms
**Principle:** The meter measures force, not mass: its scale was made on Earth by dividing the force by the number $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. On Mars we must therefore first work out the force, that is, the weight of the object, from the reading, and the mass is obtained by dividing it by Mars's $g$.
**Formula:** $L = \dfrac{G}{g_\text{Earth}}$, so $G = L g_\text{Earth}$. On Mars $G = m g_\text{Mars}$, so $m = \dfrac{L g_\text{Earth}}{g_\text{Mars}}$
**Conversions:** The reading $26\,\text{kg}$ is interpreted as a number on the scale: the force is $G = 26\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ in newtons. No other conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $m = \dfrac{26\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{3.71\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{255.06\,\text{N}}{3.71\,\text{m/s}^2} = 68.75\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $69\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures like the reading $26\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** On Mars $g$ is $\dfrac{3.71}{9.81} \approx 0.38$ of the Earth's value, so the reading is only about $0.38$ times the mass. The mass must be about $\dfrac{26\,\text{kg}}{0.38} \approx 69\,\text{kg}$, clearly greater than the reading. Check by calculating back: $69\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.71\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 256\,\text{N}$ and $\dfrac{256\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 26\,\text{kg}$.
