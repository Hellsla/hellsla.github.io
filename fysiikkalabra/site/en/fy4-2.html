Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion

Why does a stone dropped from a bridge travel a longer distance during each second than during the second before?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain what acceleration tells you about the change in velocity, and interpret its sign.
  • calculate the velocity, position and time of uniformly accelerated motion, and read them from $(t,v)$ and $(t,x)$ graphs.
  • determine the acceleration of free fall by fitting a quadratic polynomial to measured points.

1Think about it

You drop a stone from a footbridge into a river, and your friend films the fall with a phone. The video shows that during the first half second the stone falls about $1.2\,\text{m}$. After one whole second it has already fallen almost $5\,\text{m}$. So during the second half second it falls a much longer distance than during the first, even though the time is the same.

What changes in the stone's motion so that in every time interval of equal length it travels a longer distance than in the one before?

Review from the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs

The $(t,x)$ graph of a cart is a straight line through the points $(0\,\text{s}$; $2.0\,\text{m})$ and $(4.0\,\text{s}$; $14\,\text{m})$. What is the cart's velocity?

  • You divided the change in time by the change in position. Velocity is the change in position divided by the change in time: $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$.

  • Correct: the velocity is the slope of the line, $v = \dfrac{14\,\text{m} - 2.0\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$. In this chapter the same idea carries over to velocity: acceleration is the slope of the $(t,v)$ graph.

  • The number $12\,\text{m}$ is the change in position $\Delta x$, not a velocity. You get the velocity by dividing the change in position by the time it took, $4.0\,\text{s}$.

  • You divided the final position by the time: $\dfrac{14\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$. The cart does not start from the origin but from the position $2.0\,\text{m}$, so the velocity is calculated from the change in position $\Delta x = 14\,\text{m} - 2.0\,\text{m}$.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    Acceleration tells you how the velocity changes

    In the chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs the velocity stayed constant. In most motions, however, it changes: a car starts from a traffic light, a cyclist brakes and a dropped stone falls faster and faster. Acceleration $a$ tells you how much the velocity changes per unit of time. Its unit is $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}} = \text{m/s}^2$. An acceleration of $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ means that the velocity grows by $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ every second.

    Acceleration is a vector quantity $\overline{a}$, like velocity $\overline{v}$, and in rectilinear motion its direction is given by its sign. When the acceleration and the velocity point in the same direction, the speed increases. When they point in opposite directions, the speed decreases. So a negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down: if an object moves in the negative direction and $a < 0$, its speed increases. Acceleration can exist even when the velocity is zero for a moment.

    Uniformly accelerated motion and the $(t,v)$ graph

    Motion is uniformly accelerated when the acceleration stays constant: the velocity changes by the same amount in every time interval of equal length. Then the $(t,v)$ graph, with time on the horizontal axis and velocity on the vertical axis, is a straight line. The slope of the line is the acceleration. A rising line means a positive acceleration, a horizontal line uniform motion and a falling line a negative acceleration.

    Instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent

    When the velocity grows, the position changes more in each second than in the one before. That is why the $(t,x)$ graph is not a straight line but a curve that gets steeper. The velocity at a given moment, the instantaneous velocity, is found from the tangent of the curve: the line that just touches the curve at that point. Two points far apart are chosen on the tangent, and the instantaneous velocity is the change in position between them divided by the change in time. A ratio calculated through two points of the curve would give only the average velocity between them.

    Free fall

    When air resistance is small, all objects fall with the same acceleration regardless of mass. This acceleration of free fall is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ at the Earth's surface, directed downwards. The velocity of a dropped stone therefore grows by about $9.8\,\text{m/s}$ every second. That is why the stone travels a longer distance in each second than in the one before: the distances that an object starting from rest covers in successive intervals of equal length are in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5 : 7$.

    Equations of uniformly accelerated motion

    Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it took:

    $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$

    where $a$ is the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$), $\Delta v = v_2 - v_1$ the change in velocity ($\text{m/s}$) and $\Delta t$ the time it took ($\text{s}$). When the acceleration is constant and timing starts at $t = 0$, when the velocity is $v_0$, the velocity changes by the amount $a$ every second:

    $$v = v_0 + a\,t$$

    where $v$ is the velocity at time $t$ ($\text{m/s}$), $v_0$ the initial velocity ($\text{m/s}$), $a$ the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$) and $t$ the time ($\text{s}$).

    The displacement is the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis

    In uniform motion the displacement $v\,t$ is the area of the rectangle under the $(t,v)$ graph. The same holds when the velocity changes: the displacement is the area between the graph and the time axis, where the part below the time axis ($v < 0$) is counted as negative. In uniformly accelerated motion the region divides into a rectangle whose area is $v_0\,t$ and a triangle whose height is the growth of velocity $a\,t$ and whose width is $t$. The area of the triangle is $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot a\,t \cdot t = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$.

    When the object is at the position $x_0$ at time $t = 0$, its position is the initial position plus the displacement:

    $$x = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$$

    where $x$ is the position at time $t$ ($\text{m}$) and $x_0$ the initial position ($\text{m}$); the other symbols are the same as above. When the time is asked for, this becomes a quadratic equation, and of its solutions you choose the one that fits the situation.

    Determining the acceleration of free fall from measurements

    The position of a falling object is measured at many moments, for example from a video or with light gates. A quadratic polynomial $s = A\,t^2 + B\,t + C$ is fitted to the measured points with a calculator or a spreadsheet. In real series of measurements the points do not usually lie exactly on one curve because of measurement uncertainty, so the fit uses all the points at once. When the polynomial is compared with the equation of position, the coefficients of $t^2$ are the same, $A = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a$:

    $$a = 2A$$

    where $A$ is the coefficient of the $t^2$ term of the fitted polynomial ($\text{m/s}^2$). The coefficient $B$ is the initial velocity and $C$ the initial position.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the acceleration is zero whenever the velocity is zero. A ball thrown up is at rest for a moment at the highest point, but its velocity changes all the time: just before the highest point it moves up and just after it moves down. When air resistance is small, the acceleration is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards during the whole flight. Another mistake is to take a negative acceleration to always mean slowing down: the speed decreases only when the acceleration and the velocity point in opposite directions. A third mistake is to think that a heavy object falls faster than a light one. When air resistance is small, both fall with the same acceleration.

    Summary

    • Acceleration $a = \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ tells you the change in velocity per unit of time, with the unit $\text{m/s}^2$. It is a vector quantity, and on a straight line its sign tells the direction.
    • The speed increases when the acceleration and the velocity point in the same direction, and decreases when they point in opposite directions.
    • In uniformly accelerated motion the $(t,v)$ graph is a straight line whose slope is the acceleration and whose signed area between it and the time axis is the displacement; the part below the time axis ($v < 0$) is counted as negative.
    • $v = v_0 + a\,t$ and $x = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. The $(t,x)$ graph is a curve, and the instantaneous velocity is the slope of its tangent.
    • In free fall the acceleration is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards. It can be determined by fitting a quadratic polynomial to the measured points: $a = 2A$.

      Example 1: A cyclist going downhill

      A cyclist reaches the top of a hill at $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ and stops pedalling. On the downhill her acceleration is $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the hill is $60\,\text{m}$ long. How long does the descent take, and what is her velocity at the bottom?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The initial velocity is $v_0 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$, the acceleration $a = 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the displacement $\Delta x = x - x_0 = 60\,\text{m}$ in the direction of motion, which is chosen as positive. We are asked for the time $t$ and the velocity $v$ at the bottom.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The acceleration is constant, so the motion is uniformly accelerated and the displacement is $\Delta x = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. The time is the unknown, and it appears in both the first and the second power, so we get a quadratic equation. Once the time is known, the velocity comes from the equation $v = v_0 + a\,t$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. Write the equation in the form $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 + v_0\,t - \Delta x = 0$ and solve it with the quadratic formula. The positive solution is

      $$t = \frac{-v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x}}{a}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. All quantities are in SI units (m, m/s, m/s²), so no conversions are needed. Under the root the unit is $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so the root is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time comes out in seconds.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the expression under the root and its square root, then the time and the velocity:

      $$\begin{aligned} &v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x \\ &= (4.0\,\text{m/s})^2 \\ &\quad + 2 \cdot 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 60\,\text{m} \\ &= 76\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \\[4pt] &\sqrt{76\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} \approx 8.72\,\text{m/s} \\[4pt] &t = \frac{-4.0\,\text{m/s} + 8.72\,\text{m/s}}{0.50\,\text{m/s}^2} \\ &\approx 9.44\,\text{s} \\[4pt] &v = v_0 + a\,t \\ &= 4.0\,\text{m/s} + 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 9.44\,\text{s} \\ &\approx 8.72\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The descent takes about $9.4\,\text{s}$, and the velocity at the bottom is about $8.7\,\text{m/s}$. Two significant figures, like the initial velocity $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the acceleration $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$. The distance $60\,\text{m}$ is taken as an exact value. The other solution of the formula, $t \approx -25\,\text{s}$, is rejected because the motion starts at $t = 0$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The velocity grows steadily, so the average velocity is the mean of the initial and final velocities, $(4.0\,\text{m/s} + 8.72\,\text{m/s}) : 2 \approx 6.36\,\text{m/s}$, and $6.36\,\text{m/s} \cdot 9.44\,\text{s} \approx 60\,\text{m}$, the length of the hill. The final velocity $8.7\,\text{m/s} \approx 31\,\text{km/h}$ is a usual cycling speed on a downhill.

      Example 2: Find the error

      A car starts from rest and its acceleration is $3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. A student calculates how far the car travels in $4.0\,\text{s}$. First she calculates the final velocity $v = a\,t = 12\,\text{m/s}$ and then the distance $\Delta x = v\,t = 12\,\text{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s} = 48\,\text{m}$.Where is the error?

      • Correct. The velocity grows from zero to $12\,\text{m/s}$, so the average velocity is only $6.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the distance is $6.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s} = 24\,\text{m}$, the same as $\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. In the $(t,v)$ graph the distance is the area of a triangle, not of a rectangle.

      • The formula is missing the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$: in the $(t,v)$ graph the distance is the area of a triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 12\,\text{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\text{s} = 24\,\text{m}$, not $48\,\text{m}$.

      • Acceleration tells you how much the velocity grows per second, so in four seconds the velocity grows by $4 \cdot 3.0\,\text{m/s} = 12\,\text{m/s}$. The final velocity is correct; the error is in the calculation of the distance.

      • Distance is velocity times time only when the velocity stays constant. Here the velocity grows from zero to $12\,\text{m/s}$, so only the average velocity is a suitable factor.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      A passenger plane starts from rest at the beginning of the runway and accelerates uniformly. It needs a speed of $75\,\text{m/s}$ to take off, and $1500\,\text{m}$ of runway is available.

      What is the least acceleration the plane must have? Answer in m/s².

      m/s²
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      There are two unknowns, the acceleration and the time. Write separate equations for the velocity and the displacement.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      For a start from rest, $v = a\,t$ and $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. Solve $t$ from the first and substitute it into the second.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $\Delta x$$= \tfrac{1}{2}\,a \cdot \left(\tfrac{v}{a}\right)^2$
      $= \tfrac{v^2}{2a}$

      so $a = \tfrac{v^2}{2\,\Delta x}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      initial velocity $v_0 = 0$, take-off speed $v = 75\,\text{m/s}$, runway length $\Delta x = 1500\,\text{m}$; the acceleration is constant

      Find

      the least acceleration $a$, in $\text{m/s}^2$

      Principle

      The plane accelerates uniformly from rest, so $v = a\,t$ and $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$. The least acceleration is the one with which the speed $75\,\text{m/s}$ is reached just at the end of the runway. The time is not known, so it is eliminated from the equations.

      Formula

      From the first equation $t = \tfrac{v}{a}$. Substituting into the second:

      $\Delta x$$= \tfrac{1}{2}\,a \left(\tfrac{v}{a}\right)^2$
      $= \tfrac{v^2}{2a}$

      so $a = \dfrac{v^2}{2\,\Delta x}$

      Conversions

      The speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the distance in metres, so $\tfrac{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{\text{m}} = \text{m/s}^2$. No conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $a$$= \dfrac{(75\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 1500\,\text{m}}$
      $= \dfrac{5625\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{3000\,\text{m}}$
      $\approx 1.875\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Answer

      $1.9\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Two significant figures like the speed $75\,\text{m/s}$.

      Reasonableness

      With the acceleration $1.875\,\text{m/s}^2$ the speed $75\,\text{m/s}$ is reached in the time

      $t$$= 75\,\text{m/s} : 1.875\,\text{m/s}^2$
      $= 40\,\text{s}$

      The average velocity is $37.5\,\text{m/s}$, and $37.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 40\,\text{s} = 1500\,\text{m}$, exactly the length of the runway. A take-off run of about $40\,\text{s}$ and an acceleration of about $0.2\,g$ match what passengers of an airliner experience.

        A car starts from rest at a traffic light and reaches a speed of $25\,\text{m/s}$ in $8.0\,\text{s}$. The acceleration is uniform. What is the car's acceleration?

        Given

        Find

        the car's acceleration $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

Reaching $25\,\text{m/s} = 90\,\text{km/h}$ in eight seconds is a brisk acceleration for an ordinary passenger car. The acceleration is positive because the velocity grows.

        $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 27.8\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$
        $\approx 111\,\text{m}$

        The calculated distance is greater, because the car reaches a high speed early. The result is below the $222\,\text{m}$ that would be travelled at the final velocity $27.8\,\text{m/s}$ the whole time, as it should be, and an acceleration of $5\,\text{m/s}^2$ is about half of $g$, which is a usual initial acceleration for a car.

        A cart is pushed up an inclined track, and a motion sensor measures its velocity $v$ as a function of time. Up the track is the positive direction, and friction is small. The data is an example data set.

        a) Plot the $(t,v)$ graph and fit a straight line to the points. Determine the cart's acceleration and initial velocity and justify that the acceleration is constant.

        b) How far from the starting point does the cart get upwards before turning? Give the distance in the answer box in metres.

        0.02.41

        Answer

        $3.6\,\text{m}$

        Show the solution

        Given

        the $(t,v)$ points of the table in the range $0 \ldots 4.0\,\text{s}$, up the track is the positive direction; friction is small

        Find

        a) the acceleration $a$, the initial velocity $v_0$ and the justification for constant acceleration; b) the greatest distance $s$ upwards from the starting point in metres; c) the shapes of the $(t,a)$ and $(t,x)$ graphs

        Principle

        If the $(t,v)$ points are on a line, the acceleration is constant and the motion is uniformly accelerated: $v = v_0 + a\,t$, where the slope is $a$ and the intercept on the vertical axis is $v_0$. The cart turns when $v = 0$. After that it comes back down, because the acceleration still points down the track. The greatest distance is obtained from the equation of position or from the area of the triangle under the $(t,v)$ graph up to the moment $v = 0$.

        Reading the graph

        The points are approximately on a line, and the velocity decreases by about $0.8\,\text{m/s}$ for every second. The fit gives $v \approx -0.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot t + 2.42\,\text{m/s}$, so $a \approx -0.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $v_0 \approx 2.42\,\text{m/s}$. The position of the line among the points shows constant acceleration: the points are on both sides of the line without curvature, and the velocity decreases by about the same amount in every $0.5\,\text{s}$ interval ($0.37 \ldots 0.44\,\text{m/s}$).

        Formula

        The turning moment from the condition $v = v_0 + a\,t_k = 0$ is $t_k = -\dfrac{v_0}{a}$, and the distance is $s = v_0\,t_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t_k^2$, which simplifies to $s = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2\,|a|}$

        Conversions

        All quantities are in SI units. No conversions are needed.

        Substitution

        $t_k$$= \dfrac{2.42\,\text{m/s}}{0.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
        $\approx 2.99\,\text{s}$

        The distance is

        $s$$= \dfrac{(2.42\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 0.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
        $= \dfrac{5.86\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{1.62\,\text{m/s}^2}$
        $\approx 3.61\,\text{m}$

        c) The graphs are not drawn in the answer box but described in words below.

        Answer

        The cart gets about $3.6\,\text{m}$ upwards from the starting point, two significant figures, because the acceleration and the initial velocity have been fitted from scattered data. c) The $(t,a)$ graph is a horizontal line at $a \approx -0.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ for the whole interval, because the acceleration is constant and points down the track also at the turning moment. The $(t,x)$ graph is a downward-opening parabola that starts from the origin, rises steeply at first and flattens, has its peak at $x \approx 3.6\,\text{m}$ at $t \approx 3.0\,\text{s}$ (the tangent is horizontal, because $v = 0$) and falls after that; the cart returns to the starting point at $2\,t_k \approx 6.0\,\text{s}$, so in the range $0 \ldots 5.0\,\text{s}$ the graph is still positive.

        Reasonableness

        The average velocity of the ascent is $2.42\,\text{m/s} : 2 = 1.21\,\text{m/s}$, and $1.21\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.99\,\text{s} \approx 3.6\,\text{m}$. The last velocity of the data, $-0.83\,\text{m/s}$, is close to the value of the fit, $2.42 - 0.81 \cdot 4.0 \approx -0.82\,\text{m/s}$, and the data includes the turning moment $t \approx 3.0\,\text{s}$ ($v \approx 0$). The acceleration $0.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is plausible for a gentle slope: it corresponds to an angle of inclination where $g \sin\alpha \approx 0.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, that is, $\alpha \approx 5\,{}^\circ$.

      1. A cart's velocity is $-3.0\,\text{m/s}$ and its acceleration is $+1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. Does the cart's speed increase or decrease? Justify your answer.
        Answer

        The speed decreases. The velocity and the acceleration point in opposite directions, so the velocity changes by $+1.0\,\text{m/s}$ every second towards zero: $-3.0\,\text{m/s}$, $-2.0\,\text{m/s}$, $-1.0\,\text{m/s}$. A positive acceleration does not always mean speeding up.

      2. A heavy steel ball and a light wooden ball are dropped at the same time from the same height. Air resistance is small. Which hits the ground first?
        Answer

        They hit the ground at the same time. When air resistance is small, all objects fall with the same acceleration $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ regardless of mass, so their velocities and positions are the same at every moment.

      3. A cart's $(t,x)$ graph is a curve bending upwards. How do you read the cart's velocity at $t = 2.0\,\text{s}$ from the graph?
        Answer

        Draw a tangent to the curve at $t = 2.0\,\text{s}$, that is, a line that touches the curve at that point. Choose two points far apart on the tangent, and the instantaneous velocity is their change in position divided by the change in time, $v = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$.

      3. Calculation task: Reading a graph

      The graph shows the velocity of an electric scooter as a function of time.

      How long a distance does the scooter travel from time $0$ to $8.0\,\text{s}$? Answer in metres.

      m
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which part of a $(t,v)$ graph tells you the displacement?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The displacement is the area between the line and the time axis from $0$ to $8.0\,\text{s}$. The region is a trapezoid: a rectangle with a triangle on top of it.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The rectangle $2.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$ and the triangle $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      from the graph, the velocity $v_0 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$ at time $0$ and $v = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$ at $t = 8.0\,\text{s}$; the graph is a straight line

      Find

      the distance travelled $\Delta x$, in metres

      Principle

      The area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis is the displacement. The velocity is positive the whole time, so the displacement equals the distance travelled. A straight graph means uniform acceleration, so the region is a trapezoid.

      Reading the graph

      The line starts at the point $(0$; $2.0\,\text{m/s})$ and ends at the point $(8.0\,\text{s}$; $6.0\,\text{m/s})$. Both points are at crossings of grid lines, so the values are read exactly.

      Formula

      The area of the trapezoid is the average velocity times the time: $\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2} \cdot t$

      Conversions

      The velocities are in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so the result comes out in metres. No conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $\Delta x$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s} + 6.0\,\text{m/s}}{2} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 32\,\text{m}$

      Answer

      $32\,\text{m}$

      Two significant figures like the values read from the graph.

      Reasonableness

      The same area in two pieces: the rectangle $2.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s} = 16\,\text{m}$ and the triangle $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s} = 16\,\text{m}$, together $32\,\text{m}$. The result is greater than the $16\,\text{m}$ travelled at the lowest velocity and smaller than the $48\,\text{m}$ travelled at the highest velocity.

      4. Explanation task

      Explain the phenomenon at the beginning: why does a stone dropped from a bridge travel a longer distance during each second than during the second before? Use the concepts velocity, acceleration and average velocity.

      Model answer

      When air resistance is small, the stone falls with constant acceleration $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ downwards. Its velocity grows by about $9.8\,\text{m/s}$ every second: during the first second from zero to $9.8\,\text{m/s}$ and during the second from $9.8\,\text{m/s}$ to $19.6\,\text{m/s}$. Because the velocity grows steadily, the average velocity is the mean of the initial and final velocities: $4.9\,\text{m/s}$ for the first second and $14.7\,\text{m/s}$ for the second. The distance is the average velocity times the time, so the stone falls $4.9\,\text{m}$ during the first second and $14.7\,\text{m}$ during the second, three times as much. In the $(t,v)$ graph the distances are the areas under the line, which grow in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5 : 7$.
