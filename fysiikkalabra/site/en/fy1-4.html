Graphing and fitting a straight line

Why do data points not usually all land exactly on one straight line, and yet a single line fitted to them can reveal the relationship between the quantities?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • present measurements as a graph and fit a straight line to the points.
  • find the slope and constant term of a line and say what they mean.
  • recognise when a linear model does not fit the measurements.

Prior knowledge

  • Every measurement contains random error, which scatters the results in both directionschapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement
  • A quantity is given as a value and a unit, and the quantity and its unit are written on the axes of a graphchapter Quantities, units and the SI system

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

The position of a trolley is measured on a track every second, and the results are plotted on a graph. The points lie roughly along a straight line, but no line passes through all of them. Two students draw different lines, and each claims that their line shows the speed of the trolley.

How do you decide which line describes the measurements best, and what does the slope of the line tell you?

Optional Recap: measurement errors
The position of a trolley is measured many times at the same spot with the same tape measure. The results vary slightly, sometimes in one direction and sometimes in the other.Which error causes the variation?

  • A gross error would be a single clearly wrong result. The variation is part of every measurement, even if nobody makes a mistake (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

  • A systematic error would shift all the results in the same direction (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement). Here the results vary in both directions, which is the mark of random error.

  • Correct. Random error scatters the results in both directions around the mean (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement). For the same reason the data points do not usually all land exactly on a straight line, and that is why a line is fitted to them instead of joining them with a broken line.

With the checkbox you can draw the best line given by the calculation as a dashed line and compare it with your own.

Assumptions of the model

The data sets are example data, not real measurements, and their values are values from the model. The model assumes that the $x$ values are exact and that the random error is only in the $y$ values, and that the relationship between the quantities is a straight line $y = kx + b$. In data set 3 the relationship is curved, so a straight line describes it only roughly.

How the calculation works

The deviation of each point from the line is $e_i = y_i - (kx_i + b)$. The mean deviation is the square root of the mean of the squares:

$$d = \sqrt{\frac{e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \dots + e_N^2}{N}}$$

It is small when the line is close to all the points. The calculated best line is the one for which $d$ is as small as possible, that is, the least-squares line:

$$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} \\ b &= \bar{y} - k\bar{x} \end{aligned}$$

where $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the means, $S_{xy}$ is the sum of the products $(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ and $S_{xx}$ the sum of the squares $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$. You do not need to know this calculation: in upper secondary school a line is fitted by eye or with a calculator.

Adjust the line so that it runs through the middle of the set of points, and watch the mean deviation $d$.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    A graph brings the measurements together

    When you measure two quantities, for example the distance $s$ travelled by a trolley at different times $t$, each measurement is one point on the graph. The horizontal coordinate of the point is the value of one quantity and the vertical coordinate is the value of the other. The horizontal axis usually gets the quantity that the experimenter chooses or that increases steadily, such as time, and the vertical axis gets the quantity being measured.

    A graph needs axes with the quantity and its unit written at the end, such as $s\ (\text{m})$, an evenly spaced scale and tick marks that are close enough together. Choose the scale so that the points fill most of the graph.

    Fit a line, do not join the points

    In real data sets the data points do not usually lie exactly on one straight line, because of measurement uncertainty, especially random error (chapter Measuring and the reliability of a measurement). So you do not join the points into a broken line, since that line would follow the errors too. Instead you fit one straight line that runs through the middle of the set of points.

    A good fit is close to all the points and does not lean towards any single point. There are roughly as many points on each side of the line, and the deviations above and below roughly cancel each other. The line does not have to pass through any point. If one point is clearly further from the line than the others, it is worth finding out why, and you do not drop the point automatically.

    Linear model

    If the points lie roughly along a straight line, the relationship between the quantities is described by a linear model, whose equation is the equation of a straight line. The slope tells you how much the vertical quantity changes when the horizontal quantity increases by one unit, and the constant term tells you the value of the vertical quantity when the horizontal quantity is zero. A linear model is a physical model, a simplified description of a phenomenon based on measurements: the graph is its graphical model and the equation of the line its mathematical model, and because the equation gives numerical values, the model is quantitative.

    When the constant term is zero, the line passes through the origin and the quantities are directly proportional: doubling $x$ doubles $y$. The measurements support the model only in the range that was measured, so estimating a value between the measured points (interpolation) is justified. If the line is extended beyond the measured range (extrapolation), you assume that the relationship stays the same, and the result is only an estimate. If the points curve systematically around the line, for example the points at the ends are above the line and the points in the middle below it, the linear model does not fit.

    Equation of a straight line

    The graph of a linear model is a straight line with the equation

    $$y = kx + b$$

    where $y$ is the quantity on the vertical axis, $x$ the quantity on the horizontal axis, $k$ the slope and $b$ the constant term, that is, the point where the line crosses the vertical axis. The slope is calculated from two chosen points $(x_1,\ y_1)$ and $(x_2,\ y_2)$ on the line:

    $$k = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

    where $\Delta y$ is the change in $y$ between the points and $\Delta x$ the change in $x$ between them. The unit of the slope is the unit of $y$ divided by the unit of $x$: if $y$ is in metres and $x$ in seconds, $k$ is in $\text{m/s}$.

    Two things affect the result. Read the points from the fitted line and not from the data points, and choose them far apart, so that the reading error has only a small effect on the slope. Read the constant term $b$ from the point where the line crosses the vertical axis, or calculate it from one point on the line: $b = y_1 - kx_1$. If you want $x$ when $y$ is known, solve the equation: $x = \tfrac{y - b}{k}$.

    The sign of the slope tells you the direction. With a positive slope the line rises, and with a negative slope it falls. The slope usually has a physical meaning: in a distance–time graph it is the speed, and in a mass–volume graph it is the density.

    Common misconception

    Many students join the data points with a line, or force the line to pass through one of the data points, even though that point, too, has measurement uncertainty. The line is fitted to the whole set of points; it may pass through some point, but it does not have to.

    Another mistake is to calculate the slope from two data points. Then the error in each point affects the result, and if the points are also close together, the result is uncertain. Read the points from the fitted line and far apart.

    A third mistake is to assume that the line always passes through the origin. If the constant term is not zero, the quantities are not directly proportional, and $b$ tells you what $y$ is when $x$ is zero.

    Summary

    • The axes of a graph show the quantities with their units and an evenly spaced scale. The data points are not joined to each other.
    • A straight line is fitted to the data points so that it runs through the middle of the set: there are roughly as many points on each side of it.
    • The equation of the linear model is $y = kx + b$. The slope is $k = \tfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$, and it is calculated from two points on the line that are far apart.
    • The constant term $b$ is the point where the line crosses the vertical axis. If $b = 0$, the quantities are directly proportional.
    • If the points curve systematically, the linear model does not fit.

      Example 1: Fitting a line to kettle data

      The temperature $T$ of the water in a kettle was measured every minute:

      $t\ (\text{min})$ $T\ (^\circ\text{C})$
      0 20.8
      1 27.9
      2 36.5
      3 43.8
      4 52.4
      5 59.6

      Fit a straight line to the data points, find its equation $T = kt + b$ and use it to work out when the water temperature is $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The power of the kettle is constant, and the heat losses are small.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The graph has six data points, and the fitted line has been drawn through the middle of the set. Two points far apart are read from the line: $A = (t_1$; $T_1) = (0\,\text{min}$; $20\,{}^\circ\text{C})$ and $B = (t_2$; $T_2) = (5.0\,\text{min}$; $60\,{}^\circ\text{C})$. We need the slope $k$, the constant term $b$ and the time $t$ at which $T = 40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The power of the kettle is constant and the heat losses are small in this temperature range, so the water temperature rises almost steadily and the linear model $T = kt + b$ fits. The line is drawn so that there are points on both sides of it and each point is close to the line. The slope is calculated from the points $A$ and $B$ on the line, not from the data points.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The slope comes from the differences between the points, the constant term from the point $A$, and the time is solved from the equation of the line:

      $$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{T_2 - T_1}{t_2 - t_1} \\ b &= T_1 - k t_1 \\ t &= \frac{T - b}{k} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. None needed: the temperatures are in degrees Celsius and the times in minutes. The unit of the slope is ${}^\circ\text{C/min}$, and a temperature change is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} k &= \frac{T_2 - T_1}{t_2 - t_1} \\ &= \frac{60\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{5.0\,\text{min} - 0\,\text{min}} \\ &= 8.0\,\tfrac{{}^\circ\text{C}}{\text{min}} \\[6pt] b &= T_1 - k t_1 \\ &= 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 8.0\,\tfrac{{}^\circ\text{C}}{\text{min}} \cdot 0\,\text{min} \\ &= 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} \\[6pt] t &= \frac{T - b}{k} \\ &= \frac{40\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}}{8.0\,\tfrac{{}^\circ\text{C}}{\text{min}}} \\ &= 2.5\,\text{min} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The equation of the line is

      $T$$= 8.0\,\tfrac{{}^\circ\text{C}}{\text{min}} \cdot t + 20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$

      and the temperature $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is reached after about 2.5 minutes. The results are given to two significant figures, because $k$ is determined from points read to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The constant term $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is the temperature of the water at the start of heating according to the model, and it is about room temperature. The slope $8.0\,\tfrac{{}^\circ\text{C}}{\text{min}}$ tells you how fast the water warms up. In the table, $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is passed between the third and fourth measurements, since $T$ is $36.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at two minutes and $43.8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ at three minutes, so $2.5\,\text{min}$ is in the right interval. The points deviate from the line by at most about one degree.

      Example 2: Find the error

      A student finds the slope of the graph of a trolley from the data points $(1.0\,\text{s}$; $0.6\,\text{m})$ and $(2.0\,\text{s}$; $0.8\,\text{m})$:

      $$k = \frac{0.8\,\text{m} - 0.6\,\text{m}}{2.0\,\text{s} - 1.0\,\text{s}} = 0.2\,\text{m/s}$$

      The slope of the line fitted to the points, however, is about $0.5\,\text{m/s}$. Where is the error?

      • The slope is $\Delta y : \Delta x$, that is, the change in distance divided by the change in time. The inverse ratio would give the unit $\text{s/m}$, which is not a speed.

      • Correct. The points must be read from the fitted line and far apart, for example $(0\,\text{s}$; $0\,\text{m})$ and $(6.0\,\text{s}$; $3.0\,\text{m})$, which gives $k = 0.50\,\text{m/s}$. The difference between two data points close together is affected by the random error of both points, and the difference is not large compared with the error. Calculated between successive data points, the slopes would be $0.2$, $0.8$, $0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.5\,\text{m/s}$. Points far apart on the fitted line give a reliable value.

      • One point gives $0.60\,\text{m/s}$ ($1\,\text{s}$), $0.40\,\text{m/s}$ ($2\,\text{s}$) and $0.53\,\text{m/s}$ ($3\,\text{s}$): each value is off by the error of its own point. In addition, $s : t$ gives the slope only if the line passes through the origin. Two distant points read from the line are a more reliable way.

      • The result $0.2\,\text{m/s}$ differs from the other intervals, so a single interval does not tell you about the whole motion. Successive pairs would give $0.2$, $0.8$, $0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.5\,\text{m/s}$, and these values, scattered by random error, are all unreliable.

      Example 3: Read the slope from a graph

      Simulated example data

      The graph shows the distance $s$ of a cyclist from the start of the route as a function of time $t$. The clock was started only after the cyclist had already ridden part of the route. The line fitted to the data points passes through intersections of the grid, and two of them are marked as the points $P_1$ and $P_2$. The coordinates are not written out: read them from the axes.

      Read the coordinates of the points $P_1$ and $P_2$ from the graph and calculate the slope of the line. Give the answer in $\text{km/h}$.

      km/h
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Where on the axis do you read the coordinate of a point? Follow the grid from the point to the axis.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The slope is $\Delta s : \Delta t$: the vertical change divided by the horizontal change.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      When $P_1$ is at $t = 1.0\,\text{h}$ and $P_2$ at $t = 3.0\,\text{h}$, the time interval is $\Delta t = 2.0\,\text{h}$. Read the corresponding distances from the vertical axis.

      Show the solution

      Given

      the line of the graph with the points $P_1$ and $P_2$ marked; the axes show the distance $s\ (\text{km})$ and the time $t\ (\text{h})$

      Find

      the slope $k$ of the line, that is, the speed of the cyclist in $\text{km/h}$

      Principle

      The slope of the line in a distance–time graph is the speed, and it is $\Delta s : \Delta t$ for any two points on the line. Read two points far apart from the fitted line, so that the reading error has only a small effect on the result.

      Reading the graph

      The point $P_1$ is at a grid intersection where $t_1 = 1.0\,\text{h}$ and $s_1 = 20\,\text{km}$ (the number on the horizontal axis directly below, the number on the vertical axis directly to the left). The point $P_2$ is at the intersection $t_2 = 3.0\,\text{h}$ and $s_2 = 50\,\text{km}$. The slope is calculated from these readings.

      Formula

      $k$$= \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$
      $= \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

      Conversions

      None needed: the distances are in kilometres and the times in hours, and the result is asked for in $\text{km/h}$.

      Substitution

      $k$$= \dfrac{50\,\text{km} - 20\,\text{km}}{3.0\,\text{h} - 1.0\,\text{h}}$
      $= \dfrac{30\,\text{km}}{2.0\,\text{h}}$
      $= 15\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$

      Answer

      $15\,\text{km/h}$

      Two significant figures, because the times were read to two figures ($1.0\,\text{h}$ and $3.0\,\text{h}$).

      Reasonableness

      Check with a third point: when $t = 2.0\,\text{h}$, the line in the graph is at about $35\,\text{km}$, and

      $15\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{h} + 5\,\text{km}$
      $= 35\,\text{km}$

      The speed $15\,\text{km/h}$ is typical of ordinary cycling.

      Optional Example 4: Apply to a new situation

      The height $h$ of a plant was followed as a function of days $t$, and the line fitted to the data points passes through the points $(2.0\,\text{days}$; $5.6\,\text{cm})$ and $(10.0\,\text{days}$; $12.0\,\text{cm})$.

      How many days after the start of the measurements is the plant $20\,\text{cm}$ tall according to the line, when the line is extended beyond the measured range? Give the answer in days.

      days
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the slope of the line and what unit does it use? Calculate it from two points on the line.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Show the solution

      Given

      points on the line $(t_1$; $h_1) = (2.0\,\text{days}$; $5.6\,\text{cm})$ and $(t_2$; $h_2) = (10.0\,\text{days}$; $12.0\,\text{cm})$; height $h = 20\,\text{cm}$

      Find

      the time $t$ at which $h = 20\,\text{cm}$

      Principle

      The growth is steady, so the height follows the line $h = kt + b$. The slope is calculated from two points on the line that are far apart and the constant term from one point. The time is solved from the equation when the height is known. The prediction extends outside the measured range and assumes that the growth continues in the same way.

      Formula

      $k = \dfrac{h_2 - h_1}{t_2 - t_1}$, $b = h_1 - k t_1$ and $h = kt + b$, from which

      $$t = \frac{h - b}{k}$$

      Conversions

      None needed: the heights are in centimetres and the times in days.

      Substitution

      $k$$= \dfrac{12.0\,\text{cm} - 5.6\,\text{cm}}{10.0\,\text{days} - 2.0\,\text{days}}$
      $= 0.80\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{day}}$
      $b$$= 5.6\,\text{cm} - 0.80\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{day}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{days}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{cm}$
      $t$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{cm} - 4.0\,\text{cm}}{0.80\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{day}}}$
      $= 20\,\text{days}$

      $20\,\text{days}$

      Two significant figures, because the slope $0.80\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{day}}$ is known to two figures.

      A description based on numerical values that allows numerical predictions.

        The correct range $(0.475 \pm 0.075)\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$ is $0.40\text{–}0.55\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$, that is, exactly the gentlest and the steepest line. The student's range $(0.475 \pm 0.15)\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$ would be $0.33\text{–}0.63\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$, which extends clearly beyond both lines, so $32\,\%$ is double. The slope of the best line fitted to all six points, about $0.51\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$, is inside the range.

      h

      The equation of the line is $s = kt + b$. First calculate the slope $k$ from two points and then the constant term $b = s_1 - kt_1$.

      $k$$= \dfrac{19.0 - 7.0}{5.0 - 1.0}\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$

      Show the solution

      Given

      points on the line $(t_1$; $s_1) = (1.0\,\text{h}$; $7.0\,\text{km})$ and $(t_2$; $s_2) = (5.0\,\text{h}$; $19.0\,\text{km})$; distance $s = 25\,\text{km}$

      Find

      the time $t$ at which $s = 25\,\text{km}$

      Principle

      The distance increases steadily, so the distance follows the line $s = kt + b$. The slope is calculated from two points on the line and the constant term from one, and the time is solved from the equation of the line.

      Formula

      $k = \dfrac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}$, $b = s_1 - kt_1$ and $s = kt + b$, from which

      $$t = \frac{s - b}{k}$$

      Conversions

      None needed: the distances are in kilometres and the times in hours.

      Substitution

      $k$$= \dfrac{19.0\,\text{km} - 7.0\,\text{km}}{5.0\,\text{h} - 1.0\,\text{h}}$
      $= 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$
      $b$$= 7.0\,\text{km} - 3.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \cdot 1.0\,\text{h}$
      $= 4.0\,\text{km}$
      $t$$= \dfrac{25\,\text{km} - 4.0\,\text{km}}{3.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}}$
      $= 7.0\,\text{h}$

      $7.0\,\text{h}$

      Two significant figures like the slope $3.0\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}$.

      Check:

      Optional 5. Explanation task

      Explain why the data points are not joined into a broken line, and why the slope of the line is calculated from two points on the line that are far apart and not from two data points.

      Model answer

      Every measurement contains a random error, so the points scatter around the model. A broken line would join these errors too and give a sharply varying, unconvincing relationship. The fitted line runs through the middle of the set of points and shows the general relationship in the phenomenon. The slope $k = \Delta y : \Delta x$ is calculated from two points on the line, because on the line the errors have already been averaged out. The points are chosen far apart, because then $\Delta y$ is large compared with the reading error and the relative error of the slope gets smaller. For example, making $\Delta x$ ten times as large makes the relative error one tenth.
