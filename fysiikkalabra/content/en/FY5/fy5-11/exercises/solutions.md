## fy5-11-L1-01
**Given:** a straight wall, angle of incidence $\alpha = 35^\circ$
**Find:** the angle of reflection $\beta$ in degrees
**Principle:** The wall is straight and reflects the wave, so the law of reflection holds: the angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence when both are measured from the normal of the wall. The angle of incidence is given already measured from the normal.
**Formula:** $\beta = \alpha$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the angle is in degrees.
**Substitution:** $\beta = 35^\circ$
**Answer:** $35^\circ$, in whole degrees like the angle of incidence $35^\circ$.
**Reasonableness:** The reflected ray is on the other side of the normal at the same angle, and the angle is between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, as an angle measured from the normal should be.

## fy5-11-L1-02
**Given:** the angle between the incoming ray and the surface of the wall $\gamma = 20^\circ$
**Find:** the angle of incidence $\alpha$ in degrees
**Principle:** The angle of incidence is measured from the normal of the surface, not from the surface. The normal is perpendicular to the surface, so the angle between the ray and the surface and the angle between the ray and the normal add up to $90^\circ$.
**Formula:** $\alpha + \gamma = 90^\circ$, so $\alpha = 90^\circ - \gamma$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the angles are in degrees.
**Substitution:** $\alpha = 90^\circ - 20^\circ = 70^\circ$
**Answer:** $70^\circ$, in whole degrees like the given angle $20^\circ$.
**Reasonableness:** The ray arrives at a shallow angle, almost along the wall, so its angle from the normal is large. As the angle measured from the surface gets small, the angle of incidence approaches $90^\circ$, as it should.

## fy5-11-L1-03
**Given:** frequency $f = 8.0\,\text{Hz}$, speed of the wave $v = 0.24\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the wavelength $\lambda$ in centimetres
**Principle:** In the time of one period a wave travels a distance of one wavelength, so the speed, frequency and wavelength are related by the basic equation of wave motion (chapter *Mechanical wave motion*).
**Formula:** $v = f\lambda$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$
**Conversions:** The result comes out in metres, and finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $\lambda = \dfrac{0.24\,\text{m/s}}{8.0\,\text{Hz}} = 0.030\,\text{m} = 0.030 \cdot 100\,\text{cm} = 3.0\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the speed and the frequency.
**Reasonableness:** The crests of waves in a ripple tank are a few centimetres apart. Check: $8.0\,\text{Hz} \cdot 0.030\,\text{m} = 0.24\,\text{m/s}$, and the unit $\tfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{1/s}} = \text{m}$.

## fy5-11-L1-04
**Find:** the direction of the displacement of the reflected pulse and the reason for it
**Principle:** A ring that slides without friction along a rod is the free end of the rope: nothing prevents it from moving vertically, and the rod cannot pull the rope up or down.
**Model answer:** The displacement of the reflected pulse points upwards, so the pulse returns not inverted. When the upward pulse arrives at the ring, the ring rises with the pulse and swings for a moment higher than usual. Nothing pulls it down, so the rope returns to its equilibrium position in the same way as it rose, and the pulse starts back with the same sign. If the end were tied to a wall, the wall would pull the rope down and the pulse would be inverted.

## fy5-11-L1-05
**Given:** delay of the echo $t = 1.4\,\text{s}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the distance $d$ to the cliff wall in metres
**Principle:** Sound travels at a steady speed to the wall, is reflected from it and returns. So during the delay of the echo the sound travels a distance of $2d$.
**Formula:** $2d = v t$, so $d = \dfrac{v t}{2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speed is in m/s and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $d = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.4\,\text{s}}{2} = \dfrac{480.2\,\text{m}}{2} = 240.1\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $240\,\text{m}$ or $2.4 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the delay $1.4\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** Sound travels about a third of a kilometre per second, so in $1.4\,\text{s}$ about $0.48\,\text{km}$; half of that is about $0.24\,\text{km}$. Across a lake to the opposite shore that is a believable distance.

## fy5-11-L1-06
**Find:** how an upward pulse returns from the fixed end of the rope
**Principle:** An end tied to a wall cannot move. When the pulse arrives at the end, the rope tries to lift it up, and the wall responds by pulling the rope downwards. This force of the wall creates the reflected pulse.
**Model answer:** The pulse returns downwards, that is, inverted. The wall pulls the end of the rope down, so the reflected pulse starts back in the opposite direction to the incoming one. The speed of the pulse does not change, because it is determined by the rope, and the pulse is reflected almost completely, because a rigid fixing does not damp it. The claim that the pulse returns upwards would describe a free end, and the claim that the pulse disappears is not true.

## fy5-11-L1-07
**Find:** how doubling the frequency changes the bending of the wave behind the gap
**Principle:** The speed of the wave stays the same, so by the equation $v = f\lambda$ the wavelength decreases when the frequency increases. Diffraction is the stronger, the narrower the gap is compared with the wavelength.
**Model answer:** The new wavelength is half of the old one, that is, $2.0\,\text{cm}$. The width of the gap compared with the wavelength grows from the ratio $\tfrac{3.0\,\text{cm}}{4.0\,\text{cm}} = 0.75$ to the ratio $\tfrac{3.0\,\text{cm}}{2.0\,\text{cm}} = 1.5$. The gap is thus relatively wider, and the bending weakens, although it is still clear, because the gap is still of the order of the wavelength. The strength of the bending is not affected by the energy of the wave, and the width of the gap alone does not decide it, but how wide the gap is compared with the wavelength. The wave passes through the gap at both frequencies.

## fy5-11-L1-08
**Given:** depth of the bottom $d = 36\,\text{m}$, speed of sound in water $v = 1500\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the delay of the echo $t$ in milliseconds
**Principle:** The pulse travels at a steady speed to the bottom, is reflected and returns. During the delay it thus travels a distance of $2d$.
**Formula:** $2d = v t$, so $t = \dfrac{2d}{v}$
**Conversions:** The result comes out in seconds, and finally $1\,\text{s} = 1000\,\text{ms}$.
**Substitution:** $t = \dfrac{2 \cdot 36\,\text{m}}{1500\,\text{m/s}} = \dfrac{72\,\text{m}}{1500\,\text{m/s}} = 0.048\,\text{s} = 0.048 \cdot 1000\,\text{ms} = 48\,\text{ms}$
**Answer:** $48\,\text{ms}$, two significant figures like the depth $36\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check: $1500\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.048\,\text{s} = 72\,\text{m} = 2 \cdot 36\,\text{m}$. In air the same distance would take $\tfrac{72\,\text{m}}{343\,\text{m/s}} \approx 0.21\,\text{s}$, so in water the echo returns $4.4$ times faster, because sound travels $4.4$ times faster in water than in air.

## fy5-11-L2-01
**Given:** angle of incidence $\alpha = 30^\circ$, distance between points $A$ and $B$ on the wall $d = 0.60\,\text{m}$, speed of the wave $v = 0.40\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the time $\Delta t$ that the front needs to go from $A$ to $B$
**Principle:** When the front touches point $A$, the point of the front that hits $B$ is still a distance $s$ from the wall along the ray. The front is perpendicular to the ray, so the distance $s$ is a leg of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $d$ and whose opposite angle is $\alpha$, as in the Huygens construction.
**Formula:** $s = d \sin\alpha$ and $s = v\,\Delta t$, so $\Delta t = \dfrac{d \sin\alpha}{v}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the distance is in metres and the speed in m/s.
**Substitution:** $\Delta t = \dfrac{0.60\,\text{m} \cdot \sin 30^\circ}{0.40\,\text{m/s}} = \dfrac{0.30\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{m/s}} = 0.75\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $0.75\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the distance $0.60\,\text{m}$ and the speed $0.40\,\text{m/s}$.
**Reasonableness:** At perpendicular incidence ($\alpha = 0^\circ$) the front would touch the whole wall at the same time, $\Delta t = 0$. If the wave travelled along the wall, $\Delta t = \tfrac{0.60\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{m/s}} = 1.5\,\text{s}$. The result lies between these.

## fy5-11-L2-02
**Given:** length of the rope $L = 8.0\,\text{m}$, time to return to the starting point $t = 3.2\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the speed $v$ of the pulse
**Principle:** The pulse travels at a steady speed along the rope to the tree, is reflected from the fixed end and returns along the same rope. Reflection inverts the pulse but does not change its speed, so in the time $t$ the pulse travels a distance of $2L$.
**Formula:** $2L = v t$, so $v = \dfrac{2L}{t}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the length is in metres and the time in seconds.
**Substitution:** $v = \dfrac{2 \cdot 8.0\,\text{m}}{3.2\,\text{s}} = \dfrac{16\,\text{m}}{3.2\,\text{s}} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $5.0\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the length and the time.
**Reasonableness:** If only $8.0\,\text{m}$ were taken as the distance, the speed would be half, $2.5\,\text{m/s}$. The pulse travels $8\,\text{m}$ in each direction in about one and a half seconds, which suits a taut rope.

## fy5-11-L2-03
**Given:** delay of the first echo $t_1 = 0.40\,\text{s}$, of the second $t_2 = 0.70\,\text{s}$, speed of sound $v = 343\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the distance $D$ between the walls
**Principle:** Each echo is sound that has travelled to its own wall and been reflected back. The nearer wall gives the first echo. The distance to each wall is half of the distance travelled by the sound, and the walls are on opposite sides of the listener, so the distances are added.
**Formula:** $d_1 = \dfrac{v t_1}{2}$, $d_2 = \dfrac{v t_2}{2}$ and $D = d_1 + d_2 = \dfrac{v (t_1 + t_2)}{2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the speed is in m/s and the times in seconds.
**Substitution:** $D = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s} \cdot (0.40\,\text{s} + 0.70\,\text{s})}{2} = \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.10\,\text{s}}{2} = 188.65\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $190\,\text{m}$ or $1.9 \cdot 10^2\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the delays $0.40\,\text{s}$ and $0.70\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The walls are $68.6\,\text{m}$ and $120\,\text{m}$ away; the farther one gives the later echo, as it should. Without halving the result would be about $380\,\text{m}$, which would be the whole round trip of the sound and not the distance between the walls.

## fy5-11-L2-04

## fy5-11-L2-05
**Given:** first measurement $d_1 = 20.0\,\text{m}$, $t_1 = 0.157\,\text{s}$; last measurement $d_4 = 80.0\,\text{m}$, $t_4 = 0.507\,\text{s}$; the sound playback of the phone has an unknown but constant delay $t_0$ between the play command and the departure of the sound, so every measured time is too long by the same amount
**Find:** the speed of sound $v$
**Principle:** The sound travels to the wall and back, a distance of $2d$. The time measured by the app is the travel time of this plus the unknown constant delay $t_0$. When the difference of the times of two measurements is taken, the same delay cancels, and only the extra distance $2(d_4 - d_1)$ remains, which the sound travels during the time $t_4 - t_1$.
**Formula:** $t = \dfrac{2d}{v} + t_0$, so $t_4 - t_1 = \dfrac{2(d_4 - d_1)}{v}$ and $v = \dfrac{2(d_4 - d_1)}{t_4 - t_1}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the distances are in metres and the times in seconds.
**Substitution:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \frac{2 \cdot (80.0 - 20.0)\,\text{m}}{(0.507 - 0.157)\,\text{s}} \\
&= \frac{120\,\text{m}}{0.350\,\text{s}} \\
&= 342.86\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$
**Answer:** $343\,\text{m/s}$, three significant figures like the times.
**Reasonableness:** The constant delay is $t_0 = 0.157\,\text{s} - \tfrac{40.0\,\text{m}}{342.86\,\text{m/s}} \approx 0.040\,\text{s}$, which is a believable delay of sound playback for a phone. If the speed were calculated from a single measurement with the formula $\tfrac{2d}{t}$, the last one would give $\tfrac{160\,\text{m}}{0.507\,\text{s}} \approx 316\,\text{m/s}$, too small, because the time contains the constant delay. The intermediate measurement fits the model: $\tfrac{80.0\,\text{m}}{342.86\,\text{m/s}} + 0.040\,\text{s} = 0.273\,\text{s}$, when the measured time is $0.274\,\text{s}$.

## fy5-11-L2-06
**Given:** frequency $f = 12\,\text{Hz}$, speed $v = 0.216\,\text{m/s}$; gap widths in the table, an almost straight beam at the gap $b = 14.4\,\text{cm}$
**Find:** a) the wavelength $\lambda$; b) the ratio $\tfrac{b}{\lambda}$ in the measurement where the wave continued almost straight; c) a rule for when the wave bends
**Principle:** The wavelength is obtained from the equation $v = f\lambda$. The strength of diffraction depends on how wide the gap is compared with the wavelength, so the gap widths of the table should be divided by the wavelength.
**Formula:** $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$ and the ratio $\dfrac{b}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $\lambda$ comes out in metres, and $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$, so that it can be compared with the gaps.
**Substitution:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= \frac{0.216\,\text{m/s}}{12\,\text{Hz}} \\
&= 0.018\,\text{m} = 1.8\,\text{cm} \\
\frac{b}{\lambda} &= \frac{14.4\,\text{cm}}{1.8\,\text{cm}} = 8.0
\end{aligned}
$$
**Answer:** a) $\lambda = 1.8\,\text{cm}$. b) the gap is $8.0$ times as wide as the wavelength, two significant figures like the frequency and the speed. c) The wave bends strongly when $\tfrac{b}{\lambda}$ is at most about $2$, and only a little when the ratio is clearly greater.

The observations of the table thus follow the rule: the wider the gap is compared with the wavelength, the less the wave bends.

## fy5-11-L2-07
**Given:** the angle between the incoming and the reflected ray $\varphi = 64^\circ$
**Find:** the error of the student and the correct angle of incidence $\alpha$
**Principle:** The error of the student is to take the angle between the incoming and the reflected ray as the angle of incidence. The angle of incidence is measured between the incoming ray and the normal, and the angle of reflection between the reflected ray and the normal. The normal divides the angle between the rays into these two parts, so $\varphi = \alpha + \beta$. By the law of reflection $\beta = \alpha$.
**Formula:** $\varphi = \alpha + \beta = 2\alpha$, so $\alpha = \dfrac{\varphi}{2}$
**Conversions:** Not needed: the angle is in degrees.
**Substitution:** $\alpha = \dfrac{64^\circ}{2} = 32^\circ$
**Answer:** $32^\circ$, two significant figures like the given angle $64^\circ$.
**Reasonableness:** The angle of incidence and the angle of reflection are both $32^\circ$, so their sum is $64^\circ$ like the measured angle. The $64^\circ$ of the student would make the angle between the rays $128^\circ$.

## fy5-11-L3-01
**Find:** why a low sound is heard behind the corner but a high one is not, explained with diffraction and wavelengths
**Principle:** Diffraction is strong when the width of the opening is of the order of the wavelength or smaller, and weak when the opening is many wavelengths wide. The wavelength is obtained from the equation $\lambda = \tfrac{v}{f}$ (chapter *Mechanical wave motion*).
**Model answer:** The wavelength of the bass is $\lambda = \tfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{100\,\text{Hz}} = 3.43\,\text{m}$, so the window is only about a quarter of a wavelength ($b/\lambda = \tfrac{0.80\,\text{m}}{3.43\,\text{m}} \approx 0.23$). The window then acts like a single source of wavelets, and the sound spreads from the window in every direction, also behind the corner. The wavelength of the cymbal is $\lambda = \tfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{5000\,\text{Hz}} \approx 6.9\,\text{cm}$, so the window is about $12$ wavelengths wide. From it the sound continues as an almost straight beam, and bends only a little behind the corner. That is why mostly bass is heard behind the corner.

## fy5-11-L3-02
**Given:** distance between the marks $d = 0.40\,\text{m}$, delay $\Delta t = 0.50\,\text{s}$, speed of the wave $v = 0.30\,\text{m/s}$

The front is perpendicular to the ray, so $s$, $d$ and the front form a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $d$ and in which $s$ is the leg opposite the angle of incidence.
**Formula:** $s = v\,\Delta t$ and $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{s}{d}$, so $\alpha = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{v\,\Delta t}{d}$

**Reasonableness:** If the wave arrived perpendicularly, the crest would reach both marks at the same time. If it travelled along the wall, the delay would be $\tfrac{0.40\,\text{m}}{0.30\,\text{m/s}} \approx 1.3\,\text{s}$. The measured $0.50\,\text{s}$ is less than half of this, so the wave arrives fairly steeply, at a small angle of incidence.

## fy5-11-L3-04
**Given:** delays in the table, distance between the measurement points $\Delta x = 20.0\,\text{m}$, speed of sound $v = 1480\,\text{m/s}$; the pulse travels vertically and the bottom is straight between the points
**Find:** a) the depth $d$ at each point; b) the angle of slope $\theta$ of the steepest interval to the horizontal; c) justifications
**Principle:** The pulse travels to the bottom and back, so during the delay it travels a distance of $2d$. The difference $\Delta d$ of the depths of two successive points and the horizontal distance $\Delta x$ of the points form a right triangle whose angle $\theta$ is the slope of the bottom. The steepest interval is the one where the change of depth is the greatest, because the interval $\Delta x$ is always the same.
**Formula:** $d = \dfrac{v t}{2}$ and $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\Delta d}{\Delta x}$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{\Delta d}{\Delta x}$
**Conversions:** $1\,\text{ms} = 0.001\,\text{s}$, for example $54\,\text{ms} = 0.054\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** a) The depth is $d = \tfrac{1480\,\text{m/s} \cdot t}{2} = 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot t$. The depths are
$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{0} &= 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.054\,\text{s} = 40.0\,\text{m} \\
d_{20} &= 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.060\,\text{s} = 44.4\,\text{m} \\
d_{40} &= 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.071\,\text{s} = 52.5\,\text{m} \\
d_{60} &= 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.090\,\text{s} = 66.6\,\text{m} \\
d_{80} &= 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.097\,\text{s} = 71.8\,\text{m} \\
d_{100} &= 740\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.085\,\text{s} = 62.9\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$
b) The changes of depth between the points are $4.4\,\text{m}$; $8.1\,\text{m}$; $14.1\,\text{m}$; $5.2\,\text{m}$ and $-8.9\,\text{m}$. The greatest change is between the points $x = 40\,\text{m}$ and $x = 60\,\text{m}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta d &= 66.6\,\text{m} - 52.5\,\text{m} \\
&= 14.06\,\text{m} \\
\theta &= \tan^{-1}\frac{14.06\,\text{m}}{20.0\,\text{m}} \\
&= \tan^{-1} 0.703 = 35.1^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$
**Answer:** the bottom is steepest in the interval $x = 40\,\text{m}$ … $60\,\text{m}$, and its angle of slope is $35^\circ$, two significant figures like the difference of the delays $90\,\text{ms} - 71\,\text{ms} = 19\,\text{ms}$. The deepest point is $x = 80\,\text{m}$, at a depth of about $72\,\text{m}$.
**c) Justification:** The pulse travels down to the bottom and back to the surface, so the measured delay corresponds to twice the depth. Therefore $d = \tfrac{vt}{2}$, and the delay must not be multiplied by the speed as such. Between the measurement points it is known only that the depth is between the depths of the end points, because the bottom is assumed to be straight. The assumption of a straight bottom fails most where the slope changes quickly: on a steep slope a ridge or a hollow can remain between the points, so denser measurements give a more accurate profile. The model assumes that the pulse travels vertically down and the echo returns directly from below. On a steep slope the assumption does not hold, because the first echo returns from the nearest point of the bottom and not directly from below, so the model underestimates the depth at that point.
**Reasonableness:** The angles of the other intervals are $12.5^\circ$; $22.1^\circ$; $14.5^\circ$ and $23.9^\circ$ (the bottom rises), all less than $35^\circ$. Check: $\tan 35^\circ = 0.70$, and $0.70 \cdot 20.0\,\text{m} = 14\,\text{m}$ corresponds to the change of depth.

## fy5-11-L3-05
**Given:** width of the mountain $b = 600\,\text{m}$, speed of radio waves $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$, frequencies $f_\text{AM} = 900\,\text{kHz}$, $f_\text{FM} = 100\,\text{MHz}$ and $f_\text{tv} = 600\,\text{MHz}$
**Find:** a) the wavelengths; b) the ratio $\tfrac{b}{\lambda}$ for FM radio; c) justifications for the bending
**Principle:** The wavelength is obtained from the equation $c = f\lambda$, which holds for all waves. The diffraction of a wave at the edge of an obstacle is strong when the obstacle is of the order of the wavelength or smaller, and weak when the obstacle is many times wider than the wavelength. Therefore the width of the mountain is compared with the wavelength.
**Formula:** $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f}$ and the ratio $\dfrac{b}{\lambda}$
**Conversions:** $900\,\text{kHz} = 900 \cdot 10^3\,\text{Hz} = 9.00 \cdot 10^5\,\text{Hz}$; $100\,\text{MHz} = 1.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{Hz}$; $600\,\text{MHz} = 6.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{Hz}$.
**Substitution:** a) The wavelengths are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_\text{AM} &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{9.00 \cdot 10^5\,\text{Hz}} = 333\,\text{m} \\
\lambda_\text{FM} &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{1.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{Hz}} = 3.00\,\text{m} \\
\lambda_\text{tv} &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}}{6.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{Hz}} = 0.500\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$
b) For FM radio
$$
\frac{b}{\lambda_\text{FM}} = \frac{600\,\text{m}}{3.00\,\text{m}} = 200
$$
**Answer:** the mountain is $2.0 \cdot 10^2$ times as wide as the wavelength of FM radio, two significant figures like the width of the mountain.
**c) Justification:** The wavelength of AM radio, $333\,\text{m}$, is of the same order as the width of the mountain, $\tfrac{b}{\lambda} = \tfrac{600\,\text{m}}{333\,\text{m}} = 1.8$, so diffraction is strong and the AM broadcast bends behind the mountain into the valley. The wavelength of FM radio is only $\tfrac{1}{200}$ of the width of the mountain and for digital TV $\tfrac{b}{\lambda} = \tfrac{600\,\text{m}}{0.500\,\text{m}} = 1200$, so their diffraction is weak: the waves travel almost straight, and a radio shadow remains behind the mountain. The wavelength of visible light, $550\,\text{nm} = 5.5 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$, is still much shorter, $\tfrac{b}{\lambda} \approx 1 \cdot 10^9$, so light travels practically straight and does not bend behind the mountain.
**Reasonableness:** The higher the frequency, the shorter the wavelength and the weaker the diffraction: $333\,\text{m} > 3.00\,\text{m} > 0.500\,\text{m} > 5.5 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$. Check: $333\,\text{m} \cdot 9.00 \cdot 10^5\,\text{Hz} = 3.00 \cdot 10^8\,\text{m/s}$.
