Air resistance and falling through air

Why does a skydiver's speed stop increasing, even though the weight pulls the skydiver down throughout the whole fall?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain why the speed of a falling object settles at a terminal velocity and how mass and shape affect it.
  • draw the free-body diagram of a falling and a rising object when air resistance is not ignored.
  • calculate the acceleration or the air resistance from the equation of motion, also when the net force points against the motion.

Prior knowledge

  • The net force points in the direction of the acceleration, and $\Sigma\overline{F} = m\overline{a}$ (the chapter Newton's second law)
  • The weight is $G = mg$, and friction opposes sliding along a surface (the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane)

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

A skydiver steps out of an aeroplane at a height of about four kilometres. During the first seconds the speed grows quickly, but after about ten seconds the reading of the wrist computer almost stops growing and settles at about $200\,\text{km/h}$, although the skydiver still falls for tens of seconds. When the parachute opens, the speed decreases in a few seconds to below $20\,\text{km/h}$.

Why does the speed stop growing, although the weight pulls the skydiver down all the time, and what happens to the forces when the parachute opens?

Optional Recap: friction and net force
A box slides to the right on a horizontal floor and slows down. Nobody pushes or pulls it.In which direction does the net force acting on the box point?

  • Correct. In the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane you learned that sliding friction points against the sliding. It is the only horizontal force, so the net force and the acceleration point to the left, and the box slows down. In this chapter air does the same: it opposes the motion.

  • Motion does not cause a force. Nothing pushes the box, so there is no force pointing to the right. The box slows down, so the acceleration and the net force point to the left, against the motion.

  • If the net force were zero, the speed of the box would not change. The box slows down, so the net force is not zero: friction points to the left.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation. Only the weight $\overline{G}$ and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ act on the skydiver. The skydiver starts from rest relative to the air (the speed of the plane is ignored). The air is still, and its density in the model is the same at all heights; the buoyancy in air is not taken into account. Air resistance is calculated from the model $F_\text{v} = kv^2$, in which the constant $k$ depends on the position and the parachute: belly down $0.26\,\text{kg/m}$, head first $0.10\,\text{kg/m}$ and with the parachute open $30\,\text{kg/m}$. The parachute opens in $3.0\,\text{s}$. You do not need to know this model: in the chapter air resistance is treated qualitatively. In the fall before the parachute is opened, the arrow of the air resistance ends at the bottom surface, on which the air presses; after the parachute has opened, it starts from the top surface, because the parachute pulls the skydiver from above.

How the calculation works

The positive direction is down. The net force is $\Sigma F = G - F_\text{v}$, where $G = mg$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the acceleration is $a = \dfrac{\Sigma F}{m}$. A negative $\Sigma F$ or $a$ points up. The simulation calculates the motion in steps of $2\,\text{ms}$: at every step the speed changes by the product of the acceleration and the length of the step. The speed is plotted in the graph at intervals of $0.5\,\text{s}$.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Air resists motion

    When an object moves through air or water, it has to push the medium out of its way. The medium pushes back on the object: the object experiences resistance of the medium, in air air resistance (also called drag), which this book writes as $\overline{F}_\text{v}$. It points against the velocity of the object when the air is still. In the chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane, friction resisted sliding along a surface. Air resistance is a similar resistive force, but it is caused by the surrounding air, and the object does not need to touch any solid surface. A bicycle and a car also have rolling resistance $\overline{F}_\text{r}$, the resistance caused by the tyres and the road, which is a different force from air resistance.

    Air resistance differs from sliding friction in that it depends on speed. Air resistance does not act at all on an object that is at rest relative to the air, and it grows quickly when the speed of the object relative to the air grows. At the same speed, air resistance is greater the larger the cross-sectional area of the object and the worse the air can flow past it. At the same speed, the resistance in water is much greater than in air, because water is denser.

    The three stages of a fall

    The skydiver starts from rest relative to the air (the speed of the plane is ignored), so right after the jump there is no air resistance yet. The only force is the weight, and the skydiver accelerates downward with the free-fall acceleration $g$. As the speed grows, air resistance grows. The weight stays the same, so the net force becomes smaller, and the acceleration becomes smaller with it: the speed grows more and more slowly. Finally the air resistance is equal to the weight. Then the net force is zero, the acceleration is zero and the speed stays constant, as Newton's first law says. This speed is called the terminal velocity.

    Terminal velocity depends on mass and shape

    At terminal velocity, air resistance is equal to the weight. A heavier skydiver has a greater weight, so the speed has time to grow greater before air resistance has grown to the size of the weight. In the graph, the curves of a $60\,\text{kg}$ and a $100\,\text{kg}$ skydiver falling in the same position start equally steep, but they level off at different speeds, in the values of the model at about $48\,\text{m/s}$ and $61\,\text{m/s}$. A skydiver diving head first has a smaller cross-sectional area and a more streamlined shape, so the terminal velocity is greater.

    This gives the answer to the question at the start. The weight pulls the skydiver down all the time, but as the speed grows, air resistance grows until it cancels the weight. After that the speed no longer changes. Without air resistance, all objects would fall with the same acceleration $g$. In air, a light and wide object, such as a sheet of paper or a feather, reaches a small terminal velocity very soon and therefore falls slowly.

    Equation of motion with air resistance

    Air resistance is one of the forces acting on an object, so the motion follows Newton's second law. An object falling or rising through air is acted on by its weight and by air resistance:

    $$\overline{G} + \overline{F}_\text{v} = m\overline{a}$$

    where $\overline{G}$ is the weight of the object ($\text{N}$), $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ is the air resistance ($\text{N}$), $m$ is the mass of the object ($\text{kg}$) and $\overline{a}$ is its acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$). In this chapter the magnitude of air resistance is not calculated from the speed, but it is given or deduced from the motion: at terminal velocity $F_\text{v} = G$.

    A falling and a rising object

    As always, first choose the object, draw its free-body diagram and choose the positive direction, and only then write the equation. The air resistance of a falling object points up, against the weight. When the positive direction is down, $mg - F_\text{v} = ma$, so the acceleration is smaller than $g$. The air resistance of a ball thrown upward points down, in the same direction as the weight. When the positive direction is up, $-mg - F_\text{v} = ma$. The magnitude of the acceleration is then greater than $g$, and the rising ball slows down faster than without air resistance.

    When the parachute opens

    The large area of the parachute makes air resistance many times greater. When the parachute opens, the skydiver is still moving downward at terminal velocity, so for a moment the air resistance is many times the weight. The net force and the acceleration point up, even though the skydiver is moving down: the skydiver slows down but does not rise. As the speed decreases, air resistance decreases until, at a new, small terminal velocity, it is again equal to the weight. How many times the weight the air resistance is depends on the parachute and the moment: in the diagram it is three times, in the example almost three times, and in the model of the simulation at most about five times. The direction of the net force can therefore differ from the direction of motion. It always tells the direction of the acceleration, not of the velocity.

    Common misconception

    In videos, the skydiver seems to rise upward when the parachute opens. The camera operator is still falling without a parachute, and the skydiver only slows down relative to the camera operator. Relative to the ground, the skydiver moves downward all the time: the net force points up for a moment, but it decreases the speed and does not turn the motion around. Another mistake is to think that at terminal velocity air resistance is greater than the weight or that there are no forces any more. At terminal velocity the forces are equal and opposite. A third mistake is to claim that a heavy object always falls faster. Without air resistance, everything falls with the same acceleration. In air, of objects of the same size and shape the heavier one reaches the greater terminal velocity.

    Summary

    • Resistance of the medium, in air air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$, points against the velocity and grows when the speed grows. It is greater with a large cross-sectional area and a poorly streamlined shape.
    • The acceleration of a falling object decreases as air resistance grows. At terminal velocity $F_\text{v} = G$, the net force is zero and the speed stays constant.
    • Of objects of the same size and shape, the heavier one has the greater terminal velocity. A parachute lowers the terminal velocity to a safe one.
    • The equation of motion is $\overline{G} + \overline{F}_\text{v} = m\overline{a}$. For a rising object, air resistance points down together with the weight, so the deceleration is greater than $g$.
    • When the parachute opens, the net force and the acceleration point up even though the skydiver moves down: the direction of the net force can differ from the direction of motion.

      5Apply

      Worked example

      Problem

      A skydiver whose mass with equipment is $85.0\,\text{kg}$ opens the parachute. At one moment while the parachute is opening, the air resistance is $2.40\,\text{kN}$, and the skydiver is still moving downward. What is the acceleration of the skydiver at this moment, and in which direction does it point?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. $m = 85.0\,\text{kg}$, $F_\text{v} = 2.40\,\text{kN}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the acceleration $a$ and its direction.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. Choose the skydiver with the equipment and the parachute as the object. The Earth (weight) and the air (air resistance) act on it. By Newton's second law, the acceleration is determined by the sum of these forces; the direction of the velocity does not decide the direction of the acceleration.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. In the free-body diagram the skydiver is a rectangle. The weight $\overline{G}$ starts from the centre and points down. Air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ acts mainly on the parachute, so it starts from the top surface and points up. The positive direction is chosen down, the direction of motion.

      From the diagram we write the net force $mg - F_\text{v}$ and solve the acceleration:

      $$\begin{aligned} &mg - F_\text{v} = ma \\ &a = \frac{mg - F_\text{v}}{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Air resistance is converted to newtons:

      $2.40\,\text{kN}$$= 2.40\cdot 1000\,\text{N}$
      $= 2400\,\text{N}$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First calculate the weight and then the acceleration:

      $$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{mg - F_\text{v}}{m} \\ mg &= 85.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \\ \phantom{mg} &= 833.85\,\text{N} \\ a &= \frac{833.85\,\text{N} - 2400\,\text{N}}{85.0\,\text{kg}} \\ \phantom{a} &\approx -18.4\,\text{m/s}^2 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The acceleration is negative, so it points up, opposite to the direction chosen as positive. The magnitude of the acceleration is $18.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ upward, given with three significant figures like the mass $85.0\,\text{kg}$ and the air resistance $2.40\,\text{kN}$. The skydiver moves down, but the speed decreases.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. Air resistance is almost three times the weight $834\,\text{N}$, so about two weights of net force remain upward. The acceleration must therefore be about two times $g$, or about $20\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the result $18.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ fits this. To the skydiver, the opening of the parachute feels like a strong jerk, but it lasts only a few seconds.

      Optional Apply to a new situation

      A snowboarder rides straight down a steep slope that is at an angle of $30.0^\circ$ to the horizontal. The mass of the snowboarder with equipment is $75.0\,\text{kg}$, and the friction force of the snow on the board is $64.0\,\text{N}$. The snowboarder has reached terminal velocity and rides at a constant speed.

      What is the air resistance? Give the answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      What is the net force when the speed is constant? Which forces act along the slope?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Choose the downhill direction as positive. Along the slope act the component of the weight $mg\sin\alpha$ downhill and friction and air resistance uphill: $mg\sin\alpha - F_\mu - F_\text{v} = 0$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $mg\sin\alpha$$= 75.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot\sin 30.0^\circ$
      $\approx 368\,\text{N}$

      Subtract the friction force from it.

      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 75.0\,\text{kg}$, angle of the slope $\alpha = 30.0^\circ$, friction force $F_\mu = 64.0\,\text{N}$, the speed is constant; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the air resistance $F_\text{v}$ in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The speed is constant, so by Newton's second law the net force is zero both along the slope and perpendicular to it. Along the slope the component of the weight takes the snowboarder downhill, and friction and air resistance oppose the motion. Terminal velocity is the speed at which these cancel each other.

      Free-body diagram

      The snowboarder as a rectangle along the slope, positive $x$-direction downhill. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre straight down, the normal force of the snow $\overline{N}$ from the bottom surface perpendicular to the slope, the friction force $\overline{F}_\mu$ from the edge of the bottom surface uphill and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ uphill. The components of the weight with dashed lines.

      Formula

      $mg\sin\alpha - F_\mu - F_\text{v} = 0$, so $F_\text{v} = mg\sin\alpha - F_\mu$

      Conversions

      The quantities are in SI units, and the angle is in degrees with the calculator in degree mode, so no conversions are needed.

      Substitution

      $F_\text{v}$$= 75.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot\sin 30.0^\circ - 64.0\,\text{N}$
      $= 367.875\,\text{N} - 64.0\,\text{N}$
      $= 303.875\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $304\,\text{N}$

      Uphill, three significant figures like the mass $75.0\,\text{kg}$, the angle $30.0^\circ$ and the friction force $64.0\,\text{N}$.

      Reasonableness

      Since $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$, the component of the weight along the slope is half of the weight $736\,\text{N}$, or about $370\,\text{N}$. Air resistance cancels the part of it that friction does not cancel, and is therefore much smaller than the weight. Without friction the air resistance at terminal velocity would be $368\,\text{N}$.

      1. Concept

      A skydiver falls belly down at terminal velocity, and the parachute is still closed. Which statement about the forces acting on the skydiver is correct?

      2. Calculation

      After the parachute has opened, a skydiver whose mass with equipment is $64.0\,\text{kg}$ descends at a constant speed of $5.5\,\text{m/s}$.

      What is the air resistance? Give the answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Draw the free-body diagram: the weight down and the air resistance up. When the positive direction is down, $mg - F_\text{v} = 0$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      Show the solution

      Given

      $m = 64.0\,\text{kg}$, the speed is constant $5.5\,\text{m/s}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

      Find

      the air resistance $F_\text{v}$ in $\text{N}$

      Principle

      The speed is constant, so the acceleration is zero and by Newton's second law the net force is zero. The skydiver falls at a new, small terminal velocity at which air resistance cancels the weight.

      Free-body diagram

      The skydiver with the parachute as a rectangle, positive direction down. The weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ from the top surface up, equally long. The velocity down is marked separately and not as a force.

      Conversions

      The mass is in kilograms. The speed is not needed, so it is not converted.

      Substitution

      $F_\text{v}$$= 64.0\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
      $= 627.84\,\text{N}$

      $628\,\text{N}$

      Upward, three significant figures like the mass $64.0\,\text{kg}$.

      Reasonableness

      The air resistance is equal to that in the fall before the parachute is opened at terminal velocity, about $630\,\text{N}$, although the speed is a tenth of it. The large area of the parachute produces the same force at a much smaller speed. The weight of a kilogram is about $10\,\text{N}$, so $64\,\text{kg}$ corresponds to about $640\,\text{N}$.

        Finally, three quick questions. Answer first yourself and open the answer only after that.

        1. Two balls of the same size, a light plastic ball and a heavy wooden ball, are dropped at the same time from the top of a tall tower. Which hits the ground first, and why?
          Answer

          The heavy wooden ball. At the start both accelerate with the acceleration $g$, but as the speed grows, air resistance reduces the acceleration of the light ball more, because its weight is smaller. Balls of the same size have the same air resistance at the same speed, so the terminal velocity of the light ball is smaller.

        2. Why is the magnitude of the acceleration of a ball thrown upward greater than $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ during the rise, when air resistance is not ignored?
          Answer

          During the rise air resistance points against the velocity, that is down, in the same direction as the weight. The net force $mg + F_\text{v}$ is greater than the weight, so the magnitude of the acceleration $g + \tfrac{F_\text{v}}{m}$ is greater than $g$.

        3. Name two ways in which a skydiver can lower the terminal velocity, and explain why they work.
          Answer

          The skydiver can turn belly down and spread the arms and legs, which increases the cross-sectional area, or open the parachute. Both increase the air resistance at the same speed, so the air resistance grows to the size of the weight already at a smaller speed.

          N

          $24\,\text{N}$

          Given

          the speed is constant, air resistance $F_\text{v} = 18\,\text{N}$, rolling resistance (the resistance caused by the tyres and the road) $F_\text{r} = 6.0\,\text{N}$

        1. A skydiver whose mass with equipment is $72.0\,\text{kg}$ falls belly down at terminal velocity. The skydiver quickly turns to a head-first position, and the air resistance at the same speed decreases to half of what it was.

          Answer

          $4.91\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Given

          $m = 72.0\,\text{kg}$, at first terminal velocity, after the change of position the air resistance is half of the earlier value at the same speed; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the acceleration $a$ right after the change of position in $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          At terminal velocity the air resistance is equal to the weight, because the net force is zero. The net force is no longer zero, and Newton's second law gives the acceleration.

          The skydiver as a rectangle, positive direction down. Before the change, the weight $\overline{G}$ and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ are equally long.           Before the change $F_\text{v} = mg$, so after the change $F_\text{v}' = \tfrac{1}{2}mg$. The equation of motion $mg - F_\text{v}' = ma$ gives

          $a$$= \dfrac{mg - \tfrac{1}{2}mg}{m}$
          $= \dfrac{g}{2}$

          Conversions

          The mass cancels out, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $a$$= \dfrac{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{2}$
          $= 4.905\,\text{m/s}^2$

          $4.91\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Half of the weight is left uncancelled, so the acceleration is half of the free-fall acceleration. The skydiver starts to accelerate again until the air resistance has grown to the size of the weight in the new position, now at a greater terminal velocity.

        2. A ball whose mass is $0.200\,\text{kg}$ is thrown straight up. At one moment during the rise the air resistance is $0.600\,\text{N}$. Later the ball falls back down, and at one moment the air resistance is again $0.600\,\text{N}$.

          How much greater is the magnitude of the acceleration of the ball at the first moment than at the second?

          m/s²
          Answer

          $6.00\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $m = 0.200\,\text{kg}$, air resistance $F_\text{v} = 0.600\,\text{N}$ both on the way up and on the way down; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the difference of the magnitudes of the accelerations $\Delta a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          Air resistance always points against the velocity. On the way up it points down together with the weight, on the way down up against the weight. Newton's second law gives the acceleration at both moments, and they are subtracted from each other.

          Free-body diagram

          Two diagrams, the ball as a rectangle. On the way up, the weight $\overline{G}$ from the centre down and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ from the top surface down. On the way down, the weight down and the air resistance from the bottom surface up. In both, the positive direction is down.

          Formula

          On the way up $mg + F_\text{v} = ma_1$, so $a_1 = g + \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{m}$. On the way down $mg - F_\text{v} = ma_2$, so $a_2 = g - \dfrac{F_\text{v}}{m}$. The difference is

          $\Delta a$$= a_1 - a_2$
          $= \dfrac{2F_\text{v}}{m}$

          Conversions

          The quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\Delta a$$= \dfrac{2\cdot 0.600\,\text{N}}{0.200\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 6.00\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $6.00\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Three significant figures like the mass $0.200\,\text{kg}$ and the air resistance $0.600\,\text{N}$.

          Reasonableness

          Calculated separately,

          $a_1$$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 3.00\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 12.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          and

          $a_2$$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 3.00\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 6.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          and their difference is $6.00\,\text{m/s}^2$. Both accelerations point down, but on the way up it is greater than $g$ and on the way down smaller.

          The weight of the ball is $0.150\,\text{kg}\cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 1.5\,\text{N}$. The acceleration is about two thirds of the free-fall acceleration, so the air resistance cancels about a third of the weight, about $0.5\,\text{N}$. Later the curve flattens, the air resistance grows and approaches the weight.
