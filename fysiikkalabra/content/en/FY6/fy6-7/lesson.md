## Key idea

### Power tells the pace, energy the amount
The rating plate of every electrical device shows its **rated power**, for example $2000\,\text{W}$ for a kettle and $100\,\text{W}$ for a television. Power tells how fast the device takes electrical energy from the mains (chapter *Electric power and the heating of a resistor*). How much energy the device uses in all depends, besides the power, on the time of use: when the power stays constant, **energy is power times time**. An electricity bill charges mainly for energy, not for power.

So a large power alone does not mean a large consumption. A kettle with a power of $2.0\,\text{kW}$ uses in six minutes as much energy as a $0.10\,\text{kW}$ television in two hours. In a graph of power against time, the energy is the area between the graph and the time axis: a narrow, tall rectangle can be equal to a wide, low one.

### The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy
The SI unit of energy is the joule, but the electrical energy of a home is measured and charged in **kilowatt-hours**. One kilowatt-hour is the energy that a device working at a power of $1\,\text{kW}$ uses in an hour. When the power is given in kilowatts and the time in hours, the energy comes out directly in kilowatt-hours. The name tells how it is calculated: kilowatt times hour, not kilowatt per hour. One kilowatt-hour is $3.6$ million joules.

### The current of a device in the mains
The sockets of a home have a voltage of $230\,\text{V}$. It is the effective value of an alternating voltage. This chapter considers simplified circuits in which the current and voltage are in phase, as in heating devices, or the power factor is assumed to be approximately one. Then the active power of a device is calculated in the same way as with a direct voltage: $P = UI$. In general, the active power of an alternating-current circuit also depends on the phase difference between the voltage and the current. From the rated power of a device we therefore get the current $I = P : U$. A kettle draws a current of $2000\,\text{W} : 230\,\text{V} \approx 8.7\,\text{A}$, whereas a phone charger draws only a fraction of an ampere. High-power devices draw a large current, and that is why the fuses of a home's circuits limit how many such devices can be on at the same time in one circuit (chapter *Electrical safety at home and at work*).

### Where the electrical energy of a home goes
The most energy is used by the devices in which both the power and the time of use are large: electric heating, a water heater and an electric sauna stove. In their resistors electrical energy becomes the internal energy of the resistor, which is transferred as heat to water, air or stones. A six-kilowatt stove uses $9.0\,\text{kWh}$ in a sauna session of an hour and a half, as much as a $100\,\text{W}$ television does in $90$ hours.

A low-power device can still build up a considerable consumption if it is on all the time. A refrigerator runs around the clock, and a device in standby mode uses about $9\,\text{kWh}$ a year for each watt of power. Saving is therefore possible in two ways: by reducing the power, for example by replacing lamps with LED lamps, or by shortening the time of use, for example by having a sauna less often or by lowering the room temperature. The effect is greatest in the devices whose energy is the greatest.

## Formula: Electrical energy
When the power of a device stays constant, the electrical energy it uses is
```latex
E = Pt
```
where $E$ is the energy (J or kWh), $P$ the power (W or kW) and $t$ the time (s or h). When the power is in watts and the time in seconds, the energy comes out in joules; with kilowatts and hours it comes out in kilowatt-hours. The conversion is done by substituting the units: $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3\,600\,000\,\text{J} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$.

Since the electric power is $P = UI$, the energy can also be calculated from the voltage and the current:
```latex
E = UIt
```
where $U$ is the voltage of the device (V) and $I$ the current through it (A).

An electricity bill charges both for the electrical energy and for its transmission in the electricity network per kilowatt-hour, and in addition monthly basic charges:
```latex
K = aE + K_\text{base}
```
where $K$ is the total of the bill (€), $a$ the price of energy and transmission together per kilowatt-hour (€/kWh), $E$ the energy of the billing period (kWh) and $K_\text{base}$ the basic charges (€). The energy of the billing period is the difference of the readings of the electricity meter. Prices are often given in cents: $18\,\text{c/kWh} = 0.18\,\text{€/kWh}$.

## Worked example
The power of the electric underfloor heating of a bathroom is $0.80\,\text{kW}$. The thermostat keeps the heating on for $6\,\text{h}\ 15\,\text{min}$ a day on average. How much electrical energy does the underfloor heating use in $30$ days, and how much does it cost when electricity costs $18\,\text{c/kWh}$ including transmission?

<!-- vaihe -->

**Given and find.** $P = 0.80\,\text{kW}$, time of use $6\,\text{h}\ 15\,\text{min}$ per day, $30$ days, price $a = 18\,\text{c/kWh}$. We are asked for the energy $E$ in kilowatt-hours and the cost $K$ in euros.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Principle.** When the heating is on, its power is constant, so the energy is power times the total time of use. The cost is the energy times the price per kilowatt-hour; the basic charges belong to the bill of the whole home, not to one device.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Formula solved.**
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&E = Pt \\
&K = aE
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Unit conversions.** $15\,\text{min} = \tfrac{15}{60}\,\text{h} = 0.25\,\text{h}$, so the time of use in a day is $6.25\,\text{h}$ and the total time is $t = 30 \cdot 6.25\,\text{h} = 187.5\,\text{h}$. The price $18\,\text{c/kWh} = 0.18\,\text{€/kWh}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Substitution.**
```latex
\begin{aligned}
&E = Pt \\
&\phantom{E} = 0.80\,\text{kW} \cdot 187.5\,\text{h} \\
&\phantom{E} = 150\,\text{kWh} \\
&K = aE \\
&\phantom{K} = 0.18\,\tfrac{\text{€}}{\text{kWh}} \cdot 150\,\text{kWh} \\
&\phantom{K} = 27\,\text{€}
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Result.** In $30$ days the underfloor heating uses about $150\,\text{kWh}$ of electrical energy, and it costs about $27\,\text{€}$. The results are given with two significant figures like the power $0.80\,\text{kW}$ and the price $18\,\text{c/kWh}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Reasonableness.** In a day the heating uses $0.80\,\text{kW} \cdot 6.25\,\text{h} = 5.0\,\text{kWh}$, which costs about $0.90\,\text{€}$; in a month that is about $30 \cdot 0.90\,\text{€} = 27\,\text{€}$. If the minutes had been left unconverted and the time $6.15\,\text{h}$ used, the result would have been slightly too small.

## Common misconception
Many think that a high-power device is always the biggest item on the electricity bill. Power, however, only tells how fast the device uses energy. A kettle is on for only a few minutes a day, whereas a refrigerator, a water heater and heating work for hours. The consumption is decided by power times time.

Another common mistake is to read a kilowatt-hour as "kilowatts per hour". A kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, kilowatt times hour, and it does not tell how fast anything changes. A third mistake is to mix up units: $2000\,\text{W} \cdot 15\,\text{min}$ is not $30\,000\,\text{kWh}$ but $2.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 0.25\,\text{h} = 0.50\,\text{kWh}$.

## Summary
- The electrical energy used by a device working at a constant power is $E = Pt = UIt$. Power tells how fast energy is used, energy how much.
- The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy: $1\,\text{kWh} = 1000\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s} = 3.6\,\text{MJ}$. Energy calculated with kilowatts and hours comes out directly in kilowatt-hours.
- The current of a heating device in a $230\,\text{V}$ mains is $I = P : U$; a high-power device draws a large current.
- An electricity bill is the price of energy and transmission times the energy used, plus the basic charges. The energy is the difference of the meter readings.
- The most is saved by reducing the power or the time of use of the devices whose energy is the greatest: heating, hot water and the sauna.
