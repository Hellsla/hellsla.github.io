> **Why** did a hammer and a feather hit the Moon's surface together when the astronaut dropped them at the same time, even though on Earth a feather drifts slowly down?

## Key idea
When an object is released from rest, its speed increases. **Free fall** is falling with no air resistance. Measurements show that in free fall the speed increases by the same amount every second: on the surface of the Earth by $9.81\,\text{m/s}$ per second. After one second the speed is $9.81\,\text{m/s}$, after two seconds $19.6\,\text{m/s}$ and after three seconds $29.4\,\text{m/s}$. So the object does not fall at a constant speed as in uniform motion (chapter *Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs*) but accelerates.

The change in speed per unit of time is called **acceleration**. The acceleration of a freely falling object is the **acceleration of free fall** $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The unit $\text{m/s}^2$ is read "metres per second squared", that is, "metres per second per second": speed is measured in $\text{m/s}$, and it changes by this amount every second. So the number $g$ is not a speed but the rate at which the speed increases.

```pysahdy
An object falls freely from rest, and its speed after three seconds is $29.4\,\text{m/s}$. What is its speed after four seconds?
---
The speed increases by $9.81\,\text{m/s}$ every second, so it is about $39.2\,\text{m/s}$.
```

### Mass does not affect falling
When there is no air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration regardless of their mass. In a vacuum a hammer and a feather fall equally fast, and a ball with a mass of $5\,\text{kg}$ has at every moment the same speed as a ball with a mass of $1\,\text{kg}$. The reason is explained later in connection with forces. In this chapter it is taken as an experimental result that you investigate yourself.

In air the situation is different. Air slows a light, wide object, such as a feather or a sheet of paper, clearly more than a small, dense object, such as a stone or a steel ball. This is why at home a feather falls more slowly than a hammer. The difference is caused by the air, not by mass as such.

```pysahdy
In free fall all objects have the same $g$. Why does a feather still fall more slowly than a hammer at home?
---
Air slows a light, wide object more than a small, dense one. The difference is caused by the air, not by mass as such.
```

## Formula: Speed in free fall
Acceleration tells how fast the velocity changes: it is the change in velocity divided by the time interval in which the change happens. When the acceleration is constant, the change in velocity is the acceleration times the time interval:

```kaavat
average acceleration :: a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}
:: \Delta v = a\,\Delta t
```

where $a$ is the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$), $\Delta v$ the change in velocity ($\text{m/s}$) in the time interval $\Delta t$ ($\text{s}$). In free fall the acceleration is the acceleration of free fall $g$. When the object starts from rest, the change in velocity by the moment $t$ is $\Delta v = v$ and the time interval is $\Delta t = t$. So the speed increases by the same amount every second and is directly proportional to time:

```latex
v = g\,t
```

where $v$ is the downward speed ($\text{m/s}$), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the acceleration of free fall and $t$ is the time from the start of the fall ($\text{s}$). The formula is a linear model $v = kt$ in which the slope $k$ is $g$. It holds when there is no air resistance.

So the $(t, v)$ graph of a falling object is a rising straight line through the origin. Its slope $\tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is the acceleration of free fall, so $g$ can be found from the graph by reading two points far apart. Because the acceleration is constant, $\Delta v = g\,\Delta t$ holds for any time interval: the speed of an object moving downwards increases by $9.81\,\text{m/s}$ every second even when the object did not start from rest.

```pysahdy
The downward speed of an object is $5.0\,\text{m/s}$ at some moment. What is its speed $2.0\,\text{s}$ later when there is no air resistance?
---
The acceleration is constant, so $\Delta v = g\,\Delta t \approx 19.6\,\text{m/s}$ and the speed is about $25\,\text{m/s}$. The object does not need to start from rest.
```

## Worked example
A stone is dropped from rest from a bridge, and it hits the water $1.5\,\text{s}$ later. What is the speed of the stone when it hits the water, in $\text{m/s}$ and in $\text{km/h}$? Air resistance is ignored.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Given and find.**

- The duration of the fall is $t = 1.5\,\text{s}$,
- the stone starts from rest and
- the acceleration of free fall is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

We want the speed $v$ at the moment of impact, first in $\text{m/s}$ and then in $\text{km/h}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Principle.** The stone is in free fall, because air resistance is ignored, and it starts from rest. Then the speed increases by the same amount every second, so the speed is directly proportional to time: $v = g\,t$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Rearranged formula.** The formula is already solved for the quantity we want:
```latex
v = g\,t
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Unit conversions.** The time is in seconds and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes in $\text{m/s}$ without a conversion. To convert to kilometres per hour, think about what $1\,\text{m/s}$ means: in an hour ($3600\,\text{s}$) an object at this speed covers $3600\,\text{m} = 3.6\,\text{km}$, so $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$. This is substituted in place of the unit.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Substitution.**
```latex
\begin{aligned}
v &= g\,t \\
&= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{s} \\
&= 14.715\,\text{m/s}
\\[4pt]
v &= 14.715 \cdot 1\,\text{m/s} \\
&= 14.715 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} \\
&= 52.97\,\text{km/h}
\end{aligned}
```

<!-- vaihe -->

**Result.** The speed is $v \approx 15\,\text{m/s}$, that is, $v \approx 53\,\text{km/h}$. Two significant figures, because the time $1.5\,\text{s}$ is given with two figures. The conversion is made from the unrounded value, not from the rounded $15\,\text{m/s}$.

<!-- vaihe -->

**Reasonableness.** The graph shows the same: at the moment $1\,\text{s}$ the speed is $9.81\,\text{m/s}$ and at the moment $2\,\text{s}$ it is $19.6\,\text{m/s}$, so at the moment $1.5\,\text{s}$ it is about $15\,\text{m/s}$. A speed of $53\,\text{km/h}$ is a normal speed for a car in city traffic, and it is a reasonable result for a fall that lasts only one and a half seconds.

## Common misconception
A heavy object does not fall faster than a light one: when there is no air resistance, all objects have the same $g$. Everyday experience seems to show otherwise, because air holds back the fall of a light, wide object the most. Another mistake is to treat the number $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ as a speed: it tells how much the speed increases every second, and the speed itself is $g\,t$.

## Summary
- In free fall there is no air resistance. The speed of the object increases by the same amount every second.
- The acceleration of free fall on the surface of the Earth is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, and it is the same for all objects regardless of mass.
- The speed of an object that starts from rest is $v = g\,t$, and the $(t, v)$ graph is a straight line through the origin with slope $g$.
- In air, air holds back the fall of a light, wide object the most, so the formula $v = g\,t$ holds only when air resistance can be ignored.
- Conversion: at a speed of $1\,\text{m/s}$ an object travels $3600\,\text{m} = 3.6\,\text{km}$ in an hour, so $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
