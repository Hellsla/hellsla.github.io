## fy9-harjoituskoe-s1

### a)
**Given:** mass of the stones $m_\text{k} = 25\,\text{kg}$, specific heat capacity $c_\text{k} = 0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$, the temperature rises from $15\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $280\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** the energy stored in the stones $Q_\text{k}$
**Principle:** The stones are solid all the time and their phase does not change, so the increase in their internal energy is a quantity of heat that depends on the mass, the specific heat capacity and the change in temperature.
**Formula:** $Q_\text{k} = c_\text{k} m_\text{k} \Delta T$
**Conversions:** $c_\text{k} = 0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} = 0.80 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$. A change in temperature is the same in kelvins and in degrees Celsius: $\Delta T = 280\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 15\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 265\,\text{K}$.
**Substitution:** $Q_\text{k} = 800\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 25\,\text{kg} \cdot 265\,\text{K} = 5\,300\,000\,\text{J} = 5.30\,\text{MJ}$
**Answer:** $Q_\text{k} \approx 5.3\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures, because the specific heat capacity $0.80\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$ and the mass $25\,\text{kg}$ are known to two digits.
**Reasonableness:** Roughly $25 \cdot 800 \cdot 300\,\text{J} = 6\,\text{MJ}$, so the order of magnitude is right. The energy corresponds to about $1.5\,\text{kWh}$, so the stove would produce it at full power in about $15\,\text{min}$.

### b)
**Given:** power of the stove $P = 6.0\,\text{kW}$, time $t = 50\,\text{min}$, $Q_\text{k} = 5.30\,\text{MJ}$ (part a)
**Find:** the fraction of the electrical energy stored in the stones
**Principle:** The stove converts electrical energy into internal energy at the power $P$, so the electrical energy is the power multiplied by the time. The fraction is the energy stored in the stones divided by all the electrical energy.
**Formula:** $E = Pt$ and fraction $= \dfrac{Q_\text{k}}{E} = \dfrac{Q_\text{k}}{Pt}$
**Conversions:** $t = 50 \cdot 60\,\text{s} = 3000\,\text{s}$ and $P = 6.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{W} = 6000\,\text{W}$
**Substitution:** $E = 6000\,\text{W} \cdot 3000\,\text{s} = 18\,000\,000\,\text{J} = 18.0\,\text{MJ}$ and fraction $= \dfrac{5.30\,\text{MJ}}{18.0\,\text{MJ}} = 0.2944$
**Answer:** About $29\,\%$ of the electrical energy is in the stones at the end, two significant figures like the power and the result of part a.
**Reasonableness:** The fraction is below $100\,\%$, as it must be. The electrical energy $18\,\text{MJ}$ is $\tfrac{18\,\text{MJ}}{3.6\,\text{MJ/kWh}} = 5.0\,\text{kWh}$, which is a typical consumption for heating a sauna.

### c)
**Find:** whether the efficiency of the stove is only about $30\,\%$
**Principle:** Efficiency is the useful energy obtained divided by the energy used. It depends on what is counted as useful and where the boundary of the system is drawn. In an electric resistor practically all the electrical energy becomes internal energy.
**Model answer:** The stove is an electric resistor, so practically all the electrical energy it uses, $18\,\text{MJ}$, becomes internal energy of the sauna. About $29\,\%$ of it is in the stones, but the rest heats the air, the benches and the walls of the sauna, and only part of it leaks out through the walls and the ventilation. In heating a sauna, the warming of the air and the walls is also useful, so the figure of part b is the stones' share of the energy, not the efficiency of the stove. The claim does not hold: as a heater of the sauna the stove has an efficiency of almost $100\,\%$, and the only waste is the energy transferred out of the sauna.

### d)
**Given:** mass of the water $m = 1.2\,\text{kg}$ (density of water $1\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{l}}$), initial temperature $18\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c_\text{v} = 4.19\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$, $r = 2260\,\tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kg}}$; the steam forms at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** the energy $Q$ transferred from the stones to the water
**Principle:** The water first warms to its boiling point, with no change of phase, and then evaporates at constant temperature. The energy needed for both stages comes from the stones, so they are added together.
**Formula:** $Q = c_\text{v} m \Delta T + r m$
**Conversions:** $1.2\,\text{l}$ of water is $1.2\,\text{kg}$. $c_\text{v} = 4.19 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$, $r = 2260 \cdot 1000\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} = 2.26 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$ and $\Delta T = 100\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 18\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 82\,\text{K}$.
**Substitution:** $Q = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}} \cdot 1.2\,\text{kg} \cdot 82\,\text{K} + 2.26 \cdot 10^6\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}} \cdot 1.2\,\text{kg} = 412\,296\,\text{J} + 2\,712\,000\,\text{J} = 3\,124\,296\,\text{J}$
**Answer:** $Q \approx 3.1\,\text{MJ}$, two significant figures like the amount of water $1.2\,\text{l}$.
**Reasonableness:** The evaporation takes almost $90\,\%$ of the energy, which fits the fact that the latent heat of vaporisation is over five times the energy needed to heat the water to its boiling point. Without heating from the stove the stones would cool by $\tfrac{3.12\,\text{MJ}}{25\,\text{kg} \cdot 800\,\text{J/(kg K)}} \approx 160\,\text{K}$; the stove produces the same energy in $\tfrac{3.12\,\text{MJ}}{6000\,\text{W}} \approx 9\,\text{min}$, so the stove keeps the stones hot when water is thrown on them now and then.

### e)
**Find:** why the skin feels as if it were burning after water is thrown on the stove, although the air temperature hardly rises
**Principle:** Steam condenses when it meets a surface cooler than itself. Condensation releases the same energy that was needed for the evaporation.
**Model answer:** The water vapour formed on the stove spreads into the air of the sauna and meets the skin, whose temperature is only about $35$–$40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. The vapour condenses into water on the skin, and for every kilogram that condenses about $2.3\,\text{MJ}$ of energy is released and transferred to the skin. The skin therefore receives a lot of energy in a short time, although the air temperature in the sauna hardly changes. In addition, moist skin does not cool by sweating, because sweat does not evaporate as well into humid air. That is why the steam feels burning.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a the final temperature $280\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, or $553\,\text{K}$ in kelvins, is taken as the change in temperature, although the formula needs the change $\Delta T = 265\,\text{K}$.
- In part b the time is left in minutes, which makes the energy $300\,\text{kJ}$ and the fraction over $100\,\%$.
- In part c the claim is accepted because “the rest of the energy is wasted”, although the air and the walls also warm up.
- In part d the warming of the water to its boiling point or the evaporation is forgotten; the warming alone gives only $0.41\,\text{MJ}$.
- In part e it is explained that the steam is “hotter than the air”, without mentioning condensation and the energy it releases.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part c an answer stating that efficiency depends on what is defined as useful, and that the fraction of energy stored in the stones is a different thing from the efficiency of the stove.
- In part d the answer $3.12\,\text{MJ}$ when the precision is justified with the heat capacity of the water; the full precision point requires the justification.

### a)
**Find:** the free-body diagram of the box during braking, when the box stays at rest relative to the bed
**Principle:** Only the interactions acting on the box are drawn in the free-body diagram: the Earth (weight) and the bed (normal force and friction). The box slows down together with the trailer, so its acceleration points backwards, and by Newton's second law the net force must point in the same direction.
**Model answer:** The forces on the box are the weight $\overline{G}$ down, the normal force $\overline{N}$ of the bed up and the static friction $\overline{F}_{\mu 0}$ of the bed horizontally backwards, that is against the direction of travel of the car. The box does not accelerate vertically, so $N = G$. The only horizontal force is static friction, and it must point backwards, because it slows the box down as much as the brakes slow down the trailer. There is no forward “force of motion”: the box would continue moving forwards if there were no friction.

### b)
**Given:** coefficient of static friction $\mu_0 = 0.45$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the greatest deceleration $a_\text{max}$ at which the box does not slide
**Principle:** The box stays at rest relative to the bed if it has the same deceleration as the trailer. That deceleration is given by the only horizontal force, static friction. Static friction can be at most $\mu_0 N$, and on a horizontal bed $N = mg$.
**Formula:** $F_{\mu 0} = ma$ and $F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$, so $ma \le \mu_0 mg$, that is $a_\text{max} = \mu_0 g$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $a_\text{max} = 0.45 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 4.4145\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Answer:** $a_\text{max} \approx 4.4\,\text{m/s}^2$, two significant figures like the coefficient of friction.
**Reasonableness:** The deceleration is a little less than half the free-fall acceleration, which is gentle braking. In emergency braking on dry asphalt the deceleration is about $7$–$9\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the box would then slide.

### c)
**Given:** initial speed $v_0 = 80\,\text{km/h}$, final speed $0$, deceleration at most $a_\text{max} = 4.4145\,\text{m/s}^2$ (part b)
**Find:** the shortest braking distance $s$
**Principle:** The braking is uniformly decelerated motion. The smaller the deceleration, the longer the distance, so the shortest allowed distance is obtained with the greatest allowed deceleration.
**Formula:** $v^2 = v_0^2 - 2as$ and $v = 0$, so $s = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a_\text{max}}$
**Conversions:** $v_0 = 80\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{80 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 22.22\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $s = \dfrac{(22.22\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 4.4145\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{493.8\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{8.829\,\text{m/s}^2} = 55.93\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $s \approx 56\,\text{m}$, two significant figures like the coefficient of friction and the speed.
**Reasonableness:** The braking lasts $\tfrac{22.2\,\text{m/s}}{4.41\,\text{m/s}^2} \approx 5.0\,\text{s}$, and at the average speed $11.1\,\text{m/s}$ this corresponds to $56\,\text{m}$. In emergency braking with a deceleration of $7\,\text{m/s}^2$ the distance would be only about $35\,\text{m}$.

### d)
**Find:** whether increasing the mass of the box helps to keep the box in place
**Principle:** The maximum static friction is $\mu_0 N = \mu_0 mg$, and the force $ma$ is needed to slow the box down. Both are proportional to the mass.
**Model answer:** A heavier box presses on the bed harder, so the maximum possible static friction $\mu_0 mg$ increases. But a heavier box also needs a greater force $ma$ to slow it down, and this increases in the same proportion. The mass cancels from the condition $ma \le \mu_0 mg$, so the greatest allowed deceleration $a_\text{max} = \mu_0 g$ does not depend on the mass. The suggestion therefore does not help. Methods that work are tying the box to the bed with straps, or increasing the coefficient of friction, for example with a rubber non-slip mat under the box.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- A forward force that “carries the box forwards” is drawn in the free-body diagram, or the friction is drawn forwards.
- In part b one tries to calculate or guess the mass, although it cancels from the equation.
- In part c the speed is left in $\text{km/h}$, which makes the distance $725$ with no sensible unit.
- In part c the formula $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ is used without calculating the time.
- In part d it is only stated that “a heavy box stays put better”, without justifying the ratio of the forces.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a a free-body diagram without the acceleration arrow, when the text gives the direction of the acceleration.
- In part c a solution via the time: $t = \tfrac{v_0}{a}$ and $s = v_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$.

## fy9-harjoituskoe-s3

### a)
**Given:** length of the open pipe $L = 1.31\,\text{m}$, speed of sound at $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ $v = 343.2\,\text{m/s}$
**Find:** the fundamental frequency $f_1$
**Principle:** A standing wave of the air column forms in the pipe. An open pipe has a displacement antinode at both ends, and in the fundamental mode there is one node between them, so the length of the pipe is half a wavelength. The frequency follows from the wave equation $v = f\lambda$.
**Formula:** $\lambda_1 = 2L$ and $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{\lambda_1} = \dfrac{v}{2L}$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $f_1 = \dfrac{343.2\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 1.31\,\text{m}} = 130.99\,\text{Hz}$
**Answer:** $f_1 \approx 131\,\text{Hz}$, three significant figures like the length of the pipe.
**Reasonableness:** Humans hear from about $20\,\text{Hz}$ to $20\,\text{kHz}$, and $131\,\text{Hz}$ is a low note, as can be expected from a pipe about a metre long.

### b)
**Given:** $L = 1.31\,\text{m}$, speed of sound at $8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ $v' = 336.1\,\text{m/s}$, $f_1 = 130.99\,\text{Hz}$ (part a)
**Find:** the fundamental frequency $f_1'$ in the cold church and its relative change
**Principle:** The length of the pipe changes so little that the wavelength of the standing wave stays the same (this is checked in part c). The frequency is therefore proportional to the speed of sound, which decreases as the air cools.
**Formula:** $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{2L}$ and $f_1' = \dfrac{v'}{2L}$. The relative change is $\varepsilon = \dfrac{f_1' - f_1}{f_1} = \dfrac{v'/(2L)}{v/(2L)} - 1 = \dfrac{v'}{v} - 1$, since $L$ cancels
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $f_1' = \dfrac{336.1\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 1.31\,\text{m}} = 128.28\,\text{Hz}$ and $\varepsilon = \dfrac{336.1\,\text{m/s}}{343.2\,\text{m/s}} - 1 = -0.0207$
**Answer:** $f_1' \approx 128\,\text{Hz}$, and the frequency decreases by about $2.1\,\%$. Three significant figures like the length.
**Reasonableness:** The change is the same as the relative change in the speed of sound, $\tfrac{336.1 - 343.2}{343.2} = -2.1\,\%$. It is an audible difference, because a semitone is about $6\,\%$.

### c)
**Given:** $L_0 = 1.31\,\text{m}$, coefficient of linear thermal expansion $\alpha = 2.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}}$, the temperature falls from $20\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$
**Find:** the change in the length of the pipe $\Delta L$ and its effect on the frequency compared with part b
**Principle:** A solid metal contracts as it cools, and the change in length is proportional to the original length and to the change in temperature. The fundamental frequency is inversely proportional to the length, so a shortening pipe raises the frequency by the same relative amount.
**Formula:** $\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T$. The frequency is $f = \dfrac{v}{2(L_0 + \Delta L)}$, so $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f_0} = \dfrac{L_0}{L_0 + \Delta L} - 1 \approx -\dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0} = -\alpha \Delta T$ when $|\Delta L| \ll L_0$
**Conversions:** $\Delta T = 8\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 20\,{}^\circ\text{C} = -12\,\text{K}$ and finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.
**Substitution:** $\Delta L = 2.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 1.31\,\text{m} \cdot (-12\,\text{K}) = -3.62 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m} = -0.362\,\text{mm}$ and $\dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0} = \dfrac{-3.62 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{m}}{1.31\,\text{m}} = -2.8 \cdot 10^{-4} = -0.028\,\%$ and $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f_0} \approx -\alpha \Delta T = -2.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot (-12\,\text{K}) = +2.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$
**Answer:** The pipe shortens by about $0.36\,\text{mm}$ (two significant figures like the coefficient of thermal expansion). It would raise the frequency by only about $0.03\,\%$, while the cooling of the air lowers it by about $2.1\,\%$, that is about $75$ times more. The organ sounds lower because of the air, not because the metal contracts.
**Reasonableness:** Everyday experience shows that the thermal expansion of metals is small: a rod a metre long changes by only tenths of a millimetre when the temperature changes by ten degrees.

### d)
**Find:** how the note of the stopped pipe differs from the note of the open pipe
**Principle:** The nodes and antinodes of the standing wave are set by the ends of the pipe: at a closed end the air cannot move, so there is a node there, and at an open end there is an antinode.
**Model answer:** The stopped pipe has a node at its closed end and an antinode at its open end, so in the fundamental mode the length of the pipe is only a quarter of a wavelength: $\lambda_1 = 4L$. The fundamental frequency is $f_1 = \tfrac{v}{4L} = \tfrac{343.2\,\text{m/s}}{4 \cdot 1.31\,\text{m}} \approx 65.5\,\text{Hz}$, half the fundamental frequency of the open pipe, so the note is an octave lower. In addition, a stopped pipe can produce only the odd multiples of the fundamental frequency, $3f_1, 5f_1, \ldots$ ($197\,\text{Hz}$, $328\,\text{Hz}$, …), because there must always be a node at the closed end. An open pipe produces all the multiples. That is why the timbre of the stopped pipe is different: softer and more hollow-sounding.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a a quarter wavelength is used for the open pipe, which makes the frequency $65.5\,\text{Hz}$.
- In part b it is assumed that the speed of sound does not depend on the temperature, or the new frequency is calculated through thermal expansion.
- In part c the change in temperature is converted to kelvins by adding $273$, which makes the change ($261\,\text{K}$ instead of $12\,\text{K}$) and the result about $20$ times too large.
- In part c it is forgotten that a shortening pipe raises the frequency rather than lowering it.
- In part d it is only stated that “the note gets lower”, without saying how much and why.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b the relative change directly from the ratio of the speeds of sound, $\tfrac{336.1}{343.2} = 0.979$.
- In part d a drawing showing the nodes and antinodes instead of a written justification.

## fy9-harjoituskoe-s4

### a)
**Given:** length of the cord $l = 25.0\,\text{m}$, two conductors, cross-sectional area $A = 1.5\,\text{mm}^2$, resistivity $\rho = 1.68 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega \cdot \text{m}$
**Find:** the resistance of the extension cord $R_\text{j}$
**Principle:** The resistance of a conductor is proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. The current flows to the appliance along one conductor and returns along the other, so the conductor the current passes through is twice the length of the cord.
**Formula:** $R_\text{j} = \rho \dfrac{2l}{A}$
**Conversions:** $A = 1.5\,\text{mm}^2 = 1.5 \cdot (10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2 = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$ and $2l = 2 \cdot 25.0\,\text{m} = 50.0\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $R_\text{j} = 1.68 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega \cdot \text{m} \cdot \dfrac{50.0\,\text{m}}{1.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2} = 0.560\,\Omega$
**Answer:** $R_\text{j} \approx 0.56\,\Omega$, two significant figures like the cross-sectional area.
**Reasonableness:** The resistance is small compared with the resistances of the appliances (tens of ohms), as expected of a cord, but it is not zero.

### b)
**Given:** $U = 230\,\text{V}$, grill $P_1 = 1.6\,\text{kW}$ and kettle $P_2 = 1.8\,\text{kW}$ at a voltage of $230\,\text{V}$, $R_\text{j} = 0.560\,\Omega$ (part a)
**Find:** the current in the cord $I$
**Principle:** The appliances are in parallel at the end of the cord, and their combined resistance is in series with the resistance of the cord. The resistances of the appliances are obtained from their rated powers, because they are assumed constant. The current follows from Ohm's law for the whole circuit.
**Formula:** $R = \dfrac{U^2}{P}$, $\dfrac{1}{R_\text{l}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} = \dfrac{P_1 + P_2}{U^2}$, so $R_\text{l} = \dfrac{U^2}{P_1 + P_2}$ and $I = \dfrac{U}{R_\text{j} + R_\text{l}}$
**Conversions:** $P_1 + P_2 = 1.6\,\text{kW} + 1.8\,\text{kW} = 3.4 \cdot 1000\,\text{W} = 3400\,\text{W}$
**Substitution:** $R_\text{l} = \dfrac{(230\,\text{V})^2}{3400\,\text{W}} = 15.56\,\Omega$ and $I = \dfrac{230\,\text{V}}{0.560\,\Omega + 15.56\,\Omega} = \dfrac{230\,\text{V}}{16.12\,\Omega} = 14.27\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $I \approx 14\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the powers and the cross-sectional area.
**Reasonableness:** Without the cord the current would be $\tfrac{3400\,\text{W}}{230\,\text{V}} = 14.8\,\text{A}$; the cord reduces it a little, as it should. The current is below the limit of an ordinary $16\,\text{A}$ fuse.

### c)
**Given:** $R_\text{j} = 0.560\,\Omega$, $I = 14.27\,\text{A}$ (parts a and b)
**Find:** the power $P_\text{j}$ converted into heat in the cord
**Principle:** The current through the resistance of the cord converts electrical energy into internal energy of the conductor. The power of the cord follows from its own resistance and the current through it.
**Formula:** $P_\text{j} = U_\text{j} I$ and $U_\text{j} = R_\text{j} I$ (Ohm's law), so $P_\text{j} = R_\text{j} I^2$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $P_\text{j} = 0.560\,\Omega \cdot (14.27\,\text{A})^2 = 114.0\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $P_\text{j} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^2\,\text{W}$, that is about $110\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like parts a and b.
**Reasonableness:** The voltage drops by $0.560\,\Omega \cdot 14.27\,\text{A} = 8.0\,\text{V}$ in the cord, so $222\,\text{V}$ is left for the appliances. The power of the cord is about $3\,\%$ of the power of the appliances, but it is spread over the whole cord and heats it noticeably.

### d)
**Given:** cross-sectional area $A' = 2.5\,\text{mm}^2$, otherwise as above; $R_\text{l} = 15.56\,\Omega$
**Find:** the heating power of the cord $P_\text{j}'$
**Principle:** The resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area, so a thicker cord has a smaller resistance. The current is calculated again in the same way as in part b.
**Formula:** $R_\text{j}' = R_\text{j} \dfrac{A}{A'}$, $I' = \dfrac{U}{R_\text{j}' + R_\text{l}}$ and $P_\text{j}' = R_\text{j}' I'^2$
**Conversions:** None needed: the ratio of the areas is the same in square millimetres.
**Substitution:** $R_\text{j}' = 0.560\,\Omega \cdot \dfrac{1.5\,\text{mm}^2}{2.5\,\text{mm}^2} = 0.336\,\Omega$, $I' = \dfrac{230\,\text{V}}{0.336\,\Omega + 15.56\,\Omega} = 14.47\,\text{A}$ and $P_\text{j}' = 0.336\,\Omega \cdot (14.47\,\text{A})^2 = 70.35\,\text{W}$
**Answer:** $P_\text{j}' \approx 70\,\text{W}$, two significant figures like the cross-sectional area. The heating power decreases by about $40\,\%$.
**Reasonableness:** The current is almost the same, so the heating power decreases almost in the same ratio as the resistance, $\tfrac{1.5}{2.5} = 0.60$. That is why thicker cords are used for large loads.

### e)
**Find:** why a cord on a reel heats up more and why that is dangerous
**Principle:** The temperature of the cord rises until it transfers as much power to the surroundings as is produced in it. The transfer of heat depends on how much of the surface is freely in contact with the air.
**Model answer:** The same heating power, about $110\,\text{W}$, is produced in the cord whether it is on the reel or spread out. A cord that is spread out gives off heat to the air from its whole surface, so it warms only a little. On a reel the turns lie tightly on top of each other: they heat each other, and only the outer surface of the reel gives off heat to the air. The temperature therefore rises much higher before the power given off equals the power produced. The resistance of copper increases as the temperature rises, which adds a little to the heating. Hot insulation can soften and melt, which causes a short circuit or a fire. That is why an extension cord is unwound completely when a large load is connected to it.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a only the length of one conductor, $25.0\,\text{m}$, is used, which halves the resistance.
- In part a square millimetres are converted with the factor $10^{-3}$ instead of $10^{-6}$.
- In part b the resistances of the appliances are added as in a series connection.
- In part c the power of the cord is calculated with the whole mains voltage, $P = \tfrac{U^2}{R_\text{j}}$, which gives tens of kilowatts.
- In part e it is explained that on the reel “the current travels a longer distance”, although the length of the cord does not change.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In parts b and c an approximate solution in which the current is calculated from the rated powers, $I \approx \tfrac{P_1 + P_2}{U} = 14.8\,\text{A}$, and the power of the cord comes out at about $120\,\text{W}$, when the assumption is stated.
- In part d an answer through the ratio of the resistances alone: $0.60 \cdot 114\,\text{W} \approx 68\,\text{W}$, when it is justified that the current changes little.

## fy9-harjoituskoe-s5

### a)
**Given:** refractive index of water $n_1 = 1.33$, refractive index of air $n_2 = 1.00$
**Find:** the critical angle $\alpha_\text{r}$
**Principle:** When light passes from water into air, it is refracted away from the normal of the surface. At the critical angle the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$: the ray travels along the surface. Snell's law holds at the critical angle too.
**Formula:** $n_1 \sin\alpha_\text{r} = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$, so $\sin\alpha_\text{r} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $\sin\alpha_\text{r} = \dfrac{1.00}{1.33} = 0.7519$, so $\alpha_\text{r} = 48.75^\circ$
**Answer:** $\alpha_\text{r} \approx 48.8^\circ$, three significant figures like the refractive index.
**Reasonableness:** The critical angle is below $90^\circ$, and it is the larger the closer the refractive indices are to each other. The refractive indices of water and air differ less than those of glass and air, so the critical angle of water is larger than that of glass, about $42^\circ$.

### b)
**Given:** depth $h = 2.40\,\text{m}$, $\alpha_\text{r} = 48.75^\circ$ (part a)
**Find:** the radius $r$ of the bright area
**Principle:** Rays leave the light in all directions. Rays that meet the surface at an angle of incidence smaller than the critical angle get out into the air; at a larger angle they are totally reflected. At the edge of the bright area the angle of incidence is exactly the critical angle, and the angle of incidence is measured from the normal of the surface, that is from the vertical.
**Formula:** From the right-angled triangle $\tan\alpha_\text{r} = \dfrac{r}{h}$, so $r = h \tan\alpha_\text{r}$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $r = 2.40\,\text{m} \cdot \tan 48.75^\circ = 2.40\,\text{m} \cdot 1.1404 = 2.737\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $r \approx 2.74\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the depth and the refractive index.
**Reasonableness:** Because the critical angle is a little over $45^\circ$, the radius must be a little greater than the depth, as it is. The diameter of the bright area is about $5.5\,\text{m}$, which fits a diving pool.

### c)
**Find:** what the diver sees on the water surface outside the bright area, and why
**Principle:** When light in water meets the surface at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, no refracted ray forms, and all the light is reflected back into the water.
**Model answer:** Outside the bright area the diver sees the water surface as a mirror. Light from the bottom and walls of the pool reaches the surface there at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle $48.8^\circ$, so it is totally reflected back into the water. The diver sees in the surface mirror images of the bottom of the pool, the walls and other swimmers. Nor can light from the air reach the diver's eye from these directions, so she does not see the ceiling of the hall outside the bright area.

### d)
**Given:** depth $h = 2.40\,\text{m}$, diameter of the window $d = 25.0\,\text{cm}$, density of water $\rho = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the net force $F$ on the window
**Principle:** On the outside the atmospheric pressure and the hydrostatic pressure of the water act on the window, on the inside the atmospheric pressure. The atmospheric pressure cancels, so the net force is caused by the hydrostatic pressure. Pressure is force per unit area.
**Formula:** $p_\text{h} = \rho g h$ and $F = p_\text{h} A = \rho g h \pi\left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2$
**Conversions:** $d = 25.0\,\text{cm} = 25.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.250\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $p_\text{h} = 1000\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 2.40\,\text{m} = 23\,544\,\text{Pa}$, $A = \pi \cdot (0.125\,\text{m})^2 = 0.04909\,\text{m}^2$ and $F = 23\,544\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.04909\,\text{m}^2 = 1156\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $F \approx 1.16\,\text{kN}$, three significant figures like the depth and the diameter.
**Reasonableness:** The force equals the weight of a mass of about $120\,\text{kg}$. The window must therefore be made of thick glass, which fits pool lights.

### e)
**Find:** why the light, seen from the edge, seems to be shallower than it is
**Principle:** Light is refracted away from the normal of the surface as it passes from water into air. The eye and the brain assume that the light has travelled in a straight line.
**Model answer:** The rays from the light are refracted away from the normal at the water surface, so their direction becomes less steep. When a refracted ray reaches the eye, the eye extends it straight backwards, and the extensions meet above the actual position of the light. That is why the light and the bottom of the pool seem to be nearer the surface than at a depth of $2.40\,\text{m}$.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a the refractive indices are swapped, which makes the value of the sine greater than one.
- In part b the sine function is used, or the tangent the wrong way round, which makes the radius $1.80\,\text{m}$ or $2.10\,\text{m}$.
- In part b the angle of incidence is measured from the water surface instead of the normal.
- In part d the force is calculated with the whole pressure $p_0 + \rho g h$, although there is atmospheric pressure inside.
- In part d the diameter is used as the radius, which makes the area four times too large.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part c an answer that gives total internal reflection and says that the surface looks silvery or like a mirror.
- In part e a ray diagram supporting the written explanation.

## fy9-harjoituskoe-s6

### a)
**Find:** the equation of the beta decay of carbon-14 and what happens in the nucleus
**Principle:** In beta-minus decay a neutron of the nucleus changes into a proton, and the nucleus emits an electron and an antineutrino. The mass number and the charge number are conserved in the reaction.
**Model answer:** $^{14}_{6}\text{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{\phantom{-}0}_{-1}\text{e} + \overline{\nu}_\text{e}$. Mass numbers: $14 = 14 + 0$, and charge numbers: $6 = 7 + (-1)$. The nucleus of carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons. In the decay one neutron changes into a proton, so the daughter nucleus has seven protons and seven neutrons: it is nitrogen-14. The nucleus emits an electron, that is a beta particle, and an antineutrino.

### b)
**Given:** activity now $A = 0.198\,\tfrac{\text{Bq}}{\text{g}}$, activity of living wood $A_0 = 0.226\,\tfrac{\text{Bq}}{\text{g}}$, half-life $T_{1/2} = 5730\,\text{a}$
**Find:** the age of the canoe $t$
**Principle:** When the tree is felled, carbon-14 only decays and no new carbon-14 comes in. The activity is proportional to the number of nuclei, so by the law of radioactive decay it halves in every half-life. It is assumed that the carbon-14 content of the air was the same while the tree was growing as it is now.
**Formula:** $A = A_0 \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$, from which with a logarithm $\ln\dfrac{A_0}{A} = \dfrac{t}{T_{1/2}} \ln 2$, that is $t = T_{1/2} \cdot \dfrac{\ln(A_0 / A)}{\ln 2}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the activities are in the same unit, and the time comes out in years like the half-life.
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{A_0}{A} = \dfrac{0.226}{0.198} = 1.1414$ and $t = 5730\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{\ln 1.1414}{\ln 2} = 5730\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{0.13226}{0.69315} = 1093\,\text{a}$
**Answer:** $t \approx 1.09 \cdot 10^3$ years, that is about $1100$ years, three significant figures like the activities.
**Reasonableness:** The activity has decreased by only about $12\,\%$, which is much less than half, so the age must be clearly less than one half-life. A thousand-year-old canoe fits the end of the Iron Age, when dugout canoes were in common use.

### c)
**Given:** $A = (0.198 \pm 0.004)\,\tfrac{\text{Bq}}{\text{g}}$, $A_0 = 0.226\,\tfrac{\text{Bq}}{\text{g}}$, $T_{1/2} = 5730\,\text{a}$
**Find:** the uncertainty of the age $\Delta t$
**Principle:** The age is calculated with the largest and the smallest value of the activity. A larger activity means a younger sample. The uncertainty of the age is half the difference between the extreme values.
**Formula:** $t = T_{1/2} \cdot \dfrac{\ln(A_0 / A)}{\ln 2}$ and $\Delta t = \dfrac{t_\text{oldest} - t_\text{youngest}}{2}$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $t_\text{youngest} = 5730\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{\ln(0.226 / 0.202)}{\ln 2} = 928\,\text{a}$, $t_\text{oldest} = 5730\,\text{a} \cdot \dfrac{\ln(0.226 / 0.194)}{\ln 2} = 1262\,\text{a}$ and $\Delta t = \dfrac{1262\,\text{a} - 928\,\text{a}}{2} = 167\,\text{a}$
**Answer:** $\Delta t \approx 170$ years, so the age is $(1.09 \pm 0.17) \cdot 10^3$ years. The uncertainty is given to two significant figures, and the age to the same precision.
**Reasonableness:** The relative uncertainty of the activity is $\tfrac{0.004}{0.198} \approx 2\,\%$, but the relative uncertainty of the age is about $15\,\%$, because the activity changes only a little in a thousand years. That is why even a small measurement uncertainty shows clearly in the age.

### d)
**Find:** why the method is not suitable for wood that is $200\,000$ years old, nor for wood that is ten years old
**Principle:** The method can date a sample only if the change in activity is clearly greater than the measurement uncertainty and the remaining activity can be distinguished from the background radiation.
**Model answer:** In $200\,000$ years $\tfrac{200\,000\,\text{a}}{5730\,\text{a}} \approx 35$ half-lives pass, and $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{35} \approx 3 \cdot 10^{-11}$ of the original carbon-14 nuclei remain. The activity of the sample is so small that it cannot be distinguished from the background radiation, so the age cannot be determined. In ten years the activity decreases by only $1 - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{10/5730} \approx 0.1\,\%$, while the uncertainty of the measurement is about $2\,\%$. The change is lost in the uncertainty. In addition, the nuclear weapons tests of the 1950s and 1960s increased the carbon-14 content of the air, so the assumption of a constant initial activity does not hold for young samples. The method works when the age is from hundreds of years to a few tens of thousands of years.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a the daughter nucleus is written as boron, or the mass number decreases, although the mass number is conserved in beta decay.
- In part b the ratio of the activities is inverted, which makes the age negative.
- In part b a linear model is used, for example “the activity decreases by $12\,\%$, so the age is $12\,\%$ of the half-life”.
- In part c the uncertainty is given as $2\,\%$ of the age, the uncertainty of the activity, that is about $20$ years.
- In part d it is said that in the old sample “all the carbon-14 has decayed”, without justifying it with a numerical value or the background radiation.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part b a solution with the decay constant: $\lambda = \tfrac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}$ and $t = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\ln\tfrac{A_0}{A}$.
- In part c the uncertainty given as the range $930$–$1260$ years.

## fy9-harjoituskoe-s7

### a)
**Find:** a plan of the experiment: research question, variables, constants, setting and measuring the temperature, measuring the bounce height and processing the results
**Principle:** In a good experiment one variable is changed and the others are kept constant, the dependent variable is measured with repeats, and the results are presented as a graph. The ball exchanges heat with its surroundings, so its temperature must be measured just before the drop.
**Model answer:** Research question: how does the bounce height of a squash ball depend on the temperature of the ball when the drop height stays the same? The independent variable is the temperature of the ball and the dependent variable the bounce height $h_2$ or the ratio $\tfrac{h_2}{h_1}$. Kept constant are the drop height $h_1 = 1.50\,\text{m}$, the same ball, the same spot on the concrete floor and a drop from rest without spin.

The ball is cooled in the freezer and warmed in the water bath to different temperatures, for example about $0$, $10$, $20$, $30$, $40$ and $50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and it is left at each temperature for a few minutes so that it warms or cools right through. The ball is dried with a paper towel, its surface temperature is measured with the infrared thermometer and it is dropped at once, because the ball starts to approach room temperature.

The drop is filmed with the phone on the stand so that the tape measure is vertical behind the ball and the camera is roughly at the level of the bounce height. The bounce height is read from the video in the frame where the bottom of the ball is highest. At each temperature at least three drops are made, and their mean and range are recorded. The results are presented as a graph of bounce height as a function of temperature, and the conclusion is drawn from the shape of the graph, for example whether the bounce height increases steadily over the whole range.

### b)
**Given:** drop height $h_1 = 1.50\,\text{m}$, bounce height $h_2 = 0.36\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the fraction of the mechanical energy converted into other forms of energy
**Principle:** At the highest points the ball is momentarily at rest, so its mechanical energy is potential energy only. Air resistance is small over such a short distance, so energy is converted into other forms mainly in the bounce, when the ball and the floor deform and warm up.
**Formula:** $E_1 = mgh_1$ and $E_2 = mgh_2$, so fraction $= \dfrac{E_1 - E_2}{E_1} = 1 - \dfrac{h_2}{h_1}$; the mass cancels
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** fraction $= 1 - \dfrac{0.36\,\text{m}}{1.50\,\text{m}} = 1 - 0.24 = 0.76$
**Answer:** About $76\,\%$ of the mechanical energy is converted into other forms of energy in the bounce, two significant figures like the bounce height.
**Reasonableness:** A squash ball is known to bounce poorly, so a large fraction fits. A tennis ball dropped from the same height bounces to over half the height, so the fraction would be below $50\,\%$.

### c)
**Given:** bounce height $h_2 = 0.36\,\text{m}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the speed $v$ just after the bounce
**Principle:** After the bounce the ball rises to its highest point, and air resistance is small, so mechanical energy is conserved during the rise: kinetic energy becomes potential energy.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = mgh_2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh_2}$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 0.36\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{7.063\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 2.658\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 2.7\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures like the bounce height.
**Reasonableness:** Just before the impact the speed was $\sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.50\,\text{m}} = 5.4\,\text{m/s}$. The bounce halves the speed, and $\left(\tfrac{2.66}{5.42}\right)^2 = 0.24$, that is $24\,\%$, of the kinetic energy remains, which agrees with part b.

### d)
**Find:** the precision with which the bounce height can be read from the video, and two ways to improve the measurement
**Principle:** At the highest point the velocity of the ball is zero, so its position changes little between frames. The position of the camera and the scale decides whether the height is read correctly.
**Model answer:** The interval between frames is $\tfrac{1}{30}\,\text{s}$. Near the highest point the ball moves slowly: $\tfrac{1}{30}\,\text{s}$ after the highest point it has fallen only $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot \left(\tfrac{1}{30}\,\text{s}\right)^2 \approx 5\,\text{mm}$, so the highest frame shows the peak to within a few millimetres. The reading is, however, made less precise by the blurred edge of the ball and by the ball being in front of the tape measure. Overall the bounce height is obtained to a precision of about $1\,\text{cm}$. The largest source of error is parallax: if the camera is above or below the peak, the ball appears to be at the wrong reading. The measurement is improved by placing the camera at the height of the peak and further away, by putting the tape measure right behind the path of the ball, and by repeating the drops and using the mean.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a several variables are changed at the same time, for example the drop height and the temperature.
- In part a the temperature is measured from the water bath and not from the ball just before the drop.
- In part b the fraction is given as $24\,\%$, which is the fraction that remains.
- In part c the speed is calculated from the drop height $1.50\,\text{m}$, which gives the speed before the bounce.
- In part d it is only stated that “the video is imprecise”, without estimating the precision as a number.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a setting the temperature in a warming cabinet or in cold outdoor air, when the temperature is measured from the ball.
- In part d a light gate or a higher frame rate as an improvement.

## fy9-harjoituskoe-s8

### a)
**Find:** why the stack of filters finally falls at a constant speed
**Principle:** Newton's second law: the acceleration is the net force divided by the mass. Air resistance increases as the speed increases, while the weight stays the same.
**Model answer:** The forces on the stack are the weight $\overline{G}$ downwards and the air resistance $\overline{F}_\text{v}$ upwards. Just after the release the speed is small, so the air resistance is small and the net force points down: the stack accelerates downwards and its speed increases. As the speed increases, the air resistance increases, so the net force and the acceleration decrease. Finally the air resistance is equal to the weight. Then the net force is zero, the acceleration is zero and, by Newton's first law, the stack continues at a steady speed, the terminal speed.

### b)
**Given:** $N = 4$ filters, mass of one filter $m = 1.20\,\text{g}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the air resistance $F_\text{v}$ at constant speed
**Principle:** At constant speed the acceleration is zero, so by Newton's second law the net force is zero and the air resistance is equal to the weight of the stack.
**Formula:** $F_\text{v} = G = Nmg$
**Conversions:** $m = 1.20\,\text{g} = 1.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$ and finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.
**Substitution:** $F_\text{v} = 4 \cdot 1.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 0.04709\,\text{N} = 47.09\,\text{mN}$
**Answer:** $F_\text{v} \approx 47.1\,\text{mN}$, three significant figures like the mass of a filter.
**Reasonableness:** The mass of four filters is $4.8\,\text{g}$, and the weight of a $5\,\text{g}$ object is about $0.05\,\text{N}$, so the result is of the right order of magnitude.

### d)
**Given:** the graph of part c and the line $F_\text{v} = kv^2$ through the origin
**Find:** the proportionality constant $k$
**Principle:** In the model $F_\text{v} = kv^2$ the constant $k$ is the slope of the line of the $(v^2, F_\text{v})$ graph. It is read from the fitted line at two points far apart, not from the measured points.
**Reading the graph:** The points $(0;\ 0)$ and $(6.00\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2;\ 64.2\,\text{mN})$ are read from the line.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{\Delta F_\text{v}}{\Delta (v^2)}$
**Conversions:** $64.2\,\text{mN} = 64.2 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N} = 0.0642\,\text{N}$
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{0.0642\,\text{N} - 0}{6.00\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 - 0} = 0.01070\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}^2}$
**Answer:** $k \approx 0.0107\,\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}^2} = 0.0107\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}}$, three significant figures like the measurements. Unit: $\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}^2} = \tfrac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}/\text{s}^2 \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}^2} = \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}}$.
**Reasonableness:** According to the model, the terminal speed of one filter is $\sqrt{\tfrac{1.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.0107\,\text{kg/m}}} = 1.05\,\text{m/s}$, which agrees with the measurement $1.06\,\text{m/s}$.

### e)
**Given:** $N = 10$, $m = 1.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$, $k = 0.01070\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}}$ (part d), drop height $2.5\,\text{m}$
**Find:** the constant speed $v_{10}$ of a stack of ten filters and the reliability of the prediction
**Principle:** At constant speed the air resistance is equal to the weight, and according to the model of part d the air resistance is $kv^2$. The shape of the stack does not change, so the same $k$ holds.
**Formula:** $kv_{10}^2 = Nmg$, so $v_{10} = \sqrt{\dfrac{Nmg}{k}}$
**Conversions:** None needed.
**Substitution:** $v_{10} = \sqrt{\dfrac{10 \cdot 1.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.01070\,\text{kg/m}}} = \sqrt{11.00\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2} = 3.317\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v_{10} \approx 3.3\,\text{m/s}$, two significant figures, because the prediction is an extrapolation and its uncertainty is larger than that of the measurements. The prediction lies outside the measured range ($v \le 2.54\,\text{m/s}$), so it assumes that the model holds at higher speeds too. In the same setup a heavier stack needs a longer distance to reach its terminal speed, and a drop of $2.5\,\text{m}$ may not be enough: the measured speed could be smaller than predicted.
**Reasonableness:** The speed increases with the square root of the mass: $\sqrt{\tfrac{10}{6}} \cdot 2.54\,\text{m/s} \approx 3.3\,\text{m/s}$, which agrees with the result.

- In part b the mass is left in grams, which makes the air resistance $47\,\text{N}$.
- In part c the points are joined with a broken line, or a line not passing through the origin is fitted, and the model is chosen from the picture alone.
- In part d the slope is calculated from two measured points or from the $(v, F_\text{v})$ graph.
- In part e the prediction is made by multiplying the speed of six filters by the ratio $\tfrac{10}{6}$, as if the speed were proportional to the mass.

### d)
**Given:** water flow $\tfrac{m}{t} = 30.0\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}$, the temperature of the water rises from $40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ to $90\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, $c = 4190\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{K}}$

### f)
**Find:** how radioactive decay heats the rock
**Principle:** In decay, binding energy of the nucleus is released as kinetic energy of particles and radiation, which is transferred to the surrounding matter.
**Model answer:** When a uranium, thorium or potassium-40 nucleus in the rock decays, energy is released as the energy of the alpha or beta particles produced, of the daughter nucleus and of the gamma radiation. The motion of the particles of the rock increases, that is the internal energy and the temperature of the rock increase. ### c)

**Principle:** Besides the mass, the spring also moves: its lower end moves with the mass and its upper end stays still. **Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{kg} = 1000\,\text{g}$.

**Principle:** At rest the forces on the mass are the spring force up and the weight down, and they are equal. The spring force is proportional to the extension (Hooke's law).
**Formula:** $kx = mg$, so $k = \dfrac{mg}{x}$
**Conversions:** $x = 11.8\,\text{cm} = 11.8 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.118\,\text{m}$
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{0.300\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.118\,\text{m}} = 24.94\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$
**Answer:** $k \approx 24.9\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$, three significant figures like the mass and the extension. The result differs from the value of part b, $25.0\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$, by only about $0.3\,\%$, so the methods support each other.
**Reasonableness:** An uncertainty in reading the extension of, for example, $\pm 2\,\text{mm}$ is about $2\,\%$, which is larger than the difference, so the results agree.

### e)
**Find:** whether the period doubles when the mass is doubled
**Principle:** The periods in the data for masses of which one is twice the other are compared with each other and with the model $T \propto \sqrt{m + m_\text{j}}$.
**Model answer:** In the data the mass doubles, for example, from $0.300\,\text{kg}$ to $0.600\,\text{kg}$, and the period increases from $0.713\,\text{s}$ to $0.990\,\text{s}$, that is only by a factor of $\tfrac{0.990}{0.713} \approx 1.39$. The claim is wrong: the period is proportional to the square root of the oscillating mass, so without the mass of the spring doubling the mass would increase the period by a factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. When the effect of the spring's mass from part c, $m_\text{j} \approx 0.020\,\text{kg}$, is added to both masses, the model gives $\sqrt{0.620 : 0.320} \approx 1.39$, which agrees with the data. The difference is therefore due to the spring's own mass, which does not double.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a $T$ is plotted as a function of the mass and a straight line is fitted to the curved set of points.
- In part a one forgets to divide by ten, which makes $T^2$ a hundred times too large and the spring constant a hundredth.
- In part b the slope itself, or its reciprocal without the factor $4\pi^2$, is taken as the spring constant.
- In part c the deviation from the origin is explained by measurement error alone, although all the points lie consistently on the line.
- In part d the extension is left in centimetres, which makes the spring constant $0.249\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$.
