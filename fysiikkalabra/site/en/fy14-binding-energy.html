Mass defect and binding energy

Why can both joining nuclei and splitting nuclei release energy?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate a mass defect and a binding energy from atomic masses.
  • explain with the binding energy curve why fusion and fission release energy.
  • calculate the energy released in a nuclear reaction.

Prior knowledge

  • A nucleus has $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons, $A = Z + N$, written $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$chapter Building nuclei: isotopes and nuclear notation
  • The strong nuclear force attracts nucleons at short range; the protons repel each otherchapter Building nuclei: isotopes and nuclear notation

Core path: follow the steps in order. Besides calculations, the core path includes investigations, explanations and graphs. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 2.

1Think about it

Heat a cup of tea, $0.25\ \text{kg}$ of water, from $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$. It takes about $63\ \text{kJ}$ of energy. According to Einstein, the hot tea is now heavier than the cold tea, by about $7 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{kg}$. No balance in the world can measure that. In chemistry, mass is always conserved in practice. But in nuclei, the energies are millions of times larger, and the change in mass can be measured.

Where does nuclear energy come from, if mass and energy are two sides of the same thing?

Review: inside helium-4

A helium-4 nucleus is $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. What is it made of?

  • The mass number is the total $A = Z + N = 4$, not the number of neutrons. $N = 4 - 2 = 2$.

  • The upper number $4$ counts all nucleons, protons and neutrons: $Z = 2$, so $N = 4 - 2 = 2$.

  • Right: $Z = 2$ and $N = A - Z = 2$. Today you compare its mass with the mass of these four particles.

Make your predictions before investigating 2 questions

Prediction 1: the mass of helium-4

Compare the mass of a helium-4 nucleus with the total mass of $2$ free protons and $2$ free neutrons. What do you expect?

  • Right: $4.002603\ \text{u}$ against $4.032980\ \text{u}$, a mass defect of $0.0304\ \text{u}$. That mass left as energy, $28.3\ \text{MeV}$, when the nucleus formed.

  • The data show the opposite: the nucleus is lighter by $0.0304\ \text{u}$. Joining the nucleons releases energy, so the bound system has less energy and less mass.

  • In everyday chemistry the change is far too small to measure, but in nuclei it is not: helium-4 is $0.75\ \%$ lighter than its free nucleons. Mass and energy together are conserved, not mass alone.

Prediction 2: fusing iron

Imagine that two iron-56 nuclei could be fused into one nucleus with $A = 112$. Would this reaction release energy?

  • Right. Iron-56 sits at the peak, $8.79\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon; nuclei with $A \approx 112$ have about $8.5\ \text{MeV}$. Going away from the peak costs energy.

  • Only fusion of light nuclei releases energy. Iron is at the peak of the curve: a nucleus with $A = 112$ has a lower $B/A$, so this fusion would need energy.

  • It would not release any: the product would be less tightly bound than iron. Energy is released only when a reaction moves towards the peak of $B/A$.

The free particles and the atom contain the same nucleons and electrons, so any difference in mass belongs to the binding of the nucleus.

$$\Delta m = 2 m_\text{H} + 2 m_\text{n} - m_\text{He}$$

    Review the theory.

    4Theory

    Mass–energy equivalence

    In 1905 Albert Einstein showed that mass and energy are two sides of the same quantity. If a system gives away the energy $E$, its mass decreases by $\Delta m$:

    $$E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$$

    where $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ is the speed of light. Because $c^2$ is so large, a tiny mass corresponds to a huge energy: $1\ \text{kg}$ corresponds to $9.0 \cdot 10^{16}\ \text{J}$.

    In nuclear physics, masses are given in atomic mass units and energies in megaelectronvolts, $1\ \text{MeV} = 10^{6}\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$. The mass $1\ \text{u} = 1.661 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ corresponds to the energy

    $$1\ \text{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$$

    so a mass difference in $\text{u}$ times $931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$ gives the energy in $\text{MeV}$.

    Key idea

    A nucleus has less mass than the free protons and neutrons it is made of. The difference is the mass defect $\Delta m$. When nucleons join, the strong force pulls them together and energy is released, so the system loses mass. To pull the nucleus apart again, the same energy must be given back. This energy is the binding energy $B$:

    $$B = \Delta m \cdot c^2$$

    We calculate with the atomic masses of tables such as AME2020, which include the electrons. So for the protons we use the mass of a hydrogen-1 atom, $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\ \text{u}$, and the electrons cancel:

    $$\Delta m = Z \cdot m_\text{H} + N \cdot m_\text{n} - m_\text{atom}$$

    Here $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\ \text{u}$ is the mass of a neutron.

    Binding energy per nucleon

    To compare nuclei, we divide the binding energy by the number of nucleons. The binding energy per nucleon $B/A$ tells how tightly each nucleon is held on average. A large $B/A$ means a tightly bound nucleus.

    The curve of $B/A$ rises steeply among the light nuclei: $1.11\ \text{MeV}$ for deuterium, $7.07\ \text{MeV}$ for helium-4. It reaches its peak near iron and nickel ($8.79\ \text{MeV}$ for $^{56}\text{Fe}$ and $^{62}\text{Ni}$) and then falls slowly to $7.59\ \text{MeV}$ for $^{235}\text{U}$. In light nuclei, every added nucleon finds more neighbours to bind to. In heavy nuclei, the electric repulsion of the many protons wins back part of the binding.

    Fission and fusion

    A nuclear reaction releases energy when its products are more tightly bound than the nuclei it starts from. The energy released, the Q-value, is

    $$\begin{aligned} Q &= (m_\text{before} - m_\text{after}) \cdot c^2 \\ &= B_\text{after} - B_\text{before} \end{aligned}$$

    Both sides of the peak can climb towards it:

    • In fusion, two light nuclei join into a heavier one. Deuterium and tritium give helium-4 and a neutron, with $Q = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$.
    • In fission, a heavy nucleus splits into two medium-sized ones. A uranium-235 nucleus that absorbs a neutron releases about $200\ \text{MeV}$ in all.

    The figure shows one possible split of uranium-235, into barium-141, krypton-92 and three neutrons: it releases $173\ \text{MeV}$ at once, and the radioactive fragments release more later.

    Fission was discovered in Berlin in December 1938 by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained it at Christmas 1938 (published February 1939) and used $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$ to estimate the energy. Fission gives about $0.85\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon of fuel; D–T fusion gives $3.5\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon, about four times more.

      Example 1

      The atomic mass of helium-4 is $4.002603\ \text{u}$ (AME2020). Calculate the mass defect, the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon of the helium-4 nucleus.

      Step 1/7

      Given: $m(^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603\ \text{u}$, $Z = 2$, $N = 2$, $A = 4$; from AME2020, $m_\text{H} = 1.007825\ \text{u}$ and $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\ \text{u}$; ${1\ \text{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}}$. Find: the mass defect $\Delta m$, the binding energy $B$ and $B/A$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle: The nucleus has less mass than its free nucleons; the difference times $c^2$ is the energy needed to separate them. Atomic masses are used, so the two protons are counted as two hydrogen-1 atoms and the two electrons cancel.

      Step 3/7

      Formula: $$\begin{aligned} \Delta m &= Z \cdot m_\text{H} + N \cdot m_\text{n} \\ &\quad - m(^{4}\text{He}) \\ B &= \Delta m \cdot c^2 \\ \frac{B}{A} &= \frac{\Delta m \cdot c^2}{A} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions: The mass defect stays in $\text{u}$, and ${1\ \text{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}}$ turns it into an energy in megaelectronvolts.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution: $$\begin{aligned} \Delta m &= 2 \cdot 1.007825\ \text{u} \\ &\quad + 2 \cdot 1.008665\ \text{u} \\ &\quad - 4.002603\ \text{u} \\ &= 4.032980\ \text{u} - 4.002603\ \text{u} \\ &= 0.030377\ \text{u} \\[4pt] B &= 0.030377\ \text{u} \cdot 931.5\ \text{MeV/u} \\ &= 28.30\ \text{MeV} \\[4pt] \frac{B}{A} &= \frac{28.30\ \text{MeV}}{4} = 7.074\ \text{MeV} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer: $\Delta m = 0.0304\ \text{u}$, $B = 28.3\ \text{MeV}$ and $B/A = 7.07\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon. Three significant figures are enough, because $931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$ is given to four and the result is used only to three.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness: The mass defect is $0.030377 : 4.032980 = 0.75\ \%$ of the mass of the free particles: small, as expected, but far larger than in any chemical reaction. The value $7.07\ \text{MeV}$ is the point of $^{4}\text{He}$ on the binding energy curve.

      Given

      $m(^{2}\text{H}) = 2.014102\ \text{u}$, $m(^{3}\text{H}) = 3.016049\ \text{u}$, $m(^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603\ \text{u}$, $m_\text{n} = 1.008665\ \text{u}$, $1\ \text{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$

      Find

      the energy $Q$ released in one D–T reaction

      Principle

      The products are more tightly bound than the fuel, so they have less mass; the lost mass leaves as kinetic energy, $Q = \Delta m \cdot c^2$. Atomic masses work, because there are two electrons on both sides.

      Formula

      $$\begin{aligned} Q = \big(&m(^{2}\text{H}) + m(^{3}\text{H}) \\ &- m(^{4}\text{He}) - m_\text{n}\big) \cdot c^2 \end{aligned}$$

      Unit conversions

      The mass difference in $\text{u}$ is multiplied by $931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$.

      Substitution

      $$\begin{aligned} m_\text{before} &= 2.014102\ \text{u} + 3.016049\ \text{u} \\ &= 5.030151\ \text{u} \\ m_\text{after} &= 4.002603\ \text{u} + 1.008665\ \text{u} \\ &= 5.011268\ \text{u} \\ \Delta m &= 0.018883\ \text{u} \\ Q &= 0.018883\ \text{u} \cdot 931.5\ \text{MeV/u} \\ &= 17.59\ \text{MeV} \end{aligned}$$

      Answer

      $Q \approx 17.6\ \text{MeV}$

      Three significant figures.

      Reasonableness

      This is the value used for D–T fusion everywhere, $17.6\ \text{MeV}$. Check with binding energies:

      $B(^{4}\text{He}) - B(^{2}\text{H}) - B(^{3}\text{H})$
      $= 28.30\ \text{MeV} - 2.22\ \text{MeV} - 8.48\ \text{MeV}$
      $= 17.60\ \text{MeV}$

      the same within rounding.

      Optional Example 3: Find the error
      A student calculates the binding energy of helium-4 like this:

      $$\begin{aligned} \Delta m &= 2 \cdot 1.007276\ \text{u} \\ &\quad + 2 \cdot 1.008665\ \text{u} \\ &\quad - 4.002603\ \text{u} \\ &= 0.029279\ \text{u} \end{aligned}$$

      and gets $B = 27.3\ \text{MeV}$. The correct value is $28.3\ \text{MeV}$. What went wrong?

      • Right. $1.007276\ \text{u}$ is a bare proton, but $4.002603\ \text{u}$ is the helium atom with two electrons. Use the hydrogen-1 atom, $1.007825\ \text{u}$, so that the electrons cancel.

      • $931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$ holds for every mass. The masses are the problem: the two electrons of the helium atom were not matched on the other side.

      • The neutron really is heavier, $1.008665\ \text{u}$. The error is the proton: a bare proton was subtracted from a whole atom with electrons.

      Common misconception

      "Binding energy is energy stored in the nucleus, and a nucleus with a large binding energy has a lot of energy to give." It is the other way round. Binding energy is the energy you must add to take a nucleus apart. A tightly bound nucleus has already given its energy away; energy is released when nuclei move towards larger $B/A$.

      "In a nuclear reaction, protons and neutrons are destroyed and turned into energy." The number of nucleons stays the same before and after: in D–T fusion, $5$ nucleons go in and $5$ come out. Only the mass of the bound system changes.

      Optional Example 4: Lise Meitner and the explanation of fission

      The people behind fission

      Lise Meitner (1878–1968)

      Lise Meitner was an Austrian physicist who worked for about thirty years in Berlin with the chemist Otto Hahn. In July 1938 she had to flee from Nazi Germany and moved to Sweden. In December Hahn wrote to her that uranium hit by neutrons gave barium, a much lighter element. Over Christmas 1938 Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch worked out that the uranium nucleus had split in two, and with $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$ they estimated about $200\ \text{MeV}$ per split. The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1944 went to Hahn alone, which many scientists see as unjust. Element 109, meitnerium, is named after her.

      Model answer

      On the binding energy curve, iron is at the peak: its nucleons are the most tightly bound, about $8.8\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon. If iron nuclei fused, the product would be heavier and less tightly bound, so it would have more mass than the fuel. Energy is released only when a reaction moves towards larger $B/A$. Fusing iron would therefore take energy instead of giving it, and the star's core loses its source of energy.

        Review the theory.

        Summary

        • Mass and energy are equivalent: $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$, and $1\ \text{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$.
        • A nucleus has less mass than its free nucleons. The mass defect times $c^2$ is the binding energy $B$, the energy needed to take the nucleus apart.
        • The binding energy per nucleon $B/A$ peaks near iron-56 and nickel-62 at about $8.8\ \text{MeV}$.
        • Fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei both move towards the peak, so both release energy: $Q = B_\text{after} - B_\text{before}$.
        • D–T fusion releases $17.6\ \text{MeV}$; the fission of uranium-235 about $200\ \text{MeV}$, but fusion gives more energy per nucleon of fuel.
