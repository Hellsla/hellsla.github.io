## Timing (75 minutes)

- **0–7 min, Think about it.** Read the hook aloud and let pairs talk for one minute about the question. The review question on the Coulomb barrier links to chapter 3.
- **7–12 min, Predict.** Both predictions individually, each with a one-line reason. Do not discuss the answers yet: the feedback opens only when the theory sections have been read.
- **12–30 min, Investigate.** Pairs: the plasma-or-not sorting task, the data table and its ratio question, then the comparison with the word bank and sentence starters (one student speaks, the other writes, then both write their own version).
- **30–45 min, Theory summary.** Read *What is a plasma?* and *Plasma in nature* together; the vocabulary match; then the key idea and the triple product; back to the predictions.
- **45–60 min, Apply.** The worked example step by step on the board; the confinement-time task individually. The error-spotting task and the misconception text follow.
- **60–75 min, Check your understanding.** Three checks individually, the explanation to a friend (sentence starters above it), the summary and the French corner in pairs.

## Organisation and preparation
- One device per student is best (predictions and written answers are kept per device); pairs share the discussion.
- Optional demonstrations: a plasma ball or a fluorescent tube held near it, which glows without wires (a gas made conducting); a neon or spectrum tube with a hand spectroscope to show line colours, linking to the aurora's oxygen lines.
- If you have one, show a recent aurora photograph from Lapland (the Finnish Meteorological Institute's space weather pages give current aurora probabilities). Ask: "Where on the map of Finland was this taken, and how do you know?"

## Plan for fast pairs and for running out of time
- **Fast pairs:** the optional error-spotting task; then estimate the pressure of the Sun's core plasma from the table ($p = nkT$ with $n \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{32}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, $T = 1.57 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{K}$, about $2.6 \cdot 10^{16}\ \text{Pa}$) and compare with the published $2.65 \cdot 10^{16}\ \text{Pa}$.
- **Running out of time:** set the French corner and check 3 as homework; in Investigate, skip the written comparison and keep only the oral pair discussion.

## Assessment
- Written answers are self-assessed against the criteria shown after the student has written something. For a grade, use the same criteria: the comparison names a similarity and two differences with numbers and links pressure to both density and temperature; the explanation separates temperature from power and pressure, says why a reactor must be hotter than the Sun and why the wall does not melt.
- Language: correct use of at least three key words (ion, ionisation, plasma, density, pressure, confinement time) and at least one linking word (whereas, because, both … and …).

## Common difficulties
- "Hotter means more pressure / more power." Ask: "Hotter, but how many particles?" Point to $p = nkT$ and to the Sun's very low power per cubic metre.
- Forgetting the electrons in $p = nkT$ (the error-spotting task).
- Units of the triple product: the temperature stays in keV. $1\ \text{keV} \approx 1.16 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{K}$.
- "Plasma is always millions of degrees." Lightning (about $3 \cdot 10^{4}\ \text{K}$) and a neon sign show that the defining feature is free charges.
- The aurora is not reflected sunlight and not caused by heat. Its light is emitted by atoms and molecules hit by electrons that the magnetic field guides.
- The ignition value $3 \cdot 10^{21}\ \text{keV} \cdot \text{s/m}^3$ is approximate; the exact value depends on assumptions (profiles, impurities, which density and temperature are used).

## Extension ideas
- Read the Finnish Meteorological Institute's aurora forecast for tonight and explain in English what the magnetometer readings mean.
- Why is the Sun's core so dense? Estimate with a simple model how gravity compresses it (links to chapter 3).
- Look up how the triple product of the best tokamaks has grown since the 1960s and what has not yet been reached (ignition in magnetic confinement).
