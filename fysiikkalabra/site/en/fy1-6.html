Acceleration of free fall: $v = gt$

Why did the astronaut on the Moon drop a hammer and a feather at the same time and see them hit the ground together, even though on Earth a feather drifts slowly down?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the speed of a freely falling object with the formula $v = gt$.
  • read the acceleration of free fall from the slope of the $(t, v)$ graph.
  • conclude from experiments when mass affects falling and when it does not.

Prior knowledge

  • In uniform motion the distance is the product of speed and time, $s = vt$chapter Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs
  • The slope of a straight-line graph tells how much the quantity on the vertical axis changes when the quantity on the horizontal axis increases by onechapter Graphing and fitting a straight line

1Think about it

In 1971, on the Apollo 15 mission, astronaut David Scott held a hammer in one hand and a falcon feather in the other on the surface of the Moon and let them go at the same time. They hit the lunar surface at the same moment. At home the same experiment does not work: the hammer falls straight down, but the feather drifts slowly.

Does the speed of a falling object increase steadily, and does a heavy object fall faster than a light one?

Review the previous chapter

A cart moves at a constant speed of $6.0\,\text{m/s}$. How far does it travel in $5.0\,\text{s}$?

  • You divided the speed by the time. Speed tells how many metres are travelled per second, so the distance is found by multiplying the speed by the time: $s = vt$.

  • You added the speed and the time. The units $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{s}$ are not the same quantity, so they cannot be added. The distance comes from the product $s = vt$.

  • Correct: $s = vt = 6.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 30\,\text{m}$. In uniform motion the speed stays the same. In this chapter you investigate motion in which the speed itself changes.

How the calculation works

In a vacuum the speed is calculated with the formula $v = gt$. The quotient $v : t$ tells how much the speed has increased per second on average since the ball started from rest. In air the increase in speed slows down as the speed grows, and the speed approaches a terminal speed. For a light ball the terminal speed is smaller than for a heavy one, so $v : t$ stays smaller for the light ball.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    When an object is released from rest, its speed increases. Free fall is falling with no air resistance. Measurements show that in free fall the speed increases by the same amount every second: on the surface of the Earth by $9.81\,\text{m/s}$ per second. After one second the speed is $9.81\,\text{m/s}$, after two seconds $19.6\,\text{m/s}$ and after three seconds $29.4\,\text{m/s}$. So the object does not fall at a constant speed as in uniform motion (chapter Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs) but accelerates.

    The change in speed per unit of time is called acceleration. The acceleration of a freely falling object is the acceleration of free fall $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The unit $\text{m/s}^2$ is read "metres per second per second": speed is measured in $\text{m/s}$, and it changes by this amount every second. So the number $g$ is not a speed but the rate at which the speed increases.

    Mass does not affect falling

    When there is no air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration regardless of their mass. In a vacuum a hammer and a feather fall equally fast, and a ball with a mass of $5\,\text{kg}$ has at every moment the same speed as a ball with a mass of $1\,\text{kg}$. The reason is explained later in connection with forces. In this chapter it is taken as an experimental result that you investigate yourself.

    In air the situation is different. Air slows a light, wide object, such as a feather or a sheet of paper, clearly more than a small, dense object, such as a stone or a steel ball. This is why at home a feather falls more slowly than a hammer. The difference is caused by the air, not by mass as such.

    Speed in free fall

    Because the speed increases by the same amount every second, the speed is directly proportional to time. When the object starts from rest, its speed at the moment $t$ is

    $$v = g\,t$$

    where $v$ is the downward speed ($\text{m/s}$), $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the acceleration of free fall and $t$ is the time from the start of the fall ($\text{s}$). The formula is a linear model $v = kt$ in which the slope $k$ is $g$. It holds when there is no air resistance.

    So the $(t, v)$ graph of a falling object is a rising straight line through the origin. Its slope $\tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is the acceleration of free fall, so $g$ can be found from the graph by reading two points far apart. Because the acceleration is constant, $\Delta v = g\,\Delta t$ holds for any time interval: the speed increases by $9.81\,\text{m/s}$ every second even when the object did not start from rest.

    Common misconception

    A heavy object does not fall faster than a light one: when there is no air resistance, all objects have the same $g$. Everyday experience seems to show otherwise, because in air a light, wide object is slowed the most. Another mistake is to treat the number $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ as a speed: it tells how much the speed increases every second, and the speed itself is $g\,t$.

    Summary

    • In free fall there is no air resistance. The speed of the object increases by the same amount every second.
    • The acceleration of free fall on the surface of the Earth is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, and it is the same for all objects regardless of mass.
    • The speed of an object that starts from rest is $v = g\,t$, and the $(t, v)$ graph is a straight line through the origin with slope $g$.
    • In air a light, wide object is slowed the most, so $v = g\,t$ does not hold unless air resistance is ignored.
    • Conversion: in an hour $3600\,\text{m} = 3.6\,\text{km}$, so $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A stone is dropped from rest from a bridge, and it hits the water $1.5\,\text{s}$ later. What is the speed of the stone when it hits the water, in $\text{m/s}$ and in $\text{km/h}$? Air resistance is ignored.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The duration of the fall is $t = 1.5\,\text{s}$, the stone starts from rest and the acceleration of free fall is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. We want the speed $v$ at the moment of impact, first in $\text{m/s}$ and then in $\text{km/h}$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The stone is in free fall, because air resistance is ignored, and it starts from rest. Then the speed increases by the same amount every second, so the speed is directly proportional to time: $v = g\,t$.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The formula is already solved for the quantity we want:

      $$v = g\,t$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The time is in seconds and $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, so the result comes in $\text{m/s}$ without a conversion. To convert to kilometres per hour, think about what $1\,\text{m/s}$ means: in an hour ($3600\,\text{s}$) an object at this speed covers $3600\,\text{m} = 3.6\,\text{km}$, so $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$. This is substituted in place of the unit.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} v &= g\,t \\ &= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{s} \\ &= 14.715\,\text{m/s} \\[4pt] v &= 14.715 \cdot 1\,\text{m/s} \\ &= 14.715 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h} \\ &= 52.97\,\text{km/h} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The speed is $v \approx 15\,\text{m/s}$, that is, $v \approx 53\,\text{km/h}$. Two significant figures, because the time $1.5\,\text{s}$ is given with two figures. The conversion is made from the unrounded value, not from the rounded $15\,\text{m/s}$.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The graph shows the same: at the moment $1\,\text{s}$ the speed is $9.8\,\text{m/s}$ and at the moment $2\,\text{s}$ it is $19.6\,\text{m/s}$, so at the moment $1.5\,\text{s}$ it is about $15\,\text{m/s}$. A speed of $53\,\text{km/h}$ is a normal speed for a car in city traffic, and it is a reasonable result for a fall that lasts only one and a half seconds.

      1. Concept

      An object falls from rest in a vacuum. What does its $(t, v)$ graph look like?

      2. What g tells us

      The slope of the $(t, v)$ graph of a freely falling ball dropped from rest is $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. What does this number tell you about the motion of the ball?

      • Correct. Acceleration is the change in speed per second: $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. So after one second the speed is $9.81\,\text{m/s}$, after two seconds $19.6\,\text{m/s}$, and so on.

      • Without air resistance the acceleration is constant, so the speed increases by the same amount every second. That is why the graph is a straight line: after two seconds the speed is $2\cdot9.81\,\text{m/s} \approx 19.6\,\text{m/s}$, not less.

      • The unit $\text{m/s}^2$ is not a distance. The number tells how much the speed changes per second, not how far the ball travels. The distance also grows every second, because the ball moves faster and faster.

          the measurement points $(t$; $v)$ in the table: $(0.10\,\text{s}$; $0.95\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.20\,\text{s}$; $2.03\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.30\,\text{s}$; $2.91\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.40\,\text{s}$; $3.88\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.50\,\text{s}$; $4.94\,\text{m/s})$ and $(0.60\,\text{s}$; $5.83\,\text{m/s})$

          Find

          the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$ and its comparison with the table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          The object starts from rest and air resistance is small, so a straight line $v = g\,t$ fits the points. Its slope is the acceleration of free fall. The line is drawn through the middle of the set of points, and the slope is calculated from points of the line, not from measurement points.

          Reading the graph

          The points lie on a rising line with points on both sides of it, and the line passes almost through the origin. The fitted line passes approximately through the points $(0\,\text{s}$; $0\,\text{m/s})$ and $(0.60\,\text{s}$; $5.85\,\text{m/s})$. They are read from the line and chosen far apart from each other.

          Formula

          $g$$= \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$
          $= \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in $\text{s}$.

          Substitution

          $g$$= \dfrac{5.85\,\text{m/s} - 0\,\text{m/s}}{0.60\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 9.75\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Two significant figures, because the line is fitted by eye. A fit by eye varies, so about $9.3\text{–}10.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ is a good answer. The result $9.75\,\text{m/s}^2$ is $0.6\,\%$ smaller than the table value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, that is, it deviates by less than one percent, so the measurement supports the model $v = g\,t$ and its scatter is random.

          Reasonableness

          According to the line, the speed at the moment $0.30\,\text{s}$ is $9.75 \cdot 0.30 \approx 2.9\,\text{m/s}$, which matches the third row of the table. The slope is close to the known value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

        6. An object was released from rest, but the measurement was started only at the moment $t = 0$, when the object had already fallen for a while. The speed was measured at different moments. Draw the $(t, v)$ graph from the measurement points and fit a straight line to the points. The line does not pass through the origin.

          How many seconds before the start of the measurement was the object released? Explain what the intercept of the line with the $v$ axis tells.

          Speed of a falling object from the start of the measurement
          $t\,(\text{s})$$v\,(\text{m/s})$
          0.01.97
          0.12.93
          0.23.95
          0.34.89
          0.45.90
          0.56.86
          s
          Answer

          $0.20\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the measurement points $(t$; $v)$ in the table: $(0.0\,\text{s}$; $1.97\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.1\,\text{s}$; $2.93\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.2\,\text{s}$; $3.95\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.3\,\text{s}$; $4.89\,\text{m/s})$, $(0.4\,\text{s}$; $5.90\,\text{m/s})$ and $(0.5\,\text{s}$; $6.86\,\text{m/s})$; the object was released from rest before the measurement started

          Find

          the time $t_0$ between the start of the fall and the start of the measurement, in $\text{s}$

          Principle

          The speed increases steadily, so a straight line $v = k\,t + b$ fits the points, and its slope $k$ is the acceleration of free fall $g$. The line does not pass through the origin, because the object was already moving at the moment $t = 0$: the constant term $b$ is the speed at the start of the measurement. An object that starts from rest reaches the speed $b$ in the time $\dfrac{b}{g}$, and this is the time from the start of the fall to the start of the measurement.

          Reading the graph

          The points lie on a rising line that cuts the $v$ axis at $b \approx 2.0\,\text{m/s}$. The fitted line passes approximately through the points $(0.0\,\text{s}$; $2.0\,\text{m/s})$ and $(0.5\,\text{s}$; $6.9\,\text{m/s})$, so

          $k$$= \dfrac{6.9\,\text{m/s} - 2.0\,\text{m/s}}{0.5\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Formula

          $t_0 = \dfrac{b}{k}$, because $v = k\,t_0$ gives the time in which an object that starts from rest reaches the speed $b$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the speed is in $\text{m/s}$ and the slope in $\text{m/s}^2$.

          Substitution

          $t_0$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s}}{9.8\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 0.20\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $0.20\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures, because the constant term is read from the graph to a precision of two figures. The intercept of the line with the $v$ axis, $b \approx 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, is the speed of the object when the measurement starts. The line does not pass through the origin, because the time $t$ is counted from the start of the measurement and not from the start of the fall.

          Reasonableness

          The slope $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the acceleration of free fall, so the model is correct and only the zero point of the time has shifted. The object had fallen for $0.20\,\text{s}$, and the speed $9.8 \cdot 0.20 \approx 2.0\,\text{m/s}$ matches the first row of the table.

        7. Students measure the acceleration of free fall with two light gates placed one below the other vertically. The speed of an object $5.0\,\text{cm}$ long, dropped from rest, is obtained at each gate from the length of the object and the time $\Delta t_\text{light}$ for which the light is blocked. The moment of the drop is not known accurately, but a clock measures the moments $t$ when the blocking of the light is halfway, that is, when the middle of the object is at the gate. Calculate the acceleration of free fall from the measurements in the table. Explain why $g$ can be obtained from the speeds at the two gates even though the moment of the drop is not known.

          Measurement with two light gates
          Gate$\Delta t_\text{light}\,(\text{ms})$$t\,(\text{s})$
          125.00.320
          211.10.575
          m/s²
          Answer

          $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          length of the object $L = 5.0\,\text{cm}$; gate 1: $\Delta t_\text{light,1} = 25.0\,\text{ms}$ and the moment of the middle of the light blocking $t_1 = 0.320\,\text{s}$; gate 2: $\Delta t_\text{light,2} = 11.1\,\text{ms}$ and the moment of the middle of the light blocking $t_2 = 0.575\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          The speed of the object at a gate is the length of the object divided by the time for which the light has been blocked: $v = \dfrac{L}{\Delta t_\text{light}}$. This is the average speed during the blocking, and in uniformly accelerated motion it equals the speed at the middle of the blocking, so the clock records the moment when the blocking is halfway. The speed increases steadily, so the acceleration is the change in speed divided by the change in time: $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. The moment of the drop is not needed, because only the time difference $t_2 - t_1$ and the difference of the speeds affect the result. If the clock reading at the moment of the drop is wrong, it shifts both times by the same amount and cancels in the difference.

          Formula

          $v = \dfrac{L}{\Delta t_\text{light}}$ and $g = \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

          Conversions

          $L = 5.0\,\text{cm} = 0.050\,\text{m}$, because $1\,\text{cm} = 0.01\,\text{m}$.

          $\Delta t_\text{light,1}$$= 25.0\,\text{ms}$
          $= 0.0250\,\text{s}$
          $\Delta t_\text{light,2}$$= 11.1\,\text{ms}$
          $= 0.0111\,\text{s}$

          because $1\,\text{ms} = 0.001\,\text{s}$.

          Substitution

          $v_1$$= \dfrac{0.050\,\text{m}}{0.0250\,\text{s}}$
          $= 2.00\,\text{m/s}$
          $v_2$$= \dfrac{0.050\,\text{m}}{0.0111\,\text{s}}$
          $= 4.50\,\text{m/s}$

          Then

          $g$$= \dfrac{4.50\,\text{m/s} - 2.00\,\text{m/s}}{0.575\,\text{s} - 0.320\,\text{s}}$
          $= \dfrac{2.50\,\text{m/s}}{0.255\,\text{s}}$
          $= 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Two significant figures, because the length of the object $L = 5.0\,\text{cm}$ is given with two figures and limits the result. The $g$ obtained with two gates does not require knowing the moment of the drop: the speeds are obtained from the length of the object and the time for which the light is blocked, and the acceleration from the time difference $t_2 - t_1$.

          Reasonableness

          The result is close to the known value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The speed $2.00\,\text{m/s}$ requires a fall of only about $0.20\,\text{s}$, although the clock shows $0.320\,\text{s}$: the clock was started before the drop, which does not matter for the time difference. The speed $2.0\,\text{m/s}$ corresponds to a falling time of

          $\dfrac{2.0}{9.8}$$\approx 0.20\,\text{s}$

          and the speed $4.5\,\text{m/s}$ to a time of $0.46\,\text{s}$, and their difference is $0.25\,\text{s}$, like the time difference of the clock.

        1. In a vacuum chamber two balls with masses $0.50\,\text{kg}$ and $4.0\,\text{kg}$ are dropped from rest at the same time.

          How many times as large is the speed of the heavier ball as the speed of the lighter ball when $1.5\,\text{s}$ have passed since the start of the fall?

          Answer

          $1.0$

          Show the solution

          Given

          two balls in a vacuum, masses $m_1 = 0.50\,\text{kg}$ and $m_2 = 4.0\,\text{kg}$, both start from rest, $t = 1.5\,\text{s}$

          Find

          the ratio of the speeds $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}$ (no unit)

          Principle

          In a vacuum there is no air resistance, so both balls are in free fall. The acceleration of free fall $g$ is the same for all objects regardless of mass, so the speed of each is $g\,t$.

          Formula

          $v_1 = g\,t$ and $v_2 = g\,t$, so $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1} = \dfrac{g\,t}{g\,t}$

          Conversions

          The times are in seconds, and the masses are not needed in the calculation. No conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $v_1$$= v_2$
          $= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{s}$
          $= 14.715\,\text{m/s}$

          so

          $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}$$= \dfrac{14.715\,\text{m/s}}{14.715\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= 1.0$

          Answer

          $1.0$

          Two significant figures as in the time $1.5\,\text{s}$. The ratio is exactly one, because the mass cancels out.

          Reasonableness

          If the heavier ball fell faster, mass would have to appear in the formula, but the formula $v = g\,t$ has no mass. This matches the hammer and feather experiment on the Moon, where the objects hit the ground at the same time.

        2. An unknown planet has no atmosphere. There the speed of a stone dropped from rest is $14.8\,\text{m/s}$ after $4.0\,\text{s}$.

          After how many seconds from the start of the fall is the speed of the stone $30.0\,\text{m/s}$?

          s
          Answer

          $8.1\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the planet has no atmosphere, the stone starts from rest, $v_1 = 14.8\,\text{m/s}$ at the moment $t_1 = 4.0\,\text{s}$, desired speed $v_2 = 30.0\,\text{m/s}$

          Find

          the time $t_2$ in $\text{s}$ at which the speed is $30.0\,\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          With no atmosphere the stone is in free fall, so the speed is directly proportional to time: $v = g\,t$. The $g$ of the planet is not known, so it is first solved from the given measurement and then used to find the time.

          Formula

          $g = \dfrac{v_1}{t_1}$ and $t_2 = \dfrac{v_2}{g}$, so $t_2 = \dfrac{v_2\,t_1}{v_1}$

          Conversions

          The speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $g$$= \dfrac{14.8\,\text{m/s}}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
          $= 3.70\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $t_2$$= \dfrac{30.0\,\text{m/s}}{3.70\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 8.108\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $8.1\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures, because the time $4.0\,\text{s}$ is given with two figures and limits the result.

          Reasonableness

          A speed of $30.0\,\text{m/s}$ is about twice the speed $14.8\,\text{m/s}$, so the time is about twice $4.0\,\text{s}$, that is, about $8\,\text{s}$. The result $8.1\,\text{s}$ matches the estimate. The planet's $g = 3.70\,\text{m/s}^2$ is similar to that of Mars.

        3. A ball is dropped from rest in air, and its speed is measured to be $25.0\,\text{m/s}$ after $3.00\,\text{s}$.

          By how many $\text{m/s}$ is the speed smaller than the speed the ball would have in free fall without air resistance?

          m/s
          Answer

          $4.4\,\text{m/s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          measured speed in air $v_\text{measured} = 25.0\,\text{m/s}$ at the moment $t = 3.00\,\text{s}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the difference $\Delta v = v_\text{free} - v_\text{measured}$ in $\text{m/s}$

          Principle

          In free fall without air resistance the speed of an object that starts from rest would be $v_\text{free} = g\,t$. Air slows the ball, so the measured speed is smaller, and the difference tells the shortfall caused by air resistance.

          Formula

          $v_\text{free} = g\,t$ and $\Delta v = g\,t - v_\text{measured}$

          Conversions

          The time is in seconds and the speeds in $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\Delta v$$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.00\,\text{s} - 25.0\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 29.43\,\text{m/s} - 25.0\,\text{m/s}$
          $= 4.43\,\text{m/s}$

          Answer

          $4.4\,\text{m/s}$

          The time $3.00\,\text{s}$ gives the speed $29.43\,\text{m/s}$ three significant figures, but the difference has as many decimal places as the weakest number, that is, the measured value $25.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The difference is positive, because air slows the ball, and it is a small part of the speed: $\dfrac{4.4}{29.4} \approx 15\,\%$. In a three-second fall air resistance is already significant but not dominant, which fits a ball.

        5. A student dropped an object from rest and measured its speed at three clock readings $t$. The clock had been running already before the drop, but the student did not know for how long. For each row the student calculated the quotient $\dfrac{v}{t}$ and got the values $7.5\,\text{m/s}^2$, $8.9\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $9.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. However, the acceleration of free fall is constant. Explain why the quotients differ from each other and why they are too small. Calculate $g$ in another way so that the zero point of the clock does not affect the result, and justify the method.

          How many seconds before the drop had the clock been started?

          Speed of a falling object at clock readings
          $t\,(\text{s})$$v\,(\text{m/s})$
          0.654.9
          1.6514.7
          2.6524.5
          s
          Answer

          $t_0 = 0.15\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          clock readings and speeds: $(0.65\,\text{s}$; $4.9\,\text{m/s})$, $(1.65\,\text{s}$; $14.7\,\text{m/s})$ and $(2.65\,\text{s}$; $24.5\,\text{m/s})$; the object started from rest, the clock had been running already before the drop

          Find

          why the quotients $\dfrac{v}{t}$ differ, the acceleration of free fall $g$ in $\text{m/s}^2$ and the interval $t_0$ between starting the clock and the drop in $\text{s}$

          Principle

          The formula $v = g\,t$ is valid when $t$ is the time from the start of the fall. The clock reading is $t = t_0 + t_\text{fall}$, so the quotient $\dfrac{v}{t}$ is too small, and the error is proportionally largest at a short reading. The change in speed $\Delta v$ and the time difference $\Delta t$ do not depend on the zero point of the clock, because $t_0$ cancels in the difference. Therefore $g = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is the correct method.

          Formula

          $g$$= \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$
          $= \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$
          $t_0$$= t - t_\text{fall}$
          $= t - \dfrac{v}{g}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: the speeds are in $\text{m/s}$ and the times in $\text{s}$.

          Substitution

          The quotients: $\dfrac{4.9}{0.65} = 7.5$, $\dfrac{14.7}{1.65} = 8.9$ and $\dfrac{24.5}{2.65} = 9.2$ (unit $\text{m/s}^2$); they approach the value $9.8$ as the clock reading grows. The slope:

          $g$$= \dfrac{24.5\,\text{m/s} - 4.9\,\text{m/s}}{2.65\,\text{s} - 0.65\,\text{s}}$
          $= \dfrac{19.6\,\text{m/s}}{2.00\,\text{s}}$
          $= 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$

          and the pairs $(1)$ and $(2)$ and also $(2)$ and $(3)$ give the same value. The real falling time in the first row is

          $\dfrac{4.9\,\text{m/s}}{9.8\,\text{m/s}^2}$$= 0.50\,\text{s}$

          so

          $t_0$$= 0.65\,\text{s} - 0.50\,\text{s}$
          $= 0.15\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $t_0 = 0.15\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures as in the clock readings. The acceleration of free fall is $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The quotients $\dfrac{v}{t}$ are too small, because the clock reading is always $0.15\,\text{s}$ too large, and the error affects the short reading most. The slope works, because the change in speed and the time difference do not depend on the zero point of the clock.

          Reasonableness

          The clock error is the same in every row: the real falling times are $0.50\,\text{s}$, $1.50\,\text{s}$ and $2.50\,\text{s}$, and $9.8 \cdot 2.50 = 24.5\,\text{m/s}$ matches the third row. The result $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is close to the known value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.
