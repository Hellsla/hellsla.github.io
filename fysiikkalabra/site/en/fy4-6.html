Weight, friction and the inclined plane

Why does a moving box stay in place on a slanted loading ramp, but start to slide when the ramp is made a little steeper?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • distinguish static friction from kinetic friction and calculate the friction force using the coefficient of friction.
  • draw the free-body diagram of an object on an inclined plane and write its equations of motion.
  • determine the coefficient of friction experimentally.

1Think about it

A mover lifts a box onto the loading ramp of a van. The box may be empty or packed full. It stays in place even though the ramp is slanted. When the lower end of the ramp is moved closer to the van, the ramp gets steeper. At some point the box suddenly starts to slide down.

What holds the box in place on a gentle ramp, and why is it no longer enough on a steep ramp?

Optional Recall: the normal force on a slope
A box is at rest on an inclined plank. In which direction does the normal force $\overline{N}$ of the plank act on the box?

  • The normal force is perpendicular to the surface of contact. Only on a horizontal surface does it point straight up. On a slope the surface is slanted, so the normal force is slanted too.

  • Correct. The normal force is always perpendicular to the surface of contact, as you learned in the chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces. On a slope it is therefore tilted and not vertical.

  • A force parallel to the surface is friction, not the normal force. The normal force is perpendicular to the surface and pushes the box away from it.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation. The box is at rest at first. It stays in place if the static friction needed is at most $\mu_0 N$; otherwise it slides, and kinetic friction $\mu N$ acts on it. The coefficients of friction are model values: wood on wood $\mu_0 = 0.50$ and $\mu = 0.30$, plastic on wood $\mu_0 = 0.30$ and $\mu = 0.20$, rubber on wood $\mu_0 = 0.80$ and $\mu = 0.60$. Air drag is ignored. The blue arrow of the velocity is in the snapshot, not in the free-body diagram, because velocity is not a force.

How the calculation works

The components of the weight are $G_x = mg\sin\alpha$ down the slope and $G_y = mg\cos\alpha$ toward the surface, and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. In the direction perpendicular to the surface the box does not accelerate, so $N = mg\cos\alpha$. If $G_x \le \mu_0 N$, the box stays in place, the static friction is $F_\mu = G_x$ and $a = 0$. Otherwise $F_\mu = \mu N$ and along the slope $G_x - F_\mu = ma$, so $a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha)$.

Model answer

At rest the static friction acting on the box equals the component $G_x = mg\sin\alpha$ that pulls it down the slope, so it grows with the angle: in the model values $3.4\,\text{N}$ at $10^\circ$ and $8.6\,\text{N}$ at $26^\circ$. At the same time the normal force $N = mg\cos\alpha$ decreases, and with it the upper limit $\mu_0 N$ of static friction. When $G_x$ exceeds the upper limit, at about $27^\circ$, the box starts to slide, and the friction drops to the kinetic friction $\mu N$, in the model values $5.2\,\text{N}$. Both $G_x$ and the friction are proportional to the mass, so the mass cancels, and at $35^\circ$ the acceleration is $3.22\,\text{m/s}^2$ for all masses, which shows in the table of experiment 2. That the limiting angle does not depend on the mass follows from the condition $\tan\alpha_0 = \mu_0$, which contains no mass; this was not measured.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    Weight

    The weight $\overline{G}$ of an object is the force with which the Earth pulls the object. It always points straight down, also on a slope, and in a free-body diagram it is drawn from the center of the object. The magnitude of the weight is the product of the mass and the acceleration of free fall, $mg$. Mass describes the inertia of an object, that is how hard it is to change its state of motion, and it is the same everywhere. Weight, on the other hand, depends on the place: on the surface of the Moon $g = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the weight of a $60\,\text{kg}$ person there is about $97\,\text{N}$, whereas on Earth it is about $590\,\text{N}$.

    Static friction and kinetic friction

    When two surfaces touch each other, they resist sliding relative to each other. This force parallel to the surface is friction $\overline{F}_\mu$. The surfaces are not perfectly smooth: tiny bumps catch on each other, and there are attractive forces between the particles of the surfaces. The harder the surfaces are pressed against each other, that is the greater the normal force $N$ is, the greater the friction can be.

    When a box on a floor is pulled only weakly, it does not move. Then static friction acts on it, equal to the pull and in the opposite direction, so the net force is zero. Static friction is therefore not constant but adapts to the situation. It does have an upper limit, though. When the pull exceeds it, the box starts to slide, and the friction changes into kinetic friction. Kinetic friction is usually smaller than the greatest static friction, and at ordinary speeds it is approximately constant.

    In the graph the normal force of the floor is $100\,\text{N}$. Static friction grows with the pull up to $50\,\text{N}$, where the box starts to move. A sliding box is acted on by a kinetic friction of $35\,\text{N}$, even if the pull is increased. This is why a heavy cabinet is hardest to get moving, and once it is moving it goes more easily.

    The inclined plane

    On an inclined plane, that is a slope, the weight still points straight down, but the surface is slanted. The normal force is perpendicular to the surface, and the friction is parallel to the surface. For calculation the weight is split into two components: the component along the slope tends to take the object down the slope, and the component perpendicular to the surface presses the object against the surface. The components are not new forces. They replace the weight in the calculation, and in a free-body diagram they are drawn as dashed lines.

    The steeper the slope, the greater the component along the slope and the smaller the perpendicular component. When only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface and the object does not accelerate in that direction, the normal force decreases together with the perpendicular component, and with it the upper limit of static friction. This is why a box starts to slide on a steep slope: the component pulling it down the slope grows greater than the greatest static friction that the ramp can give.

    Friction does not always oppose motion

    Friction opposes the sliding of surfaces relative to each other, not necessarily the motion of the object. When a pickup truck starts to move with an acceleration, a box on its bed accelerates along with it. When air drag is ignored, the only horizontal force on the box is the friction of the bed, so it must point forward, in the direction of motion: the bed tends to slide forward from under the box, and static friction prevents this. Likewise, when we walk, the foot pushes the ground backward, and the static friction of the ground pushes the foot forward. On mirror-smooth ice, where there is very little friction, getting started is therefore difficult.

    Besides friction, other contact forces can act on an object. Buoyancy is the force with which a liquid or a gas pushes upward an object immersed in it, and the drag of the medium is the force with which air or water opposes the motion of an object. Air drag is treated in more detail in the chapter Air drag and falling in air. In this chapter air drag and buoyancy are ignored.

    The friction force and the inclined plane

    The magnitude of the weight is

    $$G = mg$$

    where $G$ is the weight (N), $m$ the mass (kg) and $g$ the acceleration of free fall, $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ on the surface of the Earth.

    Kinetic friction is proportional to the normal force:

    $$F_\mu = \mu N$$

    where $F_\mu$ is the magnitude of the kinetic friction (N), $\mu$ the coefficient of kinetic friction (no unit) and $N$ the magnitude of the normal force (N). Static friction is at most the product of the normal force and the coefficient of static friction $\mu_0$:

    $$F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N$$

    where $F_{\mu 0}$ is the magnitude of the static friction (N). The coefficients of friction depend on the materials and the condition of the surfaces, and usually $\mu < \mu_0$. The formulas are empirical models that describe dry surfaces well. In them the friction does not depend on the area of contact or on the sliding speed.

    On an inclined plane whose angle of inclination is $\alpha$, the $x$-axis is chosen along the slope and the $y$-axis perpendicular to the surface. The components of the weight are

    $$\begin{aligned} G_x &= G\sin\alpha \\ G_y &= G\cos\alpha \end{aligned}$$

    where $G_x$ is the component along the slope (N) and $G_y$ the component perpendicular to the surface (N). The angle between the weight and the component $G_y$ is the same $\alpha$ as the angle of inclination of the slope, because each is perpendicular to its counterpart: the weight to the horizontal and $G_y$ to the slope.

    If only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface and the object does not accelerate in that direction, the normal force equals $G_y$: $N = mg\cos\alpha$, which is less than the weight. (A pulling or pushing force directed toward or away from the surface would change the normal force.) The static friction acting on an object at rest equals $G_x$. The object stays in place as long as $mg\sin\alpha \le \mu_0\,mg\cos\alpha$, that is $\tan\alpha \le \mu_0$. The mass cancels, so according to this model a heavy and a light box start to slide on the same surface at the same angle.

    Determining the coefficient of friction

    In the diagrams and in the simulation, the static friction acting on an object at rest is denoted $\overline{F}_\mu$; its magnitude is $F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N$, and while sliding $F_\mu = \mu N$.

    The coefficient of static friction is obtained from an experiment in which a board is tilted slowly until the object starts to slide. At this limiting angle $\alpha_0$ the static friction is at its greatest, so $\mu_0 = \tan\alpha_0$. The coefficient of kinetic friction is obtained for example by pulling the object on a horizontal table with a spring balance, horizontally and at a constant velocity. Then the pull equals the kinetic friction and the normal force equals the weight, so $\mu = F : mg$. When the measurement is repeated with different masses, the coefficient of friction is the slope of the straight line through the origin fitted to the $(N, F_\mu)$ graph.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the friction force is always $\mu_0 N$ or $\mu N$ and always against the motion. Static friction, however, is only as large as the situation requires: if a box is pushed with a force of $20\,\text{N}$ and it does not move, the static friction is $20\,\text{N}$, even if its upper limit is greater. Friction opposes the sliding of surfaces relative to each other, so it can also point in the direction of motion, as on the bed of an accelerating truck. Another common mistake is to take the normal force to be always equal to the weight. On a slope $N = mg\cos\alpha$ is less than the weight, when only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface and the object does not accelerate in that direction, and the weight still points straight down and not perpendicular to the slope.

    Summary

    • The magnitude of the weight is $G = mg$, and the weight always points straight down. Mass is the same everywhere, but weight depends on the acceleration of free fall.
    • Friction is parallel to the surface and opposes the sliding of surfaces relative to each other, not always the motion of the object.
    • Static friction adapts to the situation: $F_{\mu 0} \le \mu_0 N$. A sliding object is acted on by kinetic friction $F_\mu = \mu N$, and usually $\mu < \mu_0$.
    • On a slope the weight is split into the components $G_x = mg\sin\alpha$ and $G_y = mg\cos\alpha$, and the normal force is $N = mg\cos\alpha$, when only the weight and the normal force act perpendicular to the surface and the object does not accelerate in that direction. The components are drawn as dashed lines, and they are not new forces.
    • The coefficient of static friction is obtained from the limiting angle at which the object starts to slide: $\mu_0 = \tan\alpha_0$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A moving box of mass $18\,\text{kg}$ slides down a loading ramp. The ramp is at an angle of $25^\circ$ to the horizontal, and the coefficient of kinetic friction between the box and the ramp is $0.30$. How large is the acceleration of the box, and how large is the friction force? Air drag is ignored.

      Step 1/8

      Given and asked. $m = 18\,\text{kg}$, $\alpha = 25^\circ$, $\mu = 0.30$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The acceleration $a$ and the friction force $F_\mu$ are asked.

      Step 2/8

      Principle. The box is chosen as the object. The Earth (weight) and the ramp (normal force and kinetic friction) act on it. The box slides down, so the kinetic friction points up the slope. Newton's second law is written along the slope, where the box accelerates, and perpendicular to the surface, where it does not accelerate: $a_y = 0$.

      Step 3/8

      Free-body diagram. The box is drawn as a rectangle along the slope, and the forces start from their points of application. The axes are chosen so that $x$ points down the slope, that is in the direction of the acceleration, and $y$ perpendicular to the surface, outward. The weight is split into the components $G_x = mg\sin\alpha$ and $G_y = mg\cos\alpha$, drawn with a dashed line.

      Step 4/8

      The formula solved. The equations of motion are read from the diagram and the asked quantities are solved:

      $$\begin{aligned} &x\text{:}\ \ mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = ma \\ &y\text{:}\ \ N - mg\cos\alpha = 0 \\ &F_\mu = \mu mg\cos\alpha \\ &a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha) \end{aligned}$$

      Step 5/8

      Unit conversions. None are needed: all quantities are in SI units, and the coefficient of friction has no unit.

      Step 6/8

      Substitution. The numerical values are substituted with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} &F_\mu = \mu mg\cos\alpha \\ &\phantom{F_\mu} = 0.30\cdot 18\,\text{kg} \\ &\quad\ \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot\cos 25^\circ \\ &\phantom{F_\mu} \approx 48.0\,\text{N} \\[4pt] &a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha) \\ &\phantom{a} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \\ &\quad\ \cdot(\sin 25^\circ - 0.30\cdot\cos 25^\circ) \\ &\phantom{a} \approx 1.48\,\text{m/s}^2 \end{aligned}$$

      Step 7/8

      Result. The acceleration of the box is $a \approx 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ down the slope and the friction force is $F_\mu \approx 48\,\text{N}$, to two significant figures like the data $\mu = 0.30$ and $m = 18\,\text{kg}$.

      Step 8/8

      Reasonableness. Without friction the acceleration would be $g\sin 25^\circ \approx 4.1\,\text{m/s}^2$, so friction reduces it, as it should. The friction force $48\,\text{N}$ is less than the component along the slope $G_x \approx 75\,\text{N}$, so the net force points down the slope and the box accelerates down. The mass does not affect the acceleration, because both $G_x$ and $F_\mu$ are proportional to the mass.

      2. Calculation

      A book is given a push on a tilted desktop, and it slides down at a constant velocity when the angle of the desktop is $17^\circ$.

      How large is the coefficient of kinetic friction between the book and the desktop?

      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Draw the free-body diagram of the book. In uniform motion the net force is zero, also along the slope.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      Along the slope $mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = 0$ and in the perpendicular direction $N = mg\cos\alpha$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      The mass cancels: $\mu = \tan\alpha$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      the angle of the desktop $\alpha = 17^\circ$, the book slides down at a constant velocity

      Find

      the coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu$ (no unit)

      Principle

      In uniform motion the acceleration is zero, so by Newton's second law the sum of the forces is zero in both directions. The book slides, so kinetic friction $F_\mu = \mu N$ acts on it.

      Free-body diagram

      The book as a rectangle along the desktop, $x$ down along the desktop and $y$ perpendicular to the desktop, outward. The weight $\overline{G}$ straight down and its components $mg\sin\alpha$ and $mg\cos\alpha$ dashed, the normal force $\overline{N}$ perpendicular to the desktop, outward, and the kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ up along the desktop, because the book slides down.

      Formula

      $y$: $N - mg\cos\alpha = 0$ and $x$: $mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = 0$, so

      $\mu$$= \dfrac{mg\sin\alpha}{mg\cos\alpha}$
      $= \tan\alpha$

      Conversions

      The angle is in degrees; no other conversions are needed, and the mass cancels.

      Substitution

      $\mu = \tan 17^\circ = 0.3057$

      Answer

      $0.31$

      Two significant figures like the angle $17^\circ$.

      Reasonableness

      The coefficient is positive and less than $1$, as is typical between a book and wood. A smaller angle would mean a smaller coefficient of friction: on ice a book would slide at a constant velocity already at a very gentle angle.

        1. A board is tilted slowly, and an object on it starts to slide at the angle $\alpha_0$. Why is the coefficient of static friction $\tan\alpha_0$?
          Answer

          Just before sliding the static friction is at its greatest, $\mu_0 N$, and it cancels the component of the weight along the slope: $mg\sin\alpha_0 = \mu_0\,mg\cos\alpha_0$. The mass cancels, so

          $\mu_0$$= \dfrac{\sin\alpha_0}{\cos\alpha_0}$
          $= \tan\alpha_0$
        2. A walker steps forward, and the foot pushes the ground backward. In which direction does the friction force of the ground act on the foot, and why?
          Answer

          Forward, in the direction of motion. The foot tends to slide backward relative to the ground, and static friction opposes this sliding. Friction opposes the sliding of the surfaces relative to each other, not the motion of the walker: it is friction that carries the walker forward.

          $G$$= mg_\text{Moon}$

        3. Below is example data: the kinetic friction of a wooden block measured at different normal forces. The graph shows the measurement points. Determine from the graph the coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the table.

          Answer

          $0.21$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the five measurement points $(N, F_\mu)$ of the graph of the example data: $(5\,\text{N}$; $1.1\,\text{N})$, $(10\,\text{N}$; $2.1\,\text{N})$, $(15\,\text{N}$; $3.3\,\text{N})$, $(20\,\text{N}$; $4.2\,\text{N})$ and $(25\,\text{N}$; $5.4\,\text{N})$

          Find

          the coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu$ (no unit)

          Principle

          Kinetic friction is proportional to the normal force, $F_\mu = \mu N$, so the $(N, F_\mu)$ graph is a straight line through the origin whose slope is $\mu$. The slope is read from a line fitted to the points, not from two neighbouring measurement points, because measurement points are usually not exactly on the same line because of measurement uncertainty.

          Reading the graph

          A line is drawn through the origin so that it passes as close as possible to all the points. A point far away is read from the line, for example at the normal force $N = 25\,\text{N}$ the value of the line is about $F_\mu = 5.35\,\text{N}$.

          Formula

          $\mu = \dfrac{\Delta F_\mu}{\Delta N}$, when $\Delta F_\mu$ and $\Delta N$ are read from the fitted line

          Conversions

          Both axes are in newtons, so the units cancel and no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $\mu$$= \dfrac{5.35\,\text{N} - 0\,\text{N}}{25\,\text{N} - 0\,\text{N}}$
          $= 0.214$

          Answer

          $0.21$

          Two significant figures, because the friction forces have been measured to two figures and the line is drawn by hand.

          Reasonableness

          The individual points give the ratios $0.21$–$0.22$, for example $4.2\,\text{N} : 20\,\text{N} = 0.21$, so the slope of the fitted line falls between them. The coefficient has no unit, as the ratio of two forces should not.

          Given

          The box as a rectangle, $x$ forward and $y$ up.           Answer

          $0.80\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          the angle of the slope $\alpha = 20^\circ$, the initial velocity $v_0 = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$ up the slope, $\mu = 0.25$, the final velocity $v = 0$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the sliding distance $s$ in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          When sliding up, both the component of the weight along the slope and the kinetic friction point down the slope, so the puck decelerates uniformly. The acceleration is obtained from Newton's second law, and the distance from the equation of uniformly accelerated motion $v^2 = v_0^2 - 2as$, where $a$ is the magnitude of the deceleration.

          Free-body diagram

          The puck as a rectangle along the slope, $x$ down the slope and $y$ perpendicular to the surface, outward. The weight $\overline{G}$ straight down and its components dashed, the normal force $\overline{N}$ perpendicular to the surface, outward, and the kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ down the slope, because the puck slides up. The velocity is up the slope, but it is not a force.

          Formula

          $y$: $N = mg\cos\alpha$ and $x$: $mg\sin\alpha + \mu mg\cos\alpha = ma$, so $a = g(\sin\alpha + \mu\cos\alpha)$. From the equation of motion $v^2 = v_0^2 - 2as$ and the condition $v = 0$ we get $s = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$, where $a = g(\sin\alpha + \mu\cos\alpha)$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $a$$= 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot(\sin 20^\circ + 0.25\cdot\cos 20^\circ)$
          $= 5.660\,\text{m/s}^2$

          and

          $s$$= \dfrac{(3.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{2\cdot 5.660\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 0.795\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          $0.80\,\text{m}$

          Two significant figures like the velocity $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the coefficient of friction $0.25$.

          Reasonableness

          Without friction the deceleration would be $g\sin 20^\circ \approx 3.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ and the distance about $1.3\,\text{m}$; friction shortens the distance. After stopping, the puck stays on the slope only if the coefficient of static friction is at least $\tan 20^\circ \approx 0.36$; otherwise it starts to slide back down.

        3. On Earth a box slides down a $30^\circ$ slope with an acceleration of $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          How large would the acceleration of the box be on the same slope and the same surface on the Moon, where the acceleration of free fall is $1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$? The Moon has no air, and air drag is ignored on Earth too.

          m/s²
          Answer

          $0.33\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          on Earth $\alpha = 30^\circ$ and $a_\text{Earth} = 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ down the slope, on the Moon the same slope and surface, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $g_\text{Moon} = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$; there is no air drag

          Find

          the acceleration on the Moon $a_\text{Moon}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

          Principle

          The box slides, so kinetic friction $\mu N$ acts on it up the slope. The coefficient of friction is not given, but it is a property of the surface and the same on Earth and on the Moon. The acceleration on Earth gives $\mu$, and on the Moon the same law gives the new acceleration. In the perpendicular direction the box does not accelerate.

          Free-body diagram

          The box as a rectangle along the slope, $x$ down the slope and $y$ perpendicular to the surface, outward. The weight $\overline{G}$ straight down and its components dashed, the normal force $\overline{N}$ perpendicular to the surface, outward, and the kinetic friction $\overline{F}_\mu$ up the slope.

          Formula

          $y$: $N - mg\cos\alpha = 0$ and $x$: $mg\sin\alpha - \mu N = ma$, so $a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu\cos\alpha)$ and

          $\mu$$= \tan\alpha - \dfrac{a_\text{Earth}}{g\cos\alpha}$

          Because the expression in the parentheses is the same in both places,

          $a_\text{Moon}$$= a_\text{Earth}\cdot\dfrac{g_\text{Moon}}{g}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed, and the mass cancels.

          Substitution

          $\mu$$= \tan 30^\circ - \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s}^2}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot\cos 30^\circ}$
          $= 0.577 - 0.235$
          $= 0.342$

          and

          $a_\text{Moon}$$= 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot(\sin 30^\circ - 0.342\cdot\cos 30^\circ)$
          $= 0.330\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Directly from the ratio:

          $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2\cdot\dfrac{1.62}{9.81}$$= 0.330\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $0.33\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Two significant figures like the acceleration on Earth $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          On the Moon both the weight and the friction are about a sixth of the values on Earth, so the acceleration is also about a sixth. The box slides on the Moon too, because the condition $\tan\alpha > \mu$ does not depend on the acceleration of free fall.
