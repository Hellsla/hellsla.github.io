Magnets, the magnetic dipole and the magnetic field

Why does a compass needle in the forest always point north, but swing away the moment a magnet is brought next to it?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain, using the poles of a magnet, magnetic domains and the magnetic field, why a magnet attracts iron and turns a compass.
  • draw and interpret the field lines of a bar magnet and of the Earth's magnetic field.
  • calculate the sum of perpendicular magnetic fields and the angle through which a compass needle turns.

1Think about it

An orienteer lays a compass on the map, and the needle obediently turns to point north. When she puts a torch with a small magnet in it beside the compass, the needle swings to a completely different direction. When the torch is taken away, the needle returns to north.

What turns the compass needle, and why does the magnet win that contest when it is brought close?

Optional Revise: the sum of vectors
A boat is acted on by a force of $30\,\text{N}$ towards the east and a force of $40\,\text{N}$ towards the north. What is the sum of the forces?

  • Correct. Perpendicular vectors form the legs of a right-angled triangle, and the sum is the hypotenuse: $\sqrt{(30\,\text{N})^2 + (40\,\text{N})^2} = 50\,\text{N}$. Magnetic fields are added together in this chapter in the same way.

  • Only vectors in the same direction can be added as plain numbers. Perpendicular vectors are the legs of a right-angled triangle, so the sum is $\sqrt{(30\,\text{N})^2 + (40\,\text{N})^2} = 50\,\text{N}$.

  • A difference would come from vectors in opposite directions. Perpendicular vectors are added with the Pythagorean theorem: $\sqrt{(30\,\text{N})^2 + (40\,\text{N})^2} = 50\,\text{N}$.

The field of each pole weakens with the square of the distance, and the field $\overline{B}_\text{m}$ of the magnet is the vector sum of the fields of the poles. The total field is $\overline{B} = \overline{B}_\text{m} + \overline{B}_\text{h}$. The direction of the needle from north is the angle $\theta$ for which $\tan\theta$ is the ratio of the east component to the north component of the total field.

    4Theory

    Key idea

    The poles of a magnet

    The effect of a magnet is strongest at its ends, which are called the poles of the magnet. When a bar magnet is hung from a thread so that it can turn freely, it settles in the north–south direction. The end that turns north is the north pole $\text{N}$ of the magnet and the end that turns south is the south pole $\text{S}$. Like poles of two magnets repel each other and unlike poles attract each other. Magnets act on each other even without touching, and the effect weakens quickly as the magnets move apart.

    The magnetic dipole

    When a bar magnet is cut in two, each piece becomes a complete magnet with both a north pole and a south pole. The same happens however many times the pieces are cut smaller. The poles of a magnet therefore always occur in pairs: a magnet is a magnetic dipole. This is where magnetic interaction differs from electric interaction. Positive and negative electric charge can be separated from each other, but a single magnetic pole has never been observed.

    Ferromagnetism and magnetic domains

    A magnet attracts iron, nickel, cobalt and many of their alloys, such as steel. These materials are called ferromagnetic. Each of their atoms is a small magnetic dipole. In a ferromagnetic material the dipoles of neighbouring atoms line up in the same direction, and the material typically divides into magnetic domains a few micrometres across, in each of which all the atomic dipoles point the same way. In unmagnetised iron the directions of the domains vary, so their effects cancel and the piece is not a magnet.

    When a piece of iron is brought near a magnet, the field of the magnet turns the directions of the domains and makes the domains that point along the field grow. The iron becomes magnetised: it becomes a magnet whose end nearest the magnet has the pole opposite to the magnet's pole. This is why a magnet attracts even an unmagnetised iron nail. Soft iron loses almost all of its magnetisation when the magnet is taken away, but in hard steel and in the alloy of a neodymium magnet the domains stay aligned. These are made into permanent magnets.

    Heating to a high enough temperature, for iron about $770\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, destroys the alignment, and the ferromagnetism disappears.

    Most other materials, such as aluminium, copper, wood, plastic and water, do not stick to a magnet: their magnetic effects are so weak that ordinary magnets do not reveal them.

    The magnetic field and field lines

    A magnet changes the space around it so that another magnet or a piece of iron experiences a force in it. The space that forms around a magnet in this way is called a magnetic field. The direction of the field at a point is the direction in which the north end of a compass needle placed at that point points. A compass is therefore a small test magnet with which the direction of the field can be found one point at a time.

    The field is visualised with field lines. The direction of a field line at each point is the direction of the field. Outside a magnet the field lines run from the north pole to the south pole, and inside the magnet from the south pole to the north pole, so each line is a closed loop. Where the lines are dense, the field is strong. Field lines never cross each other, because the field has only one direction at each point.

    The Earth's magnetic field

    The Earth has a magnetic field that resembles the field of a bar magnet. It arises from the motions of molten iron in the Earth's outer core and the electric currents associated with them. The north end of a compass needle turns towards geographic north. Because unlike poles attract each other, the Earth's magnetic south pole $\text{S}$ is near the geographic North Pole, and the magnetic north pole $\text{N}$ is near the geographic South Pole.

    The magnetic poles are not exactly at the geographic poles, and they wander slowly. A compass therefore deviates from true north: in Finland at present about $10^\circ$ east in the south and about $15^\circ$ east in Lapland. In Finland the field lines also dip steeply towards the ground, about $73^\circ$–$79^\circ$ below the horizontal. This angle is called the inclination.

    Combining magnetic fields

    The strength and direction of a magnetic field are described by the vector quantity magnetic flux density $\overline{B}$. Its direction is the direction of the field, and its magnitude $B$ tells how strong the field is. The unit of magnetic flux density is the tesla $\text{T}$. The definition of the tesla is based on the magnetic force on a moving charge, and it is introduced in the chapter Charged particle in electric and magnetic fields. The tesla is a large unit, so everyday fields are often given in millitesla, $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$, or in microtesla, $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.

    Object $B$
    Earth, southern Finland about $52\,\mu\text{T}$
    fridge magnet about $5\,\text{mT}$
    neodymium magnet about $0.5\,\text{T}$
    magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) $1.5$–$3\,\text{T}$
    laboratory magnet about $45\,\text{T}$

    The value of the Earth's field ($52$–$53\,\mu\text{T}$ in southern Finland) is from the World Magnetic Model 2025 (NOAA and the British Geological Survey), the fields of hospital MRI scanners are usually $1.5\,\text{T}$ or $3\,\text{T}$, and continuous fields of about $45\,\text{T}$ are produced in research laboratories, for example with the hybrid magnet of the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory in the United States. The values for magnets are orders of magnitude at the surface of the magnet.

    When two magnetic fields act at the same point, the total field is their vector sum:

    $$\overline{B} = \overline{B}_1 + \overline{B}_2$$

    where $\overline{B}_1$ and $\overline{B}_2$ are the magnetic flux densities of the fields (T) and $\overline{B}$ is the total field (T). A compass needle turns to point along the total field. When the fields are perpendicular to each other, the magnitude of the total field is given by the Pythagorean theorem and the direction by the tangent:

    $$\begin{aligned} B &= \sqrt{B_1^2 + B_2^2} \\ \tan\theta &= \frac{B_2}{B_1} \end{aligned}$$

    where $\theta$ is the angle between the total field and the field $\overline{B}_1$.

    This is used in a compass measurement. At the compass, the horizontal component $\overline{B}_\text{h}$ of the Earth's field points north. When a magnet is placed so that its field $\overline{B}_\text{m}$ at the compass points east, the needle turns from north towards east by an angle $\theta$ for which $\tan\theta = B_\text{m} : B_\text{h}$. The field of a magnet can therefore be measured with a compass when the horizontal component of the Earth's field is known.

    In Finland the Earth's field points obliquely into the ground. A compass needle turns only in the horizontal plane, so only the horizontal component of the field turns it. When the magnitude of the field is $B$ and the inclination is $I$, the components are

    $$\begin{aligned} B_\text{h} &= B\cos I \\ B_\text{v} &= B\sin I \end{aligned}$$

    where $B_\text{h}$ is the horizontal component and $B_\text{v}$ the vertical component. In southern Finland $B \approx 52\,\mu\text{T}$ and $I \approx 74^\circ$, so the horizontal component is only about $14\,\mu\text{T}$ and the vertical component about $50\,\mu\text{T}$. These are the course's rounded values: a more accurate model gives a horizontal component of about $15\,\mu\text{T}$ in Helsinki.

    Common misconception

    Many people think that the Earth's magnetic north pole is near the geographic North Pole, because the compass points there. However, the north end of a compass needle is the north pole of a magnet, and it is attracted by an unlike pole. So the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole is a south pole.

    Another mistake is to think that the poles of a magnet can be separated by cutting the magnet in the middle. Two new poles appear at the cut, and each piece becomes a complete dipole. A third mistake is to think that a magnet attracts all metals. An aluminium can or a copper coin does not stick to a magnet, because they are not ferromagnetic.

    In calculations the most common mistake is to add perpendicular fields like plain numbers. Fields of $14\,\mu\text{T}$ north and $18\,\mu\text{T}$ east give a total field of $\sqrt{(14\,\mu\text{T})^2 + (18\,\mu\text{T})^2} \approx 23\,\mu\text{T}$, not $32\,\mu\text{T}$.

    Summary

    • A magnet always has two poles, a north pole $\text{N}$ and a south pole $\text{S}$: it is a magnetic dipole. Like poles repel and unlike poles attract each other, and a single pole has never been observed.
    • In ferromagnetic materials, such as iron, the atomic dipoles form magnetic domains. The field of a magnet aligns them, so the material becomes magnetised and the magnet attracts it.
    • The direction of a magnetic field is the direction in which the north end of a compass needle points. Outside a magnet the field lines run from the north pole to the south pole, and their density tells the strength of the field.
    • The Earth's magnetic south pole is near the geographic North Pole. In Finland the Earth's field is about $52\,\mu\text{T}$ and points steeply into the ground.
    • Magnetic flux density $\overline{B}$ is a vector whose unit is the tesla $\text{T}$. Fields add as vectors: for perpendicular fields $B = \sqrt{B_1^2 + B_2^2}$ and $\tan\theta = B_2 : B_1$.

      Example 1: A compass and a bar magnet

      A compass lies on a horizontal table in southern Finland, where the horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$. A bar magnet is placed east of the compass, in the east–west direction, with its south pole $\text{S}$ towards the compass. The compass needle turns $52^\circ$ from north towards east. What is the magnetic flux density of the magnet at the compass?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The horizontal component of the Earth's field is $B_\text{h} = 14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north and the angle of deflection of the needle is $\theta = 52^\circ$ towards the east. We are asked for the magnetic flux density $B_\text{m}$ of the magnet at the compass.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The compass needle turns to point along the total field. At the compass, the field of the magnet points towards the south pole of the magnet, that is east, so it is perpendicular to the horizontal component of the Earth's field, which points north. The total field is the diagonal of a rectangle, and the tangent of the angle $\theta$ is the ratio of the opposite side to the adjacent side.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved.

      $$\begin{aligned} \tan\theta &= \frac{B_\text{m}}{B_\text{h}} \\ B_\text{m} &= B_\text{h}\tan\theta \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. Not needed: $\tan\theta$ is a plain number, so $B_\text{m}$ comes out in the same unit as $B_\text{h}$, that is in microtesla. If needed, $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. $$\begin{aligned} B_\text{m} &= B_\text{h}\tan\theta \\ &= 14\,\mu\text{T} \cdot \tan 52^\circ \\ &= 14\,\mu\text{T} \cdot 1.280 \\ &= 17.9\,\mu\text{T} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The magnetic flux density of the magnet at the compass is about $18\,\mu\text{T} = 1.8 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$. The result is given to two significant figures, because the horizontal component $14\,\mu\text{T}$ and the angle $52^\circ$ are known to two figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. At an angle of $45^\circ$ the field of the magnet would equal $B_\text{h}$. The angle is greater than $45^\circ$, so $B_\text{m}$ is greater than $14\,\mu\text{T}$, as the result is. The total field is

      $\sqrt{(14\,\mu\text{T})^2 + (17.9\,\mu\text{T})^2}$
      $\approx 22.7\,\mu\text{T}$

      and $B_\text{h} : \cos 52^\circ$ gives the same. The field is of the order of magnitude of the Earth's field, which fits a magnet about ten centimetres away.

      Example 2: Find the error

      At the compass the horizontal component of the Earth's field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north and the field of a magnet is $18\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the east. A student calculated the total field like this: $B = 14\,\mu\text{T} + 18\,\mu\text{T} = 32\,\mu\text{T}$.What is wrong with the calculation?

      • A difference would come only from fields in opposite directions. The fields pointing north and east are perpendicular, so $B = \sqrt{(14\,\mu\text{T})^2 + (18\,\mu\text{T})^2} \approx 23\,\mu\text{T}$.

      • The needle turns to point along the sum of both fields: it does not point straight east but about $52^\circ$ from north towards east. The total field is $\sqrt{(14\,\mu\text{T})^2 + (18\,\mu\text{T})^2} \approx 23\,\mu\text{T}$.

      • Correct. The fields are vectors and perpendicular to each other, so they form the legs of a right-angled triangle. The total field is the hypotenuse $22.8\,\mu\text{T}$, and it is smaller than the sum of the numerical values of the fields.

      • The same unit is not enough: only fields in the same direction can be added as plain numbers. For perpendicular fields $B = \sqrt{(14\,\mu\text{T})^2 + (18\,\mu\text{T})^2} \approx 23\,\mu\text{T}$.

      Example 3: Apply to a new situation

      On a table there is a compass and two magnets. The field of magnet A at the compass is $20\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the east and the field of magnet B is $12\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north. The horizontal component of the Earth's field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north.

      Through how many degrees does the compass needle turn from north towards east?

      °
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      Which of the fields are in the same direction? They can be added as plain numbers.

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      The fields pointing north add up to $12\,\mu\text{T} + 14\,\mu\text{T} = 26\,\mu\text{T}$. The field pointing east is $20\,\mu\text{T}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step
      $\tan\theta$$= \dfrac{20\,\mu\text{T}}{26\,\mu\text{T}}$

      when $\theta$ is measured from north.

      Show the solution

      Given

      field of magnet A $B_\text{A} = 20\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the east, field of magnet B $B_\text{B} = 12\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north, horizontal component of the Earth's field $B_\text{h} = 14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north

      Find

      angle of deflection $\theta$ of the needle from north towards east in degrees

      Principle

      The needle turns to point along the total field, and the total field is the vector sum of the fields. The field of magnet B and the Earth's field point in the same direction, so they are added as plain numbers. The field pointing east is perpendicular to them, so the angle is found from the tangent.

      Formula

      $B_\text{north}$$= B_\text{B} + B_\text{h}$
      $\tan\theta$$= \dfrac{B_\text{A}}{B_\text{north}}$

      so

      $\theta$$= \tan^{-1}\dfrac{B_\text{A}}{B_\text{B} + B_\text{h}}$

      Conversions

      Not needed: all fields are in microtesla, and the unit cancels in the ratio.

      Substitution

      $\theta$$= \tan^{-1}\dfrac{20\,\mu\text{T}}{12\,\mu\text{T} + 14\,\mu\text{T}}$
      $= \tan^{-1} 0.769$
      $= 37.6^\circ$

      Answer

      $38^\circ$

      From north towards east, to two significant figures like the fields.

      Reasonableness

      The north component $26\,\mu\text{T}$ is greater than the east component $20\,\mu\text{T}$, so the needle must turn less than $45^\circ$, as it does. Without magnet B the angle would be $55^\circ$: magnet B pulls the needle back towards north.

      4. Explanation task

      Using magnetic domains, explain why a magnet attracts an unmagnetised iron nail but not an aluminium nail.

      Model answer

      Iron is a ferromagnetic material. Its atoms are small magnetic dipoles that line up in the same direction in magnetic domains. In an unmagnetised nail the directions of the domains vary, so their effects cancel. When a magnet is brought close, its field turns the domains and makes those that point along the field grow. The nail becomes magnetised: if the north pole of the magnet is near, the nearer end of the nail becomes a south pole. Unlike poles attract each other, so the magnet attracts the nail. Aluminium is not ferromagnetic. It has no magnetic domains, and it does not become magnetised enough for the attraction to be noticed.

          Not needed: all components are in microtesla.

          Substitution

          Window:

          $B$$= \sqrt{11^2 + (-5.0)^2 + (-47)^2}\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= \sqrt{2355}\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= 48.5\,\mu\text{T}$

          Sofa:

          $B$$= \sqrt{12^2 + (-4.0)^2 + (-49)^2}\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= \sqrt{2561}\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= 50.6\,\mu\text{T}$

          Desk:

          $B$$= \sqrt{31^2 + 14^2 + (-62)^2}\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= \sqrt{5001}\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= 70.7\,\mu\text{T}$

          Answer

          a) Next to the desk the total field is $71\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the components; by the window it is $49\,\mu\text{T}$ and on the sofa $51\,\mu\text{T}$. b) There is probably iron or steel near the desk, because there the field is about $20\,\mu\text{T}$ greater than the Earth's field $50\,\mu\text{T}$ and all the components differ from the values at the window and the sofa. The fields at the window and the sofa are close to the Earth's field.

          Reasonableness

          The fields at the window and the sofa, $48.5\,\mu\text{T}$ and $50.6\,\mu\text{T}$, are almost the same as the Earth's field $50\,\mu\text{T}$, as in a place without iron. The $70.7\,\mu\text{T}$ at the desk is greater than the greatest component $62\,\mu\text{T}$, as a total field must be.

        7. Exercise 1-15

          A student solves the problem: “At a compass the horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north and the field of a magnet on the table is $30\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the east.

          Through how many degrees does the needle turn from north towards east?”

          Their solution: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{B_\text{h}}{B_\text{m}} = \dfrac{14\,\mu\text{T}}{30\,\mu\text{T}} = 0.467$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1} 0.467 = 25\,{}^\circ$.

          a) Why is the result implausible when the field of the magnet is much greater than the Earth's field? What is the mistake in the solution?

          b) Calculate the correct angle.

          Enter the result of part b) in degrees in the answer field.

          °
          Answer

          $65\,{}^\circ$

          Show the solution

          Given

          $B_\text{h} = 14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north, $B_\text{m} = 30\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the east; the result of the student $\theta = 25\,{}^\circ$ from the calculation $\tan\theta = B_\text{h} : B_\text{m}$

          Find

          the mistake in the student's solution and the correct turning angle $\theta$ of the needle from north towards east

          Principle

          The needle turns to the direction of the total field. The angle $\theta$ is measured from north, so the adjacent leg is the field pointing north, $B_\text{h}$, and the opposite leg is the field pointing east, $B_\text{m}$. The tangent is the ratio of the opposite leg to the adjacent leg, $\tan\theta = B_\text{m} : B_\text{h}$.

          Formula

          $\tan\theta$$= \dfrac{B_\text{m}}{B_\text{h}}$

          from which

          $\theta$$= \tan^{-1}\dfrac{B_\text{m}}{B_\text{h}}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: both fields are in microtesla, and the unit cancels.

          Substitution

          $\theta$$= \tan^{-1}\dfrac{30\,\mu\text{T}}{14\,\mu\text{T}}$
          $= \tan^{-1} 2.143$
          $= 65.0\,{}^\circ$

          Answer

          a) The student swapped the legs of the ratio: they divided the Earth's field by the field of the magnet, although the opposite leg $B_\text{m}$ belongs in the numerator. The result $25\,{}^\circ$ is implausible, because the field of the magnet is greater than the Earth's field, so the needle must turn more than $45\,{}^\circ$. b) The correct angle is $65\,{}^\circ$ from north towards east, two significant figures like the fields.

          The field of the magnet is more than twice the Earth's field, so the angle must be more than $45\,{}^\circ$ but less than $90\,{}^\circ$, and $65\,{}^\circ$ is. A check in the other direction: $14\,\mu\text{T} \cdot \tan 65.0\,{}^\circ = 30\,\mu\text{T}$.

        1. Exercise 1-16

          A bar magnet lies on a table in the north–south direction with its north pole $\text{N}$ towards the north. A compass is placed east of the magnet at the level of the middle of the magnet, where the field of the magnet is $25\,\mu\text{T}$. The horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north. Calculate the north component of the horizontal total field in microtesla, with north as the positive direction and south as the negative.

          Which way does the north end of the compass needle point?

          µT
          Answer

          $-11\,\mu\text{T}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          north pole of the magnet towards the north, compass east of the magnet at the level of its middle, field of the magnet there $B_\text{m} = 25\,\mu\text{T}$, horizontal component of the Earth's field $B_\text{h} = 14\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the north

          Find

          north component $B_y$ of the horizontal total field in microtesla (north positive) and the direction of the needle

          Principle

          Outside the magnet the field lines run from the north pole to the south pole, so at the side of the magnet at the level of its middle the field is parallel to the magnet and points from the north-pole end to the south-pole end, that is towards the south. It is opposite to the Earth's field, so the fields are added together with their signs. The needle turns to point along the total field, so the sign of the component tells whether the needle points north or south.

          Formula

          with north positive, $B_y = B_\text{h} - B_\text{m}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: both fields are in microtesla.

          Substitution

          $B_y$$= 14\,\mu\text{T} - 25\,\mu\text{T}$
          $= -11\,\mu\text{T}$

          Answer

          $-11\,\mu\text{T}$

          That is $11\,\mu\text{T}$ towards the south, two significant figures like the fields. The sign is negative, so the north end of the needle points south: the needle has turned round.

          Reasonableness

          The field of the magnet is greater than the Earth's field, so it wins and the total field points in the direction of the field of the magnet. If the field of the magnet were exactly $14\,\mu\text{T}$, the horizontal total field would be zero and the needle would settle in no direction.

        2. Exercise 1-17

          A steel paper clip is drawn several times in the same direction along a pole of a magnet, after which it picks up small pins. When the clip is heated in a flame hot enough and allowed to cool, it no longer picks up pins. Explain both observations using magnetic domains.

          Answer

          The field of a magnet aligns the magnetic domains of the steel, and in hard steel they stay aligned, so the clip is a magnet. Heating to a high enough temperature mixes up the alignment of the domains, so the clip is no longer a magnet.

          Show the solution

          Find

          why a steel clip drawn along a magnet becomes a magnet and why heating removes the magnetisation

          Principle

          Steel is ferromagnetic. Its atomic dipoles form magnetic domains whose directions vary in an unmagnetised piece. An external field aligns the domains, and in hard steel the alignment is kept. At a high enough temperature the thermal motion of the atoms destroys the alignment of the atomic dipoles.

          Model answer

          At first the domains of the clip point in different directions, and their effects cancel. When a pole of the magnet is drawn along the clip, always in the same direction, the field of the magnet turns the domains and makes those that point along the field grow. In hard steel the domains stay aligned when the magnet is taken away, so the clip becomes a permanent magnet with its own north pole and south pole. It magnetises the pins and attracts them. When the clip is heated hot enough (the Curie temperature of iron is about $770\,{}^\circ\text{C}$), the thermal motion of the atoms mixes up the alignment of the atomic dipoles and the domains disappear. When the clip cools, the domains form again but in random directions, so the clip is no longer a magnet.

        3. Exercise 1-18

          According to the model of the Earth's magnetic field (World Magnetic Model 2025) the field in Helsinki is about $53\,\mu\text{T}$ and points about $74^\circ$ below the horizontal. In Longyearbyen in Svalbard the field is about $55\,\mu\text{T}$ and points about $82.5^\circ$ below the horizontal.

          How many times greater is the horizontal component of the field in Helsinki than in Longyearbyen? Why does a compass work worse in Longyearbyen, although the whole field is greater there?

          Answer

          $2.0$

          Show the solution

          Given

          Helsinki $B_1 = 53\,\mu\text{T}$ and $I_1 = 74^\circ$, Longyearbyen $B_2 = 55\,\mu\text{T}$ and $I_2 = 82.5^\circ$ below the horizontal

          Find

          ratio of the horizontal components $k = B_\text{h1} : B_\text{h2}$, a plain number, and the reason why a compass works worse

          Principle

          A compass needle turns only in the horizontal plane, so it is steered only by the horizontal component of the field, $B_\text{h} = B\cos I$. The more steeply the field points into the ground, the smaller the horizontal part of it.

          Formula

          $k$$= \dfrac{B_\text{h1}}{B_\text{h2}}$
          $= \dfrac{B_1\cos I_1}{B_2\cos I_2}$

          Conversions

          Not needed: both fields are in microtesla, and the unit cancels.

          Substitution

          $k$$= \dfrac{53\,\mu\text{T} \cdot \cos 74^\circ}{55\,\mu\text{T} \cdot \cos 82.5^\circ}$
          $= \dfrac{14.6\,\mu\text{T}}{7.18\,\mu\text{T}}$
          $= 2.03$

          Answer

          about $2.0$ times, two significant figures like the fields $53\,\mu\text{T}$ and $55\,\mu\text{T}$. In Longyearbyen the field is almost vertical, so its horizontal component is only about half of the value in Helsinki.

          Model answer (why)

          Only the horizontal component turns the needle. In Longyearbyen it is only about half of that in Helsinki, even though the whole field is greater, because the field points almost straight into the ground. The field that turns the needle is weak, so the needle settles slowly and even small disturbances, such as an iron object nearby, turn it easily. The answer is correct if it says that a compass is steered by the horizontal component and not by the whole field.

          Reasonableness

          The whole field is a little greater in Longyearbyen, but $\cos 82.5^\circ \approx 0.13$ is much smaller than $\cos 74^\circ \approx 0.28$, so the ratio is close to two. At the magnetic pole the field would be vertical, the horizontal component zero and the compass would not work at all.
