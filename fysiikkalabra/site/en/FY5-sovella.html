Challenge tasks

In these tasks nobody tells you which formula to use. Decide yourself which phenomena and models belong to the situation, solve the task on paper and justify your solution, as in the matriculation examination.

Four tasks bring the module together: rotation and equilibrium, circular motion and gravitation, oscillation, and waves and sound. In each one you decide which models fit the situation and justify your choice.

Solve the task yourself first, on paper or on a computer. You may use a calculator, a data booklet and graphing software. Write out your reasoning, formulas, substitutions and answers with units.

These cards of the module are in your review deck. Go through them before you start the tasks.

1Tower crane 18 points

Made-up example data · The dimensions and masses of the crane are made up, but they are of the same order of magnitude as in a small tower crane on a building site.

The horizontal jib of a tower crane on a building site is at the top of the tower, and it can turn about the vertical axis of the tower. The load hangs on a cable from a trolley that can be driven along the jib. On the other side of the tower, the jib continues as a counter-jib with a concrete counterweight at its end.

Data of the crane:

  • the mass of the jib with its counter-jib is $4.0\,\text{t}$, with its centre of mass $12\,\text{m}$ from the axis of the tower on the side of the load
  • the mass of the counterweight is $9.0\,\text{t}$, with its centre of mass $10\,\text{m}$ from the axis of the tower on the other side
  • the greatest distance of the trolley from the axis of the tower is $40\,\text{m}$
  • in the crane model of this problem, the total moment of the weights about the moment axis A may be at most $600\,\text{kNm}$ in either direction.

The jib turns about the vertical axis of the tower, but the moments of the weights are calculated about the horizontal moment axis A. The axis A passes through the tower at the height of the jib and is perpendicular to the vertical plane of the jib; in the figure it appears as a point. The masses of the trolley and the cable can be ignored.

The moment limit is an idealised model made up for this problem. The safe load of a real tower crane is read from the manufacturer's load chart, and its design also takes into account, for example, wind, the swinging of the load, the strength of the structures and the foundation of the tower.

  1. a) The crane lifts a $2.0\,\text{t}$ concrete element. How far from the axis of the tower must the trolley be driven so that the total moment of the weights about the moment axis A is zero? 3 p
  2. b) How large a load may the crane lift when the trolley is at the tip of the jib? 3 p
  3. c) The site foreman suggests: "Let's add a couple of tonnes to the counterweight so that heavier loads can be lifted from the tip of the jib." Evaluate the suggestion. 4 p
  4. d) The operator turns the jib with the load of part a steadily so that one revolution would take $90\,\text{s}$. The load hangs on a $15\,\text{m}$ cable, and the cable tilts slightly from the vertical so that the load is farther from the axis of the tower than the trolley. The operator explains: "Centrifugal force throws the load outwards." Draw the free-body diagram of the load while the jib turns steadily and evaluate the operator's explanation. 4 p
  5. e) What angle does the cable make with the vertical? Does the tilt matter? 4 p
Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Given

load $m = 2.0\,\text{t} = 2000\,\text{kg}$, jib $m_\text{p} = 4.0\,\text{t}$ and $r_\text{p} = 12\,\text{m}$, counterweight $m_\text{v} = 9.0\,\text{t}$ and $r_\text{v} = 10\,\text{m}$

Find

the distance $r$ of the trolley from the axis of the tower when the total moment of the weights is zero

Principle

Three weights act on the jib: the weight of the load at the trolley (through the cable), the weight of the jib itself at its centre of mass, and the weight of the counterweight. The jib turns about the vertical axis of the tower, but the vertical weights are parallel to this axis and do not turn the jib about it. The weights tend to tip the jib in its vertical plane, so the moments are calculated about the horizontal moment axis A, which passes through the tower perpendicular to this plane. The lever arm of each weight is the perpendicular distance of its line of action from the axis A. The weights are vertical and the axis horizontal, so the lever arm is the horizontal distance of the weight from the axis of the tower. The load and the weight of the jib turn the jib towards the side of the load, the counterweight in the opposite direction. In equilibrium with respect to rotation the sum of the moments is zero.

Formula

$m_\text{p} g r_\text{p} + m g r - m_\text{v} g r_\text{v} = 0$. The acceleration of free fall cancels, so

$r$$= \dfrac{m_\text{v} r_\text{v} - m_\text{p} r_\text{p}}{m}$

Conversions

$1\,\text{t} = 1000\,\text{kg}$, so $m_\text{p} = 4000\,\text{kg}$ and $m_\text{v} = 9000\,\text{kg}$.

Substitution

$r$$= \dfrac{9000\,\text{kg} \cdot 10\,\text{m} - 4000\,\text{kg} \cdot 12\,\text{m}}{2000\,\text{kg}}$
$= \dfrac{42\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{2000\,\text{kg}}$
$= 21\,\text{m}$

Answer

$r \approx 21\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the distances and masses.

Reasonableness

The moment of the counterweight is about twice the moment of the jib, so the load has to make up the difference. A light load has to be far out, and the point is within the $40\,\text{m}$ length of the jib.

b)

Given

the data of part a, trolley at the tip of the jib $r_\text{max} = 40\,\text{m}$, permitted total moment $M_\text{max} = 600\,\text{kNm}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the greatest load $m$ at the tip of the jib

Principle

When the load is at the tip, the total moment points towards the side of the load, and with the greatest permitted load it is equal to the limit. The moments are calculated about the moment axis A as in part a, with the positive direction of rotation towards the side of the load.

Formula

$m g r_\text{max} + m_\text{p} g r_\text{p} - m_\text{v} g r_\text{v}$
$= M_\text{max}$

so

$m$$= \dfrac{M_\text{max} + m_\text{v} g r_\text{v} - m_\text{p} g r_\text{p}}{g r_\text{max}}$

Conversions

$M_\text{max}$$= 600\,\text{kNm}$
$= 600\,000\,\text{Nm}$

Substitution

$m_\text{p} g r_\text{p}$$= 4000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 12\,\text{m}$
$= 470\,880\,\text{Nm}$
$m_\text{v} g r_\text{v}$$= 9000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 10\,\text{m}$
$= 882\,900\,\text{Nm}$

The moment of the load may therefore be

$600\,000\,\text{Nm} + 882\,900\,\text{Nm} - 470\,880\,\text{Nm}$
$= 1\,012\,020\,\text{Nm}$

so

$m$$= \dfrac{1\,012\,020\,\text{Nm}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 40\,\text{m}}$
$= 2579\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$m \approx 2.6\,\text{t}$

Two significant figures like the data of the crane.

Reasonableness

Without a load the total moment is $882.9\,\text{kNm} - 470.9\,\text{kNm} = 412\,\text{kNm}$ towards the counterweight, so the limit is not exceeded even by the empty crane. The load at the tip is smaller than the load the crane carries closer to the tower, as in the load charts of cranes.

c)

Find

whether the counterweight can be increased by a couple of tonnes so that heavier loads can be lifted from the tip

Principle

The moment of the counterweight turns the jib in the opposite direction to the load. The limit applies to the total moment in both directions, so both the fully loaded and the empty crane must be checked.

Model answer

The loaded crane. A larger counterweight reduces the total moment on the side of the load, so a heavier load could be lifted at the tip. The foreman is right about this.

The empty crane. Without a load the total moment points towards the counterweight: now it is $412\,\text{kNm}$. An addition of two tonnes increases it by $2000\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 10\,\text{m} = 196.2\,\text{kNm}$, so the total moment becomes $608\,\text{kNm}$. This exceeds the limit of $600\,\text{kNm}$, and the tower is overloaded on the side of the counterweight whenever there is no load on the hook.

How much could be added. For the empty crane there is room of $600\,\text{kNm} - 412\,\text{kNm} = 188\,\text{kNm}$ below the limit, so the counterweight can be increased by at most $188\,000\,\text{Nm} : (9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 10\,\text{m}) \approx 1.9\,\text{t}$. Then the greatest load at the tip would be $1200\,\text{kNm} : 392.4\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \approx 3.1\,\text{t}$.

Conclusion. The suggestion does not work as it stands. In the model of the problem the counterweight is dimensioned so that the limit is not exceeded with the empty or the fully loaded crane. Instead of two tonnes, the counterweight could be increased by at most about $1.9\,\text{t}$, and the lifting capacity cannot be increased further without a sturdier tower. For a real crane one moment condition is not enough: the counterweight may be changed only as the manufacturer instructs, because safety also depends on, for example, the wind, the strength of the structures and the foundation.

d)

Find

the free-body diagram of the load while the jib turns steadily, and an evaluation of the operator's explanation

Principle

The load is chosen as the object. The bodies interacting with it are the Earth (weight) and the cable (tension); nothing else touches the load, and air resistance is small at low speed. When the jib turns steadily, the load moves in a horizontal circle about the axis of the tower, so it has a centripetal acceleration towards the axis. The net force must then point towards the axis.

Model answer

The load is drawn as a rectangle tilted along the cable.
- The weight $\overline{G}$ starts from the centre and points straight down.
- The tension $\overline{S}$ of the cable starts from the upper surface where the cable is attached and points along the cable upwards and slightly towards the axis of the tower, because the load is farther out than the trolley.

In the vertical direction the load does not accelerate, so the vertical component of the tension is equal to the weight, and the tension is slightly larger than the weight. The horizontal component of the tension is the net force, and it points towards the axis of the tower like the centripetal acceleration $\overline{a}_\text{n}$, which is drawn separately in its own colour. In the model picture the angle of the cable has been drawn much larger than in the problem so that the arrows can be told apart.

The operator's explanation. There is no real centrifugal force: no body pulls the load outwards, and there is no outward force in the free-body diagram. When the jib starts to turn, the load tends to continue straight ahead because of its inertia. That is why it stays on the outer edge of the circle and the cable tilts, until the horizontal component of the tension is enough to turn the motion of the load onto a circular path. So the load is not thrown outwards but lags behind its straight path only by as much as the cable needs to pull it towards the centre.

e)

Given

the radius $r = 21\,\text{m}$ of part a, period $T = 90\,\text{s}$, cable length $15\,\text{m}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the angle $\theta$ between the cable and the vertical, and whether it matters

Principle

The load moves in uniform circular motion, so its acceleration is the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the axis of the tower. The speed is obtained from the period. Newton's second law is written for the free-body diagram of part d by components: axis $x$ horizontally towards the axis of the tower and $y$ upwards. The tilt moves the load only by centimetres, so the distance of the trolley, $21\,\text{m}$, can be used as the radius of the circle.

Formula

$x$: $S\sin\theta = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $y$: $S\cos\theta - mg = 0$. Dividing the equations by each other gives $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^2}{rg}$, where $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$. The mass cancels.

Conversions

Not needed.

Substitution

$v$$= \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 21\,\text{m}}{90\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.466\,\text{m/s}$
$\tan\theta$$= \dfrac{(1.466\,\text{m/s})^2}{21\,\text{m} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= 0.01043$

so $\theta = 0.598^\circ$

Answer

$\theta \approx 0.60^\circ$

Two significant figures like the period $90\,\text{s}$. The load has moved outwards from the trolley by $15\,\text{m} \cdot 0.01043 \approx 0.16\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

The centripetal acceleration

$a_\text{n}$$= (1.466\,\text{m/s})^2 : 21\,\text{m}$
$\approx 0.10\,\text{m/s}^2$

is only about one percent of the acceleration of free fall, so the angle is small and the tension is practically equal to the weight. Steady turning therefore hardly matters in lifting work. A sudden start or stop, on the other hand, makes the load swing, because the cable cannot immediately give the load the same acceleration as the trolley.

Also accepted

  • The moments of parts a and b can be calculated as products of mass and distance without the acceleration of free fall, when $g$ cancels, as long as in part b the limit is changed to the same form: $600\,000\,\text{Nm} : 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 61\,160\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}$.
  • In part b the answers $2.6\,\text{t}$ and $2580\,\text{kg}$ are accepted.
  • In part e the angle may also be obtained by drawing the forces to scale when the centripetal acceleration has been calculated correctly, and $0.6^\circ$ is accepted as the answer.

Common mistakes

  • The moments are thought to be calculated about the vertical slewing axis of the tower, although the vertical weights do not turn the jib about it. The moments of the weights are calculated about the horizontal moment axis A.
  • The weight of the jib itself is forgotten, or its moment is calculated from the end of the jib instead of its centre of mass.
  • The directions of rotation of the moments are mixed up, so the counterweight and the weight of the jib are calculated in the same direction.
  • In part b the limit is interpreted so that the total moment must be zero, which gives the load at the tip as only $42\,000\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m} : 40\,\text{m} \approx 1.1\,\text{t}$.
  • In part c only the loaded crane is checked and it is not noticed that the limit applies to the empty crane too.
  • A centrifugal force pointing outwards, or the centripetal acceleration as a force, is drawn in the free-body diagram.
  • In part e the rotation frequency is used wrongly instead of the period, or the angle is calculated using the length of the cable, although it does not affect the angle.

2Moons of Jupiter 15 points

Statistical data · NASA JPL Solar System Dynamics, mean orbital radii and orbital periods of the moons (checked 2026), rounded.

Galileo Galilei discovered the four large moons of Jupiter in 1610. The table gives the mean radii $r$ of their orbits, measured from the centre of Jupiter, and their orbital periods $T$, together with the small moon Amalthea, which orbits closer to Jupiter. The orbits of the moons are almost circles.

moon $r\ (10^3\,\text{km})$ $T\ (\text{d})$
Amalthea $181.4$ $0.4982$
Io $421.8$ $1.769$
Europa $671.1$ $3.551$
Ganymede $1070$ $7.155$
Callisto $1883$ $16.69$

The gravitational constant is $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$.

  1. a) Model the motion of the moons as uniform circular motion maintained by the gravity of Jupiter. Make a graph from the data so that, according to the model, a straight line through the origin fits its points. 5 p
  2. b) Determine the mass of Jupiter from the graph. 3 p
  3. c) Jupiter rotates once about its axis in $9.93\,\text{h}$. Jupiter is not a solid body, and its cloud belts rotate at slightly different rates. Assume that the equatorial cloud formation in question goes round the axis of Jupiter with exactly this period of $9.93\,\text{h}$. A probe is to move in a circular orbit above the equator so that it stays above this cloud formation all the time. How far from the centre of Jupiter is the orbit of the probe? 3 p
  4. d) A student claims: "Ganymede is the most massive moon of Jupiter. If it were moved to the orbit of Io, it would orbit Jupiter faster than Io, because a larger gravitational force would act on it." Evaluate the claim. What assumptions does the result of part b require? 4 p

Model answer

a)

Find

a graph whose points, according to the circular-orbit model, fit a straight line through the origin

Graph

In the model, the moon is kept in a circular orbit by the gravity of Jupiter, which is the only significant force acting on the moon. It is the centripetal force of the circular motion of the moon: $\gamma\dfrac{Mm}{r^2} = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so $v^2 = \gamma M \cdot \dfrac{1}{r}$. The orbital speed is calculated from the period: $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$, with the radius in metres and the period in seconds ($1\,\text{d} = 86\,400\,\text{s}$). For example, for Io $v = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 421.8 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}{1.769 \cdot 86\,400\,\text{s}} = 17\,340\,\text{m/s}$, so $v^2 = 3.01 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$ and $\tfrac{1}{r} = 2.37 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{m}}$.

moon $v\ (\text{km/s})$ $\tfrac{1}{r}\ (10^{-9}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{m}})$ $v^2\ (10^{8}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2})$
Amalthea $26.5$ $5.51$ $7.01$
Io $17.3$ $2.37$ $3.01$
Europa $13.7$ $1.49$ $1.89$
Ganymede $10.9$ $0.935$ $1.18$
Callisto $8.21$ $0.531$ $0.673$

$\tfrac{1}{r}$ is chosen for the horizontal axis and $v^2$ for the vertical axis.

Model answer

All five points are on the same straight line through the origin, so the circular-orbit model, in which gravity is the centripetal force, describes the motion of the moons well. The line passes through the origin because according to the model the orbital speed would be zero infinitely far away ($\tfrac{1}{r} = 0$). According to the model, the slope of the line is $\gamma M$, which is the same for all the moons, although the masses of the moons are very different.

b)

Given

the graph of part a and $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

Find

the mass $M$ of Jupiter

Principle

According to the model $v^2 = \gamma M \cdot \tfrac{1}{r}$, so the slope $k$ of the line is $\gamma M$. The slope is read from a point of the line far from the origin, so that the relative error of the reading is small.

Reading the graph

The line passes through the origin and the point $(5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{m}}$; $6.35 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2)$, so

$k$$= \dfrac{6.35 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{m}}}$
$= 1.27 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$

Formula

$k = \gamma M$, so $M = \dfrac{k}{\gamma}$

Conversions

$1\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$$= 1\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{s}^2}$

because $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$.

Substitution

$M$$= \dfrac{1.27 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{s}^2}}$
$= 1.90 \cdot 10^{27}\,\text{kg}$

Answer

$M \approx 1.9 \cdot 10^{27}\,\text{kg}$

Two significant figures, because the slope is read from the graph to an accuracy of about two or three figures.

Reasonableness

The value in the data book is $1.898 \cdot 10^{27}\,\text{kg}$, about $320$ Earth masses, so the result is correct. Jupiter is the most massive planet in the Solar System, and its mass is about a thousandth of the mass of the Sun.

c)

Given

the rotation period of Jupiter $T = 9.93\,\text{h}$ and $\gamma M = 1.27 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ from part b

Find

the radius $r$ of the orbit of the probe

Principle

The probe stays above the same cloud formation only if it orbits Jupiter in the same direction and in the same time as the cloud formation, which the problem assumes to go round in the rotation period of Jupiter, $9.93\,\text{h}$. The orbit is thus an equatorial circular orbit synchronised with the rotation of Jupiter. The centripetal force of the circular motion of the probe is the gravity of Jupiter, as for the moons, so $v^2 = \dfrac{\gamma M}{r}$, and the orbital speed is $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$.

Formula

$\dfrac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}$$= \dfrac{\gamma M}{r}$

so

$r^3$$= \dfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}$
$r$$= \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\gamma M T^2}{4\pi^2}}$

Conversions

$T$$= 9.93 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$
$= 35\,748\,\text{s}$

Substitution

$r^3$$= \dfrac{1.27 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2 \cdot (35\,748\,\text{s})^2}{4\pi^2}$
$= 4.11 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{m}^3$

so $r = 1.60 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m}$

Answer

$r \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m} = 160\,000\,\text{km}$

From the centre of Jupiter. Two significant figures like the result of part b.

Reasonableness

The period $9.93\,\text{h}$ is shorter than that of Amalthea, $0.4982\,\text{d} \approx 12\,\text{h}$, so the orbit must be inside the orbit of Amalthea ($181\,400\,\text{km}$), as it is. The radius of Jupiter is about $71\,500\,\text{km}$, so the probe would orbit at a height of about $89\,000\,\text{km}$ above the clouds.

d)

Find

an evaluation of the claim that Ganymede would orbit faster than Io on the orbit of Io, and the assumptions of the result of part b

Principle

The orbital speed is obtained from the condition that gravity is the centripetal force of the circular motion of the moon: $\gamma\dfrac{Mm}{r^2} = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, where $m$ is the mass of the moon.

Model answer

The claim. It is true that at the same distance a larger gravitational force acts on a more massive moon, because the force is proportional to the mass $m$ of the moon. However, a more massive moon needs a correspondingly larger centripetal force to stay on the same circular orbit, because the required force $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ is also proportional to the mass. The mass of the moon cancels from the equation, leaving $v^2 = \dfrac{\gamma M}{r}$: the orbital speed depends only on the mass $M$ of Jupiter and the radius of the orbit. On the orbit of Io, Ganymede would therefore orbit with the same speed and in the same time as Io. The claim is not correct. The same can be seen from the graph of part a: moons of very different masses are on the same line.

The assumptions. The result of part b requires that
- the orbits of the moons are circles whose centre is the centre of Jupiter; in reality Jupiter and the moon orbit their common centre of mass, but the masses of the moons are so small compared with the mass of Jupiter that the difference is insignificant
- only the gravity of Jupiter acts on the moon; the attractions of the other moons and the Sun are small in comparison
- the mass of Jupiter pulls as if it were concentrated at its centre, which holds well for a spherically symmetric body.

Also accepted

  • In part a the graph can also be drawn from the quantities $r^3$ and $T^2$, because the model gives $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\gamma M}\,r^3$; then in part b $M = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\gamma k}$. The points then cluster near the origin, so the slope should be read near the point of Callisto.
  • In part b slopes of $1.22\text{–}1.32 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ and masses of $1.8\text{–}2.0 \cdot 10^{27}\,\text{kg}$ are accepted. A fit through the origin by a calculator or spreadsheet is also accepted.
  • Part c can also be calculated from the period alone and a Kepler-type comparison with some moon, for example $r = r_\text{Io}\,\left(\tfrac{T}{T_\text{Io}}\right)^{2/3}$, as long as the relationship is justified from the circular-orbit model.

Common mistakes

  • The orbital speed is calculated in kilometres and days, but $\gamma$ is substituted in base units.
  • The points are drawn in an $(r, v)$ coordinate system and a straight line is fitted to them, although the set of points is curved.
  • The height above the surface of Jupiter is taken as the radius of the orbit, or the radius is measured from the surface of the moon.
  • In part c $24\,\text{h}$ is used as the period, as for the geostationary orbit of the Earth.
  • In part d it is only stated that "the mass does not matter", without showing that it cancels from the equation.

c)

Find

what the deviation of the line from the origin can be due to and how reliable the spring constant of part b is

Principle

Random error scatters the points on both sides of the line. A systematic error or a deficiency of the model shifts the results in the same direction. The result is reliable when different methods give the same value within the uncertainties.

Model answer

Uncertainty. In hand timing the uncertainty due to reaction time is about $0.2\,\text{s}$. Spread over ten periods, it is only $0.02\,\text{s}$ in the period, which is the reason for measuring ten periods. The points deviate from the line by at most about $0.005\,\text{s}^2$, so the random variation is small and does not explain the intercept of $0.028\,\text{s}^2$ on the vertical axis.

The cause of the deviation. In the model $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}}$ only the mass oscillates and the spring is massless. The mass of the spring itself, $45\,\text{g}$, however, moves along: its lower end moves with the mass and its upper end is at rest. The oscillating mass is therefore larger than the hanging mass alone, which shifts the whole line upwards but does not change its slope. From the intercept the extra mass is $0.028\,\text{s}^2 : 1.984\,\text{s}^2/\text{kg} \approx 0.014\,\text{kg}$, about a third of the mass of the spring. The result makes sense, because the lower part of the spring moves almost as much as the mass and the upper part hardly at all.

Comparison. From the measurement at rest $k = \dfrac{mg}{x} = \dfrac{0.200\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}{0.098\,\text{m}} \approx 20.0\,\text{N/m}$. It differs from the result of part b, $19.9\,\text{N/m}$, by less than one percent, so two independent methods support each other.

Conclusion. There is no contradiction: the spring constant is reliable, because it is obtained from the slope, which the mass of the spring does not affect. If the line had been forced through the origin, the spring constant would have been too small, about $18.9\,\text{N/m}$.

Given

Find

Principle

The energy of the oscillation is conserved when friction and air resistance are small. At an extreme position the mass is momentarily at rest, and the whole energy $\tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$ is potential energy. At the equilibrium position the displacement is zero, so the whole energy is kinetic energy and the speed is greatest. For a vertical spring the weight only shifts the equilibrium position, so the displacement is measured from the equilibrium position. In the model of the problem the mass of the spring itself is taken into account as in part c: the lower part of the spring moves with the mass, so a third of the mass of the spring is added to the mass in the kinetic energy.

Formula

$\tfrac{1}{2}m_\text{eff}v_\text{max}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$, where $m_\text{eff} = m + \tfrac{1}{3}m_\text{s}$, so

$v_\text{max}$$= A\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m_\text{eff}}}$

Conversions

$A$$= 5.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 0.050\,\text{m}$
$m_\text{s}$$= 45\,\text{g}$
$= 0.045\,\text{kg}$

Substitution

$m_\text{eff}$$= 0.250\,\text{kg} + 0.045\,\text{kg} : 3$
$= 0.265\,\text{kg}$
$v_\text{max}$$= 0.050\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{19.9\,\text{N/m}}{0.265\,\text{kg}}}$
$= 0.050\,\text{m} \cdot 8.67\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
$= 0.433\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v_\text{max} \approx 0.43\,\text{m/s}$

Two significant figures like the amplitude $5.0\,\text{cm}$.

Reasonableness

The period is about $2\pi\sqrt{0.265\,\text{kg} : 19.9\,\text{N/m}} \approx 0.73\,\text{s}$, and during a period the mass travels $4A = 20\,\text{cm}$, so its average speed is about $0.28\,\text{m/s}$. a)

Given

Find

Principle

The period of a periodic motion is the time after which the whole waveform starts to repeat in the same form. The signal is not a sine wave: each period has a high and a low peak, so the distance between two adjacent peaks is not the period. The reading is more accurate when the duration of several periods is read.

Reading the graph

The high peaks are at $1\,\text{ms}$, $5\,\text{ms}$, $9\,\text{ms}$, $13\,\text{ms}$ and $17\,\text{ms}$. The graph has five whole periods in a time of $20.0\,\text{ms}$, so

$T$$= \dfrac{20.0\,\text{ms}}{5}$
$= 4.0\,\text{ms}$

Formula

$f = \dfrac{1}{T}$

Conversions

$T = 4.0\,\text{ms} = 0.0040\,\text{s}$

Substitution

$f$$= \dfrac{1}{0.0040\,\text{s}}$
$= 250\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
$= 250\,\text{Hz}$

Answer

$f = 250\,\text{Hz}$

Two or three significant figures: the duration of five periods can be read accurately from the guide lines.

Given

Principle

A standing wave forms in the air column of the pipe when the sound wave is reflected from the ends of the tube. At an open end the air can move freely, so there is an antinode of the oscillation there. When both ends are open, in the fundamental mode the tube has two antinodes at the ends and one node in the middle, so the length of the tube is half the wavelength. The wavelength is obtained from the basic equation of waves $v = f\lambda$.

Formula

$L = \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$ and $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$, so $L = \dfrac{v}{2f}$

Conversions

Not needed.

Substitution

$L$$= \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 250\,\text{Hz}}$
$= 0.686\,\text{m}$

Answer

$L \approx 0.686\,\text{m}$

Three significant figures like the speed of sound.

c)

Given

Principle

The wavelength of the standing wave is determined by the length of the pipe, $\lambda = 2L$, which practically does not change. In cold air sound travels more slowly, so with the same wavelength the frequency $f = \tfrac{v}{\lambda}$ decreases in the same proportion as the speed of sound.

$f'$$= \dfrac{335.8\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 0.686\,\text{m}}$

The fundamental frequency decreases by about $5\,\text{Hz}$ (more precisely $5.2\,\text{Hz}$) to about $245\,\text{Hz}$. The change is known to one significant figure, because the change of speed $0.6\,\text{m/s}$ per degree is given to one figure.

Answer

$t_\text{ref} \approx 1\,\text{h}$

The dose does not stay below the reference dose: three hours of practice is about three times the reference time, so the dose is about $300\,\%$ of the reference dose. The organist should use hearing protection or practise with quieter stops.

Reasonableness

A level of $94\,\text{dB}$ is of the same order as the noise of a lawn mower at the ear of the user, when hearing protection is recommended. In the point-source model the sound power of the organ would be

$P$$= I \cdot 4\pi r^2$
$\approx 0.13\,\text{W}$

which is plausible for an instrument.

e)

Find

an evaluation of the assumptions of the model of part d, the direction of the real exposure, and a reliable way to find it out

Principle

The point-source model assumes that sound starts from a single point and travels without obstacles or reflections. For each assumption we consider whether the level at the organist would in reality be larger or smaller than the model gives.

Model answer

The organ is not a point source. The pipes are spread over an area several metres wide and high, and the distance of the organist from most of the pipes is much more than $2\,\text{m}$. Near an extended source the level does not rise by $6\,\text{dB}$ every time the distance is halved, so the $94\,\text{dB}$ of part d is probably too large an estimate.

Reflections. The walls, floor and vaults of the church reflect sound, and reverberation is heard throughout the hall. Part of the level measured at a distance of $4.0\,\text{m}$ is reverberation, which does not get louder closer up. This also makes the estimate too large.

Playing. During practice the organ is seldom played at full power all the time, so the average level is lower. On the other hand, in some churches the position of the pipes and the console may direct sound straight at the organist.

A reliable way. A sound level meter measures the A-weighted level at the organist's ear throughout a normal practice and the daily noise exposure is calculated from it as an energy average and compared with the action values $80\,\text{dB}$ and $85\,\text{dB}$. The exposure is probably smaller than the estimate of part d, but only a measurement tells whether hearing protection is needed. Even staying below an action value does not guarantee safe hearing, so hearing protection is worth using in loud practices.

Also accepted

  • In part a the period can also be read from the duration of two or three periods; answers of $240\text{–}260\,\text{Hz}$ are accepted.
  • In part c the answers $5\,\text{Hz}$ and $5.2\,\text{Hz}$ are accepted. The change can also be calculated from the ratio $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f} = \dfrac{\Delta v}{v}$.
  • In part d the change of level can also be calculated from the intensities: $I_1 = 10^{-12}\,\tfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot 10^{8.8}$ and $I_2 = 4I_1$.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the distance between two adjacent peaks, $2.0\,\text{ms}$, is taken as the period, which wrongly gives a frequency of $500\,\text{Hz}$.
  • In part b the condition $L = \tfrac{\lambda}{4}$ for a tube closed at one end is used, although the pipe is open at both ends.
  • In part c it is assumed that the wavelength changes and the frequency stays the same, or the change of frequency is calculated to be the same as the change of speed.
  • In part d the level is assumed to double when the distance is halved, or the intensity is assumed to depend on the distance and not on its square.
  • The reference time is calculated by dividing $8\,\text{h}$ by the difference of the levels instead of halving the time for each step of $3\,\text{dB}$.
