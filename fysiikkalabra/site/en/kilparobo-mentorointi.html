Competitive robotics

Mentoring – students teach students

A more experienced student mentors a new student: shows, asks and gives feedback. Mentoring needs no forms. It is enough that the new student knows whom to turn to.

  • Start
  • Beginner's path
  • Season and diary
  • Mentoring
  • Safety

The mentor's tasks

  • Welcomes the new student.
  • Introduces safe working following the instructions the teacher has approved.
  • Shows the basic functions of the devices.
  • Helps with the first building and programming tasks.
  • Asks guiding questions instead of giving ready answers.
  • Encourages solving problems independently.
  • Helps with documentation.
  • Gives feedback.

The mentor's checklist

  • Introduce the team, the workspace and the robot.
  • Go through the safety instructions as the teacher has given them.
  • Show once, then let the new student do it.
  • Keep your own hands off the robot: guide with words.
  • Ask before you tell.
  • Let mistakes happen when they are safe. They teach.
  • Notice effort and progress.
  • Help write an entry in the development diary.
  • Tell the teacher if something is dangerous or if you cannot help.

Guiding questions

  • What do you think will happen if …?
  • How could you test that?
  • What is the robot doing now, and what do you want it to do?
  • What is the smallest change you could try?
  • Where could you look for information on this?
  • What did you learn from this experiment?

How mentoring is arranged

  • A new student gets a mentor at the first meeting. One mentor can have several new students.
  • You can change mentor, and you can ask anyone in the team for help.
  • Once you have gone through the beginner's path, you can become a mentor yourself.
  • A mentor does not approve the use of tools or make safety decisions. The teacher decides on those.

Mentoring also develops the mentor. You learn to explain, listen, lead and give feedback. When you teach something to someone else, you understand it better yourself.

Recognising skills

Competitive robotics builds many skills that a competition result does not show. This light model helps you notice and show what you have learnt. Your competition ranking is not the measure of your skills.

  • Technical skills

    Your skill shows when

    • you build or repair a mechanism
    • you program motors and sensors to work together

    Evidence can be: a photo of a build, a program or a measurement

  • Problem solving

    Your skill shows when

    • you look for a fault systematically
    • you try one change at a time and test it

    Evidence can be: a diary entry showing the problem, the experiments and the solution

  • Project work

    Your skill shows when

    • you plan a task and estimate how long it takes
    • you finish the work or hand it over to someone else in good order

    Evidence can be: a task list or plan and how it was carried out

  • Teamwork

    Your skill shows when

    • you agree on sharing the work with others
    • you listen to others' ideas and give reasons for your own

    Evidence can be: feedback from the team or your mentor

  • Documentation

    Your skill shows when

    • you record changes and tests so that someone else can continue the work

    Evidence can be: development diary entries

  • Mentoring and guiding others

    Your skill shows when

    • you introduce a new student
    • you guide by asking and do not do things for others

    Evidence can be: the mentored student's account or a diary entry written together

During the season, collect a few pieces of evidence, for example a photo, a diary entry, a program or your mentor's feedback, and go through them with the teacher at the end of the season.

Credits, recognition of studies and assessment are decided separately at the school. Ask the teacher about them.

Skills from competitive robotics may also be used in the portfolio of the Science and Technology Diploma (Tiede- ja teknologia -diplomi) if the work matches the diploma's goals. Ask the teacher about this.
