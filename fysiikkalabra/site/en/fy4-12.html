Motion of two bodies: meeting and overtaking

Why does a car that starts from rest catch a steadily pedalling cyclist ahead of it only far down the road, even though the car accelerates all the time?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • calculate the time and place of a meeting of two bodies using position functions.
  • read a meeting from the intersection of $(t, x)$ graphs.
  • explain how the velocities of the bodies are related to a meeting.

Prior knowledge

  • Position in uniform motion $x = x_0 + v\,t$ and the $(t, x)$ graphchapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs
  • Position in uniformly accelerated motion $x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2$chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion

1Think about it

The traffic light turns green. A car that was standing at the stop line starts moving and accelerates uniformly. At the same moment a cyclist who is $16\,\text{m}$ ahead of the line rides in the same direction at a constant velocity of $6.0\,\text{m/s}$. The car accelerates all the time, but the cyclist is still ahead for a long time.

When and where does the car catch up with the cyclist, and which of them is faster then?

Optional Recall: position of an accelerating body
A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. What is the displacement of the car from its starting place after $5.0\,\text{s}$?

  • Correct. The initial velocity is zero, so $x = \tfrac12\,a\,t^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (5.0\,\text{s})^2 = 25\,\text{m}$.

  • The half is missing here: $a\,t^2 = 50\,\text{m}$. The velocity grows from zero, so the average velocity is only half of the final velocity, and the displacement is half of what the final velocity would give.

  • The product $a\,t = 10\,\text{m/s}$ is the velocity of the car at $5.0\,\text{s}$, not the displacement. The unit of displacement is the metre, not metres per second.

How to use

Press Start or drag the Time $t$ slider and choose a Case. The blue arrow shows the velocity $\overline{v}$ and the orange arrow the acceleration $\overline{a}$, and the length of the arrow tells the magnitude. The line in the graph is drawn for the moments you have visited, and a black ring appears at the meeting when you have visited the moment of the meeting. In case 5 body $\text{A}$ is a bus and body $\text{B}$ a runner.

Assumptions of the model

The values are model values calculated by the simulation, not measurements. The bodies move along a straight road with a constant acceleration ($a = 0$ in uniform motion), they are treated as points, and they pass through each other. Friction and air resistance are not taken into account, and the acceleration takes its value immediately at the start.

How the calculation works

The position of each body is calculated with the formula $x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2$ and the velocity with $v = v_0 + a t$. The bodies meet at the moment when $x_\text{A} = x_\text{B}$. The reading $x_\text{B} - x_\text{A}$ tells how much larger the position coordinate of B is, and it is zero at the moment of meeting. When the bodies move in the positive direction, it is the lead of B.

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    The motions of two bodies can be compared only when both positions are measured in the same coordinate system. We choose one $x$-axis, one origin, one positive direction and one clock that reads zero when the motions begin. Then each body has its own position as a function of time: $x_\text{A}(t)$ and $x_\text{B}(t)$. The origin and the direction can be chosen freely, but they are chosen once and kept for the whole problem.

    The bodies meet when they are at the same place at the same moment, that is, $x_\text{A} = x_\text{B}$. A meeting always raises two questions: when does it happen, and where? If the bodies come from opposite directions, they meet each other. If the one behind catches up with the one ahead and passes it, this is overtaking. The physics is the same in both: the positions are equal.

    In the figure, body A starts from $0\,\text{m}$ and B from $60\,\text{m}$, and they move toward each other. When the positions of the bodies are marked on the same axis, you can see at once which is where and how the distance between them changes. At the moment of meeting, the distance is zero.

    In a $(t,x)$ graph, each motion is its own line in the same coordinate system. The meeting is the intersection of the lines: its horizontal coordinate is the time of the meeting and its vertical coordinate is the place. A line higher in the graph means a larger position coordinate. At the moment of meeting, the bodies' graphs intersect. Which body is ahead also depends on the direction of motion: when the bodies move in the positive $x$ direction, the higher line belongs to the body that is ahead. When they move in the negative direction, the body whose line is lower is ahead.

    A meeting does not mean that the bodies have the same velocity. At the intersection the lines have different slopes, because the velocities of the bodies are different. If two uniformly moving bodies had equal velocities, the lines would be parallel and the distance between the bodies would stay the same, so they would not meet if they started from different places. In overtaking, the body coming from behind is faster than the one being overtaken at the moment of meeting.

    Meeting condition

    The time and place of a meeting are found by writing the position of each body as a function of time and setting the positions equal:

    $$x_\text{A}(t) = x_\text{B}(t)$$

    where $x_\text{A}(t)$ and $x_\text{B}(t)$ are the positions of the bodies in the same coordinate system ($\text{m}$) and $t$ is the time of the meeting ($\text{s}$). Once $t$ is solved, it is substituted into either position function, and the result is the place of the meeting. As a check, the other position function gives the same place.

    The position function depends on the kind of motion. In uniform motion it is $x = x_0 + v\,t$ (chapter Position, velocity and uniform motion as graphs). In uniformly accelerated motion (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion) it is

    $$\begin{aligned} x &= x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac12\,a\,t^2 \\ v &= v_0 + a\,t \end{aligned}$$

    where $x_0$ is the initial position ($\text{m}$), $v_0$ the initial velocity ($\text{m/s}$), $a$ the acceleration ($\text{m/s}^2$), $t$ the time ($\text{s}$) and $v$ the velocity at time $t$ ($\text{m/s}$). The formulas hold when the motion is along a straight line and the acceleration is constant. The equation for two uniform motions is a first-degree equation, so the bodies meet at most once. If one of them accelerates, the equation is quadratic, and the lines can have two intersections.

    The simplest overtaking: a car starts from rest at the place where a cyclist passes at a constant velocity $v$. For the car $x_\text{A} = \tfrac12 a t^2$ and for the cyclist $x_\text{B} = v\,t$:

    $$\begin{aligned} &\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 = v\,t \\ &t\left(\tfrac12\,a\,t - v\right) = 0 \\ &t = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad t = \frac{2v}{a} \end{aligned}$$

    The solution $t = 0$ is the start: the bodies are at the same place then. The overtaking happens at $t = 2v/a$. The velocity of the car is then $a\,t = 2v$, twice the velocity of the cyclist.

    Why must the car reach twice the velocity? The reason can be seen in the $(t,v)$ graph. The displacement is the area between the velocity graph and the time axis, and the area between two graphs tells how much the lead changes. As long as the car is slower than the cyclist, the cyclist's lead grows. When the one behind starts slower, the lead is greatest when the velocities are equal, and that is not the moment of meeting. Only after that is the car faster and does it reduce the lead. The overtaking happens when the decrease of the lead equals the earlier growth: in the figure each area corresponds to $9\,\text{m}$. This is why the overtaking comes only far away: the car must first make up the growth of the lead, and in this example the overtaking place is $36\,\text{m}$ from the start, although the lead grew by only $9\,\text{m}$ at most.

    If the cyclist is a distance $x_{0\text{B}}$ ahead at the start, the equation is $\tfrac12 a t^2 = x_{0\text{B}} + v\,t$. It is solved with the quadratic formula, and the time of the meeting is the positive root. A negative root would describe a moment before the start and does not belong to the situation; two positive roots would mean two meetings. If there is no positive root, the bodies do not meet.

    Common misconception

    The most common mistake is to think that the bodies have the same velocity at the moment of meeting, because they are at the same place. Position and velocity are different quantities: the bodies are at the same place only at that moment, and in overtaking the one coming from behind is moving faster at the moment of overtaking.

    A second mistake is to solve the time of the meeting by setting the velocities equal. That finds the moment when the lead is greatest or smallest, not the moment when the bodies are at the same place. In a meeting, the positions are equal.

    A third mistake is to read the intersection of a $(t,v)$ graph as a meeting. There the lines intersect when the velocities are equal. A meeting can be seen only in the $(t,x)$ graph.

    Summary

    • The positions of two bodies are measured in the same coordinate system: the same origin, the same positive direction and the same clock.
    • The bodies meet when $x_\text{A}(t) = x_\text{B}(t)$. The equation gives the time of the meeting, and substituting it into a position function gives the place.
    • In a $(t,x)$ graph the meeting is the intersection of the lines. At a meeting the velocities are usually different.
    • When one motion is uniformly accelerated, the equation is quadratic. Every positive root is a meeting (there can be two), and a negative root is before the start and does not belong to the situation.
    • When the one behind starts slower, the lead is greatest when the velocities are equal, and that is not the moment of meeting.

      Worked example

      Problem

      A cyclist rides along a straight road at a constant velocity of $6.0\,\text{m/s}$. At the moment $t = 0$ she is $16\,\text{m}$ ahead of the stop line at a traffic light. At the same moment a car standing at the line starts moving and accelerates uniformly at $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the same direction. When does the car overtake the cyclist, and how far from the stop line does the overtaking happen?

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. The origin is at the stop line and the positive direction is the direction of travel. Car A: $x_{0\text{A}} = 0$, $v_{0\text{A}} = 0$, $a = 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. Cyclist B: $x_{0\text{B}} = 16\,\text{m}$, $v = 6.0\,\text{m/s}$ (constant). We want the time $t$ and the place $x$ of the overtaking.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. At the moment of overtaking the bodies are at the same place, so $x_\text{A} = x_\text{B}$. The motion of the car is uniformly accelerated and that of the cyclist is uniform, so the position of each is known as a function of time. We set the positions equal and solve for the time.

      Step 3/7

      Rearranged formula. The position of the car is $x_\text{A} = \tfrac12 a t^2$ and that of the cyclist is $x_\text{B} = x_{0\text{B}} + v\,t$. The equation is solved with the quadratic formula and the positive root is chosen:

      $$\begin{aligned} &\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 = x_{0\text{B}} + v\,t \\ &\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 - v\,t - x_{0\text{B}} = 0 \\ &t = \frac{v + \sqrt{v^2 + 2\,a\,x_{0\text{B}}}}{a} \end{aligned}$$

      The coefficients of the quadratic equation $A t^2 + B t + C = 0$ are here $A = \tfrac12 a$, $B = -v$ and $C = -x_{0\text{B}}$, and the quadratic formula

      $t$$= \dfrac{-B \pm \sqrt{B^2 - 4AC}}{2A}$

      gives the root above.

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. None are needed: all quantities are in SI units, and under the square root $v^2$ and $2\,a\,x_{0\text{B}}$ are both in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$. The expression under the root is

      $v^2 + 2\,a\,x_{0\text{B}}$$= 36\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 + 64\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
      $= 100\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. First we substitute for the time and then for the place:

      $$\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{v + \sqrt{v^2 + 2\,a\,x_{0\text{B}}}}{a} \\ &= \frac{6.0\,\text{m/s} + \sqrt{100\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}}{2.0\,\text{m/s}^2} \\ &= \frac{6.0\,\text{m/s} + 10\,\text{m/s}}{2.0\,\text{m/s}^2} \\ &= 8.0\,\text{s} \\[4pt] x &= \tfrac12\,a\,t^2 \\ &= \tfrac12 \cdot 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (8.0\,\text{s})^2 \\ &= 64\,\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Result. The car overtakes the cyclist at $t = 8.0\,\text{s}$ at the place $x = 64\,\text{m}$, measured from the stop line. The results have two significant figures, because the data $6.0\,\text{m/s}$, $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ and $16\,\text{m}$ have two.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. The position of the cyclist is $16\,\text{m} + 6.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s} = 64\,\text{m}$, so the other position function gives the same place. The velocity of the car at the moment of overtaking is

      $a\,t$$= 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$
      $= 16\,\text{m/s}$

      and that of the cyclist is $6.0\,\text{m/s}$, so the car is faster as it overtakes, as it should be. The other root

      $t$$= (6.0 - 10)\,\text{m/s} : 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$
      $= -2.0\,\text{s}$

      is before the start and does not belong to the situation.

          the first moment $t$ when the distance is $10\,\text{m}$, in $\text{s}$

          Principle

          The carts move uniformly, so the graph of each is a straight line and the velocity is the change in position divided by the change in time. Before the meeting B is ahead of A, and the distance is $x_\text{B} - x_\text{A}$. The distance is $10\,\text{m}$ for the first time before the meeting and for the second time after the meeting.

          Reading the graph

          The line of cart A goes from the point $(0\,\text{s}$; $0)$ to the point $(10\,\text{s}$; $60\,\text{m})$, so

          $v_\text{A}$$= \dfrac{60\,\text{m} - 0}{10\,\text{s} - 0}$
          $= 6.0\,\text{m/s}$

          The line of cart B goes from the point $(0\,\text{s}$; $40\,\text{m})$ to the point $(10\,\text{s}$; $20\,\text{m})$, so

          $v_\text{B}$$= \dfrac{20\,\text{m} - 40\,\text{m}}{10\,\text{s} - 0}$
          $= -2.0\,\text{m/s}$

          Formula

          $x_\text{B} - x_\text{A}$$= (40 - 2.0\,t) - 6.0\,t$
          $= 40 - 8.0\,t$

          We set it equal to $10\,\text{m}$:

          $t$$= \dfrac{40\,\text{m} - 10\,\text{m}}{8.0\,\text{m/s}}$

          Conversions

          The positions are in metres and the times in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          $t$$= \dfrac{40\,\text{m} - 10\,\text{m}}{6.0\,\text{m/s} + 2.0\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= \dfrac{30\,\text{m}}{8.0\,\text{m/s}}$
          $= 3.75\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $3.8\,\text{s}$

          (two significant figures like the distance $10\,\text{m}$). The unrounded value is $3.75\,\text{s}$.

          Reasonableness

          The distance decreases by $8.0\,\text{m}$ every second and is $40\,\text{m}$ at the start. In $3.75\,\text{s}$ it decreases by $30\,\text{m}$, so $10\,\text{m}$ is left. The moment is before the meeting at $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$, as it should be for the first time.

        Optional: application, two ideas together or a new situation where you reason for yourself.

        1. The bus stop is the origin, and a bus stands there. A runner is behind the stop at the place $-8.0\,\text{m}$ and runs in the positive direction at a constant velocity of $5.0\,\text{m/s}$. At $t = 0$ the bus starts moving and accelerates uniformly at $1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the same direction.

          When does the runner first catch up with the bus?

          s
          Answer

          $2.0\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          runner $x_{0\text{R}} = -8.0\,\text{m}$, $v = 5.0\,\text{m/s}$ (uniform); bus $x_0 = 0$, $v_0 = 0$, $a = 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the first moment $t$ when the runner is level with the bus, in $\text{s}$

          Principle

          Meeting: $x_\text{B} = x_\text{R}$. The position of the bus is $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2$ and that of the runner is $x_{0\text{R}} + v\,t$. The equation is quadratic, and both positive roots are meetings: the first time the runner catches up with the bus, and the second time the bus, already faster, passes the runner.

          Formula

          $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 = x_{0\text{R}} + v\,t$, that is, $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 - v\,t - x_{0\text{R}} = 0$. The coefficients are $\tfrac12\,a$, $-v$ and $-x_{0\text{R}}$, so the quadratic formula gives

          $t$$= \dfrac{v \pm \sqrt{v^2 + 2\,a\,x_{0\text{R}}}}{a}$

          and the first meeting is the smaller root

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          The expression under the root is

          $v^2 + 2\,a\,x_{0\text{R}}$$= (5.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (-8.0\,\text{m})$
          $= 9.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          So

          $t$$= \dfrac{5.0\,\text{m/s} - 3.0\,\text{m/s}}{1.0\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 2.0\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $2.0\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures like the given values. The larger root $5.0 + 3.0 = 8.0\,\text{s}$ is the moment when the bus passes the runner.

          Reasonableness

          At $2.0\,\text{s}$ the runner is at $-8.0\,\text{m} + 5.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 2.0\,\text{s} = 2.0\,\text{m}$, and the bus is at $\tfrac12 \cdot 1.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (2.0\,\text{s})^2 = 2.0\,\text{m}$. The velocity of the bus is then only $2.0\,\text{m/s}$, so the runner is clearly faster, as is right when catching up.

        2. A bus stands at a stop, and a runner is $20\,\text{m}$ behind it. At $t = 0$ the bus starts moving in the same direction and accelerates uniformly at $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$. The runner runs in the same direction at a constant velocity of $4.0\,\text{m/s}$.

          Does the runner catch up with the bus? How close to the bus does she get?

          m
          Answer

          $4.0\,\text{m}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          bus $x_{0\text{B}} = 0$, $v_0 = 0$, $a = 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$; runner $x_{0\text{R}} = -20\,\text{m}$, $v = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$ (uniform); both in the same direction

          Find

          whether the runner catches up with the bus, and the smallest distance $s_\text{min}$ in $\text{m}$

          Principle

          Two ideas: a meeting requires a non-negative expression under the root, and if there is no meeting, the distance is smallest when the velocities are equal (before that the runner is closing in, after that the bus pulls away).

          Formula

          The meeting $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 = x_{0\text{R}} + v\,t$ gives $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 - v\,t - x_{0\text{R}} = 0$, and the expression under the root is $v^2 + 2\,a\,x_{0\text{R}}$. The velocities are equal when $a\,t = v$, that is, $t = \dfrac{v}{a}$. The distance is

          $s$$= x_\text{B} - x_\text{R}$
          $= \tfrac12\,a\,t^2 - x_{0\text{R}} - v\,t$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          The expression under the root:

          $(4.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (-20\,\text{m})$
          $= 16 - 20$
          $= -4\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
          $< 0$

          so the runner does not catch up with the bus. The velocities are equal at

          $t$$= \dfrac{4.0\,\text{m/s}}{0.50\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 8.0\,\text{s}$

          Then

          $s_\text{min}$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 0.50\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (8.0\,\text{s})^2 + 20\,\text{m} - 4.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 8.0\,\text{s}$
          $= 16\,\text{m} + 20\,\text{m} - 32\,\text{m}$
          $= 4.0\,\text{m}$

          Answer

          The runner does not catch up with the bus; at closest she is $4.0\,\text{m}$ away, two significant figures like the given values.

          Reasonableness

          At $8.0\,\text{s}$ the bus is at $16\,\text{m}$ and the runner at $-20\,\text{m} + 32\,\text{m} = 12\,\text{m}$, so the gap is $4.0\,\text{m}$. At $6.0\,\text{s}$ and $10\,\text{s}$ the gap is $5.0\,\text{m}$, so $4.0\,\text{m}$ is the smallest. The gap never goes to zero.

        3. A scooter starts from rest with an acceleration of $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ toward a cyclist who is $180\,\text{m}$ away and rides toward the scooter at a constant velocity of $8.0\,\text{m/s}$. The scooter and the cyclist use different lanes and meet.

          When are they at the same point?

          s
          Answer

          $10\,\text{s}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          scooter $x_0 = 0$, $v_0 = 0$, $a = 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ in the positive direction; cyclist $x_0 = 180\,\text{m}$, velocity $-8.0\,\text{m/s}$ (toward the scooter, uniform)

          Find

          the time of the meeting $t$ in $\text{s}$

          Principle

          The motion of the scooter is uniformly accelerated and that of the cyclist is uniform, and they move in opposite directions. Meeting: $x_\text{S} = x_\text{C}$, when the origin is the starting place of the scooter and the positive direction is toward the cyclist.

          Formula

          $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 = D - v\,t$, where $D = 180\,\text{m}$ and $v = 8.0\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of the cyclist. So $\tfrac12\,a\,t^2 + v\,t - D = 0$, and the positive root is

          $t$$= \dfrac{-v + \sqrt{v^2 + 2\,a\,D}}{a}$

          Conversions

          All quantities are in SI units, so no conversions are needed.

          Substitution

          The expression under the root is

          $v^2 + 2\,a\,D$$= (8.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 180\,\text{m}$
          $= 784\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

          So

          $t$$= \dfrac{-8.0\,\text{m/s} + 28\,\text{m/s}}{2.0\,\text{m/s}^2}$
          $= 10\,\text{s}$

          Answer

          $10\,\text{s}$

          Two significant figures like the given values. The negative root $-18\,\text{s}$ would be before the start and does not belong to the situation.

          Reasonableness

          The scooter has driven $\tfrac12 \cdot 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (10\,\text{s})^2 = 100\,\text{m}$ and the cyclist $8.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 10\,\text{s} = 80\,\text{m}$. Together $100\,\text{m} + 80\,\text{m} = 180\,\text{m}$, so the distance between them has been used up.
