Global Fusion Explorer

Why does a map of fusion machines tell a story about politics as well as about physics?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • compare the geography of fusion machines in the early Cold War years with today's network, using a data map.
  • explain how declassification, verification and cost turned competition into collaboration.
  • evaluate the limits of a facility map: selection, unknown years and company-reported results.

Prior knowledge

  • The T-3 results of 1968 were confirmed by a British team in 1969chapter Science across the Iron Curtain
  • ITER began with the 1985 Geneva summit and has seven memberschapter ITER: why, where and who

1Think about it

In 1955 a fusion physicist in Princeton could not tell a colleague in Moscow what she was working on: both worked in secret, on opposite sides of the Iron Curtain. Today, physicists from China, India, Russia, the United States and Europe build one machine together in southern France and share their data by e-mail every day. Somewhere between these two pictures, the map of fusion research changed.

How did fusion research move from secret national projects to a worldwide network, and how can a map show it?

Review: science across the Iron Curtain

In the chapter Science across the Iron Curtain, you met the T-3 tokamak. Why was the British measurement of T-3 in 1969 so important?

  • Right. A British team measured the plasma with their own laser and confirmed about ten million degrees. After this independent check, laboratories around the world started building tokamaks.

  • No tokamak has ever produced electricity. The 1969 measurement checked the temperature of T-3's plasma.

  • The opposite: the British measurement confirmed the Soviet result, about ten million degrees.

  • Fusion research had been declassified in 1958. The 1969 visit was possible because the field had become open.

    3Investigate

    How to read the explorer

    The Global Fusion Explorer shows 63 fusion research facilities: retired, operating, under construction and planned. Each marker is a site; a number on a marker means that several devices stand at the same place. Select a marker or a line in the list to open the facility's panel: its type, status, years, achievements, importance, sources and the date they were checked.

    Use the filters to ask questions: search, country, device type, status and the year slider (operating in the year). The list under the map always shows exactly the facilities that the filters leave, so the explorer works without the map too.

    Read the data critically:

    • The list is a selection, not every fusion device ever built. A country without a marker may still have done fusion research.
    • Country is today's country. For older machines the panel also names the state of that time, such as the Soviet Union or West Germany.
    • Some years are unknown. A line such as Model A, 1953–? means that no source gives the machine's end year. The year filter then counts it only for ten years after its start, or up to the last year a source shows it running; the note at the end of the panel says which. This is a careful guess, not a fact.
    • One machine can have two records. COMPASS ran first at Culham in the United Kingdom and later in Prague, so it has a record for each place.
    • Results of private companies are usually company-reported, and every date of a planned machine is a plan.

      4Theory

      From competition to collaboration

      In the early 1950s, fusion research was secret. The United States, the Soviet Union and the United Kingdom each hoped that controlled fusion would give them an advantage, and each worked alone. In September 1958, at the second United Nations conference on the peaceful uses of atomic energy in Geneva, the three countries declassified their fusion research. They found that all of them had the same unstable, leaky plasmas. In the explorer's data, the machines that started before 1970 stand in exactly these three countries (the selection leaves out, for example, West Germany's first Wendelstein stellarators of 1960); in 1958 the year filter shows six of them.

      Openness made independent verification possible. In 1968 Soviet scientists reported that their T-3 tokamak reached about ten million degrees. In 1969 a British team from Culham took a laser to Moscow and confirmed the result, and laboratories all over the world started building tokamaks: in the explorer, France's TFR and West Germany's Pulsator both started in 1973. In 1978 European countries set up the JET Joint Undertaking to build one shared machine at Culham.

      At the Geneva summit of November 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev proposed to Ronald Reagan that their countries build a fusion experiment together. This idea became ITER. In 2006 seven members signed the ITER Agreement: China, the European Union, India, Japan, Russia, South Korea and the United States. Machines also cross borders: Germany's ASDEX became China's HL-2A, the British COMPASS moved to Prague, and Japan and Europe built JT-60SA together. In the explorer's data, 24 facilities operating in 2026 stand in 12 countries on three continents, from Canada to South Korea, many of them linked to ITER, and private companies have added new sites since the 2010s.

      Key idea

      The geography of fusion research changed from a few rival nuclear powers working in secret to a worldwide network. Three things drove the change: declassification in 1958, the need for independent checks of surprising results, and machines so expensive that only many countries together can build them. Competition did not disappear: countries and companies still race for records. But today they share data, people and parts, and the largest project, ITER, is a joint one.

          Find the error

          A student sets the year slider to 1965 and writes: "In 1965 only three countries in the world did fusion research: the list shows only American, British and Soviet machines." What is wrong with this conclusion?

          • Some did start tokamaks after 1969, but research had begun earlier: Germany's IPP was founded in 1960. The explorer is a selection of important machines, so it cannot show every lab.

          • It does not hide them: it counts them for ten years after their start, so T-3 and Scylla I are in the 1965 list. The real problem is that the explorer is a selection.

          • Right. For example, Germany's Max Planck Institute for Plasma Physics (IPP) was founded in 1960, and its first Wendelstein stellarators ran from 1960, but its first machine in the explorer, Pulsator, starts only in 1973. A short list in a selection is not proof that nobody else did research.

          • The slider shows machines operating in that year, not only those built then. The real problem is that the explorer is a selection.

          Common misconception

          "No marker means no research." The explorer is a selection of important facilities. Many smaller machines, universities and theory groups are missing, so an empty region on the map is a gap in the data, not proof that nothing happened there.

          "More markers mean a better programme." One large shared machine, such as JET or ITER, can matter more than many small ones. Count markers, but read the panels before you judge.

          4. Explanation task: Write: from competition to collaboration

          Write a paragraph of 100–140 words. Compare the geography of fusion machines before 1970 with today's network. Use at least two pieces of evidence from the explorer (counts, facilities or panels) and one event from the timeline, explain why the change happened, and name one limit of the data.

          Model answer

          Before 1970, fusion machines in the explorer stood only in the United States, the United Kingdom and the Soviet Union, and until 1958 their large programmes were secret. Today, operating facilities stand in 12 countries on three continents, and the largest machine, ITER, is built by seven members. The change happened because the 1958 Geneva conference made the field open, because surprising results such as T-3's needed independent checks, and because machines became too expensive for one country: JET (1978) and ITER (agreement in 2006) were shared. Machines even crossed borders, like Germany's ASDEX, which became HL-2A in China. However, the data has limits: it is a selection, and for old machines with unknown end years, such as T-3, the year filter can only guess. Competition has not disappeared: countries and companies still race for records.
