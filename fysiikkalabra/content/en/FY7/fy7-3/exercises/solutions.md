## fy7-3-L1-01
**Given:** long straight wire, current $I = 12\,\text{A}$, distance $r = 3.0\,\text{cm}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ in microtesla
**Principle:** The wire is long and straight, and the distance is much smaller than the length of the wire, so the formula for a long straight wire holds. The field is directly proportional to the current and inversely proportional to the distance.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $r = 3.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.030\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 12\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.030\,\text{m}} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T} = 80\,\mu\text{T}$
**Answer:** $80\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the current $12\,\text{A}$ and the distance $3.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** In the example of the chapter, $10\,\text{A}$ gives $200\,\mu\text{T}$ at $1\,\text{cm}$; three times the distance and a slightly greater current give about $200\,\mu\text{T} \cdot 1.2 : 3 = 80\,\mu\text{T}$. The field is of the same order of magnitude as the Earth's field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$, so it clearly turns a compass nearby.

## fy7-3-L1-02
**Given:** coil, $N = 500$, length $l = 25\,\text{cm}$, current $I = 1.2\,\text{A}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil in millitesla
**Principle:** The coil is long compared with its width, and the field is wanted at the centre, far from the ends. There the field is almost uniform and depends on the turn density and the current, so the formula for a long coil holds.
**Formula:** $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$
**Conversions:** $l = 25 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.25\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{500 \cdot 1.2\,\text{A}}{0.25\,\text{m}} = 3.016 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} = 3.016\,\text{mT}$
**Answer:** $3.0\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the current $1.2\,\text{A}$ and the length $25\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The turn density is $500 : 0.25\,\text{m} = 2000$ turns per metre. A thousand turns per metre and $1\,\text{A}$ give about $1.26\,\text{mT}$, so $2000$ turns per metre and $1.2\,\text{A}$ give about $1.26\,\text{mT} \cdot 2 \cdot 1.2 \approx 3\,\text{mT}$.

## fy7-3-L1-03
**Given:** wire of a power line, current $I = 800\,\text{A}$, distance $r = 16\,\text{m}$; the fields of the other wires are ignored; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at ground level in microtesla
**Principle:** A power line is hundreds of metres long and straight, so at a distance of $16\,\text{m}$ it can be treated as a long straight wire. The problem leaves out the effect of the other wires, so we calculate the field of one wire.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the current is in amperes and the distance in metres. Finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 800\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 16\,\text{m}} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T} = 10\,\mu\text{T}$
**Answer:** $10\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the distance $16\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** A large current but a large distance: $2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 800 : 16\,\text{T} = 1 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$. The field is about one fifth of the Earth's magnetic field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$.

## fy7-3-L1-04

## fy7-3-L1-06
**Find:** how the field of a vertical current-carrying wire circulates as seen from above, and how the circulation changes when the current is reversed.
**Principle:** The field lines of a current-carrying wire are concentric circles around the wire, and the direction of the field is given by the right-hand rule: when the thumb points in the direction of the current, the curled fingers go round in the direction of the field. A current upwards means, seen from above, a current towards the viewer, so the fingers curl anticlockwise. The field is produced by the current, so reversing the direction of the current also reverses the field.
**Model answer:** The field lines are circles around the wire that go anticlockwise as seen from above. When the current is reversed, the thumb points down and the circulation turns clockwise. The direction of circulation does not depend on the material of the wire but on the direction of the current. The rule is the right-hand rule, not the left-hand rule. The field lines are not straight lines pointing away from the wire, because they go round the wire as circles.

## fy7-3-L1-07

**Principle:** The coil is long, so inside it $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$. Turns are added over the same length, so the turn density $N : l$ grows and the field grows in the same ratio when the current stays the same.
**Formula:** $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N_2 I}{l}$
**Conversions:** $l = 20 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.20\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{1000 \cdot 0.75\,\text{A}}{0.20\,\text{m}} = 4.712 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} = 4.712\,\text{mT}$
**Answer:** $4.7\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the current $0.75\,\text{A}$ and the length $20\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** After the addition the turn density is $1000 : 0.20\,\text{m} = 5000$ turns per metre. One ampere would give about $6.3\,\text{mT}$, so $0.75\,\text{A}$ gives about $4.7\,\text{mT}$. At the start the field was $1.9\,\text{mT}$, and the number of turns grew by a factor of $2.5$, so the field is also $2.5 \cdot 1.9\,\text{mT} \approx 4.7\,\text{mT}$.

## fy7-3-L1-08
**Given:** current $I = 2.0\,\text{A}$ in both; coil $N = 250$ and $l = 10\,\text{cm}$; distance from the wire $r = 2.0\,\text{cm}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** the ratio $B_\text{coil} : B_\text{wire}$
**Principle:** The field at the centre of the coil is calculated with the formula for a long coil and the field of the wire with the formula for a long straight wire. Finally the ratio of the fields is calculated.
**Formula:** $B_\text{coil} = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$ and $B_\text{wire} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $l = 0.10\,\text{m}$ and $r = 0.020\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $B_\text{coil} = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{250 \cdot 2.0\,\text{A}}{0.10\,\text{m}} = 6.283 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$ and $B_\text{wire} = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 2.0\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.020\,\text{m}} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$, so $\dfrac{B_\text{coil}}{B_\text{wire}} = \dfrac{6.283 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}} = 314$
**Answer:** about $310$ times, two significant figures like the current $2.0\,\text{A}$ and the distance $2.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** The field of the coil, $6.3\,\text{mT}$, is about $130$ times the Earth's field of $50\,\mu\text{T}$, and the field of the wire, $20\,\mu\text{T}$, is about $40\,\%$ of the Earth's field. The coil concentrates the current into many turns, so its field is much stronger than the field of a single wire with the same current.

## fy7-3-L2-01
**Given:** target field $B = 20\,\text{mT}$, turns $N = 1200$, length $l = 0.30\,\text{m}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** current $I$ of the coil in amperes

**Substitution:** $I = \dfrac{20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} \cdot 0.30\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 1200} = 3.979\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the field $20\,\text{mT}$ and the length $0.30\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The turn density is $1200 : 0.30\,\text{m} = 4000$ turns per metre, so one ampere gives about $4 \cdot 1.26\,\text{mT} \approx 5\,\text{mT}$. Four amperes give about $20\,\text{mT}$, as wanted.

## fy7-3-L2-02
**Given:** long straight wire, current $I = 6.0\,\text{A}$, magnitude of the field $B = 14\,\mu\text{T}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** distance $r$ in centimetres
**Principle:** The field of a long straight wire weakens in inverse proportion to the distance, so the field is $14\,\mu\text{T}$ at exactly one distance. It is solved from the formula for a straight wire.
**Formula:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, from which $r = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi B}$
**Conversions:** $B = 14\,\mu\text{T} = 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$; finally $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $r = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}} = 0.0857\,\text{m} = 8.57\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $8.6\,\text{cm}$, two significant figures like the current $6.0\,\text{A}$ and the field $14\,\mu\text{T}$.
**Reasonableness:** Check by substituting back: $2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 6.0\,\text{A} : 0.0857\,\text{m} = 1.4 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$. Closer to the wire its field is stronger than the Earth's field, farther away it is weaker; that is why the compass must be within a few centimetres for Ørsted's experiment to show clearly.

## fy7-3-L2-04
**Given:** two long parallel wires, distance $d = 10\,\text{cm}$, currents $I = 10\,\text{A}$ in opposite directions; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ midway between the wires in microtesla
**Principle:** The vectors of the fields are added. By the right-hand rule, the fields of currents in opposite directions point the same way between the wires: when the thumb points up in one wire and down in the other, the fingers curl in the same direction between the wires. Midway, each wire is $5.0\,\text{cm}$ away, so the fields are equal and strengthen each other.
**Formula:** $B = 2 \cdot \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, where $r = \dfrac{d}{2}$, so $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.050\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 10\,\text{A}}{\pi \cdot 0.050\,\text{m}} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T} = 80\,\mu\text{T}$
**Answer:** $80\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the currents $10\,\text{A}$ and the distance $10\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** One $10\,\text{A}$ wire gives $40\,\mu\text{T}$ at a distance of $5\,\text{cm}$, and two fields in the same direction give twice that. If the currents were in the same direction, the fields would cancel each other midway.

## fy7-3-L2-05
**Given:** measurement points of the table $(I;\ B)$: $(0.0\,\text{A};\ 0.05\,\text{mT})$, $(0.5\,\text{A};\ 1.31\,\text{mT})$, $(1.0\,\text{A};\ 2.60\,\text{mT})$, $(1.5\,\text{A};\ 3.80\,\text{mT})$, $(2.0\,\text{A};\ 5.12\,\text{mT})$, $(2.5\,\text{A};\ 6.31\,\text{mT})$ and $(3.0\,\text{A};\ 7.60\,\text{mT})$; $N = 600$, $l = 0.300\,\text{m}$
**Find:** an estimate of the permeability of free space $\mu_0$ in the unit $\mu\text{Vs/(Am)}$
**Principle:** By the formula for a long coil $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N}{l} I$, $B$ is directly proportional to the current, and the slope is $A = \mu_0 \tfrac{N}{l}$. The constant term describes the zero point of the sensor and the Earth's field. The line is drawn through the middle of the set of points, and the slope is calculated from points of the line, not from measurement points.
**Reading the graph:** The points lie on a rising straight line with points on both sides of it. The fitted line passes approximately through the points $(0\,\text{A};\ 0.06\,\text{mT})$ and $(3.0\,\text{A};\ 7.59\,\text{mT})$, which have been read from the line far apart. The slope is $A = \dfrac{7.59\,\text{mT} - 0.06\,\text{mT}}{3.0\,\text{A} - 0\,\text{A}} = 2.51\,\tfrac{\text{mT}}{\text{A}}$.
**Formula:** $A = \mu_0 \dfrac{N}{l}$, from which $\mu_0 = \dfrac{A l}{N}$
**Conversions:** $A = 2.51\,\tfrac{\text{mT}}{\text{A}} = 2.51 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{\text{T}}{\text{A}}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{Vs/(Am)} = 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$.
**Substitution:** $\mu_0 = \dfrac{2.51 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{\text{T}}{\text{A}} \cdot 0.300\,\text{m}}{600} = 1.26 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Answer:** $1.26\,\mu\text{Vs/(Am)}$, three significant figures like the slope and the length $0.300\,\text{m}$. Fitting by eye varies, so about $1.21\text{–}1.31\,\mu\text{Vs/(Am)}$ ($\pm 4\,\%$) is a good answer.
**Reasonableness:** The result is almost the same as the literature value $4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} = 1.257 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$. The constant term $0.06\,\text{mT}$ is of the same order of magnitude as the Earth's field $0.05\,\text{mT}$, which is a part of the constant term; the rest is the zero point of the sensor. At the current $1.5\,\text{A}$ the line gives $0.06\,\text{mT} + 2.51\,\tfrac{\text{mT}}{\text{A}} \cdot 1.5\,\text{A} = 3.83\,\text{mT}$, which agrees with the $3.80\,\text{mT}$ of the table.

## fy7-3-L2-06
**Given:** measurement points of the table $(r;\ B)$: $(1.0\,\text{cm};\ 161\,\mu\text{T})$, $(2.0\,\text{cm};\ 79\,\mu\text{T})$ and $(4.0\,\text{cm};\ 41\,\mu\text{T})$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at a distance of $3.0\,\text{cm}$ in microtesla
**Principle:** For the field of a long straight wire $B = \tfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, so $B \cdot r = \tfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}$ is constant if $B$ is inversely proportional to the distance. The current is obtained from the constant, and the current is used to calculate the field at the new point.
**Reading the graph:** The products are $1.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 161\,\mu\text{T} = 161\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm}$, $2.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 79\,\mu\text{T} = 158\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm}$ and $4.0\,\text{cm} \cdot 41\,\mu\text{T} = 164\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm}$. They differ from their mean $161\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm}$ by less than $2\,\%$, so the product is practically constant and $B$ is inversely proportional to the distance $r$.
**Formula:** $I = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot B r}{\mu_0}$ and $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
**Conversions:** $B r = 161\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm} = 161 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 1.61 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}\,\text{m}$ and $r = 3.0\,\text{cm} = 0.030\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** The current is $I = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 1.61 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}} = 8.05\,\text{A}$, and the field is $B = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 8.05\,\text{A}}{2\pi \cdot 0.030\,\text{m}} = 5.37 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$
**Answer:** $54\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the measurement results.
**Reasonableness:** The distance $3.0\,\text{cm}$ is between the measured distances $2.0\,\text{cm}$ and $4.0\,\text{cm}$, and $54\,\mu\text{T}$ is between $79\,\mu\text{T}$ and $41\,\mu\text{T}$. Also $79\,\mu\text{T} \cdot 2.0 : 3.0 = 53\,\mu\text{T}$ gives the same order of magnitude.
**Justification for part d):** The field is inversely proportional to the distance, so $B \cdot r = 161\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm}$ stays the same. At a distance of $0.50\,\text{cm}$ the field is $B = \dfrac{161\,\mu\text{T}\,\text{cm}}{0.50\,\text{cm}} = 322\,\mu\text{T} \approx 320\,\mu\text{T}$. The result is about twice as great as at a distance of $1.0\,\text{cm}$ ($161\,\mu\text{T}$), as it should be when the distance is halved.

## fy7-3-L2-07
**Find:** how the $60\,{}^\circ$ tilt of the sensor relative to the axis affects the measurement of the field inside the coil.
**Principle:** The sensor measures the component of the magnetic flux density in the direction of the sensor itself. Inside the coil the field is uniform and along the axis, so a sensor at an angle $\alpha$ to the axis shows the value $B \cos \alpha$. The field itself does not change, only the part of the field that the sensor sees.
**Model answer:** The reading is too small, because the sensor measures only the component in its own direction. At an angle of $60\,{}^\circ$ the reading is $B \cos 60\,{}^\circ = 0.50\,B$, that is half of the true value. The uniformity of the field does not make the direction of the sensor unimportant, and the field does not become stronger or weaker because of the tilt. If all the measurements are made with the same tilt, the slope of the graph and the $\mu_0$ calculated from it come out too small. The Earth's field adds a constant term to the reading, which is removed by zeroing the sensor while the current is off; it does not change the slope. The error is avoided by setting the sensor along the axis and turning it until the reading is greatest.

## fy7-3-L3-01
**Given:** wire at $r = 5.0\,\text{cm}$ above the compass, deflection of the needle $\alpha = 30^\circ$, horizontal component of the Earth's field $B_\text{h} = 14\,\mu\text{T}$; the field of the wire $B_\text{w}$ is perpendicular to $B_\text{h}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** current $I$ in the wire in amperes
**Principle:** The compass needle turns to point along the total field. The total field is the vector sum of two perpendicular fields, so for the deflection of the needle $\tan \alpha = B_\text{w} : B_\text{h}$. The wire is long and straight, so the current is found from its field with the formula for a straight wire.
**Formula:** $\tan \alpha = \dfrac{B_\text{w}}{B_\text{h}}$ and $B_\text{w} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, from which $I = \dfrac{2\pi r B_\text{h} \tan \alpha}{\mu_0}$
**Conversions:** $r = 5.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.050\,\text{m}$ and $B_\text{h} = 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B_\text{w} = 14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T} \cdot \tan 30^\circ = 8.083 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$ and $I = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 0.050\,\text{m} \cdot 8.083 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{T}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}} = 2.02\,\text{A}$
**Answer:** $2.0\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the distance $5.0\,\text{cm}$ and the field $14\,\mu\text{T}$.
**Reasonableness:** The angle is less than $45^\circ$, so the field of the wire must be weaker than the Earth's field; $8\,\mu\text{T} < 14\,\mu\text{T}$ satisfies this. A current of two amperes is ordinary for a school power supply, and it was with an experiment like this that Ørsted noticed the phenomenon.

## fy7-3-L3-02
**Given:** coil of an MRI scanner, $B = 1.5\,\text{T}$, length $l = 1.6\,\text{m}$, diameter of a turn $d = 1.0\,\text{m}$, current $I = 400\,\text{A}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** total length of wire $L$ in kilometres
**Principle:** The problem assumes the coil is long, so the number of turns is solved from $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$. Each turn uses a length of wire equal to the circumference $\pi d$, so the length of the wire is the number of turns times the circumference.
**Formula:** $N = \dfrac{B l}{\mu_0 I}$ and $L = N \pi d$, so $L = \dfrac{\pi d B l}{\mu_0 I}$
**Conversions:** None needed before substitution: all quantities are in SI units. Finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$.
**Substitution:** $N = \dfrac{1.5\,\text{T} \cdot 1.6\,\text{m}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 400\,\text{A}} = 4775$ and $L = 4775 \cdot \pi \cdot 1.0\,\text{m} = 15\,000\,\text{m} = 15\,\text{km}$
**Answer:** about $15\,\text{km}$, two significant figures like the field $1.5\,\text{T}$ and the diameter $1.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** The field is about $30\,000$ times the Earth's field, so both a large current and thousands of turns are needed: the turn density is about $3000$ turns per metre. An ordinary copper wire would overheat at such a current, which is why the wire is a superconductor. The estimate is rough, because a real coil is not long compared with its width. When the length is only $1.6$ times the diameter, the field at the centre is about $85\,\%$ of the value given by the formula for a long coil, so a real coil needs more wire than $15\,\text{km}$.

## fy7-3-L3-03
**Given:** two long parallel wires, distance $d = 10\,\text{cm}$, currents in the same direction $I_1 = 4.0\,\text{A}$ and $I_2 = 6.0\,\text{A}$
**Find:** distance $x$ of the point where the field is zero from the wire carrying current $I_1$, in centimetres
**Principle:** By the right-hand rule, the fields of currents in the same direction point in opposite directions between the wires, so at some point between them they cancel. There the magnitudes of the fields are equal. The wire with the weaker current must be nearer, so that its field is equally strong.
**Formula:** $\dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi x} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_2}{2\pi (d - x)}$, from which $I_1 (d - x) = I_2 x$ and $x = \dfrac{I_1 d}{I_1 + I_2}$
**Conversions:** None needed: $\mu_0$ and $2\pi$ cancel, and when $d$ is substituted in centimetres, $x$ comes out in centimetres.
**Substitution:** $x = \dfrac{4.0\,\text{A} \cdot 10\,\text{cm}}{4.0\,\text{A} + 6.0\,\text{A}} = 4.0\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** $4.0\,\text{cm}$ from the $4.0\,\text{A}$ wire, two significant figures like the currents.
**Reasonableness:** The point is nearer the wire with the weaker current, as it should be. Check: $4.0\,\text{A} : 4.0\,\text{cm} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{A}}{\text{cm}}$ and $6.0\,\text{A} : 6.0\,\text{cm} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{A}}{\text{cm}}$, so the fields are equal.

## fy7-3-L3-04
**Given:** height of the wire above the compass $h = 4.0\,\text{cm}$, points of the table $(I;\ \alpha)$: $(1.0\,\text{A};\ 17.5\,{}^\circ)$, $(2.0\,\text{A};\ 32.0\,{}^\circ)$, $(3.0\,\text{A};\ 43.5\,{}^\circ)$, $(4.0\,\text{A};\ 51.0\,{}^\circ)$ and $(5.0\,\text{A};\ 57.5\,{}^\circ)$; the field of the wire $B_\text{w}$ is perpendicular to the horizontal component $B_\text{h}$ of the Earth's field; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** horizontal component $B_\text{h}$ of the Earth's field in microtesla
**Principle:** Linearisation: the angle $\alpha$ is not directly proportional to the current, but $\tan \alpha$ may be, so the graph chosen is $\tan \alpha$ as a function of the current. The needle turns to the direction of the total field. The fields $B_\text{h}$ and $B_\text{w}$ are perpendicular to each other, so $\tan \alpha = B_\text{w} : B_\text{h}$. The wire is long and straight, so $B_\text{w} = \tfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi h}$. Combining these gives $\tan \alpha = \tfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi h B_\text{h}} \cdot I = k I$, so $\tan \alpha$ is directly proportional to the current if $B_\text{w}$ is, and the slope $k$ of the line gives $B_\text{h}$.
**Reading the graph:** The tangents are $0.315$, $0.625$, $0.949$, $1.235$ and $1.570$. The ratios $\tan \alpha : I$ are $0.315$, $0.312$, $0.316$, $0.309$ and $0.314\,\tfrac{1}{\text{A}}$, that is constant to within about $2\,\%$. The points lie on a line through the origin, so the field of the wire is directly proportional to the current. The slope of the line is $k = 0.313\,\tfrac{1}{\text{A}}$.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi h B_\text{h}}$, from which $B_\text{h} = \dfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi h k}$
**Conversions:** $h = 4.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m} = 0.040\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\mu\text{T} = 10^{-6}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** $B_\text{h} = \dfrac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}}{2\pi \cdot 0.040\,\text{m} \cdot 0.313\,\tfrac{1}{\text{A}}} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{T}$
**Answer:** $16\,\mu\text{T}$, two significant figures like the height $4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the angles.
**Reasonableness:** The horizontal component is smaller than the total Earth's field of about $50\,\mu\text{T}$, as it should be. At the current $3.0\,\text{A}$ the field of the wire is $2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 3.0\,\text{A} : 0.040\,\text{m} = 15\,\mu\text{T}$, almost as great as $B_\text{h}$, so the needle should turn almost $45\,{}^\circ$. In the table the angle is $43.5\,{}^\circ$.
**Justification for part c):** The angle is $\alpha = \arctan (k I)$. The tangent is not directly proportional to the angle, so the angle is not directly proportional to the current. The growth of the angle slows down: when the current increases from $1.0\,\text{A}$ to $2.0\,\text{A}$ the angle grows by $14.5\,{}^\circ$, but from $4.0\,\text{A}$ to $5.0\,\text{A}$ only by $6.5\,{}^\circ$. When the current is large, the field of the wire is much greater than $B_\text{h}$, and the total field points almost in the direction of the field of the wire. That is perpendicular to the original north direction, so $\alpha$ approaches $90\,{}^\circ$ but never reaches it, because $B_\text{h}$ does not vanish.

## fy7-3-L3-05
**Given:** length of the wire $L = 30\,\text{m}$, diameter of the metal $0.50\,\text{mm}$, with insulation $d_\text{i} = 0.55\,\text{mm}$, $\rho = 1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m}$, diameter of the tube $d = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, voltage $U = 6.0\,\text{V}$; $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}}$
**Find:** magnetic flux density $B$ at the centre of the coil in millitesla
**Principle:** The length of wire in one turn is $\pi d$, and in a tight layer each turn takes up a length equal to the thickness $d_\text{i}$ of the insulated wire. The current is limited by the resistance of the wire, $R = \rho L : A$ and $I = U : R$ (FY6). The coil is assumed to be long, so the field is $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$.
**Formula:** $N = \dfrac{L}{\pi d}$, $l = N d_\text{i}$, $A = \pi \left(\dfrac{d_\text{m}}{2}\right)^2$, $R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}$, $I = \dfrac{U}{R}$ and $B = \mu_0 \dfrac{N I}{l}$
**Conversions:** $d = 0.030\,\text{m}$, $d_\text{i} = 0.55 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$ and the diameter of the metal $d_\text{m} = 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$; finally $1\,\text{mT} = 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$.
**Substitution:** a) $N = \dfrac{30\,\text{m}}{\pi \cdot 0.030\,\text{m}} = 318$ and $l = 318.3 \cdot 0.55 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} = 0.175\,\text{m}$. The length is about $5.8$ times the diameter, so the coil is long enough. b) $A = \pi \cdot (0.25 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m})^2 = 1.96 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$, $R = \dfrac{1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m} \cdot 30\,\text{m}}{1.96 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2} = 2.60\,\Omega$ and $I = \dfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{2.60\,\Omega} = 2.31\,\text{A}$. c) $B = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{318.3 \cdot 2.31\,\text{A}}{0.175\,\text{m}} = 5.28 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T} = 5.28\,\text{mT}$
**Answer:** $5.3\,\text{mT}$, two significant figures like the voltage $6.0\,\text{V}$ and the diameter $3.0\,\text{cm}$.
**Reasonableness:** In a tight single-layer coil $N : l = 1 : d_\text{i}$, so $B = \mu_0 I : d_\text{i} = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot 2.31\,\text{A} : 0.55 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m} = 5.28\,\text{mT}$, the same result. The field is about $100$ times the Earth's field. The power is $U I = 6.0\,\text{V} \cdot 2.31\,\text{A} = 13.9\,\text{W}$, so the thin wire warms up and the electromagnet can be used only for short periods.
**Justification for part d):** When the wire is twice as long, the resistance $R = \rho L : A$ doubles and the current $I = U : R$ halves to $1.16\,\text{A}$. There are twice as many turns, but they are in a single layer, so the length $l$ of the coil doubles and the turn density $N : l$ stays the same. The field $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N}{l} I$ therefore halves to $2.6\,\text{mT}$. If there were two layers, $l$ would stay the same and the field would stay the same, but in this problem the coil is a single layer.

## fy7-3-L2-08
**Find:** how the magnetic flux density at the centre of the coil changes when a coil of $240$ turns is stretched from $12\,\text{cm}$ to $24\,\text{cm}$ and the current is increased from $0.60\,\text{A}$ to $1.8\,\text{A}$.
**Principle:** Inside a long coil $B = \mu_0 \tfrac{N I}{l}$. The field is directly proportional to the current and to the turn density $N : l$, so it depends on the number of turns per unit length and not on the total number of turns. The thickness of the coil does not affect the field.
**Model answer:** The field becomes $1.5$ times as large. The changes are

$\dfrac{I_2}{I_1} = \dfrac{1.8\,\text{A}}{0.60\,\text{A}} = 3$

$\dfrac{l_2}{l_1} = \dfrac{24\,\text{cm}}{12\,\text{cm}} = 2$

The number of turns stays the same, so the turn density is halved. The ratio of the fields is

$\dfrac{B_2}{B_1} = \dfrac{I_2}{I_1} \cdot \dfrac{l_1}{l_2}$

$\dfrac{B_2}{B_1} = 3 \cdot \dfrac{1}{2} = 1.5$

Check with numbers:

$B_1 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{240 \cdot 0.60\,\text{A}}{0.12\,\text{m}}$

$B_1 \approx 1.51 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$

$B_2 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{Vs}}{\text{Am}} \cdot \dfrac{240 \cdot 1.8\,\text{A}}{0.24\,\text{m}}$

$B_2 \approx 2.26 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{T}$

So the field grows from about $1.5\,\text{mT}$ to about $2.3\,\text{mT}$. Tripling the current alone would give three times the field if the total number of turns were what mattered, but stretching the coil makes the turns sparser and weakens the field.
