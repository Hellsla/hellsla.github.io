Electric field and the capacitor

Why does the spark that jumps from a door handle on a frosty day do no harm, although its voltage is thousands of volts?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • solve charge, field, potential and capacitor tasks by choosing a suitable principle
  • draw and interpret the graph and the fit of a capacitance measurement
  • assess claims about electrical safety and measurement reports with justification

Prior knowledge

  • Coulomb's law is $F = k\tfrac{\lvert q_1 q_2 \rvert}{r^2}$, and charge is conservedchapter Electric charge and Coulomb's law
  • In a field a charge experiences the force $\overline{F} = q\overline{E}$, and between plates $E = \tfrac{U}{d}$chapter Uniform electric field
  • The work done by the field is $W = qU$, and for a particle starting from rest $\tfrac12 mv^2 = \lvert q \rvert U$chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field
  • Capacitance is $C = \tfrac{Q}{U}$, and the energy is $W = \tfrac12 CU^2$chapter The capacitor and its energy
  • The series resistor of an LED is $R = \tfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{I}$chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell
  • A fuse protects the cable and a residual current device protects the personchapter Electrical safety at home and at work
  • The power of a three-phase device is $P = 3UI$chapter Applied chapter: the electric car and the home electrical system

1Think about it

On a frosty day the indoor air is dry. You walk in woollen socks across a woollen rug and reach a finger towards the metal handle of the front door. Just before you touch it, a spark of about $3\,\text{mm}$ jumps between the finger and the handle, and your finger stings. The voltage of the spark is thousands of volts, but no harm is done. In the exam a situation like this can be the start of a task: the phenomenon and the numbers are given, but the law is not.

What does the length of the spark tell you, and why is a spark of thousands of volts not dangerous, although the $230\,\text{V}$ of a socket can be lethal?

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 6 questions

Diagnostic 1: Coulomb's law

Two small charged balls are $4.0\,\text{cm}$ apart, and the force between them is $F$. The charge of one ball is doubled, and the balls are moved $8.0\,\text{cm}$ apart.How large is the force now?

  • The changes do not have an equally strong effect: the charge is in the law to the first power, the distance to the second. Twice the distance gives a quarter of the force. Review in the chapter Electric charge and Coulomb's law.

  • Doubling the charge does double the force, but the distance doubled as well. In Coulomb's law the distance is squared, so on its own it reduces the force to a quarter. Review in the chapter Electric charge and Coulomb's law.

  • Correct. $F' = k\tfrac{2q_1 q_2}{(2r)^2} = \tfrac{2}{4}F = \tfrac{F}{2}$. The charge doubles the force and twice the distance reduces it to a quarter. Review if needed in the chapter Electric charge and Coulomb's law.

Diagnostic 2: an electron in a field

Between two horizontal plates the electric field points from top to bottom. An electron at rest is brought between the plates.In which direction does the electric force on it point?

  • Correct. $\overline{F} = q\overline{E}$ and $q = -e$, so the force points against the field, towards the upper, positive plate. Review if needed in the chapter Uniform electric field.

  • A field line shows the direction of the force on a positive charge. The charge of the electron is negative, so $\overline{F} = q\overline{E}$ points against the field. Review in the chapter Uniform electric field.

  • The electric force depends on the charge, not on the mass: $F = eE$. A small mass only means that the same force gives the electron an enormous acceleration. Review in the chapter Uniform electric field.

Diagnostic 3: the same potential difference

A proton and an electron start from rest, and each travels through a potential difference of $500\,\text{V}$ in its own direction. Forces other than the electric force are not taken into account.Which statement is true?

  • The work done by the field is $qU$, and it does not depend on the mass. The charges have the same magnitude $e$, so both get the same kinetic energy $500\,\text{eV}$. Review in the chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field.

  • Correct. The field does the work $eU = 500\,\text{eV}$ on each. The mass of the electron is about $1800$ times smaller, so its speed is $\sqrt{1800} \approx 43$ times larger. Review if needed in the chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field.

  • The kinetic energies are equal, but $\tfrac12 mv^2 = eU$ gives the light electron a much larger speed, about $43$ times as large. Review in the chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field.

Diagnostic 4: a disconnected capacitor

An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor is charged and disconnected from the source. After that the plates are pulled twice as far apart.How does the voltage between the plates change?

  • The capacitance is halved, but $U = \tfrac{Q}{C}$: when the charge stays the same and $C$ decreases, the voltage increases. Review in the chapter The capacitor and its energy.

  • Correct. $C = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$ is halved, and the $Q$ of a disconnected capacitor stays the same, so $U = \tfrac{Q}{C}$ doubles. Separating the plates requires work, so the energy also increases. Review if needed in the chapter The capacitor and its energy.

  • The voltage would stay the same only if the source were still connected. The charge of a disconnected capacitor stays the same, so the voltage changes with the capacitance. Review in the chapter The capacitor and its energy.

Diagnostic 5: an LED in the reverse direction

A red LED and a series resistor are connected in series to a $3.0\,\text{V}$ battery, but the LED has been turned to the reverse direction.How large is the voltage across the LED?

  • About $1.9\,\text{V}$ is the voltage of a red LED in the forward direction, when a current flows. In the reverse direction no current flows. Review in the chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell.

  • The LED is still in the circuit, although it does not conduct. An open point in a circuit is exactly the point across which the voltage is. Review in the chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell.

  • Correct. In the reverse direction $I = 0$, so the voltage of the resistor is $RI = 0$, and by Kirchhoff's voltage law the whole voltage of the source is across the LED. Review if needed in the chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell.

Diagnostic 6: the residual current device

The socket circuit of a bathroom has a residual current device with a tripping current of $30\,\text{mA}$.What does the residual current device compare when it decides whether to cut the current?

  • Correct. In an intact circuit the currents are equal. If part of the current gets to earth through a fault and, for example, a person, the returning current is smaller, and the device trips when the difference exceeds $30\,\text{mA}$. Review if needed in the chapter Electrical safety at home and at work.

  • The total current is monitored by the fuse, which protects the cable from overheating. Its rated current is amperes, so it does not notice a current through a person. Review in the chapter Electrical safety at home and at work.

  • A residual current device does not measure voltage. It detects a leakage current that flows to earth by some other route than along the neutral conductor. Review in the chapter Electrical safety at home and at work.

Potential between the plates

Values calculated by a model · The graph has been calculated from the uniform field model $V = Ex$, as in the chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field.

The graph shows the potential $V$ between two plates as a function of the distance $x$. The distance is measured from the negative plate, whose potential is zero, and the positive plate is at $x = 5.0\,\text{cm}$. First think about what the slope of the graph tells you about the field and in which direction an electron starting from rest moves. Answer below with the speed of the electron.

An electron starts from rest at the negative plate. Read the potential at $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$ from the graph.

How large is the speed of the electron at this point? The mass of the electron is $9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$. Give the answer in $\text{km/s}$.

km/s
Hint 1: a guiding question

Read the potential at $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$. The electron moves towards the higher potential, so its potential energy decreases by $eU$, where $U$ is the potential difference between the starting point and the end point.

Hint 2: what is the connection

Only the electric force does work. First say in words which energy is conserved and what the decrease in potential energy turns into.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

The kinetic energy increases as much as the potential energy decreases: $\tfrac12 mv^2 = eU$, so

$v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 150\,\text{V}}{9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}}}$

and finally $1\,\text{km/s} = 1000\,\text{m/s}$.

Show the solution

Given

from the graph the potential $V = 150\,\text{V}$ at $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$, the potential of the starting point $V_0 = 0\,\text{V}$; $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, $m = 9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, initial speed zero

Find

the speed $v$ of the electron at $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$

Principle

The electric force is conservative and the other forces are negligible, so the sum of the kinetic energy and the potential energy is conserved. The charge of the electron is negative, so it moves towards the higher potential, and its potential energy decreases by $eU$, where $U$ is the potential difference travelled (chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field).

Reading the graph

The line rises from the origin to the point $(5.0\,\text{cm}; 250\,\text{V})$, so each centimetre raises the potential by $50\,\text{V}$. At $x = 3.0\,\text{cm}$ the line is at the grid line $150\,\text{V}$.

Formula

The work done by the field is $W = QU_{AB}$, where $U_{AB} = V_0 - V$ is the voltage from the start to the point. For the electron $Q = -e$, so $W = (-e)(V_0 - V) = e(V - V_0) = eU$, where $U = V - V_0$ is the potential difference travelled. The work becomes kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac12 mv^2$. At the start the kinetic energy is zero, so $\tfrac12 mv^2 - 0 = eU$, and the speed is $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2e(V - V_0)}{m}}$

Conversions

$1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$ and $\sqrt{1\,\text{J} : 1\,\text{kg}} = 1\,\text{m/s}$. Finally $1\,\text{km/s} = 1000\,\text{m/s}$.

Substitution

$v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot (150\,\text{V} - 0\,\text{V})}{9.109 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}}}$
$= \sqrt{5.276 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
$= 7.264 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$
$= 7260\,\text{km/s}$

Answer

$v \approx 7.3 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{km/s}$

Two significant figures, because the point $3.0\,\text{cm}$ read from the graph has the precision of two figures.

Reasonableness

The slope of the graph is the field strength

$E$$= \tfrac{250\,\text{V}}{0.050\,\text{m}}$
$= 5000\,\text{V/m}$

and the field points from the positive plate to the negative one, so the force on the electron points towards the positive plate, as assumed. The speed is about $2.4\,\%$ of the speed of light, so the classical kinetic energy is good enough. At the positive plate the speed would be $\sqrt{\tfrac{250}{150}} \approx 1.3$ times as large.

4Theory

Key idea

The tasks of the lesson start from the same idea: a charge creates an electric field around itself, and the field exerts a force on another charge, $\overline{F} = q\overline{E}$. On a positive charge the force points in the direction of the field and on a negative charge against the field. When the field moves a charge, it does work, and the potential energy of the charge changes like the gravitational potential energy of a ball in the gravitational field. A capacitor stores energy in the field between its plates. Semiconductor components and electrical safety return to the same quantities: voltage, current and the field a dielectric can withstand.

The spark of the opening is solved with these laws. When you walk on a woollen rug, charge builds up on your body, and because of induction a charge of opposite sign moves to the nearby surface of the metal plate of the handle. The spark jumps when the field in the air gap exceeds the dielectric strength of air, about $3\,\text{MV/m}$. A spark of about $3\,\text{mm}$ therefore tells of a voltage of about $9\,\text{kV}$.

The voltage is large, but the body is a small capacitor with a capacitance of about $100\,\text{pF}$, so the charge is only about $0.9\,\mu\text{C}$ and the energy about $4\,\text{mJ}$. The discharge lasts about a microsecond. A dangerous current, about $50\,\text{mA}$ for one second, would carry $0.05\,\text{C}$ through the body, over $50\,000$ times more charge.

Electric field and the capacitor

Charge, force and field

$$Q = ne$$
magnitude of the Coulomb force
$$F_E = k\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}$$
electric field strength
$$E = \frac{F_E}{Q}$$
voltage in a uniform electric field
$$U = Ed$$

where $n$ is the number of elementary charges, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ the elementary charge, $F_E$ the magnitude of the electric force ($\text{N}$), $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ the magnitudes of the charges ($\text{C}$), $r$ their distance ($\text{m}$), $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$, $E$ the electric field strength ($\text{N/C} = \text{V/m}$) at the charge $Q$, $U$ the voltage between the plates ($\text{V}$) and $d$ the distance between the plates ($\text{m}$). The force on a positive charge is along the field, on a negative one opposite to it; as vectors $\overline{F}_E = Q\overline{E}$. Charge is conserved. Coulomb's law holds for point charges and uniformly charged spheres, and the forces of several charges are added as vectors. The relation $U = Ed$ holds only between large parallel plates, where the field is uniform.

Potential and energy

electric potential
$$V = \frac{E_\text{p}}{Q}$$
work done by an electric field
$$W = QU$$

where $V$ is the potential at a point ($\text{V}$) relative to a chosen zero, $E_\text{p}$ the electric potential energy of the charge $Q$ ($\text{J}$), $W$ the work done by the field ($\text{J}$) when the charge $Q$ moves from the point A to the point B, and $U = U_{AB} = V_A - V_B$ the voltage between the points. The work does not depend on the path. The field points from higher potential to lower, and a charge starting from rest moves in the direction in which its potential energy decreases. The energy unit $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.

When a particle of mass $m$ ($\text{kg}$) and charge $Q$ starts from rest and is accelerated in an electric field through the voltage $U$, the work done by the field turns into the kinetic energy of the particle. Its final speed is $v$ ($\text{m/s}$).

Derive it yourself. A particle (mass $m$, charge $Q$) starts from rest and is accelerated in an electric field between two points with the voltage $U$ between them. Only the electric force does work. Derive an equation for the final speed $v$ of the particle.

Show the derivation

By the work-energy principle of mechanics, the work done on the particle changes its kinetic energy. Only the electric force does work, and the particle starts from rest, so its initial kinetic energy is zero:

$$W = E_\text{k} - 0 = \tfrac12 mv^2$$

The work done by an electric field is $W = QU$. A charge starting from rest always moves in the direction in which the field does positive work, so the magnitude of the work is $\lvert Q \rvert U$, where $U$ is the magnitude of the voltage:

$$\tfrac12 mv^2 = \lvert Q \rvert U$$

Solve for the speed:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2\lvert Q \rvert U}{m}}$$

For example, an elementary charge accelerated through $1\,\text{V}$ gains the kinetic energy $1\,\text{eV}$.

The capacitor

capacitor law
$$Q = CU$$
capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor
$$C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_\text{r}\,\frac{A}{d}$$
energy of a capacitor
$$E = \tfrac12 CU^2$$

where $C$ is the capacitance ($\text{F}$), $A$ the area of a plate ($\text{m}^2$), $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$, $\varepsilon_\text{r}$ the relative permittivity of the dielectric, $E$ the energy stored in the capacitor ($\text{J}$), also $E = \tfrac12 QU$. The formula of the parallel-plate capacitor holds when $d$ is small compared with the size of the plates and the dielectric fills the gap. For a capacitor connected to a source the voltage stays the same; for a disconnected one, the charge.

The dielectric strength $E_\text{max}$ ($\text{V/m}$) of a dielectric is the largest electric field strength it can withstand. If the field grows larger, the dielectric starts to conduct and breakdown occurs between the plates, so the voltage of the capacitor has a largest value $U_\text{max}$ ($\text{V}$).

Derive it yourself. The plates of a parallel-plate capacitor are a distance $d$ apart, and the dielectric filling the gap has the dielectric strength $E_\text{max}$. Derive the largest voltage $U_\text{max}$ the capacitor can withstand.

Show the derivation

Between the plates the field is uniform, so the voltage in a uniform electric field is

$$U = Ed$$

When the voltage grows, the field grows in the same proportion. Breakdown occurs when the field reaches the dielectric strength, $E = E_\text{max}$:

$$U_\text{max} = E_\text{max}d$$

The same reasoning gives the voltage of the spark at the start: across an air gap $d$ the voltage can be at most the one at which the field equals the dielectric strength of air.

Components and safety

Extra information
$$\eta = \frac{P}{GA}$$
voltage drop across a conductor
$$U = RI$$

where $\eta$ is the efficiency of the solar cell, $P$ the electric power of the cell at the maximum power point ($\text{W}$), $G$ the intensity of the light falling on the cell, that is the power per unit area ($\text{W/m}^2$), and $A$ the area of the cell ($\text{m}^2$). The equation for the efficiency holds when the light falls perpendicular to the cell. The equation is extra information: it is not in the MAFY tables, and an exam gives it in the task when it is needed.

In the equation for the voltage drop $U$ is the voltage ($\text{V}$), $R$ the resistance ($\Omega$) and $I$ the current ($\text{A}$). The equation gives the current through the body, $I = \tfrac{U}{R}$, when $U$ is the touch voltage and $R$ the resistance of the whole current path. A diode conducts only in the forward direction, when its voltage exceeds the threshold voltage. A fuse protects the cable, a residual current device the person.

An LED is connected to a voltage source $U$ ($\text{V}$) in series with a series resistor $R$ ($\Omega$), which limits the current $I$ ($\text{A}$) to a suitable value. The voltage $U_\text{D}$ ($\text{V}$) of a conducting LED stays almost the same whatever the current.

Derive it yourself. An LED and a series resistor are connected in series to a voltage source $U$. The voltage of the LED is $U_\text{D}$, and the current in the circuit is to be $I$. Derive the resistance $R$ of the series resistor.

Show the derivation

Go once round the circuit in the direction of the current using Kirchhoff's second law. The potential rises by $U$ in the source. It falls by the voltage drop $RI$ in the resistor (voltage drop across a conductor) and by $U_\text{D}$ in the LED:

$$U - RI - U_\text{D} = 0$$

Solve for the resistance:

$$R = \frac{U - U_\text{D}}{I}$$

Across the resistor there is therefore only the part of the source voltage that is not across the LED. The internal resistance of the source is assumed to be small.

Common mistakes

  • The field and the force always in the same direction. On a negative charge, such as an electron, the force points against the field.
  • Distance without the square. In Coulomb's law twice the distance gives a quarter of the force.
  • The field increases when approaching a plate. Between the plates the field is uniform: the force is the same everywhere.
  • Energy $QU$. The capacitor stores $\tfrac12 QU$; the other half turns into internal energy of the conductors during charging.
  • A dielectric in a disconnected capacitor. The charge stays the same, so the voltage and the energy decrease, not increase.
  • The series resistor without the voltage of the LED. Across the resistor there is only $U - U_\text{D}$.
  • A high voltage is always dangerous. The danger comes from a current through the body, driven by the voltage between two points.

5Examples

Once you have studied the chapter's theory, move on to the guided examples. In the first you follow a worked solution step by step; in the next ones you make the choices yourself, and the hints help if you get stuck.

Find the mistake: the series resistor of an LED

The voltage of a green LED is $2.2\,\text{V}$ when its current is $15\,\text{mA}$. The LED is connected in series with a series resistor to a $5.0\,\text{V}$ source. A student calculated the resistor like this: $R = \tfrac{U}{I} = \tfrac{5.0\,\text{V}}{0.015\,\text{A}} \approx 330\,\Omega$.What is wrong in the solution?

  • An LED does not obey Ohm's law: its voltage stays almost the same when the current changes. Ohm's law holds only for the resistor, across which there is $U - U_\text{D}$. Review in the chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell.

  • That conversion is the wrong way round: $15\,\text{mA} = 15 \cdot 0.001\,\text{A} = 0.015\,\text{A}$, as the student did. The mistake is in the voltage. Review in the chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell.

  • Correct. $R = \tfrac{U - U_\text{D}}{I} = \tfrac{2.8\,\text{V}}{0.015\,\text{A}} \approx 190\,\Omega$. The student's $330\,\Omega$ would give only about $8.5\,\text{mA}$, and the LED would glow more dimly.

  • In a series circuit the voltage of the source is shared between the resistor and the LED, $U = U_R + U_\text{D}$, so there is less than $5.0\,\text{V}$ across the resistor. Review in the chapter Semiconductors, the diode, the LED and the solar cell.

Read the graph: charging a capacitor

The graph shows the voltage $U$ of a capacitor as a function of its charge $Q$ during charging. Which statement is true according to the graph?

  • Capacitance is the charge divided by the voltage, $C = \tfrac{Q}{U} = \tfrac{70\,\mu\text{C}}{14\,\text{V}}$, not its reciprocal. Review in the chapter The capacitor and its energy.

  • Correct. $C = \tfrac{70\,\mu\text{C}}{14\,\text{V}} = 5.0\,\mu\text{F}$ and $W = \tfrac12 QU = \tfrac12 \cdot 70 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{C} \cdot 14\,\text{V} = 0.49\,\text{mJ}$, the area of the triangle under the line.

  • $QU$ would be the energy if the whole charge were moved across the full voltage. The voltage grows from zero, so the energy is the area of the triangle, $\tfrac12 QU$. Review in the chapter The capacitor and its energy.

  • The graph is a straight line through the origin, so $\tfrac{Q}{U}$ is constant: the capacitance depends only on the structure, not on the voltage. Review in the chapter The capacitor and its energy.

Which description: a floating oil droplet

A small charged oil droplet floats at rest between two horizontal plates. The upper plate is positive and the lower one negative.Which verbal description of the free-body diagram of the droplet is correct?

  • Correct. The droplet is at rest, so $qE = mg$. The field points down, so an upward force requires a negative charge: $\overline{F} = q\overline{E}$ points against the field.

  • The field is not a force. The field tells what kind of force acts on a charge, and only the electric force $q\overline{E}$ is drawn in the free-body diagram. Review in the chapter Uniform electric field.

  • If both forces pointed down, the net force would not be zero, and the droplet would accelerate downwards. The sum of the forces on a droplet at rest is zero. Review in the chapter Uniform electric field.

  • The weight of an electron is negligible, but that of an oil droplet is not. If only an upward force acted on the droplet, it would accelerate upwards and not float. Review in the chapter Uniform electric field.

Claim: the fuse and the residual current device

The socket circuit of a bathroom has a $10\,\text{A}$ fuse and a $30\,\text{mA}$ residual current device. Which statement is true?

  • Correct. The leakage current does not return along the neutral conductor, so the currents of the phase and neutral conductors differ, and the device trips when the difference exceeds $30\,\text{mA}$.

  • The size of the fuse is chosen according to the cable. A larger fuse would let into the cable a current that can heat it and start a fire. Review in the chapter Electrical safety at home and at work.

  • Then the current returns through the person to the neutral conductor, and no difference arises between the currents of the phase and neutral conductors. The device does not trip. Review in the chapter Electrical safety at home and at work.

  • The rated current of the fuse is $10\,\text{A}$, two hundred times larger. A fuse protects the cable from overheating, not the person. Review in the chapter Electrical safety at home and at work.

Three charges on a rail

Three small charged balls are on an insulating rail on the same line. Ball A, whose charge is $+4.0\,\text{nC}$, is at $0\,\text{cm}$, ball B, whose charge is $-6.0\,\text{nC}$, is at $5.0\,\text{cm}$ and ball C, whose charge is $+2.0\,\text{nC}$, is at $10.0\,\text{cm}$.

How large is the total electric force on ball C?

µN
Hint 1: a guiding question

Calculate the forces of A and B on ball C separately with Coulomb's law. Remember the distances to ball C: $10.0\,\text{cm}$ and $5.0\,\text{cm}$.

Hint 2: what is the connection

A and C have the same sign, so A pushes C away, to the right. B and C have opposite signs, so B pulls C to the left. Forces in opposite directions are subtracted.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$F_\text{A}$$= k\dfrac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.100\,\text{m})^2}$
$F_\text{B}$$= k\dfrac{6.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.050\,\text{m})^2}$
Show the solution

Given

$q_\text{A} = +4.0\,\text{nC}$ at $0\,\text{cm}$, $q_\text{B} = -6.0\,\text{nC}$ at $5.0\,\text{cm}$, $q_\text{C} = +2.0\,\text{nC}$ at $10.0\,\text{cm}$, $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$

Find

the magnitude $F$ of the total force on ball C

Principle

The balls are small, so they are point charges. Each of the other balls acts on C as if the other one were not there, and the total force is the vector sum of the forces. The positive direction is chosen to the right. A and C have the same sign, so the force of A points away from C, that is, to the right. B and C have opposite signs, so the force of B points towards B, that is, to the left (chapter Electric charge and Coulomb's law).

Formula

$F_\text{A}$$= k\dfrac{\lvert q_\text{A} q_\text{C} \rvert}{r_\text{AC}^2}$
$F_\text{B}$$= k\dfrac{\lvert q_\text{B} q_\text{C} \rvert}{r_\text{BC}^2}$

$F = F_\text{A} - F_\text{B}$

Conversions

$r_\text{AC}$$= 10.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 0.100\,\text{m}$
$r_\text{BC}$$= 5.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 0.050\,\text{m}$

$1\,\text{nC} = 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 10^{6}\,\mu\text{N}$.

Substitution

$F_\text{A}$$= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \dfrac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{C}^2}{0.0100\,\text{m}^2}$
$= 7.19 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{N}$
$F_\text{B}$$= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \cdot \dfrac{12 \cdot 10^{-18}\,\text{C}^2}{0.0025\,\text{m}^2}$
$= 43.14 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{N}$
$F$$= 7.19\,\mu\text{N} - 43.14\,\mu\text{N}$
$= -35.95\,\mu\text{N}$

Answer

$F \approx 36\,\mu\text{N}$

To the left, towards ball B. Two significant figures, as in the charges.

Reasonableness

The force of B is larger, because B is half as far away and its charge is larger: the distance alone makes the force four times as large and the charge one and a half times as large, six times in total. That is why the total force points towards B.

Model answer

a)

Given

the mass of a ball $m = 0.50\,\text{g}$, the length of the thread $L = 40.0\,\text{cm}$, the distance between the centres of the balls $r = 8.0\,\text{cm}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

Find

the electric force $F_\text{s}$ between the balls

Free-body diagram

The object is the right-hand ball, drawn as a rectangle. The angle is drawn larger than in the task so that the arrows can be told apart.

Principle

One ball is chosen as the object. The forces on it are the weight $\overline{G}$ down, the tension $\overline{T}$ of the thread along the thread towards the hook, and the electric force $\overline{F}_\text{s}$ of the other ball. The balls are at the same height, so the electric force is horizontal and points away from the other ball: charges of the same sign repel. The ball is at rest, so the sum of the forces is zero. The horizontal component of the force of the thread cancels the electric force and the vertical component cancels the weight. The angle $\alpha$ of the thread from the vertical comes from the geometry, because the ball is half the distance away from the vertical through the hook (chapter Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces).

Formula

$T\sin\alpha = F_\text{s}$ and $T\cos\alpha = mg$, so $F_\text{s} = mg\tan\alpha$, where $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{r/2}{L}$

Conversions

$m$$= 0.50\,\text{g}$
$= 0.50 \cdot 0.001\,\text{kg}$
$= 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{kg}$

The ratio of the lengths needs no conversion. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.

Substitution

$\sin\alpha$$= \dfrac{4.0\,\text{cm}}{40.0\,\text{cm}}$
$= 0.100$

so $\alpha = 5.74^\circ$ and $\tan\alpha = 0.1005$. The weight is

$mg$$= 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
$= 4.905 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N}$

so

$F_\text{s}$$= 4.905 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{N} \cdot 0.1005$
$= 4.930 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$
$= 0.493\,\text{mN}$

Answer

$F_\text{s} \approx 0.49\,\text{mN}$

Two significant figures, as in the mass $0.50\,\text{g}$ and the distance $8.0\,\text{cm}$.

Reasonableness

The threads are almost vertical, so the horizontal force must be small compared with the weight: it is about $10\,\%$ of the weight $4.9\,\text{mN}$. The force of the thread

$T$$= \tfrac{mg}{\cos\alpha}$
$\approx 4.93\,\text{mN}$

is only slightly larger than the weight, as it should be at a small angle.

b)

Given

from part a $F_\text{s} = 0.493\,\text{mN}$; $r = 8.0\,\text{cm}$; $k = 8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$

Principle

The balls are small compared with the distance, so they can be treated as point charges, and Coulomb's law gives the force. The charges are equal, so the product of the charges is $q^2$. Charge consists of elementary charges, so the positive balls lack $n = \tfrac{q}{e}$ electrons (chapter Electric charge and Coulomb's law).

Formula

$F_\text{s} = k\dfrac{q^2}{r^2}$, so $q = r\sqrt{\dfrac{F_\text{s}}{k}}$ and $n = \dfrac{q}{e}$

Conversions

$r$$= 8.0\,\text{cm}$
$= 8.0 \cdot 0.01\,\text{m}$
$= 0.080\,\text{m}$
$F_\text{s}$$= 0.493\,\text{mN}$
$= 0.493 \cdot 0.001\,\text{N}$
$= 4.93 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$

Substitution

$q$$= 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{4.93 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\text{N}}{8.988 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2}}$
$= 0.080\,\text{m} \cdot 2.342 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\tfrac{\text{C}}{\text{m}}$
$= 1.874 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{C}$
$= 18.7\,\text{nC}$

$= 1.17 \cdot 10^{11}$

$q \approx 19\,\text{nC}$

And about $1.2 \cdot 10^{11}$ electrons have moved away from each ball. Two significant figures, as in the data.

Check:

$k\tfrac{q^2}{r^2}$$= 8.988 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \tfrac{(1.874 \cdot 10^{-8})^2}{0.080^2}\,\text{N}$

c)

Given

the charges of the balls before touching $q$ and $q$; the third identical ball uncharged; $r = 8.0\,\text{cm}$; from part a $F_\text{s} = 0.493\,\text{mN}$

Find

the electric force $F'$ at the same distance and a comparison of the forces

Principle

Charge is conserved. When two identical conducting balls touch, they share their common charge equally, so the charge of the touched ball is $\tfrac{q + 0}{2} = \tfrac{q}{2}$. By Coulomb's law the force is proportional to the product of the charges. The forces on the balls are force and counterforce, so they are equal and opposite (chapters Electric charge and Coulomb's law and Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws).

Formula

$F'$$= k\dfrac{q \cdot \tfrac{q}{2}}{r^2}$
$= \tfrac12 \cdot k\dfrac{q^2}{r^2}$
$= \tfrac12 F_\text{s}$

Conversions

None needed: the force is obtained as a fraction of the force of part a.

Substitution

$F'$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 0.493\,\text{mN}$
$= 0.2465\,\text{mN}$

Answer

$F' \approx 0.25\,\text{mN}$

Two significant figures. An equal force acts on each ball, although the charges are $q$ and $\tfrac{q}{2}$.

Reasonableness

The charges appear symmetrically in the formula, so the same number gives the force on each ball. When the forces and the masses are the same, the threads still stay symmetrical, although the charges are different.

d)

Given

the charges of part c, $q$ and $\tfrac{q}{2}$; the original distance $r_0 = 8.0\,\text{cm}$; $L = 40.0\,\text{cm}$; the masses the same as before

Find

the new distance $r$ between the balls

Principle

In the new position of rest the same equilibrium condition $F = mg\tan\alpha$ holds, but the electric force and the angle change together. The angle is small, so $\tan\alpha \approx \sin\alpha = \tfrac{r}{2L}$. Then the equation contains only powers of the distance, and the old and the new situation can be compared as a ratio (chapters Free-body diagrams and the combined effect of forces and Electric charge and Coulomb's law).

Formula

Before

$k\dfrac{q^2}{r_0^2}$$= mg\dfrac{r_0}{2L}$

and now $k\dfrac{q^2/2}{r^2} = mg\dfrac{r}{2L}$, so $r^3 = \tfrac12 r_0^3$ and $r = \dfrac{r_0}{\sqrt[3]{2}}$

Conversions

None needed: the distance is obtained in centimetres directly from $r_0$.

Substitution

$r$$= \dfrac{8.0\,\text{cm}}{\sqrt[3]{2}}$
$= \dfrac{8.0\,\text{cm}}{1.260}$
$= 6.35\,\text{cm}$

Answer

$r \approx 6.3\,\text{cm}$

Two significant figures, as in the distance $8.0\,\text{cm}$.

Reasonableness

A smaller force gives a smaller angle, so the balls approach each other. The distance does not, however, decrease as much as the force: as the balls approach each other, the electric force grows again. The small-angle assumption is good, since $\tfrac{3.2\,\text{cm}}{40.0\,\text{cm}} = 0.08$, and the exact calculation gives $6.35\,\text{cm}$.

Common mistakes

  • The whole distance $8.0\,\text{cm}$ is used in the sine of the angle instead of half of it, which makes the force twice as large.
  • The electric force is drawn along the thread or towards the other ball, although charges of the same sign repel.
  • The square of the distance is forgotten in Coulomb's law, or the distance is substituted in centimetres.
  • In part c the charge moves entirely to the third ball, or it is thought to stay unchanged, although identical balls share the charge equally.
  • In part c a larger force is thought to act on the ball with the larger charge.

Also accepted

  • In part a the small-angle approximation $\tan\alpha \approx \sin\alpha = 0.100$, which gives $F_\text{s} \approx 0.491\,\text{mN}$.
  • In part d the exact solution without the small-angle approximation, $r \approx 6.35\,\text{cm}$.

Model answer

a)

Find

an assessment of the claim that the bird on the conductor is safe because its feet insulate

Principle

A current flows through the body only if there is a voltage, that is, a potential difference, between two points of the body. The voltage between different points of a conductor comes from Ohm's law when the resistance of the conductor between them and the current are known (chapters Electrical safety at home and at work and Potential energy and potential in an electric field).

Model answer

The bird's feet are about $5\,\text{cm}$ apart. The resistance of the conductor between the feet is $0.30\,\tfrac{\Omega}{\text{km}} \cdot 0.05 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{km} = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\Omega$, so the voltage between the feet is $U = RI = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\Omega \cdot 100\,\text{A} = 1.5\,\text{mV}$. Such a small voltage does not drive a noticeable current through the bird's body. The bird is indeed at almost the same potential as the conductor, about $12\,\text{kV}$ relative to earth (the $20\,\text{kV}$ of the line is the voltage between the phase conductors), but potential alone is not dangerous: a current needs two points at different potentials. The bird does not touch the ground, the pole or another conductor, so no current flows through its body. The conclusion of the claim is right, but the justification is wrong: the bird would be safe even if its feet conducted electricity well. The bird is in danger if it touches another conductor or an earthed part of the pole with its wing at the same time.

b)

Find

Principle

Air is an insulator only when the electric field is smaller than the dielectric strength of air. In a uniform field an air gap $d$ withstands the voltage $E_\text{max} d$ (chapters The capacitor and its energy and Electrical safety at home and at work).

Model answer

Carbon fibre conducts electricity. When the angler stands on the ground, the tip of the rod is almost at earth potential, so the whole voltage between the conductor and earth, about $12\,\text{kV}$, is across the air gap between the tip and the conductor. The nominal voltage of the line, $20\,\text{kV}$, is between two phase conductors, and one conductor is at a voltage of $\tfrac{20\,\text{kV}}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 12\,\text{kV}$ relative to earth. With the uniform field model the smallest gap that breaks down is $d = \tfrac{U}{E_\text{max}} = \tfrac{12 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}}{3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}} \approx 4\,\text{mm}$. The estimate is a lower limit for the safety distance, not a safe distance: near a thin conductor and the tip of the rod the field is much stronger than $\tfrac{U}{d}$, and humid air, dirt and the fishing line weaken the insulation, so an arc can strike across a clearly longer gap too. After the breakdown the air conducts, and the current flows through the arc, the rod and the angler's body to earth. If the resistance of the current path is, for example, $10\,\text{k}\Omega$, $12\,\text{kV}$ drives a current of about $1.2\,\text{A}$ through the body, tens of times larger than the about $50\,\text{mA}$ that is enough for ventricular fibrillation. That is why you do not fish or fly a kite under power lines, and you do not approach the conductors with any object.

Principle

Model answer

There is therefore a voltage between two points, and it is largest near the point of contact, where the potential changes most steeply. If a person stands with the feet apart, there is a step voltage between the feet, and the current flows up one leg and down the other. The step voltage is the larger, the closer you are to the point of contact and the longer the step is. That is why you do not approach a fallen conductor. If you are already close, you leave with short steps taken with the feet together or by jumping with both feet together, so that the feet are at almost the same potential. The damage is reported by calling the emergency number $112$, and you do not return to the area before the network company has cut the power. The distance between the front and hind legs of a cow is over a metre, so the voltage between them is much larger than across the short step of a person. In addition, the current flows from the front legs of the cow to the hind legs through the chest and the heart, so even a smaller voltage is dangerous.

Common mistakes

  • The safety of the bird is explained by the bird not being earthed, without mentioning that there is no voltage between its feet.
  • A high potential as such is interpreted as dangerous, although the danger comes only from a potential difference.
  • In part b the voltage of the air gap is taken as the $20\,\text{kV}$ between the phase conductors, although between the tip of the rod and the conductor there is the about $12\,\text{kV}$ between the conductor and earth.
  • The $4\,\text{mm}$ of part b is presented as a safe distance, although it is the lower limit of a uniform field.
  • In part c the advice is to run away with long steps, which increases the step voltage.

Exercise 5.2-6 The ion thruster of a satellite 15 points

A communications satellite is raised to a higher orbit with an ion thruster. In the thruster xenon atoms are made into positive ions $\text{Xe}^+$, whose charge is $+e$. The ions start almost from rest, accelerate in the electric field between two metal grids and fly out into space. Model answer

a)

Given

the accelerating voltage $U = 1.10\,\text{kV}$, the distance between the grids $d = 1.0\,\text{mm}$, the mass of an ion $m = 2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$ and its charge $+e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, the initial speed almost zero

Find

the speed $v$ of the ions and the strength $E$ of the field between the grids

Principle

Between the grids only the electric force acts on an ion: the weight of the ions is negligible, and there is no air resistance in space. The electric force is conservative, so the decrease $eU$ in the potential energy of the ion turns into kinetic energy. The grids are close to each other, so the field between them is uniform (chapters Potential energy and potential in an electric field and Uniform electric field).

Formula

$\tfrac12 mv^2 = eU$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m}}$, and $E = \dfrac{U}{d}$

Conversions

$U$$= 1.10\,\text{kV}$
$= 1.10 \cdot 1000\,\text{V}$
$= 1100\,\text{V}$
$d$$= 1.0\,\text{mm}$
$= 1.0 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
$= 0.0010\,\text{m}$

In addition $1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{J}$, and finally $1\,\text{km/s} = 1000\,\text{m/s}$.

Substitution

$v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 1100\,\text{V}}{2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}}}$
$= \sqrt{1.617 \cdot 10^{9}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}$
$= 4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m/s}$
$= 40.2\,\text{km/s}$
$E$$= \dfrac{1100\,\text{V}}{0.0010\,\text{m}}$
$= 1.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}$

Answer

$v \approx 40.2\,\text{km/s}$

Three significant figures, as in the voltage $1.10\,\text{kV}$, and $E \approx 1.1\,\text{MV/m}$, two significant figures, as in the distance $1.0\,\text{mm}$.

Reasonableness

The kinetic energy of the ion is $1100\,\text{eV}$. An electron would get the same energy, but a xenon ion is about $2.4 \cdot 10^{5}$ times heavier, so its speed is $\sqrt{2.4 \cdot 10^{5}} \approx 490$ times smaller. Still, the speed is about ten times the exhaust speed of a chemical rocket engine, about $3\text{–}4\,\text{km/s}$. In air the field $1.1\,\text{MV/m}$ would come close to breakdown, but in a vacuum there is no gas that could conduct.

Given

Find

Principle

Current is the charge flowing per unit time, so it tells how many ions the thruster sends out per second. The mass $\Delta m$ of the ions leaving in the time $\Delta t$ gets the speed $v$ from almost rest. By the impulse principle their change in momentum $\Delta m \cdot v$ is the impulse $F\,\Delta t$ of the force the thruster exerts on them. By Newton's third law the ions exert an equal and opposite force on the thruster, which pushes the satellite (chapters Momentum and impulse and Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws).

$N = \dfrac{I}{e}$, $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = Nm$ and $F\,\Delta t = \Delta m \cdot v$, so

$F$$= \dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,v$
$= \dfrac{Imv}{e}$

Conversions

$1\,\text{A} = 1\,\text{C/s}$. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 1000\,\text{mN}$.

Substitution

$N$$= \dfrac{1.60\,\text{C/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}}$

$\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$$= 9.988 \cdot 10^{18}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot 2.180 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{kg}$
$= 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{kg/s}$
$F$$= 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{kg/s} \cdot 4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m/s}$
$= 0.08754\,\text{N}$
$= 87.5\,\text{mN}$

Answer

$F \approx 87.5\,\text{mN}$

Three significant figures, as in the current and the voltage.

Reasonableness

The force is as large as the weight of an object of about $9\,\text{g}$ at the surface of the Earth, that is, very small. The unit is correct:

$\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$$= \text{kg} \cdot \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$
$= \text{N}$

d)

Find

Principle

A current flowing across the accelerating voltage gets the power $P = UI$. When only the electric force does work, the same power goes into the kinetic energy of the ions. The thrust is the change in momentum of the ions per second (chapters Potential energy and potential in an electric field and Momentum and impulse).

Model answer

The ion beam needs the power $P = UI = 1100\,\text{V} \cdot 1.60\,\text{A} = 1.76\,\text{kW}$. It is as large as the kinetic energy of the ions per second, $\tfrac12 \cdot 2.177 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}} \cdot (4.021 \cdot 10^{4}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}})^2 = 1.76\,\text{kW}$, as the conservation of energy requires. The $2.5\,\text{kW}$ of the panels is therefore enough for the beam, and the remaining $0.7\,\text{kW}$ goes to ionisation, neutralisation and the other devices of the satellite. The thrust is $F = \tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,v$ and the power $P = \tfrac12 \tfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,v^2$, so $F = \tfrac{2P}{v}$. With a given power a large ion speed gives a small thrust, only $87.5\,\text{mN}$. On the other hand, a large speed means that each kilogram of propellant gives a lot of momentum, so little xenon is used. An ion thruster is not suitable for launch or fast manoeuvres, but for long orbit changes of weeks or months it is good, because it saves tens of kilograms of mass.

  • In part a the mass of the electron is used as the mass of the ion, or kilovolts are not converted to volts.
  • In part b the current is interpreted directly as the number of ions per second without dividing it by the elementary charge.
  • In part b the thrust is calculated from the electric force $eE$ on one ion and not from the momentum of all the ions leaving per second.
  • In part c the days are converted to seconds wrongly, for example $30 \cdot 3600\,\text{s}$.
  • In part d a large power is assumed to mean a large thrust.

Also accepted

  • In part b the same result from the forces between the grids: the field exerts the force $eE$ on each ion between the grids, and an ion stays between them for the time $\tfrac{2d}{v}$, so there are $N \cdot \tfrac{2d}{v}$ ions between them at a time and $F = N \cdot \tfrac{2d}{v} \cdot e\tfrac{U}{d} = \tfrac{2UI}{v} \approx 87.5\,\text{mN}$.

Hint 2: what is the connection

Hint 3: an intermediate step

Show the solution

Given

the capacitance $C = 2.2\,\mu\text{F}$, the thickness of the dielectric $d = 0.020\,\text{mm}$, the dielectric strength $E_\text{max} = 16\,\text{MV/m}$

Find

the largest energy that can be stored, $W_\text{max}$

Principle

The field between the plates is $E = \tfrac{U}{d}$. Breakdown does not occur when the field is at most the dielectric strength of the dielectric, so the largest allowed voltage is $E_\text{max}d$. The energy grows with the square of the voltage, so it is largest at this voltage (chapter The capacitor and its energy).

Formula

The voltage in a uniform electric field is $U = Ed$. The largest allowed field is $E = E_\text{max}$, so $U_\text{max} = E_\text{max}d$. The energy of the capacitor is then $W_\text{max} = \tfrac12 CU_\text{max}^2$

Conversions

$d$$= 0.020\,\text{mm}$
$= 0.020 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$
$= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{m}$

$E_\text{max} = 16 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{V/m}$ and $C = 2.2 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F}$. Finally $1\,\text{J} = 1000\,\text{mJ}$.

Substitution

$U_\text{max}$$= 16 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{m}} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\text{m}$
$= 320\,\text{V}$
$W_\text{max}$$= \tfrac12 \cdot 2.2 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{F} \cdot (320\,\text{V})^2$
$= 0.1126\,\text{J}$

Answer

$W_\text{max} \approx 0.11\,\text{J}$

Or about $110\,\text{mJ}$, two significant figures, as in the data.

Reasonableness

In practice the largest allowed voltage marked on a capacitor is lower than $320\,\text{V}$, because the dielectric may have weak spots. The energy is small, but a $320\,\text{V}$ capacitor can still give a painful shock.

4. Explanation task

A balloon rubbed on a woollen jumper sticks to a wall, although the wall has no net charge. Explain with the movement of charges and Coulomb's law why the wall attracts the balloon. Also say where the part of opposite sign of the charge created by rubbing went.

Model answer

When the balloon is rubbed, electrons move from the wool onto the surface of the balloon. The balloon gets a negative charge, and an equal positive charge is left on the woollen jumper, because charge is conserved: it only moves from one object to another. The wall is an insulator, so its electrons cannot move freely, but the field of the balloon shifts the charges of each molecule slightly. The molecules become polarised: their positive parts are slightly closer to the balloon and the negative parts slightly further away. The wall as a whole stays neutral. By Coulomb's law the force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, so the attraction of the closer positive parts is larger than the repulsion of the more distant negative parts. The net force pulls the balloon towards the wall. The normal force of the wall and friction hold the balloon in place until the charge gradually leaks away into the humid air.
