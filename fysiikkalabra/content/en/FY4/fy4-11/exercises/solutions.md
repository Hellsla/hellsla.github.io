## fy4-11-L1-01
**Given:** $v_0 = 72.0\,\text{km/h}$, reaction time $t_\text{r} = 1.20\,\text{s}$
**Find:** the reaction distance $s_\text{r}$ in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** During the reaction time the driver is not yet braking, so the car moves at a constant speed. In uniform motion the distance travelled is speed times time.
**Formula:** $s_\text{r} = v_0\,t_\text{r}$
**Conversions:** $72.0\,\text{km/h} = \dfrac{72.0 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20.0\,\text{m/s}$
**Substitution:** $s_\text{r} = 20.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 1.20\,\text{s} = 24.0\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $24.0\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the speed $72.0\,\text{km/h}$ and the reaction time $1.20\,\text{s}$.
**Reasonableness:** The car travels $20\,\text{m}$ every second, so in a little over a second the distance is a little over $20\,\text{m}$. That is about five car lengths that the car covers before braking even begins.

## fy4-11-L1-02
**Given:** speed at the start of braking $v_0 = 25.0\,\text{m/s}$, deceleration $a = 8.00\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the braking distance $s_\text{b}$ in $\text{m}$
**Principle:** The deceleration is constant, so on the $(t,v)$ graph the speed falls in a straight line to zero. The braking distance is the area of the triangle under the graph, $\tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,t_\text{b}$, where the braking time is $t_\text{b} = \tfrac{v_0}{a}$.
**Formula:** $s_\text{b} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,t_\text{b}$ and $t_\text{b} = \dfrac{v_0}{a}$, so $s_\text{b} = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a}$
**Conversions:** The speed is already in $\text{m/s}$, so no conversions are needed.
**Substitution:** $s_\text{b} = \dfrac{(25.0\,\text{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 8.00\,\text{m/s}^2} = \dfrac{625\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{16.0\,\text{m/s}^2} = 39.0625\,\text{m}$
**Answer:** $39.1\,\text{m}$, three significant figures like the speed $25.0\,\text{m/s}$ and the deceleration $8.00\,\text{m/s}^2$.
**Reasonableness:** Braking takes $25 : 8 \approx 3\,\text{s}$, and the average speed is half the initial speed, about $12.5\,\text{m/s}$. The distance is about $12.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 3.1\,\text{s} \approx 39\,\text{m}$, as in the calculation.

## fy4-11-L1-07
**Find:** why a seat belt reduces the average force on the passenger.
**Principle:** By the impulse–momentum principle, the impulse of the force acting on the passenger equals the change in their momentum, $F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t = m\,\Delta v$. When the passenger stops, the momentum changes from $m\,v$ to zero.
**Model answer:** The change in the passenger's momentum is the same with and without a belt, because they stop in both cases. So the belt does not reduce the change in momentum or the impulse. The belt lengthens the time $\Delta t$ in which the change takes place, because it lets the passenger slow down more gently and over a longer time. When $m\,\Delta v$ is constant, a longer time means a smaller average force $F_\text{avg} = \dfrac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. The belt does not slow the passenger before the collision and does not take part of their momentum: the passenger moves with the car until the car stops.
