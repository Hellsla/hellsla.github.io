Apply independently

In these tasks nobody tells you which formula to use. Decide yourself which phenomena and models belong to the situation, solve the task on paper and justify your solution, as in the matriculation examination.

Five tasks bring the module together: the motion of a charged particle in electric and magnetic fields, a generator and induction, the transmission of electrical energy and the force between current-carrying conductors, thermal radiation, the path of light in an optical fibre, interference in a thin film, and the interference of light at a grating. In each one you decide which models fit the situation and justify your choice.

Solve the task yourself first, on paper or on a computer. You may use a calculator, a data booklet and graphing software. Write out your reasoning, formulas, substitutions and answers with units.

1Ion implanter 16 points

Made-up example data · The dimensions and voltages of the device are made up but of the same order of magnitude as in a small ion implanter. Masses of the boron atoms: AME2020 atomic mass evaluation (Wang et al., 2021); share of boron-10 in natural boron: IUPAC (2013).

In the manufacture of microchips, boron ions are shot into a silicon wafer so that the electrical conductivity of the silicon changes in the desired way. In an ion implanter, positive ions with a charge of one elementary charge come from the ion source and are accelerated by a voltage, pass through a velocity selector and are guided through an analysing magnet to the wafer. In the field of the magnet the ions travel along a semicircle, and only ions whose path hits a slit in the wall of the chamber reach the wafer. About $19.9\,\%$ of natural boron is the isotope boron-10 and the rest is boron-11. Only boron-11 ions are wanted at the wafer.

Data:

  • mass of a boron-11 ion $m_{11} = 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$ and of a boron-10 ion $m_{10} = 1.6627 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$
  • charge of an ion $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$
  • accelerating voltage $U = 30.0\,\text{kV}$; the kinetic energy of the ions before acceleration is negligibly small
  • distance between the plates of the velocity selector $d = 2.00\,\text{cm}$ and magnetic field $B_\text{s} = 0.200\,\text{T}$
  • radius of the path of the boron-11 ions in the analysing magnet $r = 40.0\,\text{cm}$.

In the figure both magnetic fields point into the page. The effect of gravity on the ions is negligibly small.

  1. a) The voltage between the plates of the selector is adjusted so that the boron-11 ions pass through the selector in a straight line. Which forces act on an ion travelling straight through the selector, in which directions do they point and how do their magnitudes compare? Which plate is positive? Justify your answer. You may illustrate your answer with a force diagram. 3 p
  2. b) What is the speed of the boron-11 ions after the acceleration? 3 p
  3. c) What voltage must be connected between the plates of the selector? 3 p
  4. d) How strong must the magnetic field of the analysing magnet be? If boron-10 ions could also pass through the selector at the same speed, how far from the slit of the boron-11 ions would they hit the wall of the chamber? 4 p
  5. e) In reality the kinetic energy of the ions after the acceleration varies by as much as $10\,\%$ because the conditions in the ion source and the accelerating voltage vary. An engineer suggests: “The analysing magnet is unnecessary. All the ions are accelerated by the same voltage, so the boron-10 ions are faster and do not get through the selector.” Evaluate the suggestion. 3 p

Model answer

a)

Find

the forces acting on a boron-11 ion travelling straight, and which plate of the selector is positive

Principle

In the selector the ion is acted on by a magnetic force, because it moves in a magnetic field, and by an electric force, because there is an electric field between the plates. Gravity is negligibly small. The ion travels straight at constant velocity only if the sum of the forces is zero, that is, the forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The magnetic force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, and its direction is given by the right-hand rule. The electric force on a positive charge is in the direction of the electric field.

Model answer

The ion moves to the right and the magnetic field points into the page. By the right-hand rule, the magnetic force $\overline{F}_\text{m}$ on a positive ion points upwards.
- For the ion to travel straight, the electric force $\overline{F}_\text{s}$ must point downwards and be equal in magnitude to the magnetic force. The force arrows are drawn equally long.
- The velocity $\overline{v}$ is drawn separately in its own colour, because it is not a force.

The electric force on a positive ion is in the direction of the electric field, so the electric field $\overline{E}$ points downwards. The electric field points from the positive plate to the negative one, so the upper plate is positive. The choice does not depend on the mass of the ion: the same polarity suits all positive ions.

b)

Given

accelerating voltage $U = 30.0\,\text{kV}$, charge of the ion $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ and mass $m_{11} = 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$; the kinetic energy of the ion before acceleration is negligibly small

Find

the speed $v$ of the ion after the acceleration

Principle

The electric field in the accelerating gap does work $W = eU$ on the ion. Because the kinetic energy of the ion before acceleration is negligibly small and there are no other forces, the work becomes entirely the kinetic energy of the ion (the energy principle).

Formula

$eU = \tfrac{1}{2} m_{11} v^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m_{11}}}$

Conversions

$U$$= 30.0\,\text{kV}$
$= 30.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}$

Substitution

$v$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 30.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}}{1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}}}$
$= \sqrt{5.258 \cdot 10^{11}\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$
$= 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$

Answer

$v \approx 7.25 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$

Three significant figures like the accelerating voltage.

Reasonableness

The speed is about $0.24\,\%$ of the speed of light, so the classical expression for kinetic energy is sufficient. The kinetic energy of the ion, $4.8 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$, is small, but the ion is very light, so the speed is high.

c)

Given

$v = 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$ (part b), $B_\text{s} = 0.200\,\text{T}$ and the distance between the plates $d = 2.00\,\text{cm}$

Find

the voltage $U_\text{s}$ between the plates

Principle

The ion travels straight when the electric force $eE$ and the magnetic force $evB_\text{s}$ are equal in magnitude (part a). The electric field between the plates is uniform, so its strength is the voltage between the plates divided by the distance between the plates.

Formula

$eE = evB_\text{s}$, so $E = vB_\text{s}$. When $E = U_\text{s} : d$, we get $U_\text{s} = Ed = vB_\text{s}d$.

Conversions

$d$$= 2.00\,\text{cm}$
$= 0.0200\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$E$$= 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s} \cdot 0.200\,\text{T}$
$= 1.450 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m}$
$U_\text{s}$$= 1.450 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{V/m} \cdot 0.0200\,\text{m}$
$= 2900\,\text{V}$

Answer

$U_\text{s} \approx 2.90\,\text{kV}$

Three significant figures like the data.

Reasonableness

The units are consistent, because

$1\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot 1\,\text{T}$$= 1\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$

The voltage is about a tenth of the accelerating voltage, which is easy to achieve with a plate separation of $2\,\text{cm}$.

d)

Given

$v = 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$, $m_{11} = 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, $m_{10} = 1.6627 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ and the radius of the path of the boron-11 ions $r = 40.0\,\text{cm}$

Find

the magnetic flux density $B$ of the analysing magnet and the distance of the point of impact of the boron-10 ions from the slit of the boron-11 ions

Principle

The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does not change the speed but turns the direction of motion. In a uniform field the ion moves in uniform circular motion, and the magnetic force is its centripetal force. After the selector all the ions have the same velocity, so the radius of the path is directly proportional to the mass of the ion. After the semicircle the ion hits the wall at the far end of a diameter $2r$ from the point of entry, so the distance between the points of impact of the two kinds of ion is the difference of their diameters.

Formula

$evB = \dfrac{m v^2}{r}$, so $B = \dfrac{m_{11} v}{e r}$. At the same speed and in the same field

$r_{10}$$= \dfrac{m_{10} v}{eB}$
$= \dfrac{m_{10}}{m_{11}}\,r$

and the distance between the points of impact is

$\Delta x$$= 2r - 2r_{10}$
$= 2(r - r_{10})$

Conversions

$r = 40.0\,\text{cm} = 0.400\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$mv$$= 1.8281 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg} \cdot 7.251 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$
$= 1.3256 \cdot 10^{-20}\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$

so

$B$$= \dfrac{1.3256 \cdot 10^{-20}\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 0.400\,\text{m}}$
$= 0.2069\,\text{T}$

The radius of the boron-10 ions is

$r_{10}$$= \dfrac{1.6627}{1.8281} \cdot 0.400\,\text{m}$
$= 0.3638\,\text{m}$

so

$\Delta x$$= 2 \cdot (0.400\,\text{m} - 0.3638\,\text{m})$
$= 0.0724\,\text{m}$

Answer

$B \approx 0.207\,\text{T}$

And $\Delta x \approx 7.2\,\text{cm}$: the boron-10 ions would hit the wall about $7\,\text{cm}$ closer to the point of entry of the ions than the slit. The field has three significant figures like the data; the distance is the difference of two almost equal numbers, so it is left with two.

Reasonableness

The field is of the same order of magnitude as that of an ordinary electromagnet. The masses differ by about $9\,\%$, so the radii also differ by about $9\,\%$, that is, about $3.6\,\text{cm}$, and the diameters by twice that. The difference is much larger than the size of the slit, so the magnet certainly separates the isotopes.

e)

Find

whether the analysing magnet can be left out when the kinetic energy of the ions varies by as much as $10\,\%$ after the acceleration

Principle

In the balance of forces on an ion passing through the selector, $eE = evB_\text{s}$, the charge cancels and the mass does not appear at all. The selector therefore lets straight through every ion whose speed is $E : B_\text{s}$. The speed after the acceleration depends on both the mass and the kinetic energy.

Model answer

Without variation in energy. A boron-10 ion that has received the full acceleration is lighter, so its speed is greater: $v_{10} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eU}{m_{10}}}$. Substituting the values of part b and the mass $m_{10} = 1.6627 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$ gives $v_{10} \approx 7.60 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$. The magnetic force at that speed is greater than the electric force, so the ion curves upwards in the selector and does not get through. Here the engineer is right.

With varying energy. A boron-10 ion passes through the selector if its speed is the same as that of the boron-11 ions, that is, $7.25 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$. Its kinetic energy is then $\tfrac{1}{2} m_{10} v^2$, while that of a boron-11 ion is $\tfrac{1}{2} m_{11} v^2$, so the ratio is $m_{10} : m_{11} = 1.6627 : 1.8281 \approx 0.91$. A boron-10 ion whose kinetic energy is about $9\,\%$ smaller than the full value therefore passes straight through. When the energy varies by as much as $10\,\%$, there are certain to be such ions.

Conclusion. The suggestion does not work. The selector selects speed, not mass, and when the energy varies, boron-10 ions also get through it. Only the analysing magnet separates ions travelling at the same speed according to their mass, because the radius of the path is then directly proportional to the mass.

Also accepted

  • The speed in part b can also be calculated through the kinetic energy: first $W = eU = 4.806 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$ and then $v = \sqrt{2W : m_{11}}$.
  • The distance in part d can also be calculated by first finding the radius of the boron-10 ions directly from the equation $r_{10} = \dfrac{m_{10} v}{eB}$. The answer $7.2$–$7.3\,\text{cm}$ is accepted.
  • In part e it is also enough to give a justification that calculates the accelerating voltage at which a boron-10 ion would get the right speed: $0.91 \cdot 30.0\,\text{kV} \approx 27.3\,\text{kV}$.

Common mistakes

  • The magnetic force is drawn in the direction of the velocity or the field, or its direction is deduced with the rule for a negative charge.
  • The electric force is drawn regardless of the plates, and from the upper plate it is concluded that the electric field points towards the positive plate.
  • The velocity is drawn in the force diagram as a force or in the same colour as the forces.
  • The kilovolts of the accelerating voltage are forgotten when converting to volts, so the speed is about $32$ times too small.
  • In part c the voltage is calculated with the formula $U = Ed$ without taking into account that $E$ must first be solved from the balance of forces.
  • In part d the difference of the radii is given as the distance between the points of impact instead of the difference of the diameters.

2Hub dynamo 16 points

Made-up example data · The data of the dynamo and the graph of the voltage are made up but typical: a bicycle dynamo light is usually $6\,\text{V}$ and $3\,\text{W}$, and a hub dynamo often has $28$ magnetic poles.

In a dynamo built into the hub of a bicycle's front wheel, a ring of permanent magnets with $28$ magnetic poles, alternately north and south poles, rotates with the wheel. The stationary coil has an iron core, and the magnetic flux through it changes direction each time the next pole passes it. The voltage therefore has $14$ cycles for every revolution of the wheel. The diameter of the wheel with its tyre is $0.70\,\text{m}$. A light with a rated voltage of $6.0\,\text{V}$ and a power of $3.0\,\text{W}$ can be connected to the dynamo.

A cyclist measured the voltage of the dynamo with an oscilloscope while the light was off and the cyclist rode at a steady speed. The graph is below.

  1. a) From the graph, determine the frequency and the effective value of the voltage. 3 p
  2. b) At what speed was the cyclist riding? 3 p
  3. c) The light shines properly when the effective value of the voltage of the dynamo is at least the rated voltage of the light. Estimate the lowest speed at which this is so, assuming that the light receives the same voltage as was measured with no load. 3 p
  4. d) The cyclist claims: “When the light is on, pedalling is harder, even though nothing in the dynamo rubs against anything else. When the light is off, the dynamo does not brake the wheel at all.” Evaluate both parts of the claim. 3 p
  5. e) When the light is on, the efficiency of the dynamo is $60\,\%$. How large an extra force must the cyclist overcome at the speed of part b? Does it matter, when air resistance and rolling resistance at this speed total about $10\,\text{N}$? 4 p

Model answer

a)

Given

the graph of the voltage of the dynamo with the light off

Find

the frequency $f$ and the effective value $U$ of the voltage

Principle

The voltage varies sinusoidally, so the period is the duration of one complete oscillation and the frequency is its reciprocal. The effective value is the direct voltage that would produce the same average power in a resistor. For a sinusoidal voltage it is the peak value divided by $\sqrt{2}$.

Reading the graph

The voltage starts from zero and returns to zero, changing in the same direction, at $30\,\text{ms}$, $60\,\text{ms}$ and $90\,\text{ms}$: three cycles last $90\,\text{ms}$, so $T = 90\,\text{ms} : 3 = 30\,\text{ms}$. Reading three cycles is more accurate than reading one. The peaks are at $7.5\,\text{ms}$, $37.5\,\text{ms}$ and $67.5\,\text{ms}$ on the grid line $14\,\text{V}$, so $e_0 = 14\,\text{V}$.

Formula

$f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ and $U = \dfrac{e_0}{\sqrt{2}}$

Conversions

$T = 30\,\text{ms} = 0.030\,\text{s}$

Substitution

$f$$= \dfrac{1}{0.030\,\text{s}}$
$= 33.3\,\text{Hz}$
$U$$= \dfrac{14\,\text{V}}{\sqrt{2}}$
$= 9.90\,\text{V}$

Answer

$f \approx 33\,\text{Hz}$

And $U \approx 9.9\,\text{V}$, two significant figures like the values read from the graph.

Reasonableness

The frequency is lower than the $50\,\text{Hz}$ of the mains, which suits a wheel that turns only a few revolutions per second. The effective value is about $70\,\%$ of the peak value, as it should be for a sinusoidal voltage.

b)

Given

$f = 33.3\,\text{Hz}$ (part a), $14$ cycles of the voltage per revolution of the wheel and the diameter of the wheel $D = 0.70\,\text{m}$

Find

the speed $v$ of the cyclist

Principle

The magnet ring has $14$ pairs of north and south poles. When one pair of poles passes the coil, the flux through the coil makes one complete oscillation, so the voltage has $14$ cycles per revolution of the wheel. The rotation frequency of the wheel is therefore $14$ times smaller than the frequency of the voltage. When the wheel rolls without slipping, in one revolution it advances the length of its circumference $\pi D$.

Formula

$f_\text{w}$$= \dfrac{f}{14}$
$v$$= \pi D f_\text{w}$
$= \pi D \cdot \dfrac{f}{14}$

Conversions

The answer is converted at the end: $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.

Substitution

$f_\text{w}$$= \dfrac{33.3\,\text{Hz}}{14}$
$= 2.381\,\text{Hz}$
$v$$= \pi \cdot 0.70\,\text{m} \cdot 2.381\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}$
$= 5.236\,\text{m/s}$
$= 5.236 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h}$
$= 18.8\,\text{km/h}$

Answer

$v \approx 5.2\,\text{m/s} \approx 19\,\text{km/h}$

Two significant figures like the diameter and the period.

Reasonableness

The speed is a normal commuting cycling speed. The wheel turns only about two and a half revolutions per second, but $28$ poles still make the frequency of the voltage more than $30\,\text{Hz}$.

c)

Given

the effective voltage with no load $U = 9.90\,\text{V}$ at the speed $v = 5.236\,\text{m/s}$ (parts a and b), rated voltage of the light $U_\text{lamp} = 6.0\,\text{V}$

Find

the lowest speed $v_\text{min}$ at which the effective voltage is at least $6.0\,\text{V}$

Principle

The peak value of the voltage of a generator is $e_0 = NBA \cdot 2\pi f$. The number of turns of the coil, the field of the magnets and the area stay the same, so the peak value, and with it the effective value, is directly proportional to the frequency. The frequency in turn is directly proportional to the rotation frequency of the wheel and to the speed. The effective voltage is therefore directly proportional to the speed: the more slowly one rides, the more slowly the magnets pass the coil and the more slowly the flux changes.

Formula

$\dfrac{v_\text{min}}{v}$$= \dfrac{U_\text{lamp}}{U}$

so

$v_\text{min}$$= v \cdot \dfrac{U_\text{lamp}}{U}$

Conversions

The answer is converted at the end to kilometres per hour: $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.

Substitution

$v_\text{min}$$= 5.236\,\text{m/s} \cdot \dfrac{6.0\,\text{V}}{9.90\,\text{V}}$
$= 3.17\,\text{m/s}$
$= 3.17 \cdot 3.6\,\text{km/h}$
$= 11.4\,\text{km/h}$

Answer

$v_\text{min} \approx 3.2\,\text{m/s} \approx 11\,\text{km/h}$

Two significant figures like the data.

Reasonableness

The result is slow cycling but brisker than walking, so at walking speed the light stays dim. The assumption is rough, however: when the light is on, a current flows in the coil and part of the voltage is left in the coil itself, so the light gets less than the no-load voltage. The real lowest speed is therefore somewhat greater.

d)

Find

whether the dynamo brakes the wheel when the light is on and when it is off

Principle

When the coil is in a closed circuit, the changing flux produces an induced current in it. By Lenz's law, the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change that produces it. By conservation of energy, the energy radiated by the light must come from somewhere. A changing magnetic field also produces eddy currents in conducting metal parts.

Model answer

Light on. An induced current flows in the coil, and its magnetic field opposes the change in flux: it pushes an approaching magnetic pole away and pulls a receding one back. The rotating magnet ring is therefore acted on by a braking magnetic force, even though nothing touches anything else. The same follows from conservation of energy: the light radiates energy that comes from the work done by the cyclist. If there were no braking force, the light would get energy from nothing. The first part of the claim is true.

Light off. The circuit is open, so no current flows in the coil and the coil does not brake. The changing field of the magnet ring does, however, produce eddy currents in the metal parts of the dynamo, for example in the iron core of the coil and in the metal hub. The eddy currents heat the metal and brake the rotation in accordance with Lenz's law. In addition, the magnetisation of the iron core reverses continuously, which takes a little energy.

Conclusion. The second part of the claim is not exactly true: the dynamo brakes the wheel a little even when the light is off. The braking is then much smaller than when the light is on, because the iron core is made of thin insulated sheets, in which the eddy currents stay small.

e)

Given

power of the light $P_\text{lamp} = 3.0\,\text{W}$, efficiency of the dynamo $\eta = 60\,\% = 0.60$, speed $v = 5.236\,\text{m/s}$ (part b) and air resistance and rolling resistance together about $10\,\text{N}$

Find

the extra braking force $F$ of the dynamo and its significance

Principle

The dynamo converts the cyclist's work into electrical energy. The light receives the fraction of the power taken by the dynamo given by the efficiency, so the power taken by the dynamo is $P_\text{lamp} : \eta$. When the braking force acts on a wheel moving at speed $v$, the power of the work it does is $P = Fv$, as in an eddy-current brake.

Formula

$P_\text{in}$$= \dfrac{P_\text{lamp}}{\eta}$

$P_\text{in} = Fv$, so $F = \dfrac{P_\text{lamp}}{\eta v}$

Conversions

Not needed: the power is in watts and the speed in metres per second.

Substitution

$P_\text{in}$$= \dfrac{3.0\,\text{W}}{0.60}$
$= 5.0\,\text{W}$
$F$$= \dfrac{5.0\,\text{W}}{5.236\,\text{m/s}}$
$= 0.955\,\text{N}$

Answer

$F \approx 0.95\,\text{N}$

Two significant figures like the power of the light and the efficiency.

Reasonableness

Air resistance and rolling resistance at this speed total about $10\,\text{N}$, so the light adds about a tenth to the resistance. The difference can be felt, but it is small; for example, a $1\,\%$ uphill gradient adds about $9\,\text{N}$ for a $90\,\text{kg}$ cyclist and bicycle. When riding more slowly the same power requires a larger force, so the light is felt most on an uphill.

Also accepted

  • In part a the period can also be read from a single cycle, for example between two successive peaks: $37.5\,\text{ms} - 7.5\,\text{ms} = 30\,\text{ms}$.
  • The answers to parts b and c can be given in metres per second; in part c, $11$–$12\,\text{km/h}$ is accepted.
  • In part c the peak values can be compared: the light needs a peak value of $6.0\,\text{V} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 8.5\,\text{V}$, and $v_\text{min} = v \cdot 8.5\,\text{V} : 14\,\text{V}$.

Common mistakes

  • The period is read as the interval between zero crossings, which is only half a cycle, so the frequency is twice too large.
  • The effective value is calculated by multiplying the peak value by $\sqrt{2}$ instead of dividing by it.
  • In part b the frequency of the voltage is used as the rotation frequency of the wheel, so the speed comes out at over $250\,\text{km/h}$.

Given

Principle

The current flows to the mine along one conductor and returns along the other, so the length of the conductors in the circuit is $2 \cdot 25\,\text{km}$. The resistance is obtained from the resistivity, the length and the cross-sectional area. The transmitted power is the product of the voltage and the current, so the current is the power divided by the voltage. The power converted into heat in the resistance of the line is the product of the resistance and the square of the current.

Formula

$R = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}$, $I = \dfrac{P}{U}$ and $P_\text{h} = R I^2$

Conversions

$l$$= 2 \cdot 25\,\text{km}$
$= 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m}$
$A$$= 150\,\text{mm}^2$
$= 150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$

$P = 6.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W}$ and $U = 20 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{V}$

$R$$= 2.82 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m} \cdot \dfrac{5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m}}{150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2}$

$= 300\,\text{A}$
$P_\text{h}$$= 9.40\,\Omega \cdot (300\,\text{A})^2$
$= 846\,000\,\text{W}$

The share is

$= 0.141$
$= 14.1\,\%$

$P_\text{h} \approx 850\,\text{kW}$

That is, about $14\,\%$ of the transmitted power, two significant figures like the power and the voltage.

The losses of the whole electricity grid in Finland are only a few per cent in total, so a loss of $14\,\%$ in a single line is far too large. At a low voltage a large current is needed, and the loss grows as the square of the current.

Conversions

$U = 20\,\text{kV} = 20\,000\,\text{V}$

d)

Find

whether the loss of the $20\,\text{kV}$ line can be made as small by using thicker conductors

Principle

The power loss is $P_\text{h} = RI^2$. At a voltage of $20\,\text{kV}$ the current is always $300\,\text{A}$, so the only way to reduce the loss is to reduce the resistance. The resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area, and the mass of the conductors is directly proportional to their volume.

Model answer

Required thickness. For the loss to be the same as in the $110\,\text{kV}$ line, the resistance must be $846\,\text{kW} : 28.0\,\text{kW} \approx 30$ times smaller. The cross-sectional area must therefore be $30.25 \cdot 150\,\text{mm}^2 \approx 4500\,\text{mm}^2$. The diameter of a solid round conductor would be $2\sqrt{4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2 : \pi} \approx 7.6\,\text{cm}$.

Amount of aluminium. The mass of the present conductors is $2700\,\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\text{m} \cdot 150 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2 \approx 20\,\text{t}$. The mass of the thick conductors would be $30$ times as much, about $610\,\text{t}$. The poles would have to carry all this, and the aluminium would cost many times the price of a transformer.

Why it pays to raise the voltage. Raising the voltage reduces the current in the same ratio, and the loss depends on the square of the current: $5.5$ times the voltage reduces the loss by a factor of about $30$. A thicker conductor reduces the loss only in the same ratio as the cross-sectional area increases, so to get the same result the cross-sectional area of the conductor has to be made $30$ times as large.

Conclusion. The suggestion would work in principle, but the line would be enormously heavy and expensive. That is why long transmission is done at high voltage and the voltage is stepped down with a transformer only near the place of use.

Principle

The current flows to the mine along one conductor and returns along the other, so currents of equal magnitude flow in the conductors in opposite directions. Each conductor is a long straight conductor, and its magnetic field at the other conductor is obtained from the formula for the field of a straight conductor, because the conductors are much longer than the distance between them. The field is perpendicular to the other conductor, so a force $F = IlB$ acts on it. By the right-hand rule, currents in opposite directions repel each other. With alternating current the currents change direction at the same time, so they are always in opposite directions and the conductors repel each other all the time. The weight of a metre of conductor is obtained from its mass, which is the product of the density and the volume.

$\dfrac{F}{l}$$= \dfrac{\mu_0 I^2}{2\pi r}$

$I_\text{sc}$$= 8.0\,\text{kA}$

In normal operation the force does not matter, but in a short circuit it is about twice the weight of the conductor.

Reasonableness

In normal operation the force is only about $0.3\,\%$ of the weight of the conductor, about $4.0\,\text{N}$ per metre. For a black body, the product of the wavelength of the peak of the spectrum and the absolute temperature is a constant (Wien's displacement law). The slab is assumed to be a black body, as the graph does.

The peak of the curve is on the vertical grid line at $2.0\,\mu\text{m}$, so $\lambda_\text{max} = 2.0\,\mu\text{m}$. The peak is flat, so its position can be read to an accuracy of about $\pm 0.05\,\mu\text{m}$ (part d).

$\lambda_\text{max}$$= 2.0\,\mu\text{m}$
$= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$

b)

Find

why the slab glows orange-red although the peak of the spectrum is in the infrared

Principle

The spectrum of thermal radiation is continuous: the object radiates at all wavelengths, but a different amount at each. The eye sees only wavelengths of about $0.38$–$0.75\,\mu\text{m}$, and the colour of the glow depends on how the radiation is distributed in this range.

Model answer

In the graph the curve begins to rise already in the range of visible light, although it looks almost zero there. Visible light is therefore only a small part of the total radiation, but it is there. In the visible range the intensity increases steeply with increasing wavelength, so there is much more red and orange light than green or blue. That is why the glow looks orange-red and not white.

As a whole, the hot slab radiates with an enormous power, so even a small visible fraction is bright to the eye. The peak of the spectrum of an object at room temperature is at about $10\,\mu\text{m}$, and its share of visible light is vanishingly small, so it does not glow. If the slab were heated further, the peak would shift to shorter wavelengths and the glow would become yellower and brighter.

The light passes through two interfaces. At the end face it enters the core from the air and is refracted towards the normal to an angle $\beta$ (the law of refraction). At the side surface of the core the light is totally reflected if its angle of incidence is at least the critical angle. The end face is perpendicular to the side surface, so the normal of the end face is parallel to the side surface and the angle of incidence of the ray at the side surface is $90^\circ - \beta$. The greater $\theta$ is, the greater $\beta$ is and the smaller the angle of incidence at the side surface, so at the greatest allowed angle of incidence the angle of incidence at the side surface is exactly the critical angle.

Formula

$n_1 \sin\alpha_\text{r} = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$, so

$\sin\alpha_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$

At the end face $1.00 \cdot \sin\theta = n_1 \sin\beta$, where $\beta = 90^\circ - \alpha_\text{r}$, so $\sin\theta = n_1 \sin(90^\circ - \alpha_\text{r})$.

Conversions

Not needed: refractive indices have no units, and the angles are in degrees.

Substitution

$\sin\alpha_\text{r}$$= \dfrac{1.42}{1.44}$
$= 0.9861$

so $\alpha_\text{r} = 80.44^\circ$. Then

$\beta$$= 90^\circ - 80.44^\circ$
$= 9.56^\circ$
$\sin\theta$$= 1.44 \cdot \sin 9.56^\circ$
$= 1.44 \cdot 0.1661$
$= 0.2392$

so $\theta = 13.84^\circ$.

Answer

$\alpha_\text{r} \approx 80.4^\circ$

And $\theta \approx 13.8^\circ$, three significant figures like the refractive indices.

Reasonableness

The refractive indices of the core and the cladding are almost equal, so the critical angle is close to $90^\circ$ and only light that arrives almost parallel to the fibre gets into it. The pyrometer therefore has to look at the slab along the direction of the fibre, and a lens is needed in front of the fibre to gather the light into a narrow cone. The angle is of the same order of magnitude as in telecommunications fibres.

Find

whether the result of part a conflicts with the thermocouple reading $1210\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ and what the difference may be due to

Principle

Two measurements conflict only if the difference between them is greater than their uncertainty. In addition, one must consider whether the methods measure the same thing and whether the assumptions of the model hold.

Model answer

Uncertainty. The peak of the spectrum is flat, so its position can be read from the graph to an accuracy of about $\pm 0.05\,\mu\text{m}$. The temperature is inversely proportional to the wavelength of the peak, so the relative uncertainty is the same: $0.05 : 2.0 = 2.5\,\%$, that is, $0.025 \cdot 1449\,\text{K} \approx 36\,\text{K}$. The difference from the thermocouple is $1210\,{}^\circ\text{C} - 1176\,{}^\circ\text{C} = 34\,\text{K}$, so it is within the reading accuracy.

What the methods measure. The pyrometer measures the radiation of the surface, the thermocouple the temperature of the interior of the slab. The surface cools faster than the interior through radiation and through the effect of the air and the rolls, so the surface may really be tens of degrees cooler.

Assumptions of the model. The pyrometer assumes the slab is a black body. If the steel radiated at every wavelength the same fraction of the radiation of a black body, the spectrum would simply be lower with the peak in the same place, and the result would not change. However, the emissivity of real steel and of the scale layer on its surface depends on the wavelength, so the shape of the spectrum and the position of the peak differ from the spectrum of a black body. The temperature given by the model is therefore only approximate.

Conclusion. The results do not conflict: the difference is within the reading accuracy and is also explained by the cooling of the surface. The pyrometer cannot be said to read wrongly. For a more accurate comparison the pyrometer should be calibrated with an object at a known temperature.

e)

Given

refractive index of the film $n = 1.38$, of the glass $1.50$, of air $1.00$ and wavelength $\lambda = 2.0\,\mu\text{m}$; the light arrives perpendicularly

Find

the thickness $d$ of the thinnest anti-reflection film and whether the greenish reflection is a sign of a wrong film

Principle

The waves reflected from the upper and lower surfaces of the film interfere. When the light arrives perpendicularly, the wave reflected from the lower surface travels an extra distance $2d$ in the film, where the wavelength is $\lambda : n$, so the path difference is $2nd$ measured in wavelengths in air. Both reflections take place against a material with a higher refractive index ($1.00 \to 1.38 \to 1.50$), so each reverses the wave and the reversals cancel each other. The waves then weaken each other when $2nd = (k + \tfrac{1}{2})\lambda$ and reinforce each other when $2nd = k\lambda$. The thinnest weakening film is obtained with $k = 0$.

Formula

$2nd = \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda$, so $d = \dfrac{\lambda}{4n}$

Conversions

$\lambda$$= 2.0\,\mu\text{m}$
$= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$d$$= \dfrac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}}{4 \cdot 1.38}$
$= 3.62 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$
$= 362\,\text{nm}$

Answer

The thinnest film is $d \approx 0.36\,\mu\text{m}$, that is, about $360\,\text{nm}$ thick, two significant figures like the wavelength. A greenish reflection is not a sign of a wrong film; it is to be expected with a film designed for the infrared.

Reasonableness

The film is a quarter of the wavelength in the film: $2000\,\text{nm} : 1.38 = 1449\,\text{nm}$, and a quarter of that is $362\,\text{nm}$, as it should be for an anti-reflection film. The path difference is

$2nd$$= 2 \cdot 1.38 \cdot 362\,\text{nm}$
$= 1000\,\text{nm}$

In the visible range it is twice $500\,\text{nm}$, so greenish light of $500\,\text{nm}$ is reinforced ($2nd = 2\lambda$). Red light of $667\,\text{nm}$ ($2nd = 1.5\lambda$) and violet of $400\,\text{nm}$ ($2nd = 2.5\lambda$) are weakened. That is why the window reflects greenish light, and the installer's suspicion is unfounded.

Also accepted

  • In part a the temperature can be converted to degrees Celsius by subtracting $273$; the answer $1180\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ or $1176\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ is accepted.
  • In part c the angle $\beta$ can first be calculated from the condition $\cos\beta = \sin\alpha_\text{r}$, or directly from the equation $\sin\theta = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}$, which follows from the same laws.
  • In part d a justified estimate of the reading accuracy between $\pm 0.05$ and $0.1\,\mu\text{m}$ is accepted.

Common mistakes

  • The temperature is calculated in degrees Celsius from Wien's law, or the wavelength is not converted to metres.
  • The wavelength of the peak is read in the range of visible light because the slab glows.
  • In part b it is claimed that infrared radiation is seen as red.
  • In part c the angle of refraction at the end face is used directly as the angle of incidence at the side surface, or the critical angle is calculated for the interface between air and the core.
  • In part c the refractive indices are swapped in the expression for the critical angle, so the value of the sine comes out greater than $1$.
  • In part d it is stated that the pyrometer reads wrongly without comparing the difference with the uncertainty of the measurement.
  • In part d it is said that a radiation weaker than that of a black body shifts the peak, although the peak shifts only if the emissivity depends on the wavelength.
  • In part e it is assumed that only one reflection reverses the wave, as in a soap film, so the thickness comes out twice as large, $\lambda : (2n) \approx 725\,\text{nm}$.
  • In part e the refractive index is forgotten, so the thickness comes out as $\lambda : 4 = 500\,\text{nm}$.

Principle

Two alternatives can be told apart by a measurement only if the difference between them is greater than the uncertainty of the measurement. The uncertainty of the angle is carried over to the wavelength through the grating equation.

Model answer

Uncertainty. The reading accuracy of the angle is $0.1^\circ$. e)

Find

whether either observation is wrong when in a prism violet and in a grating red is deviated the most

Principle

A grating and a prism separate colours by different phenomena. In a grating the direction is determined by the interference of waves, in a prism by refraction, which depends on the refractive index.

Model answer

Grating. A maximum forms in the direction in which the waves from adjacent slits meet in the same phase, that is, their path difference $d\sin\theta$ is a multiple of the wavelength. A longer wavelength requires a larger path difference and therefore a larger angle, so red light is deviated the most and violet the least, as in the table.

Prism. Light is refracted when it enters the glass and when it leaves it. The refractive index of glass depends on the wavelength (dispersion): it is greater for violet light than for red, because violet light travels more slowly in glass. Violet is therefore refracted the most.

Conclusion. The claim is wrong: both observations are correct. The colours are separated by different phenomena, in the grating by interference and in the prism by refraction, so the order of the colours can be the opposite.
