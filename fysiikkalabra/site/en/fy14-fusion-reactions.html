Fusion reactions: from the Sun to deuterium and tritium

Why must a fusion reactor on Earth be hotter than the centre of the Sun?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • explain the proton–proton chain and the role of the Coulomb barrier and tunnelling.
  • calculate how the energy of D–T fusion is shared between the neutron and the alpha particle.
  • compare fusion in the Sun with fusion in a laboratory or reactor.

Prior knowledge

  • Fusion of light nuclei releases energy because the products are more tightly bound, and D–T fusion gives $Q = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$chapter Mass defect and binding energy
  • A particle can tunnel through a barrier that it classically cannot crosschapter Waves, probability and quantum tunnelling
  • Momentum $p = mv$ is conserved in a collision, and $E_\text{k} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ (earlier physics courses)

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

The centre of the Sun has a temperature of about $15$ million kelvin. ITER, the international fusion experiment under construction in the south of France (as of November 2026), plans to heat its fuel to about $150$ million kelvin, ten times hotter. That sounds strange: the Sun has been fusing hydrogen for $4.6$ billion years at a much lower temperature. And in 1920, when Eddington proposed fusion in the Sun, his critics said that even the Sun was far too cold for it.

How can fusion happen at all, and why does a machine on Earth need so much more heat than the Sun?

Review: where the energy comes from

In D–T fusion, $^{2}\text{H} + {}^{3}\text{H} \to {}^{4}\text{He} + \text{n}$, the energy $Q = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$ is released (chapter Mass defect and binding energy). Why?

  • Right: $B/A$ climbs from $1.1$ and $2.8\ \text{MeV}$ to $7.1\ \text{MeV}$, so the products have less mass; the difference is $17.6\ \text{MeV}$.

  • The collision energy is only some $\text{keV}$; the $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ comes from the mass defect of the more tightly bound helium-4.

  • The neutron was already in the fuel: deuterium has one and tritium two. Nucleons are rearranged, not created.

2Predict

Make your predictions before investigating 3 questions

Prediction 1: over the barrier?

Two nuclei must come within about $3\ \text{fm}$ to fuse, and their electric repulsion at that distance corresponds to about $500\ \text{keV}$. In the Sun's core, a typical nucleus has a kinetic energy of only about $2\ \text{keV}$.How can the Sun fuse hydrogen at all?

  • Gravity keeps the core dense and hot, but it is far too weak between two nuclei to beat their electric repulsion. Fusion happens by tunnelling.

  • In the core the atoms are fully ionised, and electrons screen the charge only a little. The nuclei still repel; they fuse by tunnelling through the barrier.

  • Solar models, tested by measurements of the neutrinos from the core, give about $15.7$ million kelvin. The barrier is not climbed over; it is passed by tunnelling.

  • Right: quantum tunnelling. A nucleus behaves partly like a wave and has a small chance to appear on the other side of the barrier. The fastest nuclei are most likely to tunnel.

Prediction 2: who gets the energy?

In D–T fusion, $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ is shared between the helium-4 nucleus and the neutron. The helium nucleus is about four times heavier. How is the energy shared?

  • The opposite: the products have equal momenta, so the lighter neutron moves four times faster and gets four times the kinetic energy, $14.1\ \text{MeV}$.

  • Right: the momenta are equal and opposite, and $E_\text{k} = p^2/(2m)$, so the neutron gets about $4/5$ of the energy, $14.1\ \text{MeV}$, and the helium nucleus $3.5\ \text{MeV}$.

  • Momentum decides the share: with equal momenta $p$, $E_\text{k} = p^2/(2m)$ is larger for the lighter particle. The neutron gets $14.1\ \text{MeV}$, the helium nucleus $3.5\ \text{MeV}$.

Prediction 3: why not copy the Sun?

The Sun fuses ordinary hydrogen, and hydrogen is cheap on Earth. Why will fusion power plants not use the Sun's reaction?

  • Right: a proton must turn into a neutron, which needs the weak interaction. An average proton in the Sun waits about $9$ billion years. A machine needs a much faster reaction: D–T.

  • Four protons give $26.7\ \text{MeV}$, more than one D–T reaction. The problem is the rate: the first step is extremely slow, because it needs the weak interaction.

  • Hydrogen is very common: every water molecule has two atoms. The problem is that the proton–proton reaction is extremely slow.

3Investigate

Investigation 1: the first fusion experiment, 1934

M. L. Oliphant, P. Harteck and Lord Rutherford, "Transmutation Effects observed with Heavy Hydrogen", letter to Nature 133, 413, dated 9 March 1934. What they reported, in our words:

  • They fired a beam of deuterons, accelerated by a high voltage, at compounds that contained deuterium.
  • Already at $20\,000$ volts they detected fast protons. At $100\,000$ volts the effects were too large for their amplifier and recorder to follow.
  • They found two groups of particles with equal numbers: fast protons and a group of slow, singly charged particles, which they explained as a new hydrogen nucleus of mass $3$.
  • They also found neutrons, which they explained by a second reaction that makes a helium nucleus of mass $3$.
  • Beams of ordinary protons on the same targets gave no comparable effect.
  • They wrote that "it is too early to draw definite conclusions".
Which conclusion is best supported by this evidence?

  • The proton beams gave no such effect on the same targets. Proton–proton fusion is extremely slow; deuterons fuse far more easily.

  • Each reaction released much more energy than the deuteron brought, but only a tiny fraction of the beam fused. The experiment as a whole used far more energy than it released.

  • Right: the products had mass $3$, the new isotopes tritium and helium-3, and only deuteron beams gave them. It is the first laboratory fusion of light nuclei made on purpose.

Describe the process

Discuss with a partner, then write a description of the D–T reaction in four or five steps, in your own words. Say what happens to the nuclei, which force wins at each moment, and how the energy is shared at the end.

Model answer

In the first step, a deuterium nucleus and a tritium nucleus move towards each other. Then the electric force pushes them apart, because both are positive, and they slow down: this is the Coulomb barrier. When the fastest nuclei are close enough, about $3 \cdot 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$, they tunnel through the rest of the barrier, the strong force wins, and they fuse into a very short-lived helium-5 nucleus. It breaks up at once into a helium-4 nucleus and a neutron, which have less mass than the fuel, so $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ is released. The products fly apart with equal and opposite momenta, so the lighter neutron moves faster and carries $14.1\ \text{MeV}$, while the helium nucleus carries $3.5\ \text{MeV}$.

    4Theory summary

    The proton–proton chain

    The Sun turns hydrogen into helium in three steps, called the proton–proton chain. Hans Bethe and Charles Critchfield worked out its first step in 1938. Energies are from the AME2020 masses:

    1. Two protons fuse into a deuteron, $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$. One proton turns into a neutron, so a positron $\text{e}^+$ and a neutrino $\nu$ are emitted.
    2. The deuteron captures another proton and becomes helium-3; a gamma-ray photon $\gamma$ carries off energy.
    3. Two helium-3 nuclei, each from steps 1 and 2, fuse into helium-4 and give back two protons.

    In total, four protons become one helium-4 nucleus, and $26.7\ \text{MeV}$ is released; about $2\ \%$ of it escapes with the neutrinos. The first step is extremely slow, because turning a proton into a neutron needs the weak interaction. An average proton in the Sun's core waits about $9$ billion years before it fuses. The Sun shines only because it holds an enormous number of protons.

    Key idea

    Two nuclei are both positive, so they repel each other. To fuse, they must come within about $3\ \text{fm}$ of each other, where the strong force takes over. At this distance their electric potential energy is about $0.5\ \text{MeV}$. This energy hill is the Coulomb barrier.

    The temperature of the Sun's core is about $15.7$ million kelvin. There, the average kinetic energy of a nucleus is only $\frac{3}{2}kT \approx 2\ \text{keV}$, more than $200$ times too little. By classical physics, practically no nucleus would ever get over the barrier, and the Sun could not shine.

    The answer is quantum tunnelling. A nucleus behaves partly like a wave, and it has a small probability of appearing on the other side of the barrier without having the energy to climb over it. George Gamow found this effect in 1928 for alpha decay, and in 1929 Robert Atkinson and Fritz Houtermans used it to calculate fusion in stars. Tunnelling is most likely for the fastest nuclei and for barriers that are low and thin, so fusion needs high temperatures, and it is easiest for the hydrogen isotopes, which have the smallest charge. Because of tunnelling, deuterium and tritium already fuse readily at collision energies of about $0.1\ \text{MeV}$, well below the $0.5\ \text{MeV}$ top of the barrier, and in a reactor plasma (about $15\ \text{keV}$) the fastest nuclei do most of the fusing.

    Sharing the energy of D–T fusion

    The easiest reaction for a reactor is the fusion of deuterium and tritium:

    $${}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + 17.6\ \text{MeV}$$

    The reaction is balanced: $2 + 3 = 4 + 1$ nucleons and $1 + 1 = 2 + 0$ elementary charges. The energy appears as kinetic energy of the products. If the fuel nuclei are almost at rest, the total momentum is about zero, so the two products fly apart with equal and opposite momenta, $p_\alpha = p_\text{n} = p$. With $E_\text{k} = p^2/(2m)$, the lighter particle gets more energy:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{E_\text{n}}{E_\alpha} &= \frac{m_\alpha}{m_\text{n}} \approx 4 \\ E_\text{n} &= \frac{m_\alpha}{m_\alpha + m_\text{n}} \cdot Q \end{aligned}$$

    So the neutron carries $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ and the helium nucleus, the alpha particle, $3.5\ \text{MeV}$.

      Worked example

      Problem

      This example repeats the calculation of the reaction diagram and the formula step by step, so that you can do it yourself. In D–T fusion, $Q = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$ is released. The nuclear masses are $m_\alpha = 4.0015\ \text{u}$ for the helium nucleus and $m_\text{n} = 1.0087\ \text{u}$ for the neutron. Calculate the kinetic energy of each product, assuming that the fuel nuclei are almost at rest.

      Step 1/7

      Given: $Q = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$, $m_\alpha = 4.0015\ \text{u}$, $m_\text{n} = 1.0087\ \text{u}$; the initial momentum is about zero. Find: $E_\text{n}$ and $E_\alpha$.

      Step 2/7

      Principle: Momentum is conserved: the products move in opposite directions with momenta of equal size $p$. Energy is conserved: $E_\text{n} + E_\alpha = Q$. The kinetic energy of a particle with momentum $p$ is $E_\text{k} = p^2/(2m)$.

      Step 3/7

      Formula: With the same $p$, the kinetic energies are inversely proportional to the masses, and they add up to $Q$:

      $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{n} &= \frac{m_\alpha}{m_\alpha + m_\text{n}} \cdot Q \\ E_\alpha &= \frac{m_\text{n}}{m_\alpha + m_\text{n}} \cdot Q \end{aligned}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions: None: only the ratio of the masses is needed, so they can stay in $\text{u}$, and $Q$ stays in $\text{MeV}$. The sum of the masses is $4.0015\ \text{u} + 1.0087\ \text{u} = 5.0102\ \text{u}$.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution: $$\begin{aligned} E_\text{n} &= \frac{4.0015\ \text{u}}{5.0102\ \text{u}} \cdot 17.6\ \text{MeV} \\ &= 0.7987 \cdot 17.6\ \text{MeV} \\ &= 14.06\ \text{MeV} \\[4pt] E_\alpha &= \frac{1.0087\ \text{u}}{5.0102\ \text{u}} \cdot 17.6\ \text{MeV} \\ &= 0.2013 \cdot 17.6\ \text{MeV} \\ &= 3.54\ \text{MeV} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer: The neutron gets $E_\text{n} \approx 14.1\ \text{MeV}$ and the alpha particle $E_\alpha \approx 3.5\ \text{MeV}$, as precise as $Q$ allows.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness:

      $14.1\ \text{MeV} + 3.5\ \text{MeV}$
      $= 17.6\ \text{MeV}$
      $= Q$

      The neutron is about four times lighter, so it gets about four times as much energy: $14.06 : 3.54 = 3.97$. In a hot plasma the fuel nuclei are not quite at rest, so the measured neutron energies spread a little around $14.1\ \text{MeV}$.

      Fusion in the laboratory

      A power plant cannot simply copy the Sun:

      Sun's core ITER (plan)
      fuel protons D and T
      how hot $1.6 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{K}$ $1.5 \cdot 10^{8}\ \text{K}$
      density $150\ \text{g/cm}^3$ $10^{-6}$ of air
      held by gravity magnetic fields
      power per $\text{m}^3$ $280\ \text{W}$ $0.6\ \text{MW}$

      The Sun's reaction is far too slow for a machine, and its gravity holds a dense core for billions of years. A reactor must use the fastest reaction, D–T, in a thin plasma that it can hold only for seconds or less. To make enough nuclei tunnel in that short time, the plasma must be about ten times hotter than the Sun's core, which ITER plans at about $150$ million kelvin. "Hotter than the Sun" refers to the temperature only; the Sun's core has a far higher density and pressure.

      The neutron has no charge, so magnetic fields cannot hold it: it leaves the plasma with $80\ \%$ of the energy and heats the wall around it, where its energy can be collected and where it can breed new tritium from lithium. The alpha particle is charged and stays in the plasma, keeping it hot.

      Compare: the Sun and a fusion reactor

      Discuss with a partner, then write three or four sentences of your own that answers the opening question: why must a fusion reactor on Earth be hotter than the centre of the Sun? Compare the fuel, the density, how the plasma is held and for how long. Also say what "hotter than the Sun" does not mean.

      Model answer

      The main difference between the Sun and a reactor is how the fuel is held. The Sun's gravity holds a huge, dense core, $150\ \text{g/cm}^3$, for billions of years, so it can use the very slow proton–proton reaction. A reactor cannot do this: its magnetic fields hold only a thin plasma, about a million times thinner than air, for seconds or less. So it must use the fastest reaction, deuterium–tritium, and make the nuclei so fast that many of them tunnel through the Coulomb barrier in that short time. That needs about $150$ million kelvin, ten times the temperature of the Sun's core. "Hotter than the Sun" refers only to the temperature: the Sun's core has a far higher density and pressure.

        Optional How much coal is one kilogram of D–T fuel?

        One kilogram of D–T fuel releases $3.37 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J}$ by fusion, and one kilogram of anthracite coal releases $3.25 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{J}$ by burning.

        How many kilograms of coal give the same energy as one kilogram of D–T fuel?

        kg
        Hint 1: a guiding question

        The same energy: $m_\text{coal} \cdot q_\text{coal} = 1\ \text{kg} \cdot q_\text{DT}$.

        Hint 2: what is the connection

        Divide the two energies per kilogram.

        Hint 3: an intermediate step

        $3.37 \cdot 10^{14} : 3.25 \cdot 10^{7} = 1.04 \cdot 10^{7}$.

        Show the solution

        Given

        $q_\text{DT} = 3.37 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J/kg}$, $q_\text{coal} = 3.25 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{J/kg}$, $m_\text{DT} = 1\ \text{kg}$

        Find

        the mass of coal $m_\text{coal}$ that releases the same energy

        Principle

        Both fuels must release the same energy $E$; the energy is the mass times the energy per kilogram.

        Formula

        $$m_\text{coal} = \frac{q_\text{DT} \cdot m_\text{DT}}{q_\text{coal}}$$

        Unit conversions

        None: both energies are in $\text{J/kg}$.

        Substitution

        $$\begin{aligned} m_\text{coal} &= \frac{3.37 \cdot 10^{14}\ \text{J/kg} \cdot 1\ \text{kg}}{3.25 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{J/kg}} \\ &= 1.04 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{kg} \end{aligned}$$

        Answer

        about $10^{7}\ \text{kg}$, ten thousand tonnes of coal, three significant figures as in the data.

        Reasonableness

        A D–T reaction releases $17.6\ \text{MeV}$, while burning releases a few $\text{eV}$ per atom: the ratio per reaction is millions, and so is the ratio per kilogram. Ten thousand tonnes of coal fill more than a hundred railway wagons.

        Optional Practice: the proton–proton chain

        Common misconception

        "The Sun fuses deuterium and tritium, like a fusion reactor." The Sun fuses ordinary hydrogen through the slow proton–proton chain. D–T fusion is chosen for reactors because it is the fastest reaction at reachable temperatures; tritium hardly exists in nature and must be made.

        "A reactor plasma is hotter than the Sun, so it is more extreme in every way." Only the temperature is higher. The Sun's core, $150\ \text{g/cm}^3$, is more than a hundred billion times denser than a reactor plasma, which is about a million times thinner than air, and its pressure is far higher.

        "Nuclei fuse when they have enough energy to get over the Coulomb barrier." At the temperatures of the Sun and of reactors, almost all fusions happen by tunnelling, below the top of the barrier.

        The people behind the first fusion experiment

        Mark Oliphant (1901–2000)

        Mark Oliphant was an Australian physicist from Adelaide. In 1927 he came to the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge to work with Ernest Rutherford. When a small amount of heavy water became available, he, Paul Harteck and Rutherford fired deuterons at deuterium and observed the first fusion reactions made on purpose, discovering tritium and helium-3 in 1934. Later, in Birmingham, his group developed the cavity magnetron, which made microwave radar possible. Back in Australia he founded a research school of physics in Canberra and served as Governor of South Australia from 1971 to 1976.

        1. Concept: the neutron's energy

        Why does the neutron get about $80\ \%$ of the energy of D–T fusion?

        • Right: $E_\text{k} = p^2/(2m)$ with the same $p$, so the lighter particle gets more: $14.1\ \text{MeV}$ against $3.5\ \text{MeV}$.

        • The energy comes from the mass defect of the whole reaction, and momentum conservation shares it: the lighter product gets more.

        • Charge does not decide the share at the moment of the reaction. Momentum conservation does: equal momenta, so the lighter neutron gets more kinetic energy.

        2. Representation: balance a reaction

        Complete the reaction from 1934: $^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} \to \,? + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$

        • Right: nucleons $2 + 2 = 3 + 1$, charges $1 + 1 = 2 + 0$. This is helium-3, one of the two isotopes discovered in 1934.

        4. Transfer: what the neutrons do

        In a D–T reactor, the plasma is held by magnetic fields. Explain what happens to the two products of the reaction, the alpha particle and the neutron, and why this matters for the design of a reactor.

        Sentence starters

        • The alpha particle is charged, so …
        • The neutron has no charge, so …
        • Because the neutron carries … of the energy, …
        • This means that the wall of the reactor must …

        Model answer

        The alpha particle is charged, so the magnetic field keeps it in the plasma, and its $3.5\ \text{MeV}$ heats the plasma. The neutron has no charge, so the magnetic field cannot hold it, and it flies out of the plasma with $14.1\ \text{MeV}$. Because the neutron carries about $80\ \%$ of the energy, the wall around the plasma must catch the neutrons and turn their energy into heat, which could be used to make electricity. This means that the wall must also stand the neutron radiation, and it can contain lithium to breed new tritium.
