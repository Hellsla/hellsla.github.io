The gravitational force between bodies

Why does a dropped apple rush towards the Earth, while the Earth does not seem to move towards the apple at all?

In this lesson, you will learn to

  • compare the forces and accelerations of two bodies that attract each other.
  • calculate the gravitational force and the gravitational field strength using Newton's law of gravitation.
  • explain how gravitation gathers matter into stars, planets and galaxies.

Prior knowledge

  • In an interaction, bodies exert forces of equal magnitude and opposite directions on each otherchapter Interaction and force: Newton's first and third laws
  • The weight of a body is $G = mg$, and on the Earth's surface $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$chapter Weight, friction and the inclined plane
  • In uniform circular motion a body has a centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre of the pathchapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle

Core path: follow the steps in order. Tasks marked Optional give extra practice. Optional tasks: 4.

1Think about it

On a September evening, a dull thud is heard from under an apple tree at regular intervals as a ripe apple comes loose and falls onto the lawn. The apple starts from rest and accelerates downwards, towards the Earth. Isaac Newton pondered exactly this kind of situation and asked whether the same pull that makes the apple fall reaches all the way to the Moon.

If the Earth pulls the apple, does the apple also pull the Earth? And if it does, why does the Earth not move towards the apple?

Optional Review: acceleration in circular motion
The Moon moves round the Earth along a nearly circular path at a nearly constant speed. Which statement about the Moon's motion is correct?

  • No force is needed to keep a body moving (Newton's first law). On a circular path a force is needed to change the direction of the velocity, and it points towards the centre of the path, not along the path.

  • If the forces cancelled, the Moon would move in a straight line and would not curve round the Earth. In circular motion the net force points towards the centre of the path.

  • The speed stays the same, but velocity is a vector whose direction changes all the time. The change of velocity towards the centre of the path is the centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ (chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle).

  • Correct. In uniform circular motion the direction of the velocity changes all the time, so the body has a centripetal acceleration $a_\text{n} = \tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre of the path (chapter Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle). By Newton's second law the net force points in the same direction. In this lesson you find out where that force comes from.

How the calculation works

The magnitude of the force is calculated from Newton's law of gravitation $F = \gamma \tfrac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$, where $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$ and the masses are converted to kilograms ($1\,\text{t} = 1000\,\text{kg}$). $F_1$ is the magnitude of the force acting on boulder 1 and $F_2$ that on boulder 2. The accelerations come from Newton's second law: $a_1 = \tfrac{F_1}{m_1}$ and $a_2 = \tfrac{F_2}{m_2}$. The forces are shown in micronewtons, $1\,\mu\text{N} = 10^{-6}\,\text{N}$, and the accelerations in nanometres per second squared, $1\,\text{nm/s}^2 = 10^{-9}\,\text{m/s}^2$.

$742 \cdot 9.0$$\approx 185 \cdot 36$

    4Theory summary

    Key idea

    In the 17th century Isaac Newton concluded that the same interaction that makes an apple fall also keeps the Moon in its orbit. This interaction is called gravitation: every body that has mass attracts every other body. You and your desk attract each other, and so do two students sitting side by side. In everyday life we do not notice this, because the force is very small unless one of the bodies is enormous. The gravitational force between two people of $70\,\text{kg}$ and $60\,\text{kg}$ one metre apart is only about $0.28\,\mu\text{N}$. The pull of the Earth on the same person is almost $700\,\text{N}$.

    Gravitation is an interaction, so Newton's third law applies to it. The Earth pulls the apple with a force $\overline{F}_1$, and the apple pulls the Earth with a force $\overline{F}_2$ of equal magnitude and opposite direction. The larger body does not pull the smaller one harder than the smaller pulls the larger: the forces are always equal, $F_1 = F_2$.

    The difference is in the accelerations. By Newton's second law the acceleration of a body is $a = \tfrac{F}{m}$. When a force of about $1.96\,\text{N}$ acts on an apple of $0.20\,\text{kg}$, the acceleration of the apple is about $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The same force gives the Earth, whose mass is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, an acceleration of about $3 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\text{m/s}^2$. The Earth really does move towards the apple, but so little that it cannot be measured.

    Newton's law of gravitation

    The gravitational force between two bodies is greater the greater the masses of the bodies, and smaller the farther apart they are. Newton's law of gravitation says that the force is directly proportional to each mass and inversely proportional to the square of the distance (an inverse-square law):

    $$F = \gamma \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

    where $F$ is the magnitude of the gravitational force acting on each body (N), $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of the bodies (kg), $r$ is the distance between the centres of the bodies (m) and $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$. (Many English books write the gravitational constant as G; in this book G is the weight of a body, so the constant is written γ.) The force acts on each body towards the other body. Henry Cavendish first measured the constant in 1798 from the attraction between lead spheres.

    The law holds as such for point masses. It also holds for uniform spheres and other spherically symmetric bodies, such as planets and stars, when $r$ is measured between the centres: such a body attracts outside bodies as if its whole mass were at its centre. The distance of a person standing on the Earth's surface from the Earth's centre is therefore the radius of the Earth.

    The distance is squared in the formula. When the distance doubles, the force falls to a quarter, and when the distance triples, to a ninth. When one mass doubles, the force doubles too. The gravitational force decreases quickly as the distance grows, but it never becomes exactly zero. The graph shows the force when the mass of each body is $10\,\text{t}$.

    Weight and the gravitational field

    The weight $G = mg$ of a body is the gravitational force the Earth exerts on it. When the mass of the Earth is $M$ and its radius $R$, the law of gravitation gives the same force, so $mg = \gamma \tfrac{Mm}{R^2}$. The mass $m$ of the body cancels, and the acceleration due to gravity is left as $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}$. This is why all bodies fall with the same acceleration on the Earth's surface when air resistance is ignored: a greater force acts on a heavier body, but it is also harder to accelerate.

    Gravitational field strength

    The Earth changes its surroundings so that at every point a certain pull is ready: a force $\overline{F} = m\overline{g}$ acts on a body brought there. This is called the gravitational field of the Earth. The field strength at a distance $r$ from the centre of a body is

    $$g = \gamma \frac{M}{r^2}$$

    where $M$ is the mass of the body that creates the field (kg) and $r$ is the distance from its centre (m). The direction of the field is towards the centre of the body, and the unit of the field strength is $\text{m/s}^2$. If only gravitation acts on a body, its acceleration by Newton's second law is $a = \tfrac{F}{m} = \tfrac{mg}{m} = g$. The gravitational field strength is therefore the same thing as the acceleration of a freely falling body at that point.

    The field on different celestial bodies

    The field strengths in the table have been calculated from the formula $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}$ using the masses and equatorial radii from NASA's Planetary Fact Sheet. The value $9.80\,\text{m/s}^2$ for the Earth is calculated without the effect of the Earth's rotation; the measured value is slightly different, and the tasks of this book use the value $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The mass of a body is the same everywhere, but its weight depends on the field of which celestial body it is in.

    Gravitation in the universe

    Gravitation is the weakest of the fundamental interactions, but on large scales it is decisive. Electric forces are much stronger, but positive and negative charges cancel each other's effects, so large bodies are nearly electrically neutral. Gravitation, on the other hand, is always attractive: every mass adds to the pull, and nothing cancels it. In addition, the effect of gravitation reaches far, because $\tfrac{1}{r^2}$ becomes smaller but never becomes zero.

    The early universe was an almost uniform cloud of gas. Places where there was slightly more matter than average pulled the surrounding matter harder, so they became denser still. The condensations grew into gas clouds whose central parts collapsed under their own weight. As it collapsed, the gas became denser and hotter, until nuclear reactions started in its centre and a star was born. The matter left in the flat disc around the star gathered into planets. Gravitation also gathers stars into galaxies and galaxies into clusters of galaxies, and it keeps the planets in their orbits around their star.

    Common misconception

    "The Earth pulls the Moon harder than the Moon pulls the Earth, because the Earth is much bigger." Gravitation is an interaction, so by Newton's third law the forces are always equal and opposite. The difference in mass shows in the accelerations: the same force accelerates the lighter body more. Another common belief is that gravitation ends above the atmosphere. In fact it only weakens as the distance grows: for example, at a height of $300\,\text{km}$ the field strength is still about $8.9\,\text{m/s}^2$.

    Summary

    • All bodies attract each other with the gravitational force $F = \gamma \tfrac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$, where $r$ is the distance between the centres and $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$.
    • The gravitational forces between two bodies are equal and opposite; the lighter body accelerates more.
    • When the distance doubles, the force falls to a quarter.
    • The gravitational field strength $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{r^2}$ is the acceleration of a freely falling body, with the unit $\text{m/s}^2$; the weight of a body is $G = mg$.
    • Gravitation is always attractive and reaches far, so it gathers matter into stars, planets and galaxies.

      Problem

      With what force do the Earth and the Moon attract each other? The mass of the Earth is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, the mass of the Moon $7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$ and the mean distance between their centres $3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$.

      Step 1/7

      Given and find. Mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, mass of the Moon $m = 7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$, distance $r = 3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$ and gravitational constant $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$. We want the magnitude $F$ of the gravitational force.

      Step 2/7

      Principle. The Earth and the Moon are almost spherically symmetric, so the force between them can be calculated with Newton's law of gravitation when $r$ is the distance between the centres. By Newton's third law the same force acts on both the Moon and the Earth.

      Step 3/7

      Formula solved. The asked force is already solved for:

      $$F = \gamma \frac{M m}{r^2}$$

      Step 4/7

      Unit conversions. The distance is converted to metres:

      $r$$= 3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$
      $= 3.84 \cdot 10^{5} \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{m}$
      $= 3.84 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m}$

      The masses are already in kilograms.

      Step 5/7

      Substitution. Substitute the values with their units:

      $$\begin{aligned} F &= \gamma \frac{M m}{r^2} \\ &= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg} \cdot 7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}}{(3.84 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m})^2} \\ &\approx 1.99 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{N} \end{aligned}$$

      Step 6/7

      Answer. The Earth and the Moon attract each other with a force $F \approx 1.99 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{N}$. The answer is given to three significant figures, because the masses and the distance are given to three figures.

      Step 7/7

      Reasonableness. With rounded numbers

      $7 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot \tfrac{6 \cdot 10^{24} \cdot 7 \cdot 10^{22}}{(4 \cdot 10^{8})^2}\,\text{N}$
      $\approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{20}\,\text{N}$

      so the order of magnitude is right. The force is enormous, but the acceleration of the Moon is still only

      $a$$= \tfrac{F}{m}$
      $\approx 2.7 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m/s}^2$

      because the mass of the Moon is large.

      The Earth's gravitational field strength is $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{r^2}$, where $r$ is the distance from the Earth's centre, because the Earth attracts as if its mass were at its centre. On the surface $r = R$, at height $h$ it is $r = R + h$. The mass of the Earth is not needed, because it cancels.

      Formula

      $\gamma \dfrac{M}{(R + h)^2}$$= \dfrac{1}{2} \gamma \dfrac{M}{R^2}$

      so $(R + h)^2 = 2R^2$ and $h = (\sqrt{2} - 1) R$

      Conversions

      None needed: the radius is in kilometres, and the answer is asked in kilometres.

      Substitution

      $h$$= (\sqrt{2} - 1) \cdot 6378\,\text{km}$
      $= 0.41421 \cdot 6378\,\text{km}$
      $= 2641.9\,\text{km}$

      Answer

      $2640\,\text{km}$

      To three significant figures; with four figures of the radius the result would be $2642\,\text{km}$.

      Reasonableness

      Check:

      $\left(\tfrac{6378}{6378 + 2642}\right)^2$
      $= \left(\tfrac{6378}{9020}\right)^2$
      $= 0.500$

      The height is smaller than the radius of the Earth, which fits the fact that twice the distance from the centre would already reduce the field to a quarter.

      1. Mass and weight

      An astronaut whose mass with equipment is $80\,\text{kg}$ on the Earth lands on the surface of the Moon. Which statement is correct on the Moon's surface?

      • Mass does not change when a body moves to another place. The Moon's weaker field reduces the weight $G = mg$, not the mass: the astronaut's mass is $80\,\text{kg}$ on the Moon too.

      • Weight does not come from the atmosphere; it is the gravitational force between the Moon and the astronaut. The Moon pulls the astronaut with a force of about $130\,\text{N}$ even though there is no air.

      • The gravitational constant $\gamma$ is the same everywhere, but the field strength $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}$ depends on the mass and radius of the celestial body. On the Moon $g = 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2$, so the weight is smaller.

      • Correct. Mass tells the amount of matter and does not depend on place. The weight $G = mg$ depends on the field strength: on the Moon $80\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.62\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 130\,\text{N}$, on the Earth $80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 785\,\text{N}$.

      2. Change of the force

      The gravitational force between two bodies is $12\,\text{N}$. The mass of one body is tripled, and the distance between the centres of the bodies is doubled.

      How large is the gravitational force now? Give the answer in newtons.

      N
      Hint 1: a guiding question

      How does the force change when only the mass is tripled? And when only the distance is doubled?

      Hint 2: what is the connection

      $F \propto m_1 m_2$ and $F \propto \tfrac{1}{r^2}$, so the new force is $F' = F \cdot \tfrac{3}{2^2}$.

      Hint 3: an intermediate step

      $F' = 12\,\text{N} \cdot \tfrac{3}{4}$.

      Show the solution

      Given

      original force $F = 12\,\text{N}$; second mass $3m_2$; distance $2r$

      Find

      the new gravitational force $F'$ in newtons

      Principle

      By Newton's law of gravitation the force is directly proportional to each mass and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. When the ratio of the new and old forces is calculated, $\gamma$, $m_1$, $m_2$ and $r$ cancel.

      Formula

      $\dfrac{F'}{F}$$= \dfrac{\gamma \tfrac{m_1 \cdot 3m_2}{(2r)^2}}{\gamma \tfrac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}}$
      $= \dfrac{3}{4}$

      so $F' = \dfrac{3}{4} F$

      Conversions

      None needed: the force is in newtons, and the ratio has no unit.

      Substitution

      $F'$$= \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 12\,\text{N}$
      $= 9.0\,\text{N}$

      Answer

      $9.0\,\text{N}$

      Two significant figures as in the force $12\,\text{N}$.

      Reasonableness

      Tripling the mass alone would give $36\,\text{N}$ and doubling the distance alone $3.0\,\text{N}$. Together the effects almost cancel, so the force is a little smaller than at the start, as $9.0\,\text{N}$ is.

      3. Gravitation and the structures of the universe

      Gravitation is the weakest of the fundamental interactions. Explain why it is gravitation that has gathered stars and galaxies out of gas, although the electric interaction is much stronger.

      Model answer

      Gravitation is always attractive: every mass adds to the pull, and nothing cancels it. In the electric interaction positive and negative charges cancel each other's effects, so large bodies and gas clouds are nearly neutral and the electric force does not gather them together. Gravitation also reaches far, because $F \propto \tfrac{1}{r^2}$ becomes smaller but never becomes zero. When there is a place in the gas where there is slightly more matter than usual, it pulls its surroundings harder and keeps growing. In this way the condensations grew into clouds that collapsed into stars, and the stars gathered into galaxies.

        Finally, two quick questions. Answer first by yourself and open the answer only afterwards.

        1. How large is the gravitational force between two bodies of mass $1.0\,\text{kg}$ when their centres are $1.0\,\text{m}$ apart?
          Answer
          $F$$= \gamma \tfrac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$
          $= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot \tfrac{1.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.0\,\text{kg}}{(1.0\,\text{m})^2}$
          $\approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\text{N}$

          The numerical value of the gravitational constant is exactly the magnitude of this force in newtons, and the force is so small that it is not noticed in everyday life.

        2. Why do a heavy and a light stone fall in a vacuum with the same acceleration, although a greater gravitational force acts on the heavier one?
          Answer

          The gravitational force $F = \gamma \tfrac{Mm}{R^2}$ is directly proportional to the mass $m$ of the stone, but the inertia of the stone also grows with its mass. The acceleration

          $a$$= \tfrac{F}{m}$
          $= \gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}$
          $= g$

          therefore does not depend on the mass of the stone.

          In a laboratory the gravitational force between two lead spheres of equal mass is measured. When the distance between the centres of the spheres is $0.40\,\text{m}$, the force is $0.60\,\mu\text{N}$.

          Answer

          $38\,\text{kg}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          distance between the centres of the spheres $r = 0.40\,\text{m}$, force $F = 0.60\,\mu\text{N}$, equal masses $m_1 = m_2 = m$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

          Find

          the mass $m$ of each sphere

          Principle

          The lead spheres are uniform spheres, so the law of gravitation holds when $r$ is the distance between the centres. Because the masses are equal, the law contains the product $m \cdot m = m^2$, from which the mass is solved.

          Formula

          $F = \gamma \dfrac{m^2}{r^2}$, so $m = r\sqrt{\dfrac{F}{\gamma}}$

          Conversions

          $F$$= 0.60\,\mu\text{N}$
          $= 0.60 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{N}$

          Substitution

          $m$$= 0.40\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{0.60 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{N}}{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}}}$
          $= 0.40\,\text{m} \cdot 94.82\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}}$
          $= 37.93\,\text{kg}$

          Answer

          $38\,\text{kg}$

          Two significant figures as in the force and the distance.

          Reasonableness

          Check by substituting back:

          $6.674 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot \tfrac{38^2}{0.40^2}\,\text{N}$
          $\approx 6.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\text{N}$

          The density of lead is about $11\,300\,\text{kg/m}^3$, so the radius of a sphere of $38\,\text{kg}$ is about $9\,\text{cm}$: the spheres fit easily at a distance of $0.40\,\text{m}$ from each other.

        2. Core

          On the surface of Venus the gravitational field strength is $8.87\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the radius of Venus is $6052\,\text{km}$.

          How many times as large is the mass of Venus as the mass of the Earth, $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$?

          Answer

          $0.815$

          Show the solution

          Given

          on the surface of Venus $g = 8.87\,\text{m/s}^2$, radius $R = 6052\,\text{km}$, mass of the Earth $M_\text{E} = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

          Find

          the ratio of the mass of Venus to the mass of the Earth, $\tfrac{M}{M_\text{E}}$, without a unit

          Principle

          The gravitational field strength on the surface of a spherically symmetric planet is $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}$, so the mass of the planet can be solved from the measured $g$ and the radius. The mass is then compared with the mass of the Earth by dividing.

          Formula

          $g = \gamma \dfrac{M}{R^2}$, so $M = \dfrac{g R^2}{\gamma}$ and the ratio is

          $\dfrac{M}{M_\text{E}}$$= \dfrac{g R^2}{\gamma M_\text{E}}$

          Conversions

          $R$$= 6052\,\text{km}$
          $= 6052 \cdot 10^{3}\,\text{m}$
          $= 6.052 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          $R^2$$= (6.052 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m})^2$
          $= 3.6627 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}^2$
          $g R^2$$= 8.87\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3.6627 \cdot 10^{13}\,\text{m}^2$
          $= 3.2488 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$
          $M$$= \dfrac{g R^2}{\gamma}$
          $= \dfrac{3.2488 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}}$
          $= 4.868 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$
          $\dfrac{M}{M_\text{E}}$$= \dfrac{4.868 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}}{5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}}$
          $= 0.8154$

          Answer

          $0.815$

          Three significant figures as in the field strength $8.87\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          Venus is almost the size of the Earth and its field is a little weaker than the Earth's, so its mass must be a little smaller than the Earth's. According to NASA's table the mass of Venus is $4.87 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, which agrees with the result.

        3. A space probe of mass $1200\,\text{kg}$ leaves the Earth for Mars.

          At what height above the Earth's surface is the gravitational force exerted by the Earth on the probe $1.00\,\text{kN}$? The mass of the Earth is $5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius $6378\,\text{km}$. Give the answer in kilometres.

          km
          Answer

          $15\,500\,\text{km}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the probe $m = 1200\,\text{kg}$, force $F = 1.00\,\text{kN}$, mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6378\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$

          Find

          the height $h$ above the Earth's surface in kilometres

          Principle

          The Earth attracts the probe as if its mass were at its centre, so the law of gravitation first gives the distance $r$ from the Earth's centre. The height above the surface is this distance minus the radius of the Earth.

          Formula

          $F = \gamma \dfrac{M m}{r^2}$, so

          $r$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma M m}{F}}$
          $h$$= r - R$
          $= \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma M m}{F}} - R$

          Conversions

          $F = 1.00\,\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$;

          $R$$= 6378\,\text{km}$
          $= 6.378 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$

          finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$

          Substitution

          The numerator is

          $\gamma M m$$= 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg} \cdot 1200\,\text{kg}$
          $= 4.7812 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$

          so

          $r$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{4.7812 \cdot 10^{17}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{1000\,\text{N}}}$
          $= 2.1866 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m}$
          $h$$= 2.1866 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m} - 6.378 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$
          $= 1.5488 \cdot 10^{7}\,\text{m}$
          $= 15\,488\,\text{km}$

          Answer

          $15\,500\,\text{km}$

          Three significant figures as in the force $1.00\,\text{kN}$.

          Reasonableness

          On the Earth's surface the weight of the probe is $1200\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 11.8\,\text{kN}$. The force $1.00\,\text{kN}$ is about $\tfrac{1}{11.8}$ of that, so the distance from the centre must be $\sqrt{11.8} \approx 3.4$ Earth radii: $3.4 \cdot 6378\,\text{km} \approx 21\,900\,\text{km}$, which agrees with the value of $r$.

        4. An airliner flies at a height of $11.0\,\text{km}$.

          By what percentage is the Earth's gravitational field strength smaller there than on the Earth's surface? The radius of the Earth is $6378\,\text{km}$.

          %
          Answer

          $0.344\,\%$

          Show the solution

          Given

          flight altitude $h = 11.0\,\text{km}$, radius of the Earth $R = 6378\,\text{km}$

          Find

          by what percentage the field strength $g_h$ is smaller than the surface value $g_0$

          Principle

          The Earth's gravitational field strength is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the Earth's centre. When the ratio $\tfrac{g_h}{g_0}$ is calculated, $\gamma$ and the mass of the Earth cancel, so they are not needed.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{g_h}{g_0}$$= \dfrac{\gamma \tfrac{M}{(R + h)^2}}{\gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}}$
          $= \left(\dfrac{R}{R + h}\right)^2$

          so the relative change is $1 - \left(\dfrac{R}{R + h}\right)^2$

          Conversions

          None needed: the radius and the altitude are both in kilometres, and the unit cancels. Finally the ratio is converted to a percentage by multiplying by $100\,\%$.

          Substitution

          $1 - \left(\dfrac{6378\,\text{km}}{6378\,\text{km} + 11.0\,\text{km}}\right)^2$
          $= 1 - 0.996560$
          $= 0.003440$
          $= 0.3440\,\%$

          Answer

          $0.344\,\%$

          Three significant figures as in the altitude $11.0\,\text{km}$.

          Reasonableness

          At small heights the change is about

          $\tfrac{2h}{R}$$= \tfrac{22.0}{6378}$
          $\approx 0.345\,\%$

          At cruising altitude the field strength is still about $9.78\,\text{m/s}^2$, so gravity does not disappear even at flight altitude.

        1. A space probe flies from the Earth straight towards the Moon.

          At what point are the gravitational forces exerted on the probe by the Earth and the Moon equal?           km
          Answer

          $346\,000\,\text{km}$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, mass of the Moon $m_\text{M} = 7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$, distance between the centres $d = 3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$

          Find

          the distance $x$ from the Earth's centre at which the forces exerted on the probe by the Earth and the Moon are equal

          Principle

          When the probe is between the Earth and the Moon, its distance from the Earth's centre is $x$ and from the Moon's centre $d - x$. Each force is obtained from the law of gravitation, and they are set equal; the mass of the probe and $\gamma$ cancel.

          Formula

          $\gamma \dfrac{M m}{x^2}$$= \gamma \dfrac{m_\text{M} m}{(d - x)^2}$

          so

          $\dfrac{d - x}{x}$$= \sqrt{\dfrac{m_\text{M}}{M}}$
          $x$$= \dfrac{d}{1 + \sqrt{\tfrac{m_\text{M}}{M}}}$

          Conversions

          None needed: the distance can be kept in kilometres, because the answer is asked in kilometres, and in the ratio of the masses the unit cancels.

          Substitution

          $\sqrt{\dfrac{7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}}{5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}}}$$= 0.11096$
          $x$$= \dfrac{3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}}{1.11096}$
          $= 3.456 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$

          Answer

          $346\,000\,\text{km}$

          That is, $3.46 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$, three significant figures as in the masses and the distance.

          Reasonableness

          The point is about $90\,\%$ of the way to the Moon, which makes sense because the mass of the Earth is about $81$ times the mass of the Moon: the forces are equal when the distance to the Earth is $\sqrt{81} = 9$ times the distance to the Moon. Here $\tfrac{345\,600}{38\,400} \approx 9.0$.

        2. A distant exoplanet has the same mean density as the Earth, but its radius is twice the radius of the Earth.

          How large is the gravitational field strength on the surface of the planet, when on the Earth's surface it is $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$? Assume both planets to be spherically symmetric.

          m/s²
          Answer

          $19.6\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Show the solution

          Given

          radius of the planet $R_\text{p} = 2R$, the same mean density $\rho$ as the Earth, on the Earth's surface $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Find

          the gravitational field strength $g_\text{p}$ on the surface of the planet

          Principle

          The field on the surface of a spherically symmetric planet is $g = \gamma \tfrac{M}{R^2}$. When the density is the same, the mass is the density times the volume of the sphere, $M = \rho \cdot \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3$, so the mass grows with the cube of the radius while the denominator grows only with the square. Then $g$ is directly proportional to the radius.

          Formula

          $g$$= \gamma \dfrac{\rho \cdot \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3}{R^2}$
          $= \tfrac{4}{3}\pi\gamma\rho R$

          so

          $\dfrac{g_\text{p}}{g}$$= \dfrac{R_\text{p}}{R}$
          $g_\text{p}$$= \dfrac{R_\text{p}}{R}\, g$

          Conversions

          None needed: the ratio of the radii has no unit.

          Substitution

          $g_\text{p}$$= \dfrac{2R}{R} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
          $= 19.62\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Answer

          $19.6\,\text{m/s}^2$

          Three significant figures as in the value for the Earth, $9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

          Reasonableness

          By another route: twice the radius with the same density gives a mass $2^3 = 8$ times as great, and the square of the distance grows by a factor $2^2 = 4$, so the field is $\tfrac{8}{4} = 2$ times as strong. The result lies between the field of Jupiter ($24.8\,\text{m/s}^2$) and the field of the Earth.

        3. Which pulls the Earth harder, the Sun or the Moon, and how many times as hard? The mass of the Sun is $1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg}$ and the distance of its centre from the Earth's centre $1.50 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{km}$. The mass of the Moon is $7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$ and its distance $3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$. Give the ratio of the force exerted on the Earth by the Sun to the force exerted on it by the Moon.

          Answer

          $177$

          Show the solution

          Given

          mass of the Sun $M_\text{S} = 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg}$ and distance $r_\text{S} = 1.50 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{km}$; mass of the Moon $M_\text{M} = 7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$ and distance $r_\text{M} = 3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}$

          Find

          the ratio $\tfrac{F_\text{S}}{F_\text{M}}$ of the forces exerted on the Earth by the Sun and the Moon

          Principle

          Each force is obtained from Newton's law of gravitation when the distances are measured between the centres. In the ratio $\gamma$ and the mass of the Earth cancel, so only the masses and distances are needed.

          Formula

          $\dfrac{F_\text{S}}{F_\text{M}}$$= \dfrac{\gamma \tfrac{M_\text{S} m}{r_\text{S}^2}}{\gamma \tfrac{M_\text{M} m}{r_\text{M}^2}}$
          $= \dfrac{M_\text{S}}{M_\text{M}} \cdot \left(\dfrac{r_\text{M}}{r_\text{S}}\right)^2$

          Conversions

          None needed: the distances are both in kilometres, and the units cancel.

          Substitution

          $\dfrac{F_\text{S}}{F_\text{M}}$$= \dfrac{1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\text{kg}}{7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\text{kg}} \cdot \left(\dfrac{3.84 \cdot 10^{5}\,\text{km}}{1.50 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{km}}\right)^2$
          $= 2.707 \cdot 10^{7} \cdot 6.554 \cdot 10^{-6}$
          $= 177.4$

          Answer

          The Sun attracts the Earth about $177$ times as strongly as the Moon does; three significant figures as in the data.

          Reasonableness

          The mass of the Sun is about $27$ million times as great, but it is about $390$ times farther away, which reduces the force by a factor $390^2 \approx 150\,000$: $\tfrac{2.7 \cdot 10^{7}}{1.5 \cdot 10^{5}} \approx 180$. The Sun holds the Earth in its orbit, although the Moon is much closer.
