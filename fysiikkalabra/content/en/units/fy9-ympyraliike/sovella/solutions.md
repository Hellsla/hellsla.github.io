## fy9-ympyraliike-s1

### a)
**Given:** mass of the trailer $m_\text{t} = 165\,\text{kg}$ and centre of gravity $0.58\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle; mass of the boat and the motor $m_\text{b} = 430\,\text{kg}$ and centre of gravity $0.12\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle; coupling $3.25\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the vertical force $F$ of the coupling on the drawbar and the nose weight $F : g$
**Principle:** Choose the trailer and the boat together as the body. In the free-body diagram the forces on them are the weight of the trailer $\overline{G}_\text{t}$ and the weight of the boat $\overline{G}_\text{v}$ down from the centres of gravity, the normal force of the ground $\overline{N}$ up at the wheels and the force of the coupling $\overline{F}$ up at the end of the drawbar. At rest the horizontal force of the coupling is zero. The trailer is at rest, so it is in equilibrium: the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments are zero. The axle is chosen as the pivot point, so the lever arm of the unknown normal force is zero. The forces are vertical and the distances horizontal, so the distances are the lever arms of the forces (chapter *Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments*).
**Formula:** $F \cdot 3.25\,\text{m} - m_\text{t} g \cdot 0.58\,\text{m} - m_\text{b} g \cdot 0.12\,\text{m} = 0$, so $F = \dfrac{(m_\text{t} \cdot 0.58\,\text{m} + m_\text{b} \cdot 0.12\,\text{m})\,g}{3.25\,\text{m}}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the masses are in kilograms and the distances in metres.
**Substitution:** The sum of the moments of the weights about the axle is $(165\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.58\,\text{m} + 430\,\text{kg} \cdot 0.12\,\text{m}) \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 147.3\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 1445\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}$, so $F = \dfrac{1445\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}}{3.25\,\text{m}} = 444.6\,\text{N}$, and the nose weight is $\dfrac{444.6\,\text{N}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2} = 45.3\,\text{kg}$.
**Answer:** $F \approx 440\,\text{N}$ and the nose weight about $45\,\text{kg}$, two significant figures like the distances of the centres of gravity $0.58\,\text{m}$ and $0.12\,\text{m}$. The nose weight is below the lower limit of the instructions, $50\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The common centre of gravity is $\tfrac{147.3\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{595\,\text{kg}} \approx 0.25\,\text{m}$ in front of the axle, that is, about $\tfrac{0.25}{3.25} \approx 8\,\%$ of the distance of the coupling. So the coupling carries about $8\,\%$ of the total mass $595\,\text{kg}$, about $45\,\text{kg}$, as found.

### b)
**Given:** from part a $F = 444.6\,\text{N}$; masses $m_\text{t} = 165\,\text{kg}$ and $m_\text{b} = 430\,\text{kg}$; $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the normal force $N$ of the ground on the wheels
**Principle:** The trailer is at rest, so the sum of the vertical forces is zero: the upward normal force and the force of the coupling together carry the weights of the trailer and the boat (chapter *Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments*).
**Formula:** $N + F - (m_\text{t} + m_\text{b})g = 0$, so $N = (m_\text{t} + m_\text{b})g - F$
**Conversions:** Finally $1\,\text{N} = 0.001\,\text{kN}$.
**Substitution:** $N = 595\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 - 444.6\,\text{N} = 5837\,\text{N} - 444.6\,\text{N} = 5392\,\text{N} = 5.39\,\text{kN}$
**Answer:** $N \approx 5.39\,\text{kN}$, three significant figures, because most of it comes from the weight, whose masses are given to three figures.
**Reasonableness:** The wheels carry $\tfrac{5392}{5837} \approx 92\,\%$ of the weight, because the common centre of gravity is close to the axle. The normal force corresponds to a mass of about $550\,\text{kg}$, and the masses total $595\,\text{kg}$: the coupling and the wheels together carry the whole weight.

### c)
**Given:** target nose weight $60\,\text{kg}$; the masses and distances of part a; the boat moved forwards by $\Delta x$
**Find:** the move $\Delta x$
**Principle:** The moment condition about the axle still holds. The move changes only the lever arm of the weight of the boat, from $0.12\,\text{m}$ to $0.12\,\text{m} + \Delta x$. The nose weight $60\,\text{kg}$ corresponds to the force $F = 60\,\text{kg} \cdot g$ (chapter *Moment: the turning effect of a force*).
**Formula:** $60\,\text{kg} \cdot g \cdot 3.25\,\text{m} = m_\text{t} g \cdot 0.58\,\text{m} + m_\text{b} g\,(0.12\,\text{m} + \Delta x)$, so $g$ cancels and $m_\text{b} \Delta x = 60\,\text{kg} \cdot 3.25\,\text{m} - m_\text{t} \cdot 0.58\,\text{m} - m_\text{b} \cdot 0.12\,\text{m}$
**Conversions:** The result is converted to centimetres: $1\,\text{m} = 100\,\text{cm}$.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{b} \Delta x = 195\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} - 95.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} - 51.6\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} = 47.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}$, so $\Delta x = \dfrac{47.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{430\,\text{kg}} = 0.1109\,\text{m} = 0.1109 \cdot 100\,\text{cm} = 11.1\,\text{cm}$
**Answer:** The boat is moved about $11\,\text{cm}$ forwards, two significant figures like the distances of the centres of gravity.
**Reasonableness:** When $430\,\text{kg}$ moves $0.111\,\text{m}$, the moment grows by $47.7\,\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}$, and divided by the lever arm of the coupling, $3.25\,\text{m}$, it adds $\tfrac{47.7}{3.25}\,\text{kg} \approx 15\,\text{kg}$ to the nose weight: $45\,\text{kg} + 15\,\text{kg} = 60\,\text{kg}$. A small move is enough, because the boat is heavy.

### d)
**Find:** how $30\,\text{kg}$ loaded behind the axle changes the nose weight, and why too small a nose weight is dangerous
**Principle:** A weight behind the axle turns the trailer about the axle in the opposite direction to the weights in front of the axle, so it reduces the moment the coupling has to provide (chapter *Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments*).
**Model answer:** The moment of the load about the axle is $30\,\text{kg} \cdot g \cdot 1.40\,\text{m}$, and it reduces the moment of the coupling force. The nose weight decreases by $\tfrac{30\,\text{kg} \cdot 1.40\,\text{m}}{3.25\,\text{m}} \approx 13\,\text{kg}$, from the $45\,\text{kg}$ of part a to about $32\,\text{kg}$. If there were even more load at the back, the common centre of gravity of the trailer and the load would move behind the axle. Then the nose weight would be negative: the coupling would have to pull the drawbar down, and the trailer would lift the rear of the car. The normal force on the rear wheels, and so the upper limit of their static friction, would decrease, and the grip in braking and on bends weakens. A small positive nose weight keeps the centre of gravity of the trailer in front of the axle. That is why a heavy load is placed at the axle or a little in front of it.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- Moments are taken about the coupling, but the normal force $N$ is not included, or both $N$ and its lever arm about the axle are written into the moment condition.
- Distances are measured from the wrong place: the distances of the centres of gravity are from the axle, and the lever arm of the coupling force is $3.25\,\text{m}$, not $3.25\,\text{m} - 0.58\,\text{m}$.
- The nose weight is given with newtons and mass mixed up: $445\,\text{N}$ is not $445\,\text{kg}$.
- In part d the weight loaded behind the axle is added to the nose weight, although its moment is in the opposite direction.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a via the common centre of gravity: $x_\text{cg} = \tfrac{m_\text{t} x_\text{t} + m_\text{b} x_\text{b}}{m_\text{t} + m_\text{b}} \approx 0.248\,\text{m}$ and $F = (m_\text{t} + m_\text{b})g \cdot \tfrac{x_\text{cg}}{3.25\,\text{m}}$, the same result.

## fy9-ympyraliike-s2

### a)
**Given:** height $h = 830\,\text{km}$, mass of the Earth $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and radius $R = 6371\,\text{km}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$
**Find:** the orbital speed $v$ and the period $T$ in minutes
**Principle:** Only the gravitational force of the Earth acts on the satellite, so it is the net force. The orbit is a circle, so by Newton's second law the gravitational force gives the satellite the centripetal acceleration $\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ towards the centre of the Earth. The radius of the orbit is measured from the centre of the Earth, $r = R + h$, and in one revolution the satellite travels the circumference $2\pi r$ (chapter *Satellites and planets in circular orbits*).
**Formula:** $\gamma\dfrac{Mm}{r^2} = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, so the mass of the satellite cancels and $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma M}{r}}$; $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$
**Conversions:** $r = 6371\,\text{km} + 830\,\text{km} = 7201\,\text{km} = 7201 \cdot 1000\,\text{m} = 7.201 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$. Finally $1\,\text{km} = 1000\,\text{m}$ and $1\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $\gamma M = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg} = 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}}$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}}{7.201 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}} = 7438\,\text{m/s} = 7.438\,\text{km/s}$ and $T = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot 7.201 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}}{7438\,\text{m/s}} = 6083\,\text{s} = \dfrac{6083}{60}\,\text{min} = 101.4\,\text{min}$
**Answer:** $v \approx 7.44\,\text{km/s}$ and $T \approx 101\,\text{min}$, three significant figures like the height $830\,\text{km}$ and the mass of the Earth.
**Reasonableness:** The orbital speed is a little smaller than the ISS's roughly $7.7\,\text{km/s}$ and the period a little longer than the ISS's roughly $93\,\text{min}$, because the orbit is higher. In a day the satellite makes $\tfrac{86\,400\,\text{s}}{6083\,\text{s}} \approx 14$ revolutions.

### b)
**Given:** $m = 4100\,\text{kg}$, $r = 7.201 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m}$, $M = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$, $\gamma = 6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}$, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the gravitational force $F$ in orbit and its share of the weight $mg$ at the surface
**Principle:** By the law of gravitation the force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance measured from the centres. The satellite is about $13\,\%$ further from the centre of the Earth than at the surface (chapter *The gravitational force between bodies*).
**Formula:** $F = \gamma\dfrac{Mm}{r^2}$ and the share $\dfrac{F}{mg}$
**Conversions:** $r$ was converted to metres in part a. Finally $1\,\text{N} = 0.001\,\text{kN}$.
**Substitution:** With the product of part a, $\gamma M = 3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}}$, we get $F = \dfrac{3.984 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg} \cdot 4100\,\text{kg}}{(7.201 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{m})^2} = 31\,503\,\text{N} = 31.5\,\text{kN}$. The weight at the surface is $mg = 4100\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 40\,221\,\text{N}$, so $\dfrac{F}{mg} = \dfrac{31\,503\,\text{N}}{40\,221\,\text{N}} = 0.783$.
**Answer:** $F \approx 31.5\,\text{kN}$, three significant figures like the data. The force is about $78\,\%$ of the weight of the satellite at the surface.
**Reasonableness:** The ratio of the distances, $\left(\tfrac{6371\,\text{km}}{7201\,\text{km}}\right)^2 \approx 0.783$, gives the same share. Gravitation is still strong in orbit, and it is exactly what keeps the satellite in its orbit.

### c)
**Given:** period $T = 6083\,\text{s}$ (part a), rotation period of the Earth $T_\text{Earth} = 23\,\text{h}\;56\,\text{min}$, $R = 6371\,\text{km}$
**Find:** the rotation of the Earth in degrees during one revolution and the distance moved by a point on the equator
**Principle:** The Earth rotates steadily, so the rotation is in the same ratio to a full turn as the time is to the rotation period. A point on the equator is in uniform circular motion of radius $R$ (chapter *Moving in a circle: period and speed*).
**Formula:** rotation $\varphi = 360^\circ \cdot \dfrac{T}{T_\text{Earth}}$ and distance $s = 2\pi R \cdot \dfrac{T}{T_\text{Earth}}$
**Conversions:** $T_\text{Earth} = 23 \cdot 3600\,\text{s} + 56 \cdot 60\,\text{s} = 86\,160\,\text{s}$
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{T}{T_\text{Earth}} = \dfrac{6083\,\text{s}}{86\,160\,\text{s}} = 0.07060$, so $\varphi = 360^\circ \cdot 0.07060 = 25.4^\circ$ and $s = 2\pi \cdot 6371\,\text{km} \cdot 0.07060 = 2826\,\text{km}$
**Answer:** The Earth turns through about $25.4^\circ$, and a point on the equator moves about $2830\,\text{km}$, three significant figures like the period.
**Reasonableness:** The rotation is about a fourteenth of a full turn, as it should be when the satellite goes round about $14$ times a day: $14 \cdot 25.4^\circ \approx 360^\circ$. That is why the satellite images a different strip on every revolution, and at the latitude of Finland, where the circumference of the circle of latitude is smaller, the strips are closer together.

### d)
**Find:** why an orbit over the poles rather than a geostationary one is used for accurate northern measurements
**Principle:** The period of a geostationary satellite is the rotation period of the Earth, so its orbit is above the equator at a fixed distance. The height of an orbit over the poles can be chosen (chapter *Satellites and planets in circular orbits*).
**Model answer:** The suggestion has a good side: a geostationary satellite sees the same area all the time, so it can follow, for example, the motion of clouds continuously. For accurate measurements of northern regions it is, however, poorly suited for two reasons. First, it is at a height of $35\,800\,\text{km}$, about $\tfrac{35\,800}{830} \approx 43$ times higher than the satellite orbiting over the poles, and looking at Finland at a slant the distance is even larger, over $38\,000\,\text{km}$. So with the same instrument details look much less sharp. Second, the geostationary orbit is in the plane of the equator, so it sees Finland at a very oblique angle, low above the horizon, and the polar regions not at all. A satellite orbiting over the poles passes over the northern regions on every revolution at a height of about $830\,\text{km}$. That is why both orbits are used: geostationary ones for continuous monitoring and polar ones for accurate measurements.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- The height $h$ is substituted as the radius of the orbit instead of the distance from the centre of the Earth, $R + h$.
- Kilometres are not converted to metres before substituting into a formula in which the unit of the gravitational constant contains the metre.
- In part b the share is calculated from the ratio of the distances without squaring, which gives about $88\,\%$.
- In part c the rotation period of the Earth is taken as $24\,\text{h}$; the difference is small, but the given value is $23\,\text{h}\;56\,\text{min}$.

### Hyväksytään myös
- In part a the period directly from the formula $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{r^3}{\gamma M}}$, the same result.

### c)
**Given:** points of the fitted line $(0.50\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}; 1.01\,\text{N})$ and $(2.50\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}; 4.91\,\text{N})$; radius $r = 0.250\,\text{m}$; weighed mass $0.200\,\text{kg}$
**Find:** the slope $k$ of the line and the mass $m$ of the cart determined from it
**Principle:** According to the model $F = 4\pi^2 mr \cdot \tfrac{1}{T^2}$, so the slope of the line is $k = 4\pi^2 mr$. The slope is read from two points of the fitted line far from each other, not from measured points (chapters *Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle* and *Graphing and fitting a straight line*).
**Reading the graph:** The points $\tfrac{1}{T^2} = 0.50\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$, $F = 1.01\,\text{N}$ and $\tfrac{1}{T^2} = 2.50\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$, $F = 4.91\,\text{N}$ are read from the line; they are circled in the graph.
**Formula:** $k = \dfrac{\Delta F}{\Delta(1/T^2)}$ and $k = 4\pi^2 mr$, so $m = \dfrac{k}{4\pi^2 r}$
**Conversions:** None needed: $\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2 = \text{kg} \cdot \text{m}$, so $\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}} = \text{kg}$.
**Substitution:** $k = \dfrac{4.91\,\text{N} - 1.01\,\text{N}}{2.50\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2} - 0.50\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}} = \dfrac{3.90\,\text{N}}{2.00\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}} = 1.95\,\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2$ and $m = \dfrac{1.95\,\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{4\pi^2 \cdot 0.250\,\text{m}} = 0.1976\,\text{kg}$
**Answer:** $k \approx 1.95\,\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2$ and $m \approx 0.198\,\text{kg}$, three significant figures like the radius $0.250\,\text{m}$ and the forces. The result is about $1\,\%$ smaller than the weighed mass $0.200\,\text{kg}$.
**Reasonableness:** The difference is smaller than the uncertainty of the measurements: the uncertainty of ten revolutions timed by hand is about $0.2\,\text{s}$, about $3\,\%$ at the shortest period, and the force depends on the square of the period. The units of mass and force agree: $\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}} = \tfrac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2 \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{m}} = \text{kg}$.

### d)
**Given:** largest allowed force of the string $F_\text{max} = 8.0\,\text{N}$; from part c $k = 1.95\,\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2$; constant term about $0.03\,\text{N}$
**Find:** the shortest allowed period $T_\text{min}$ and the reliability of the prediction
**Principle:** A shorter period needs a larger force, so the shortest allowed period is the one at which the force of the string is $8.0\,\text{N}$. It is found from the equation of the fitted line when the small constant term is ignored. The value is outside the measured range, so it is an extrapolation (chapter *Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle*).
**Reading the graph:** The extension of the line reaches the force $8.0\,\text{N}$ when $\tfrac{1}{T^2} \approx 4.1\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$, as the orange guide line of the graph shows.
**Formula:** $F_\text{max} = k \cdot \dfrac{1}{T_\text{min}^2}$, so $T_\text{min} = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{F_\text{max}}}$
**Conversions:** None needed: $\sqrt{\tfrac{\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{\text{N}}} = \text{s}$.
**Substitution:** $T_\text{min} = \sqrt{\dfrac{1.95\,\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^2}{8.0\,\text{N}}} = \sqrt{0.244\,\text{s}^2} = 0.494\,\text{s}$
**Answer:** $T_\text{min} \approx 0.49\,\text{s}$, about $2$ revolutions per second, two significant figures like the strength of the string $8.0\,\text{N}$.
**Reasonableness:** The shortest measured period was $0.60\,\text{s}$ and the force $5.42\,\text{N}$. The force grows as the inverse of the square of the period: $5.42\,\text{N} \cdot \left(\tfrac{0.60}{0.49}\right)^2 \approx 8.1\,\text{N}$, so the result is consistent. The prediction is an extrapolation: $\tfrac{1}{T^2} \approx 4.1\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$, while the largest measured value is $2.8\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2}$. It holds if the cart stays at the same distance and the string does not stretch. In addition, the uncertainty of the period is a few per cent, so a safe period is chosen clearly longer, for example at least $0.6\,\text{s}$.

### a)
**Find:** whether the claim that there is no gravity at a height of $400\,\text{km}$ is true
**Principle:** The gravitational field strength $g = \gamma\tfrac{M}{r^2}$ decreases with the square of the distance but never becomes zero. Weightlessness is free fall: only gravitation acts on the body, and the normal force is zero (chapter *Satellites and planets in circular orbits*).
**Model answer:** The claim is wrong. At a height of $400\,\text{km}$ the distance from the centre of the Earth is $6771\,\text{km}$, only about $6\,\%$ more than at the surface. The field strength there is $\gamma\tfrac{M}{r^2} = \tfrac{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}}{(6.771 \cdot 10^{6})^2}\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 8.7\,\text{m/s}^2$, almost $90\,\%$ of its value at the surface. It is gravitation that keeps the station in its orbit: it is the only force on the station and turns the direction of its velocity all the time. Without gravitation the station would carry straight on out of its orbit. Astronauts float because the station and the astronaut fall freely together: both get the same acceleration towards the Earth. So the floor does not press on the astronaut, the normal force is zero, and a scale would read zero, although the weight acts all the time.

### b)
**Find:** why an astronaut feels heavy on the floor of the rotating ring, which direction is “down”, and at what period the force of the floor matches the Earth's surface
**Principle:** An astronaut moving with the ring is in uniform circular motion, so the sum of the forces on them points to the centre of the ring: $\Sigma F = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. The orbital speed is $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ (chapters *Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle* and *Moving in a circle: period and speed*).
**Model answer:** Inside the station only the normal force of the floor acts on the astronaut, when the gravitation of the Earth is ignored because it acts on the station in the same way. The astronaut moves in a circle with the ring, so the net force on them must point to the centre of the ring. That is why the outer wall, the floor, pushes on the soles of their feet towards the centre with the force $N = m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$. On the Earth's surface the same push on the soles feels like supporting one's weight, so the astronaut feels heavy, and “down” is away from the centre of the ring, towards the outer wall. There is no outward centrifugal force: without the floor the astronaut would carry straight on. The floor pushes with as large a force as the Earth's surface when $\tfrac{v^2}{r} = g$. With $v = \tfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ we get $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{r}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{110\,\text{m}}{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}} \approx 21\,\text{s}$, so the ring turns about three times a minute, and the orbital speed of the floor is about $33\,\text{m/s}$.

### c)
**Find:** the motion of the key after it is released as seen from outside the station and from the astronaut's point of view
**Principle:** When no forces act on a body, by Newton's first law it carries on in uniform straight-line motion with the velocity it had. In a circular path the velocity is along the tangent of the path (chapter *Moving in a circle: period and speed*).
**Model answer:** In the hand the key goes round in a circle with the astronaut, and the hand gives it a force pointing to the centre. When the astronaut lets go, no forces act on the key any more, because the gravitation of the Earth is ignored. So, seen from outside, the key carries straight on along the tangent of the path with the velocity it had at the moment of release. The floor, however, curves beneath the path of the key, so the straight path hits the floor after a moment. To the astronaut the key seems to fall “down” to the floor just as on the Earth. The key does not accelerate outwards, and no force pushes it: the floor comes to meet it. Because the hand is closer to the centre than the floor, the orbital speed of the hand is a little smaller than that of the floor, so the key hits the floor a little behind the point right below the feet, against the direction of rotation.

### Yleisiä virheitä
- In part a weightlessness is explained by gravitation being absent, or by a centrifugal force cancelling it.
- In part b an outward centrifugal force that holds the astronaut against the floor is drawn.
- In part b the period is calculated from the formula $T = \tfrac{2\pi r}{v}$ without the speed, or the diameter is substituted in place of the radius.
- In part c the key is said to fly outwards along the radius.

**Given:** knife edge at the $40.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; weight $m_\text{p} = 200\,\text{g}$ at the $10.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; stone at the $82.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; metre stick $m_\text{m} = 120\,\text{g}$, uniform, centre of gravity at the $50.0\,\text{cm}$ mark; reading of the scale $113\,\text{g}$
**Find:** the mass $m_\text{s}$ of the stone and a comparison with the reading of the scale
**Principle:** The stick is at rest, so the sum of the moments about the edge is zero. The edge is not at the centre of gravity of the stick, so the weight of the stick also turns the stick: its lever arm is $10.0\,\text{cm}$, and it turns in the same direction as the stone. The forces are vertical and the stick horizontal, so the lever arms are the distances from the edge (chapter *Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments*).
**Formula:** $m_\text{p} g \cdot 30.0\,\text{cm} - m_\text{s} g \cdot 42.0\,\text{cm} - m_\text{m} g \cdot 10.0\,\text{cm} = 0$, so $g$ cancels and $m_\text{s} = \dfrac{m_\text{p} \cdot 30.0\,\text{cm} - m_\text{m} \cdot 10.0\,\text{cm}}{42.0\,\text{cm}}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the centimetres cancel, and the mass comes out in grams.
**Substitution:** $m_\text{s} \cdot 42.0\,\text{cm} = 200\,\text{g} \cdot 30.0\,\text{cm} - 120\,\text{g} \cdot 10.0\,\text{cm} = 6000\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm} - 1200\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm} = 4800\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}$, so $m_\text{s} = \dfrac{4800\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}}{42.0\,\text{cm}} = 114.3\,\text{g}$
**Answer:** $m_\text{s} \approx 114\,\text{g}$, three significant figures like the measured positions. The result differs from the reading of the scale, $113\,\text{g}$, by about $1\,\%$.
**Reasonableness:** Reading the balance point to $\pm 0.5\,\text{cm}$ changes the result by about $\pm 1.5\,\text{g}$, so the difference from the reading of the scale is within the accuracy of the measurement. The $142.9\,\text{g}$ of the report is $26\,\%$ too large, because the moment of the stick's own weight, $1200\,\text{g} \cdot \text{cm}$, was left out.

### b)
**Find:** an assessment of the conclusion and the reliability of the report and justifications of its shortcomings
**Principle:** The moment condition includes every force that has a lever arm about the chosen point. A systematic error shifts the result in one direction, a random error scatters the results in both directions (chapters *Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments* and *Measuring and the reliability of a measurement*).
**Model answer:** The conclusion of the report is wrong. The biggest shortcoming is in the calculation model: the knife edge was not at the centre of gravity of the stick, so the stick's own weight acted with the lever arm $10.0\,\text{cm}$ and turned the stick in the same direction as the stone. When its moment was left out, too much was left for the moment of the stone, and the result was systematically about $26\,\%$ too large. With the correct moment condition the result is $114\,\text{g}$, and the difference from the scale is only about $1\,\%$. Friction does not explain the difference: friction at the edge would make the balance point slightly uncertain in either direction, but it would not cause a large error in one direction. In addition, the report does not back up friction with any observation. The measurement was made only once, so the size of the random error cannot be estimated. The uncertainty is missing altogether, and the result $142.857\,\text{g}$ gives too many figures: reading the balance point to about $\pm 0.5\,\text{cm}$ changes the mass by about $\pm 1.5\,\text{g}$.

### c)
**Find:** a plan for an improved experiment with the same equipment
**Principle:** A reliable result is obtained when the model includes all the forces, there are several measurements and the result is determined from a graph by fitting. A quantity that is kept constant is recorded (chapters *Equilibrium of a body: forces and moments* and *Your own experimental investigation: planning, carrying out and reporting*).
**Model answer:** The simplest improvement is to place the knife edge at the centre of gravity of the stick, at the $50.0\,\text{cm}$ mark, so that the lever arm of the stick's weight is zero. First check that the empty stick stays horizontal. The support and the string of the stone are kept the same throughout the experiment. The weight is hung in turn at, for example, five different distances from the edge, and each time the balance point of the stone is found and its distance $d_\text{s}$ from the edge is read. Each balance is repeated three times, and the mean of the results is calculated. From the moment condition $m_\text{p} d_\text{p} = m_\text{s} d_\text{s}$, so the graph of the moment of the weight $m_\text{p} d_\text{p}$ as a function of the lever arm of the stone $d_\text{s}$ is a straight line through the origin whose slope is the mass of the stone. If the edge is left somewhere other than the centre of gravity, the line gets a constant term, which is the moment of the stick's weight, and the slope is still the mass of the stone. The uncertainty is estimated from the scatter of the points or with the steepest and the least steep line, and the result is compared with the reading of the scale with their uncertainties.

## fy9-ympyraliike-s6

### a)
**Given:** difference in height from the top of the first hill to the bottom of the loop $h = 30.0\,\text{m}$, initial speed $0$, $12\,\%$ of the mechanical energy turns into internal energy, $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$
**Find:** the speed $v_\text{b}$ at the bottom of the loop
**Principle:** Choose the bottom of the loop as the zero level. The mechanical energy of the car at the top of the first hill is $mgh$. Friction and air resistance do negative work that turns $12\,\%$ of it into internal energy, so by the work–energy principle the kinetic energy at the bottom is $88\,\%$ of the initial potential energy (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*).
**Formula:** $\tfrac12 mv_\text{b}^2 = 0.88 \cdot mgh$, so the mass cancels and $v_\text{b} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 0.88 \cdot gh}$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in base units. For comparison $1\,\text{m/s} = 3.6\,\text{km/h}$.
**Substitution:** $v_\text{b} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 0.88 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 30.0\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{518.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 22.76\,\text{m/s}$
**Answer:** $v_\text{b} \approx 22.8\,\text{m/s}$, about $82\,\text{km/h}$, three significant figures like the height $30.0\,\text{m}$.
**Reasonableness:** Without losses the speed would be $\sqrt{2gh} \approx 24.3\,\text{m/s}$. The losses reduce it by a factor of about $\sqrt{0.88} \approx 0.94$, and $82\,\text{km/h}$ is a usual top speed for a roller coaster.

### b)
**Given:** $v_\text{b} = 22.76\,\text{m/s}$, radius of the circle $r = 10.0\,\text{m}$, mass of the passenger $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$
**Find:** the force $N$ of the seat at the bottom and its ratio to the weight $mg$
**Principle:** Choose the passenger as the body. In the free-body diagram the forces on them are the weight $\overline{G}$ down and the normal force of the seat $\overline{N}$ up; no centrifugal force is drawn. At the bottom the passenger is for a moment on a circular path whose centre is above. At the bottom there is no net force along the tangent, so the net force points up and is $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ (chapter *Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle*).
**Formula:** With the direction up towards the centre positive, $N - mg = m\dfrac{v_\text{b}^2}{r}$, so $N = m\left(g + \dfrac{v_\text{b}^2}{r}\right)$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in base units.
**Substitution:** $\dfrac{v_\text{b}^2}{r} = \dfrac{518.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{10.0\,\text{m}} = 51.80\,\text{m/s}^2$, so $N = 60.0\,\text{kg} \cdot (9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 + 51.80\,\text{m/s}^2) = 3696\,\text{N}$. Compared with the weight $\dfrac{N}{mg} = \dfrac{3696\,\text{N}}{588.6\,\text{N}} = 6.28$.
**Answer:** $N \approx 3.70\,\text{kN}$, three significant figures like the mass and the radius. The force is about $6.3$ times the weight of the passenger.
**Reasonableness:** The centripetal acceleration is over five times the acceleration of free fall, so the seat must press on the passenger with over six times the weight: one $mg$ holds up the weight and the rest turns the motion. Such a large force would feel violent, which is the reason for the shape in part d.

### c)
**Given:** $v_\text{b}^2 = 518.0\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, the top $2r = 20.0\,\text{m}$ above the bottom, $r = 10.0\,\text{m}$, $m = 60.0\,\text{kg}$; no losses in the loop
**Find:** the speed $v_\text{t}$ at the top and the force $N$ of the seat at the top
**Principle:** In the loop mechanical energy is conserved, so kinetic energy turns into potential energy in the rise $2r$ (chapter *Conservation of mechanical energy and the work–energy principle*). At the top the passenger is upside down, and the centre is below them. The weight and the normal force of the seat both point down towards the centre, and their sum is $m\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ (chapter *Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle*).
**Formula:** $\tfrac12 mv_\text{b}^2 = \tfrac12 mv_\text{t}^2 + mg \cdot 2r$, so $v_\text{t} = \sqrt{v_\text{b}^2 - 4gr}$; at the top $N + mg = m\dfrac{v_\text{t}^2}{r}$, so $N = m\left(\dfrac{v_\text{t}^2}{r} - g\right)$
**Conversions:** None needed: the quantities are in base units.
**Substitution:** $v_\text{t} = \sqrt{518.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} - 4 \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 10.0\,\text{m}} = \sqrt{125.6\,\tfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = 11.21\,\text{m/s}$ and $N = 60.0\,\text{kg} \cdot \left(\dfrac{125.6\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{10.0\,\text{m}} - 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\right) = 60.0\,\text{kg} \cdot 2.75\,\text{m/s}^2 = 165\,\text{N}$
**Answer:** $v_\text{t} \approx 11.2\,\text{m/s}$ and $N \approx 165\,\text{N}$, three significant figures. The seat presses on the passenger even at the top, but only with about $0.28$ times the weight.
**Reasonableness:** The passenger stays in the seat without a safety bar if $N \ge 0$, that is, $v_\text{t}^2 \ge gr$: the limit is $v_\text{t} \ge \sqrt{9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot 10.0\,\text{m}} \approx 9.9\,\text{m/s}$. The speed $11.2\,\text{m/s}$ exceeds the limit only a little, so the safety bar is still needed. The difference between the forces at the top and at the bottom is about $6\,mg$.

### d)
**Find:** why a loop whose radius of curvature is small at the top and large in the lower part is better for the passenger
**Principle:** The force of the seat depends on the centripetal acceleration $\tfrac{v^2}{r}$: at the bottom $N = m\left(g + \tfrac{v^2}{r}\right)$ and at the top $N = m\left(\tfrac{v^2}{r} - g\right)$ (chapter *Centripetal acceleration and the equation of motion in a circle*).
**Model answer:** In the circle model the passenger feels over six times their weight at the bottom, but only $0.28$ times at the top. In the lower part the speed is greatest, so a large radius of curvature reduces the centripetal acceleration $\tfrac{v^2}{r}$ and the force of the seat exactly where it would otherwise be most violent. At the top the speed is smallest, and the condition $v^2 \ge gr$ decides whether the passenger stays in the seat. A small radius of curvature at the top meets the condition even at a lower speed, so the loop can be made higher or the first hill lower. So the shape evens out the force of the seat at different points of the loop: at the bottom it is smaller and at the top surely positive.
